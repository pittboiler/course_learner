# Apologetics II: The Catholic Claims — Syllabus

> Catholic Theology · Tier 2 · ~29 lessons · Prereqs: [`ecclesiology-and-mariology`](../ecclesiology-and-mariology/syllabus.md), [`sacraments-and-liturgy`](../sacraments-and-liturgy/syllabus.md) · Roadmap id: `apologetics-catholic-claims`

## Goal

This is the intra-Christian half of the apologetics pair, and the half where almost everything is shared ground. Scripture, the creeds of Nicaea and Constantinople, the Trinity, the Incarnation, baptism, the resurrection, the moral law: none of that is in dispute here. What is in dispute is **authority and interpretation** — who may say what the shared texts mean, and by what warrant. You will finish able to state *sola scriptura* as the Westminster Confession, the Book of Concord and Calvin state it, and as its serious modern defenders restate it, and then argue against it on the canon, on sufficiency and on perspicuity without once attacking the version no Protestant holds; to say who fixed the canon and on what authority, and to find the actual crux — not "who made the list" but whether a recognition can be more certain than the body recognizing it; to state justification by faith alone from the Augsburg Confession and its Apology, the Formula of Concord and Westminster, set it against Trent Session VI clause by clause, say exactly what the 1999 Joint Declaration achieved and what it left standing, and explain Catholic merit so that the objection it usually draws misses; to argue the Petrine texts and the patristic record for primacy while conceding what that record will not carry, and to meet the Protestant and Orthodox counter-readings in their authors' terms; to read *Apostolicae Curae* and the Anglican archbishops' reply to it and say what each argument turns on; to state the Orthodox case — the filioque in doctrine and in the lawfulness of its addition, primacy as the East reads it, Vatican I as Orthodox theologians read it, essence and energies, marriage and *oikonomia*, the state of the dead — as Lossky, Meyendorff, Ware and Romanides state it, with real concessions and no pretence that the dialogue documents have settled more than they have; to defend the contested Catholic distinctives — the Marian dogmas, the saints and their images, penance, purgatory and indulgences, the real presence, the sacrifice of the Mass — each against the specific confession that attacks it rather than against "Protestantism" in general; to show that development of doctrine is the question underneath all of them, and to run Newman's notes on a case where they tell against you; and to say what the Catholic case must simply concede about the Inquisition, Galileo, the sale of indulgences and the abuse crisis, and what it can still answer.

It deliberately skips everything that divides Christians from non-Christians — the existence of God, the resurrection as history, Judaism, Islam, pluralism — which is [`apologetics-foundations`](../apologetics-foundations/syllabus.md)'s, and which that course's Scope table reserves to itself as firmly as it hands this course everything intra-Christian. It skips the doctrines themselves: it does not teach ecclesiology, sacramental theology, grace or the Trinity, because four courses on disk teach them properly and cede only the arguing. It skips the events — 1054, the Reformation, Trent, Vatican I, Vatican II — to [`church-history`](../church-history/syllabus.md). It skips development as a method to [`fundamental-theology`](../fundamental-theology/syllabus.md) Module 5, whose notes it uses without re-deriving. And it skips any survey of Protestant or Orthodox theology for its own sake: other traditions appear here only where a Catholic claim is under attack, stated at the strength their own confessions and theologians give them.

## How practice works in this course

Problems follow [HUMANITIES-BUILD-BRIEF.md](../../HUMANITIES-BUILD-BRIEF.md). This is the second of the two courses where the **apologetic** kind dominates, and it inherits the sibling's mechanics unchanged:

- **Every apologetic problem has two parts, and the first is exegetical.** Part (a) asks for the opposing position — Westminster I on scripture, Apology IV on justification, Calvin on the Supper, Article XXXI on the sacrifices of Masses, Lossky on the filioque, Meyendorff on primacy, the archbishops' reply to *Apostolicae Curae* — stated as its own confession or author states it. It is graded **strictly, as exegesis**, and a wrong statement fails the problem however good the reply that follows. Caricature is the error this course marks hardest, and the commonest caricature here is not malice but blur: attacking *solo scriptura* ("me and my Bible") while calling it *sola scriptura*, or attributing Zwingli's memorialism to Calvin.
- **Part (b) is the reply, graded on its quality, not its conclusion.** A reply that concludes the objection succeeds passes if it is a good reply. Several problems require a **named concession**: the one thing the other side has right, stated flatly, before anything is said against it. The concessions are written into this syllabus, not left to charity.
- **"Protestant" is not a position, and stating it as one is an exegetical error.** Lutheran, Reformed, Anglican and Baptist confessions differ from each other on the Supper, on baptism, on the Church and on the relation of law and gospel, sometimes more than any of them differs from Rome. Every problem names the confession it is arguing with. The same discipline applies eastward: **Orthodoxy has no magisterium that issues binding definitions the way the West does**, so a position may not be stated from the seventeenth-century confessions alone (Dositheus, Mogila), which modern Orthodox theologians themselves treat as of limited authority and partly Latinized. Where Orthodoxy has no defined position — the state of the dead is the clearest case — saying so *is* the right answer.
- **Exegetical parts are strict about weight on both sides.** Overstating a Catholic level (saying Trent defined what its chapters only expound, or that Vatican I defined papal impeccability) is an error; so is overstating a Protestant one (treating the Book of Concord as a magisterium, or a theologian's book as a confession), and so is overstating an ecumenical text (saying the Joint Declaration revoked Trent's canons, or that Ravenna settled primacy). Levels come from [`fundamental-theology`](../fundamental-theology/syllabus.md) 4.4–4.5 and nowhere else.
- **Evaluative parts, verdict-neutral, are kept for questions genuinely open**: whether material sufficiency is the better Catholic account of Tradition; how much weight the first-millennium record can bear for universal jurisdiction; whether the essence-energies distinction is compatible with divine simplicity as Latin theology states it; whether the Joint Declaration's differentiated consensus is a real agreement or a postponement; whether the Anglican orders question is closed; whether the development of Marian doctrine satisfies Newman's notes. Any verdict passes if the moves are made.
- **Close reading (archetype 7) is the workhorse,** and everything read closely is public domain: the Westminster Confession and the Thirty-Nine Articles in Schaff's *Creeds of Christendom*; the Augsburg Confession, its Apology and the Formula of Concord in the 1921 Triglot Concordia; Calvin's *Institutes* in Beveridge; Trent in Waterworth's 1848 translation; *Pastor Aeternus* in Schaff; Nicaea II and the councils in Percival's volume of the *Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers*; *Apostolicae Curae* (1896) and the Anglican archbishops' *Saepius Officio* (1897); Newman's *Essay on Development* (1845), *Lectures on Justification* (1838, written as an Anglican) and *Letter to Pusey* (1866); Francis de Sales's *The Catholic Controversy* (Mackey, 1886); scripture in the Douay-Rheims (Challoner), with the lesson's own rendering where a Greek word decides a reading.
- **Find the crux (archetype 5) is the second workhorse,** because in this course nearly every dispute has one: the single premise one side must deny. Whether an infallible canon requires an infallible recognizer; whether justifying righteousness can be both imputed and infused; whether a Petrine function can become a Petrine office without a theory of development; whether an addition to a creed can be doctrinally true and canonically unlawful at once; whether the Mass can be a true sacrifice without being another one.
- **Diagnose (archetype 4), exegetical, and pointed in both directions.** Invented tract paragraphs, debate openings, apologetics blog posts and parish talks that get it wrong — a Catholic tract claiming Protestants "made up their own Bible"; a Reformed post claiming Catholics teach that we earn salvation; an Orthodox post claiming Vatican I made the pope a fourth person of the Trinity; a Catholic talk claiming the Joint Declaration ended the Reformation. Invented material is never attributed to a real person.
- **There are no formal parts.** The sibling's odds-form work belongs to a historical case; nothing here is settled by a likelihood ratio, and a probability attached to "the Fathers believed X" would be a decoration. The formal discipline in this course is exegetical precision instead.

**Accuracy discipline.** No invented quotations, ever, on any side. No session number, canon number, Denzinger number, confessional article or document section that has not been checked; where a number is uncertain the document is named without one. Modern Orthodox and Protestant theologians are paraphrased and cited by work. "Most Protestants hold" and "the Orthodox believe" are written only where accurate, and always as reports.

## Dangerous Checklist

When you finish, you can:

- [ ] Name what is shared — canon of the creeds, Trinity, Incarnation, scripture as the word of God, baptism — and locate any intra-Christian dispute on the two axes that actually carry it: the seat of authority and the rule of interpretation
- [ ] State *sola scriptura* as Westminster I, the Formula of Concord and Calvin state it, tell it apart from *solo scriptura*, and give its best modern restatement before arguing against it
- [ ] Argue the canon question to its real crux — not who drew up the list, but whether a recognition can carry more certainty than the body that recognizes — stating the self-authentication model and what it explains
- [ ] Distinguish material from formal sufficiency, say why conceding the first costs nothing, argue from the New Testament's own appeals to tradition without proof-texting them, and test perspicuity as the confessions actually state it rather than as caricature states it
- [ ] State justification as Augsburg IV, Apology IV, Formula III and Westminster XI state it — imputation, the alien righteousness, faith as instrument, assurance — and set it against Trent Session VI clause by clause, naming where the two are contradictory and where they are talking past each other
- [ ] Read Paul and James together without flattening either, and say exactly what the New Perspective did and did not do to the sixteenth-century dispute
- [ ] Say precisely what the 1999 Joint Declaration claims, what its Annex adds, what its Lutheran and Catholic critics said, and what Trent's canons still condemn — then explain Catholic merit so that the objection it usually draws misses
- [ ] Argue the Petrine texts, meet Calvin's and Cullmann's readings in their own terms, and say what the patristic record for Roman primacy will and will not carry without a theory of development
- [ ] Read *Apostolicae Curae* and *Saepius Officio* against each other, name what each argument turns on, and state where the question of Anglican orders now stands
- [ ] State the Orthodox case on the filioque in its two separate questions — the doctrine, and the lawfulness of the addition — and say what the 1995 clarification concedes
- [ ] State papal primacy as Meyendorff, Ware, Afanasiev and Romanides read it, say what Ravenna and the later dialogues achieved and what they postponed, and give the Orthodox reading of Vatican I without softening universal ordinary jurisdiction
- [ ] State the essence-energies distinction as Palamas's defenders state it, say what Catholic theologians have said for and against it, and be clear that no magisterial condemnation of it exists
- [ ] Defend the Marian dogmas, the invocation of saints, the veneration of images, penance, purgatory, indulgences, the real presence and the sacrifice of the Mass, each against the specific confession that attacks it and with the abuse conceded first — and keep the Lutheran, Reformed and Zwinglian doctrines of the Supper apart while doing it
- [ ] Run Newman's notes on a Catholic distinctive where they are hard to satisfy, and say what the argument from silence proves and what it cannot
- [ ] Say what the Catholic case must concede about the Inquisition, Galileo, the sale of indulgences and the abuse crisis, and what indefectibility and infallibility actually claim about a Church that did those things

## Modules

### Module 1: Arguing among Christians

Before anything is argued: what is already agreed, what the Church says about the people on the other side of the table, what four centuries of polemic cost, and how to use historical evidence without abusing it.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1.1 | Shared ground, and where the argument actually is | Locate any intra-Christian dispute on the two axes that carry it, and say what both sides already grant | the common inheritance: the canon of the creeds, the Trinity and the Incarnation, scripture as the inspired word of God, baptism, the moral law, the resurrection and the last judgment — none of it in dispute here, and an apologist who argues as if it were has lost the thread; the **two axes**: the seat of authority (scripture alone / scripture and Tradition read by a magisterium / scripture and Tradition received by the conciliar consciousness of the Church) and the rule of interpretation; why almost every downstream dispute is a special case of one of the two; how this course differs from [`apologetics-foundations`](../apologetics-foundations/syllabus.md), whose account of what apologetics is and cannot do (its 1.1) is assumed here and not restated; the asymmetry of burdens — the Catholic claim is the larger one and carries the larger burden, and saying so early is honest rather than weak |
| 1.2 | What the Church says about other Christians | State the Catholic doctrine about other Christians accurately, and make it a constraint on how you argue | taken whole from [`ecclesiology-and-mariology`](../ecclesiology-and-mariology/syllabus.md) 5.2–5.3 and not re-derived: *subsistit in*; elements of sanctification and truth outside the visible boundaries; the Eastern churches called Churches and the Reformation bodies ecclesial communities, with the criterion that does the work; baptism as a real sacramental bond already existing; *Unitatis Redintegratio*'s acknowledgement that blame for the divisions falls on both sides and that those born into them are not personally guilty of schism; the hierarchy of truths and what it does *not* relativize; what all this forbids an apologist — treating the other side as outside Christ, arguing for a rhetorical win, or letting "they are separated brethren" become a reason not to argue at all; false irenicism named as the opposite failure |
| 1.3 | Arguing from history without abusing it | Say what historical evidence can prove in a doctrinal dispute, and name the four ways it is habitually misused | the four abuses, each with a real example on the **Catholic** side first: the argument from silence run as proof (a doctrine unattested in 150 is not thereby denied in 150 — and is not thereby attested either); anachronism (reading a fourth-century word with its sixteenth-century sense); the cherry-picked Father, against [`patristics`](../patristics/syllabus.md) 1.1 on weighing one Father against a consensus; and the manufactured consensus ("the Fathers unanimously taught"), which the same lesson's criteria dismantle; what a *development* would look like in the evidence versus what a *corruption* would look like, using Vincent's canon and Newman's notes as tests rather than conclusions ([`fundamental-theology`](../fundamental-theology/syllabus.md) 5.1–5.2); grading an argument by its weakest locus (its 5.5) applied to historical premises; the history of polemic and its costs — Eck and Luther, Bellarmine's *Controversies*, Francis de Sales's *The Catholic Controversy* as a method that was better than its era's, and the forgeries, atrocity stories and confessional histories on every side |

Overstating or understating a level is an error. *(Exegetical, strict.)* (c) Four invented paragraphs are supplied: a Catholic tract, a Reformed blog post, an Orthodox forum comment and a parish ecumenism talk. Use *Unitatis Redintegratio*'s own statements rather than a slogan, name the failure mode at each end, and say what your answer implies about how a Catholic should open a conversation with a Lutheran pastor. *(Evaluative.)*

### Module 2: Against sola scriptura

The formal principle of the Reformation, stated from the confessions and from its best modern defenders, and then tested at its three joints: the canon, sufficiency, and clarity.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2.1 | Sola scriptura at its best | State the doctrine as its confessions and its serious defenders state it, and stop before replying | Westminster ch. 1 read closely: scripture's authority depending on God and not on the testimony of any man or Church; the whole counsel of God either expressly set down or deducible by good and necessary consequence; the inward work of the Spirit; and the supreme judge of controversies being the Holy Spirit speaking in the scripture; Calvin's *Institutes* I.7 on the self-authenticating word and the inward testimony; the Lutheran shape of the principle and how it differs — the Book of Concord as a *norma normata* under scripture as *norma normans*, a real subordinate authority the Reformed confessions describe differently; **the distinction that has to be got right**: Heiko Oberman's Tradition I and Tradition II, and Keith Mathison's use of it to mark *sola scriptura* (scripture as sole infallible authority, with the creeds and the Church as real but fallible authorities) off from *solo scriptura* (the individual with a Bible), which the confessions themselves reject; Michael Kruger and Kevin Vanhoozer as the position's current defenders; what a Catholic may not say after this lesson |
| 2.2 | The canon question | Argue the canon dispute down to its real crux, and state the self-authentication model before answering it | the history assumed, not retold ([`old-testament`](../old-testament/syllabus.md) 6.1, [`new-testament`](../new-testament/syllabus.md) 6.6): the Jamnia story and its collapse, Melito and Origen's lists, Jerome's *Hebraica veritas* against Augustine, Hippo and Carthage, Marcion as catalyst, the books disputed longest; Trent Session IV defining the canon "entire with all their parts" ([`fundamental-theology`](../fundamental-theology/syllabus.md) 3.3), and the Catholic claim that the Church *recognizes* rather than confers inspiration — which is also what the Reformed confessions say, so the disagreement is not where tracts put it; **the crux**: can the list be infallible if the recognizing body is not? the Reformed answers — the Spirit's internal testimony, and Kruger's self-authenticating model (the canon bears divine attributes, is corporately received, and is apostolic), stated at full strength; the Catholic reply and its cost; the honest concessions: the Reformers did not invent their Old Testament canon out of nothing, Jerome and Cajetan doubted the deuterocanon, and the Catholic canon was not universally and explicitly closed before 1546; Luther's judgements on James, Hebrews, Jude and Revelation stated accurately — a prefatory judgement, not an excision |

| 2.4 | Perspicuity tested | State the clarity thesis as the confessions state it, and test it against the record without cheating | Westminster ch. 1's careful version — not all things alike plain, but those necessary to be known for salvation clearly propounded somewhere, so that learned and unlearned alike, in a due use of the ordinary means, may attain a sufficient understanding — and the three qualifiers a Catholic apologist usually drops; Luther's distinction between the external and internal clarity of scripture, stated as he states it; the Catholic argument from the record: the sixteenth-century divisions over baptism and the Supper were between men all holding perspicuity, and Acts 8:31 and 2 Peter 3:16 are scripture's own comment; the Protestant replies at full strength — that the divisions concern what the thesis never claimed to settle, that "ordinary means" includes pastors, creeds and the community, and that Catholic unity is achieved by authority rather than by clarity and so is not a like-for-like comparison; the counter-reply about *which* disputes turned out to be about things necessary; the crux: whether a rule of faith that needs an authority to apply it is still a rule of faith alone |

| 6.6 | Development, or corruption? | Run Newman's notes on a case where they are hard to satisfy, and say what the argument from silence can prove | the question under every lesson of this module: each distinctive is attested later and less clearly than a Catholic would like, and the Catholic answer in every case is development ([`fundamental-theology`](../fundamental-theology/syllabus.md) 5.2); Newman's *Essay* (1845, public domain) and the seven notes, applied here as tests and not as a formula that always returns the desired verdict; Vincent's canon and the tension between "everywhere, always, by all" and any development at all, which Newman's *Essay* exists to resolve; the Protestant counter-theory at full strength — that development is an unfalsifiable device that can legitimate anything, and that the sixteenth century's *ad fontes* was the right response to real accretion; the Orthodox counter-theory, which is different again: not accretion but the claim that the faith is not developed but received, and that the West has mistaken theological elaboration for doctrinal growth; the honest test — pick the Catholic distinctive with the thinnest early attestation and run the notes on it, saying which note it satisfies weakly; what the argument from silence proves, which is less than either side wants |

**Boss problem 6:** Supplied, public domain: Thirty-Nine Articles XXII, XXVIII and XXXI; Trent Session XIII's chapter and canons on the real presence and Session XXII's chapter on the sacrifice (Waterworth); and a short passage from Newman's *Essay on Development* on one of the notes. (a) State the Lutheran, Reformed and Zwinglian doctrines of the Eucharistic presence in three short paragraphs, each in words that tradition would sign, and name the point at which each differs from the other two. Collapsing any two of them fails the part. *(Exegetical, strict.)* (b) Close-read Article XXXI beside Trent Session XXII's chapter: state exactly what the Article condemns, exactly what the chapter claims, and whether the two statements contradict each other or describe different things. Name the words that decide your answer. *(Exegetical, strict.)* (c) Choose one Catholic distinctive from this module and run at least three of Newman's notes on it, including the one it satisfies **least** well, and say so explicitly. *(Exegetical, then evaluative.)* (d) In 400–500 words, reply: defend one of the Mass as sacrifice, the invocation of saints, or indulgences, against the confessional text that attacks it. Concede the historical abuse in the first sentence, without qualification. State the objection as a considered doctrinal position rather than a misunderstanding. Then argue, and say what evidence would show your distinctive to be a corruption rather than a development. Any verdict passes on how strong the case is; a reply that treats its opponent as merely confused fails. *(Apologetic.)*

### Module 7: The hard history

The arguments that are not about doctrine at all, and that land hardest. The rule of this module is the rule of 1.3, applied to the Catholic side: state the record first, from [`church-history`](../church-history/syllabus.md), and concede before answering.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 7.1 | The Inquisition and the Galileo affair | Answer the argument from institutional error against the Church's claim to be divinely guided | the record first, taken from [`church-history`](../church-history/syllabus.md) 5.3 and 7.3 and not softened: the medieval inquisitions and what the inquisitors' own registers show, the Spanish Inquisition as a royal institution with the numbers as historians now give them and the Black Legend corrected in **both** directions, and the condemnation of Galileo as a real error really made; the argument as a Protestant or Orthodox controversialist makes it: a body that condemned Galileo and burned dissenters cannot be the body Christ preserves from error; **the Catholic answer, which is a distinction and not an excuse**: what infallibility actually claims and the conditions attached to it ([`ecclesiology-and-mariology`](../ecclesiology-and-mariology/syllabus.md) 4.3–4.4), so that a disciplinary decree of the Holy Office is not a definition; indefectibility distinguished from impeccability (its 5.4); the limits of the distinction, said out loud — it answers the doctrinal charge and does not answer the moral one; why this course does **not** re-run the conflict-thesis material of [`apologetics-foundations`](../apologetics-foundations/syllabus.md) 4.1 or the Bellarmine exercise of [`church-history`](../church-history/syllabus.md) Boss 7 |
| 7.2 | The Reformation's grievances | Say which of the sixteenth-century grievances were justified, and what follows for the Catholic case | the grievances as the reformers made them, distinguished from the doctrines: simony, pluralism and absenteeism, episcopal sees held as property, the Renaissance papacy, clerical ignorance, and the financing of St Peter's through the indulgence campaign ([`church-history`](../church-history/syllabus.md) 6.1 for the events, 6.3 for indulgences as doctrine); the *Consilium de Emendanda Ecclesia* (1537) as a Catholic indictment written by cardinals before Trent, named and paraphrased; Duffy and the historiographical dispute over how decayed the late medieval Church actually was, with both sides; **the concession that matters**: most of the disciplinary grievances were justified, Trent conceded them by legislating against them, and the honest Catholic argument is not that the Church was healthy but that a corrupt Church can still be the Church — which is the Donatist question returning in a new form ([`ecclesiology-and-mariology`](../ecclesiology-and-mariology/syllabus.md) 2.2); the counter-question to the other side, asked without triumphalism: what the reformers' own settlements produced where they held power; the disciplinary/doctrinal distinction as the load-bearing move, and the objection that it is too convenient |
| 7.3 | The abuse crisis, and the argument from scandal | Answer the hardest contemporary argument without a defensive move the facts do not support | the record from [`church-history`](../church-history/syllabus.md) 9.3 and the ecclesiology from [`ecclesiology-and-mariology`](../ecclesiology-and-mariology/syllabus.md) 5.4, both taken as given and neither softened; the argument in its intra-Christian form: an institution that concealed the abuse of children for decades cannot be the one Christ founded, and its claim to teaching authority is discredited by its exercise of authority; the comparative move ("it happened everywhere") named as the single worst answer available and refused outright — it is true, it is irrelevant, and using it is a moral failure as well as an argumentative one; what the tradition's resources actually reach: the *corpus permixtum*, indefectibility as distinct from impeccability, and the exact point at which each stops being an answer; clericalism as named in the 2018 Letter to the People of God; the structures created since 2019, described as acts rather than as arguments; **the concession the module ends on**: on this question the Catholic case does not win, and the honest position is that the argument from scandal is not answered but survived — if the Church's claim is true, it is true of a Church that did this |

**Boss problem 7:** (a) State, from the record in [`church-history`](../church-history/syllabus.md) 5.3 and 7.3 and without softening, what the Galileo condemnation actually decided, by which body, and at what level; and state what the Spanish Inquisition was institutionally and what historians now give as the scale of its executions, marking the figures as estimates and naming the correction the Black Legend needs in both directions. Overstating or minimizing either record is an error of the same kind. *(Exegetical, strict.)* (b) An invented Reformed apologetics post and an invented Orthodox forum comment are supplied, each arguing from the Church's historical crimes to the falsity of her claims. Reconstruct the shared argument in numbered premises, and identify the single premise the Catholic answer of 7.1 denies — and the premise it does **not** deny. *(Exegetical, archetype 1 and 5.)* (c) List the sixteenth-century grievances of 7.2 in two columns: those a Catholic should concede were justified, and those the historical record does not support. Every entry cites its evidence. *(Exegetical.)* (d) Capstone, 500 words, any verdict: has this course's cumulative case — authority (Modules 2 and 4), justification (Module 3), the Orthodox questions (Module 5), the distinctives (Module 6) — survived Module 7? Name the single weakest point in the whole Catholic case as you now see it, name the strongest thing you would concede to a Lutheran, to an Anglican and to an Orthodox theologian, and say what would have to be true for the Catholic claim to fail. An answer that reaches a confident conclusion without engaging the abuse crisis on its own terms fails the rubric, whatever its verdict. *(Evaluative.)*

## Sources of truth

If a lesson's statement of a Protestant position cannot be matched to a clause in one of these, it is wrong.
- **Serious modern Protestant theologians, paraphrased and cited by work:** Heiko Oberman on Tradition I and Tradition II; Keith A. Mathison, *The Shape of Sola Scriptura*; Michael J. Kruger, *Canon Revisited*; Kevin Vanhoozer on a "mere Protestant" retrieval; Oscar Cullmann, *Peter: Disciple, Apostle, Martyr*; Martin Chemnitz, *Examination of the Council of Trent* (Latin original public domain; the standard English translation is modern, so it is paraphrased); Anthony Lane on the justification dialogue; Brian Tierney on the origins of the infallibility doctrine.

## Notes

- **Why this course argues, and how it is kept honest.** Four courses on disk — [`ecclesiology-and-mariology`](../ecclesiology-and-mariology/syllabus.md), [`sacraments-and-liturgy`](../sacraments-and-liturgy/syllabus.md), [`creation-grace-last-things`](../creation-grace-last-things/syllabus.md) and [`trinity-and-god`](../trinity-and-god/syllabus.md) — state other traditions' positions carefully and then refuse to reply, and two of them say in their Notes that they are built that way so this course can argue from a base where the Catholic doctrine has been taught properly and the alternatives have been stated without a rebuttal attached. That is the load-bearing arrangement, and it produces the three rules the lessons enforce: the opposing view is stated first and graded first; every module carries at least one concession written into this syllabus; and no rubric anywhere requires Jacob to reach the Catholic conclusion.
- **Ownership collisions found and resolved.** (1) [`ecclesiology-and-mariology`](../ecclesiology-and-mariology/syllabus.md) 4.1–4.5 and Module 6 cover exactly the material of Modules 4 and 6.1 — and its Notes name this course as the place where they are argued, so 4.1–4.2 and 6.1 argue from its statements and re-teach none of them. (2) [`sacraments-and-liturgy`](../sacraments-and-liturgy/syllabus.md)'s Notes list what it hands over, item by item; 4.3, 5.5 and 6.3–6.5 are that list and nothing more. (3) [`creation-grace-last-things`](../creation-grace-last-things/syllabus.md) 5.1–5.3 and 6.3 supply Module 3 and 6.3 with doctrine already stated, including the Joint Declaration read as a text; Module 3 argues and does not re-read it. (4) [`trinity-and-god`](../trinity-and-god/syllabus.md) 5.1–5.3 supplies 5.1 with both doctrines and the history of the addition; 5.1 adds only the argument and the lawfulness question. (5) [`theology-of-debt`](../theology-of-debt/syllabus.md) 3.4–3.6 owns satisfaction, the treasury and Trent on indulgences, and its Boss 3 already runs Luther's Theses 56–62 as an apologetic exercise; 6.3 cites that exercise and does not repeat it. (6) [`patristics`](../patristics/syllabus.md) Boss 1, Boss 2 and Boss 5 already work 1 Clement and Ignatius on church offices, the two recensions of Cyprian ch. 4 with Irenaeus 3.3.2, and the patristic readings of "this rock"; 4.2 argues about what that evidence proves and reads none of those passages again. (7) [`church-history`](../church-history/syllabus.md) cedes the Reformation and Orthodox disputes here by name, and its Boss 7 (Bellarmine and the *Letter to the Grand Duchess Christina*) is deliberately not reused in 7.1. (8) [`christology`](../christology/syllabus.md) 5.4 already states penal substitution from the Reformed confessions and assesses it; Module 3 cites it and does not re-open the atonement, which is the one Reformation dispute this course leaves entirely to another course. (9) [`apologetics-foundations`](../apologetics-foundations/syllabus.md) 4.1 owns the conflict thesis and the Galileo affair as a test of it; 7.1 uses the same affair for a different argument — institutional error against the claim of divine guidance — and says so. (10) [`new-testament`](../new-testament/syllabus.md), [`old-testament`](../old-testament/syllabus.md) and [`fundamental-theology`](../fundamental-theology/syllabus.md) all name this course in their scope tables for the justification dispute, the canon dispute and *sola scriptura*. (11) No built lesson anywhere in the library touches any of this material.
- **The line back to [`apologetics-foundations`](../apologetics-foundations/syllabus.md).** That course argues only what divides Christians from non-Christians; this one argues only what divides Christians from each other. Neither is a prerequisite of the other and neither repeats the other. Where the two would touch — the account of what apologetics is and cannot do, the ethics of persuasion, salvation outside the visible Church — this course cites and moves on. In particular, no argument for the existence of God, for the resurrection, or for the reliability of scripture is made here: all of it is shared ground with every opponent in this course, and an apologist who reaches for it has misread the room.
- **Fidelity discipline, and where it bites hardest.** The single most common failure in intra-Christian apologetics is fighting a position nobody holds. Three specific traps get named in the lessons and graded in the rubrics: *sola scriptura* is not *solo scriptura*, and the confessions themselves say so (2.1); the Reformed doctrine of the Supper is not Zwingli's (6.4); and the Orthodox objection to Vatican I is usually about jurisdiction rather than infallibility (5.3). A fourth, running throughout: Lutheran, Reformed, Anglican and Orthodox positions are four different things, and "they believe" is almost always the beginning of an error.
- **Concessions written into the syllabus, by module.** (1) Blame for the divisions falls on both sides, and Catholic polemic has its own record of forgery and atrocity story. (2) Material sufficiency is a legitimate Catholic option; Jerome and Cajetan doubted the deuterocanon; the Catholic canon was not explicitly closed before 1546. (3) The Reformation doctrine protects something late-medieval preaching genuinely obscured, and the Joint Declaration's Annex concedes more than Catholic apologists usually admit. (4) The papal claims grew, forgeries were used in their service, a pope was condemned by an ecumenical council, and the exegesis of Matthew 16 alone will not reach an office. (5) The manner of the filioque's addition is hard to defend; the 1995 clarification concedes the monarchy of the Father; universal ordinary jurisdiction has cost the local church something real; and no magisterial condemnation of Palamas exists. (6) Marian devotion has gone past its doctrine, relic traffic and indulgence sales were real, private Masses and stipends were abused, and the doctrine of the real presence does not depend on Aristotle. (7) The Galileo condemnation was an error really made; most of the sixteenth-century disciplinary grievances were justified; and the argument from the abuse crisis is survived rather than answered.
- **Accuracy: verify before writing each lesson, never assert from memory.** Confessional article numbers and their exact wording in Schaff and in the Triglot; chapter numbers in Calvin's *Institutes* beyond I.7, III.11, III.20 and IV.17, which are corroborated here; the session and chapter structure of Trent in Waterworth; the grounds and the operative judgement of *Apostolicae Curae* and the argument of *Saepius Officio*; the dates of the Constantinopolitan councils that received Palamas; the canon of Ephesus that Orthodox writers cite against creedal addition, which is named by content rather than by number; the date, signatories and structure of the 1999 Joint Declaration and which later communions associated themselves with it; the title and issuing body of the 1995 clarification, the Ravenna document and the 2024 study document on the bishop of Rome; the *Consilium de Emendanda Ecclesia* (1537) and its authorship; the Spanish Inquisition execution estimates, which are given as historians' estimates with the range and never as a single number; and every Denzinger and Catechism reference. Where a number cannot be confirmed, the document is named without it.
- **Claims deliberately softened or left out.** Denominational counts are not used as an argument, because there is no agreed counting method and the figure usually quoted does not mean what it is used to mean. The claim that Trent's canons condemn a position Luther never held is presented as a question Catholic scholars raise, not as an established result. Romanides's thesis is stated at full strength and then placed as a minority view among Orthodox historians, which is what it is. Disputed intra-Orthodox eschatological material is left out entirely rather than presented as the Orthodox position. No claim is made about how the Orthodox churches as a whole would receive any dialogue document, since there is no body competent to say. The level of *Apostolicae Curae* is described as differently assessed by Catholic theologians, because it is.
- **Lesson count.** The roadmap estimated ~26; the syllabus runs 29 (+12%, inside the band). Module 5 needs five lessons because the filioque, primacy, Vatican I, the energies and the practical disputes have four separate literatures and one of them (the energies) cannot be stated in Latin categories at all, so a merged lesson would misstate it. Module 6 needs six because each distinctive is attacked by a different confession on different grounds, and merging the real presence with the sacrifice of the Mass — the usual compression — is exactly the merge that produces the standard apologetic failure of 6.5. Compression happened where it could: Module 1 is three lessons because [`philosophical-method`](../philosophical-method/syllabus.md) 4.2 and [`apologetics-foundations`](../apologetics-foundations/syllabus.md) 1.1 already teach the method and the ethics; Module 2 is four because material sufficiency and the New Testament's tradition texts answer one question and belong in one lesson; Module 3 is four because [`creation-grace-last-things`](../creation-grace-last-things/syllabus.md) has already read Trent Session VI and the Joint Declaration as texts; and Module 7 is three, with the Galileo affair, the Inquisition and the conflict thesis compressed into one lesson because two other courses own most of that material. If a lesson overflows when built, split it and merge elsewhere rather than grow past 30.
