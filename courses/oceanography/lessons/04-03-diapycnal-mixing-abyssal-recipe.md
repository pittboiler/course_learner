# Physical Oceanography · Lesson 4.3: Diapycnal mixing and the abyssal recipe

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 4: The deep ocean and the overturning · Builds on: [1.4](01-04-stratification-buoyancy-frequency.md), [4.1](04-01-water-masses-world-ocean.md) · Unlocks: [4.4](04-04-what-drives-the-overturning.md), [5.1](05-01-internal-waves.md)

## Why this matters

Dense water sinks at the poles at about 25 to 30 Sv. It must come back up, or the abyss would fill and the process would stop. But coming back up means crossing density surfaces from cold water to warm — which requires mixing, and mixing requires energy.

In 1966 Walter Munk asked how much mixing that demands, got $\kappa \approx 10^{-4}\ \mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}}$, and called the calculation "Abyssal recipes". Twenty-five years later, when the diffusivity was finally measured directly by releasing a tracer into the open thermocline, the answer came back **ten times too small**. That discrepancy — a factor of ten between what the circulation requires and what the ocean appears to supply — organized a generation of ocean research, and its resolution changed how the entire overturning is understood.

The resolution is that Munk asked for an *average*, and averages hide distributions. The mixing is not spread evenly; it is concentrated by a factor of a hundred over rough topography, in narrow canyons and above mid-ocean ridges. Once you know that, the question stops being "where is the missing mixing?" and becomes "**where does the energy come from, and does the ocean's stratification therefore depend on the tides and the wind rather than on the polar cooling?**" That reframing is [4.4](04-04-what-drives-the-overturning.md)'s subject and it is the most important idea in Module 4.

## The idea

**The abyssal temperature profile is exponential, and that is a clue.** Below about 1000 m, temperature falls toward the bottom roughly as $e^{z/h}$ with $h \approx 1\ \mathrm{km}$. An exponential is the signature of a balance between two processes with opposite tendencies and different derivative orders — here, upwelling carrying cold water up (first derivative) against downward diffusion of heat (second derivative).

**Setting them equal gives the mixing rate the circulation requires.** The balance $w\,\partial T/\partial z = \kappa\,\partial^2T/\partial z^2$ has exponential solutions with scale height $h = \kappa/w$. Measure $h$ from a profile, get $w$ from the deep-water formation rate divided by the ocean's area, and $\kappa$ follows. Munk's answer was $10^{-4}\ \mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}}$.

**The measurement said $10^{-5}$.** The 1992 North Atlantic Tracer Release Experiment put a slug of sulphur hexafluoride into the thermocline at 300 m and watched it spread for two years. The vertical diffusivity came out at $1.2\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}}$ — the cleanest measurement of the quantity ever made, and a factor of eight below what Munk needed.

**The resolution is that the mixing is where the topography is.** Later measurements over the flank of the Mid-Atlantic Ridge in the Brazil Basin found $\kappa$ reaching $10^{-3}$ within a kilometre of rough bottom, falling to $10^{-5}$ over the smooth abyssal plain a few hundred kilometres away. Internal waves generated by tidal and geostrophic flow over topography break where they are made ([5.1](05-01-internal-waves.md)). **A quantity that varies by two orders of magnitude in space cannot be summarized by its value at one convenient location**, and the area-weighted mean can be $10^{-4}$ while almost everywhere reads $10^{-5}$.

**And then the energy question becomes central.** Mixing across a stable stratification does work against gravity, so it needs a power supply. The requirement is about 2 TW. The candidates are the tides — which dissipate 3.5 TW globally, about a third of it in the deep ocean rather than in shallow seas — and the wind, which does roughly 1 TW of work on near-inertial motions and on the geostrophic flow over topography. Neither is polar cooling. **The deep ocean's stratification is maintained by mechanical energy from the moon and the wind, not by the buoyancy forcing that makes the dense water in the first place.**

## The formal version

**Munk's balance.** In the abyssal interior, assume a steady one-dimensional balance between vertical advection and vertical diffusion of heat:

$$w\frac{\partial T}{\partial z} = \kappa\frac{\partial^2T}{\partial z^2}.$$

*In words: upwelling cold water is warmed from above by downward diffusion of heat, at just the rate that keeps the profile steady.* Try $T \propto e^{z/h}$:

$$w\frac{1}{h} = \kappa\frac{1}{h^2} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \boxed{\ h = \frac{\kappa}{w}\ }$$

*In words: the scale height of the abyssal thermocline is the diffusivity divided by the upwelling velocity.* Two observables, one unknown.

**The recipe.** With deep water formed at $Q = 25\ \mathrm{Sv}$ and upwelling over the abyssal area $A = 3.6\times10^{14}\ \mathrm{m^2}$:

$$w = \frac{Q}{A} = \frac{2.5\times10^{7}}{3.6\times10^{14}} = 6.94\times10^{-8}\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}} = 2.2\ \mathrm{m\,yr^{-1}},$$

$$\kappa = w\,h = 6.94\times10^{-8}\times1000 = 6.9\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}},$$

which Munk rounded to $10^{-4}$.

**The measurements.**

| Location / method | $\kappa$ (m² s⁻¹) |
|---|---|
| NATRE tracer release, open thermocline, 300 m | $1.2\times10^{-5}$ |
| Smooth abyssal plain, microstructure | $10^{-5}$ |
| Brazil Basin, above the Mid-Atlantic Ridge flank | $10^{-4}$ to $10^{-3}$ |
| Deep canyons, Romanche Fracture Zone | up to $10^{-2}$ |
| Southern Ocean, Drake Passage | $10^{-4}$ |
| **Required (Munk)** | $10^{-4}$ |

*In words: the required value is not observed in the open ocean and is exceeded near topography, so the global average can be right while the typical value is wrong by a factor of ten.*

**The Osborn relation.** Turbulence dissipating at a rate $\varepsilon$ (W kg⁻¹) against stratification $N^2$ produces a diapycnal diffusivity

$$\kappa = \Gamma\frac{\varepsilon}{N^2}, \qquad \Gamma \approx 0.2,$$

where $\Gamma$ is the **mixing efficiency** — the fraction of turbulent energy that goes into raising the centre of mass rather than into heat. *In words: only a fifth of the energy you spend on stirring actually accomplishes any mixing.* This is the bridge from an energy budget to a diffusivity, and it is how microstructure profilers convert measured shear into $\kappa$.

**The energy budget.** The power required to lift $Q$ of dense water with density excess $\Delta\rho$ through a height $H$:

$$P_{\text{PE}} = g\,\Delta\rho\,Q\,H = 9.81\times1.0\times3\times10^{7}\times1000 = 2.9\times10^{11}\ \mathrm{W} = 0.29\ \mathrm{TW}.$$

Dividing by the mixing efficiency, the turbulent energy that must be supplied is

$$P = \frac{P_{\text{PE}}}{\Gamma} = \frac{0.29}{0.2} = 1.5\ \mathrm{TW},$$

and Munk and Wunsch's fuller accounting — which includes the full stratification rather than a single density step — gives **2.1 TW**.

**Where it comes from.**

| Source | Total | Available to the deep ocean |
|---|---|---|
| Tidal dissipation | 3.5 TW | about 1.0 TW (the rest goes in shallow seas) |
| Wind work on near-inertial motions | 0.5–1.0 TW | most of it |
| Wind work on the geostrophic flow, lee waves over topography | 0.2–0.4 TW | most of it |
| Geothermal heating | 0.03 TW | negligible for mixing |
| **Required** | | **about 2 TW** |

*In words: the budget closes, but only just, and only if essentially all the deep tidal and wind energy goes into mixing rather than being dissipated harmlessly.* This is not a comfortable margin, and it is the quantitative core of the argument that the overturning is **mechanically** rather than thermally driven.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (mechanical — Munk's recipe, and the discrepancy).** Deep water forms at 25 Sv and upwells through an abyssal area of $3.6\times10^{14}\ \mathrm{m^2}$; the abyssal temperature profile has a scale height of 1 km.

(a) $$w = \frac{2.5\times10^{7}}{3.6\times10^{14}} = 6.94\times10^{-8}\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}.$$
In metres per year: $6.94\times10^{-8}\times3.156\times10^{7} = 2.19\ \mathrm{m\,yr^{-1}}$.

(b) $$\kappa = wh = 6.94\times10^{-8}\times1000 = 6.94\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}}.$$

(c) Against the NATRE value of $1.2\times10^{-5}$:
$$\frac{6.94\times10^{-5}}{1.2\times10^{-5}} = 5.8.$$

**A factor of about six**, or of about eight if $\kappa = 10^{-4}$ is used. The resolution is spatial concentration: mixing over rough topography is 10 to 100 times the open-ocean value, and the ocean's abyssal boundaries and ridge flanks — perhaps 10 percent of the area — supply most of the area-weighted total.

(d) *A consistency check.* If 10 percent of the area has $\kappa = 5\times10^{-4}$ and 90 percent has $1.2\times10^{-5}$, the area-weighted mean is

$$0.1\times5\times10^{-4} + 0.9\times1.2\times10^{-5} = 5.0\times10^{-5} + 1.1\times10^{-5} = 6.1\times10^{-5},$$

which is Munk's required value to within 12 percent. The reconciliation works, and it requires nothing exotic — just the observed values applied over the observed areas.

**Example 2 (why you'd care — the ocean's stratification depends on the moon).** Suppose lunar tidal forcing were reduced by half. Estimate the effect on the abyssal stratification, and explain why this reframes what "drives" the overturning.

*The energy chain.* Deep tidal dissipation supplies about 1.0 TW of the 2 TW required. Halving the tide reduces tidal *dissipation* by a factor of four, since dissipation scales as the square of the tidal velocity: 1.0 TW becomes 0.25 TW. Total available power falls from about 2.0 to 1.25 TW, a 38 percent reduction.

*The consequence for $\kappa$.* By Osborn, $\kappa \propto \varepsilon \propto P$, so $\kappa$ falls by 38 percent to $4.3\times10^{-5}$.

*The consequence for the circulation.* Here there is a genuine choice of what to hold fixed, and it matters.

- If the *scale height* is held fixed at 1 km (say, set by basin geometry), then $w = \kappa/h$ falls by 38 percent, so the overturning slows to 15 Sv.
- If the *overturning rate* is held fixed by polar buoyancy forcing, then $h = \kappa/w$ falls by 38 percent, so the abyssal thermocline compresses to 620 m and the deep ocean becomes colder and more sharply stratified.

Which happens depends on which end of the circulation is rate-limiting, and **that is precisely the question [4.4](04-04-what-drives-the-overturning.md) is about**. Sandström's theorem gives the classical argument for the first answer: a fluid heated and cooled at the same level cannot maintain a circulation against friction without a mechanical energy source, so if the mechanical supply falls, the circulation must slow.

*The point.* The result is genuinely startling on first meeting: **the rate of the global ocean overturning may be set by tidal dissipation over mid-ocean ridges** — that is, by the moon's orbit and the shape of the sea floor — rather than by how cold the Labrador Sea gets. It has real consequences. It means the overturning's response to greenhouse warming is not simply "less cooling, less sinking". It means glacial-period overturning changes must be evaluated with tidal dissipation changed too, since a 120 m lower sea level moves tidal energy from shallow seas (where it is wasted) to the open ocean (where it mixes) — a mechanism seriously proposed for glacial ocean circulation changes.

**The general lesson: identifying "what drives" a circulation means finding the rate-limiting step, and the rate-limiting step is not always the most conspicuous forcing.** Buoyancy loss at the poles is conspicuous and necessary; it may not be limiting.

## Watch out

- **You might think** the missing mixing was a measurement problem. **Actually** the NATRE measurement was excellent and the value it returned is correct for where it was made. The error was in assuming a spatially uniform $\kappa$ — the model was wrong, not the instrument.
- **You might think** $\kappa$ and the eddy diffusivity of [3.4](03-04-mesoscale-eddies-baroclinic-instability.md) are the same kind of quantity. **Actually** they differ by eight orders of magnitude ($10^{-5}$ against $10^{3}\ \mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}}$) because one acts *across* isopycnals and the other *along* them. Any statement about ocean mixing must specify which.
- **You might think** the energy budget closing at 2 TW is comfortable. **Actually** it requires nearly all deep tidal and wind energy to go into mixing, with almost nothing lost to bottom drag or radiated away. The budget is closed by construction as much as by measurement, and the individual terms carry uncertainties of a factor of two.

## One-liner

> The dense water made at the poles must be mixed back up across the stratification, which requires a diffusivity ten times larger than the open ocean actually has and about two terawatts of mechanical power — supplied by tides and wind rather than by cooling, and concentrated over rough topography rather than spread evenly.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢)** Deep water forms at 30 Sv and upwells over $3.4\times10^{14}\ \mathrm{m^2}$; the abyssal scale height is 800 m. (a) Compute $w$ in m s⁻¹ and m yr⁻¹. (b) Compute the required $\kappa$. (c) Compare with a measured open-ocean value of $1.5\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}}$ and state the factor.

**P2 (🟡)** A microstructure profiler measures a turbulent dissipation rate $\varepsilon = 3.0\times10^{-9}\ \mathrm{W\,kg^{-1}}$ in a region with $N = 1.5\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$. (a) Compute $\kappa$ using $\Gamma = 0.2$. (b) A second profile 200 km away, over rough topography, gives $\varepsilon = 2.0\times10^{-7}\ \mathrm{W\,kg^{-1}}$ with the same $N$. Compute $\kappa$ there. (c) If the rough region occupies 8 percent of the area, compute the area-weighted mean $\kappa$ and compare with Munk's $10^{-4}$. (d) Comment on how many profiles at randomly chosen locations you would need before your sample mean was likely to be within a factor of two of the true area mean.

**P3 (🔴, optional)** Munk and Wunsch's energy argument. (a) The deep ocean must lift $Q = 30\ \mathrm{Sv}$ of water with a density excess of $\Delta\rho = 0.8\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$ through $H = 1500\ \mathrm{m}$. Compute the rate of potential-energy increase. (b) With a mixing efficiency $\Gamma = 0.2$, compute the turbulent power required. (c) Global tidal dissipation is 3.5 TW, of which 30 percent reaches the deep ocean; wind work supplies 1.0 TW. Compute the supply and compare with (b). (d) At the Last Glacial Maximum sea level was 120 m lower, which removed most of the shallow shelf seas where tidal energy is preferentially dissipated. Argue qualitatively what this does to the deep-ocean tidal energy supply and hence to the glacial overturning, and state one observation that could test it.

<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** (a) $$w = \frac{3.0\times10^{7}}{3.4\times10^{14}} = 8.82\times10^{-8}\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}} = 2.78\ \mathrm{m\,yr^{-1}}.$$

(b) $$\kappa = wh = 8.82\times10^{-8}\times800 = 7.06\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}}.$$

(c) $$\frac{7.06\times10^{-5}}{1.5\times10^{-5}} = 4.7,$$

a factor of about five. Note that the answer is remarkably insensitive to the inputs: raising the overturning from 25 to 30 Sv and lowering the scale height from 1000 to 800 m changed $\kappa$ by only 2 percent, because the two effects nearly cancel. **The required $\kappa$ is a robust number**, which is why the discrepancy with the measurement could not be explained away.

**P2** (a) $$\kappa = \Gamma\frac{\varepsilon}{N^2} = 0.2\times\frac{3.0\times10^{-9}}{\left(1.5\times10^{-3}\right)^2} = 0.2\times\frac{3.0\times10^{-9}}{2.25\times10^{-6}} = 0.2\times1.333\times10^{-3} = 2.67\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}}.$$

Hmm — that is already above Munk's requirement from a dissipation rate described as ordinary. It is worth checking whether $\varepsilon = 3\times10^{-9}\ \mathrm{W\,kg^{-1}}$ is a plausible open-ocean value: typical thermocline dissipation is $10^{-10}$ to $10^{-9}$, so $3\times10^{-9}$ is at the energetic end, and the resulting $\kappa$ is correspondingly high. Taking the more typical $\varepsilon = 10^{-10}$ would give $\kappa = 8.9\times10^{-6}$, right at the NATRE value.

(b) $$\kappa = 0.2\times\frac{2.0\times10^{-7}}{2.25\times10^{-6}} = 0.2\times8.889\times10^{-2} = 1.78\times10^{-2}\ \mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}}.$$

Very large — comparable to values found in the Romanche Fracture Zone, where flow is forced through a narrow deep passage.

(c) $$\bar\kappa = 0.08\times1.78\times10^{-2} + 0.92\times2.67\times10^{-4} = 1.42\times10^{-3} + 2.46\times10^{-4} = 1.67\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}},$$

**seventeen times Munk's requirement**. The reconciliation has overshot badly, which is the honest outcome for these particular numbers: 8 percent of the ocean at $\kappa = 1.8\times10^{-2}$ would mix the abyss far faster than observed. Either the rough region occupies far less than 8 percent of the area, or such extreme dissipation is confined to a thin layer near the bottom rather than filling the water column — and the latter is what is observed. Real hot-spot mixing decays with height above the topography with a scale of a few hundred metres.

(d) The distribution is extremely skewed: 92 percent of the area contributes 15 percent of the mean, and 8 percent contributes 85 percent. A random profile has a 0.92 probability of landing in the quiet region and returning a value six times below the area mean.

To estimate the mean within a factor of two you need enough samples to capture the hot spots reliably — roughly, enough that the *expected number of hot-spot hits* is large enough for Poisson fluctuations to be under about 30 percent, i.e. $n_{\text{hits}} \gtrsim 10$, hence

$$n \gtrsim \frac{10}{0.08} = 125\ \text{profiles}.$$

And that is optimistic, since it assumes the hot-spot values are themselves uniform. **This is the central sampling difficulty of ocean microstructure**: a heavy-tailed distribution means the sample mean converges slowly and is biased low in any small sample, so the historical picture of a uniformly weakly-mixing ocean was exactly what a sparse random sample would produce even if the truth had always been otherwise. The practical response was to abandon random sampling and go deliberately to the topography — which is how the Brazil Basin experiment was designed.

**P3** (a) $$P_{\text{PE}} = g\,\Delta\rho\,Q\,H = 9.81\times0.8\times3.0\times10^{7}\times1500 = 3.53\times10^{11}\ \mathrm{W} = 0.353\ \mathrm{TW}.$$

(b) $$P = \frac{0.353}{0.2} = 1.77\ \mathrm{TW}.$$

(c) $$P_{\text{supply}} = 0.30\times3.5 + 1.0 = 1.05 + 1.0 = 2.05\ \mathrm{TW}.$$

Supply exceeds demand by 16 percent — the budget closes, with almost no margin. Given that each term carries an uncertainty of a factor of roughly two, the honest statement is that supply and demand are **the same order of magnitude and cannot be distinguished**, which is exactly the state of the field.

(d) Lowering sea level by 120 m removes the shallow shelf seas — the Yellow Sea, the Bering shelf, Hudson Bay, the European shelf — where a large majority of present-day tidal energy is dissipated. Tidal energy that is not dissipated there must be dissipated elsewhere, and the elsewhere is the deep ocean, where it generates internal tides over topography and therefore *mixes*. So the glacial deep ocean plausibly received **more** mixing energy than today, from the same lunar forcing.

The consequence, following Example 2's logic, is a **stronger or at least differently-structured glacial overturning** than a purely thermal argument would predict — which is interesting because the paleoceanographic evidence indicates a *shallower and weaker* North Atlantic overturning at the LGM, with glacial NADW replaced by a shallower water mass and AABW filling more of the basin. Reconciling more mixing energy with a weaker overturning requires the energy to have gone into the Southern Ocean cell rather than the Atlantic one, which is a live hypothesis rather than a settled result.

**A test.** Tidal models can be run with LGM bathymetry and sea level, and they are: published simulations find deep-ocean tidal dissipation increasing by roughly a factor of two at the LGM, concentrated in the North Atlantic. An observational test is harder, but the distribution of glacial-age sediment redistribution and the $^{231}\mathrm{Pa}/^{230}\mathrm{Th}$ ratio in North Atlantic cores — a proxy sensitive to overturning rate — provide the data against which such simulations are checked. The comparison remains contested, which is itself informative: it means the mechanical-energy argument has real, testable consequences that the field has not yet settled.

## Flashback

**From Lesson 4.1 (Water masses of the world ocean):** A sample at 3500 m in the western South Atlantic reads $(\theta,S) = (0.60\,^\circ\mathrm{C},\ 34.74)$. Take the end-members as NADW $(2.50,\ 34.95)$ and AABW $(-0.50,\ 34.65)$. (a) Compute the NADW fraction from $\theta$ and from $S$. (b) State whether two components suffice. (c) The sample's silicate is $75\ \mathrm{\mu mol\,kg^{-1}}$, against $15$ for NADW and $120$ for AABW. Compute the NADW fraction from silicate and comment on the agreement.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

(a) From $\theta$:
$$x = \frac{0.60-(-0.50)}{2.50-(-0.50)} = \frac{1.10}{3.00} = 0.367.$$

From $S$:
$$x = \frac{34.74-34.65}{34.95-34.65} = \frac{0.09}{0.30} = 0.300.$$

(b) The two differ by 0.067, about 20 percent of the mean — larger than the 3 percent agreement of a clean two-component case, and about at the limit of what end-member uncertainty can excuse. **Marginal**: two components are approximately adequate but a third contribution of order 10 to 20 percent cannot be ruled out.

(c) From silicate, treating it as conservative over this distance:

$$x_{\text{NADW}} = \frac{75-120}{15-120} = \frac{-45}{-105} = 0.429.$$

Three estimates: 0.367, 0.300, 0.429. The silicate value is the *highest* NADW fraction, and the spread across all three is 0.13 — worse, not better.

This is the honest and instructive outcome. Silicate was brought in for its large contrast ([4.1](04-01-water-masses-world-ocean.md), Example 2), and the contrast is indeed large (105 units against 0.30 in salinity), so the estimate is well-conditioned. But silicate is **not conservative** — it is regenerated from sinking biogenic opal, so the sample has gained silicate since leaving its sources, which biases the AABW fraction *up* and hence the NADW fraction... down, not up. The observed bias is the other way.

So the disagreement points somewhere else: most likely to a third water mass. Lower Circumpolar Deep Water, intermediate between NADW and AABW in $\theta$ and $S$ but with silicate nearer the AABW end, would raise the apparent NADW fraction from silicate relative to the others — which is what is seen.

*The general point.* **Three tracers giving three answers is more informative than two giving two.** With two, all you learn is that they disagree. With three, the *pattern* of disagreement identifies the direction of the missing component, and that is what turns a failed fit into a detection.
