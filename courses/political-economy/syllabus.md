# Political Economy & Social Choice — Syllabus

> Politics & Society · Tier 2 · ~25 lessons · Prereqs: [`social-choice`](../social-choice/syllabus.md), [`grad-game-theory`](../grad-game-theory/syllabus.md) · Roadmap id: `political-economy`

## Goal

This is positive political theory: game theory aimed at elections, legislatures, interest groups and the budget, to ask what a given set of rules actually produces. [`social-choice`](../social-choice/syllabus.md) showed what aggregation can and cannot do with fixed preferences over fixed alternatives; this course makes the alternatives *platforms chosen by candidates*, the voters *strategic and badly informed*, and the outcome *an equilibrium*. You will finish able to compute a pivot probability and say why it breaks the simple theory of turnout, design an optimal persuasion signal, solve Downsian and probabilistic-voting elections and show exactly where convergence fails, derive Duverger's law as a strategic-voting equilibrium, solve threshold and commons games and read Ostrom's design principles into the incentive constraint, solve agenda-setter and gridlock models, compute a re-election cutoff, a Grossman–Helpman tariff and a Tullock dissipation, solve Baron–Ferejohn bargaining, and derive the Meltzer–Richard tax rate. Where a model bears on whether an institution is good, the course says precisely what the model assumes and shows, and leaves the verdict open. It deliberately skips finite-alternative social choice ([`social-choice`](../social-choice/syllabus.md)), public goods and optimal taxation as policy ([`public-economics`](../public-economics/syllabus.md)), democratization, autocracy and credible commitment (`institutions-and-development`), the bargaining model of war ([`conflict-and-bargaining`](../conflict-and-bargaining/syllabus.md)), causal identification of political effects (`empirical-political-economy`), and every normative question about democracy ([`political-philosophy`](../political-philosophy/syllabus.md)).

## How practice works in this course

Problems follow [HUMANITIES-BUILD-BRIEF.md](../../HUMANITIES-BUILD-BRIEF.md). This is a formal course (archetype 8): most parts are **formal** and graded as in any math course — solve for an equilibrium, compute a cutoff, prove that no platform is a Condorcet winner. Four archetypes carry the load:

- **Formal model** — solve a small game with written-for-the-problem numbers. Every equilibrium, threshold and tax rate in a lesson or boss problem is recomputed by script before it ships.
- **Counterexample** — build the configuration that breaks a result: a voter distribution with no Downsian equilibrium, a status quo the setter cannot move, a coalition that Gamson's law misprices. Formal.
- **Diagnose** — an invented op-ed, reform memo or regulator's press release relies on a model without saying so; name the model and the assumption doing the work. Exegetical, graded strictly.
- **Find the crux / steelman and reply** — reserved for interpretation: do term limits help voters, does low turnout show irrationality, does Ostrom refute the tragedy of the commons? **Evaluative** and verdict-neutral: any conclusion passes if the moves are made, and the must-hit moves always include stating what the model assumes and which assumption the verdict turns on.

Exegetical parts about what a model says (its timing, who commits, what is common knowledge) are strict: misstating the game is an error even inside an evaluative answer. Modern sources are paraphrased and cited, never quoted.

## Dangerous Checklist

When you finish, you can:

- [ ] Derive a voter's induced preferences over a policy from an economic model, check single-crossing, and say which [`social-choice`](../social-choice/syllabus.md) result makes the median voter decisive
- [ ] Compute a pivot probability, show why it collapses in large unbalanced electorates, and say what group-based and ethical-voter models change about turnout
- [ ] Solve a small swing-voter's-curse game, and construct the optimal persuasion signal for a binary state and a threshold receiver
- [ ] Solve two-candidate Downsian competition for equilibrium platforms, and name which of policy motivation plus uncertainty, valence, or entry produces divergence
- [ ] Show that a second policy dimension empties the majority core, and restore an equilibrium with probabilistic voting by computing the implied weighted-welfare policy
- [ ] Derive Duverger's law as a strategic-voting equilibrium, and exhibit two Duvergerian equilibria on the same electorate
- [ ] Solve a threshold public-good game for its pure and mixed equilibria, compute Olson's provision shortfall as the group grows, and find the discount factor that sustains a commons agreement
- [ ] Solve an agenda-setter model and a gridlock interval, and choose a Buchanan–Tullock decision threshold
- [ ] Compute the re-election cutoff in an accountability model, and explain how career concerns, pandering and term limits change it
- [ ] Compute a Grossman–Helpman tariff and the dissipation in a Tullock contest
- [ ] Solve Baron–Ferejohn legislative bargaining and compare its division with Gamson's law
- [ ] Solve the Meltzer–Richard model for the equilibrium tax rate, and name the assumptions any explanation of observed redistribution must drop

## Modules

### Module 1: Voters - preferences, turnout and information

Before candidates compete, the voter: what she wants over policy, why she votes at all, and what she knows.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1.1 | From ballots to policy space | Turn an economic model into voters' preferences over a policy and say when a median voter is decisive | the bridge from [`social-choice`](../social-choice/syllabus.md) (Black and single-crossing recalled from 3.3–3.4, Arrow's proof cited to [`grad-game-theory`](../grad-game-theory/syllabus.md) 5.1); Euclidean and quadratic-loss utility; induced preferences over a tax rate; what a positive model of politics specifies (players, timing, commitment) |
| 1.2 | Turnout and the paradox of voting | Compute the value of a vote and see why the simple calculus predicts almost no turnout | Downs's paradox; the Riker–Ordeshook calculus; pivot probability under a binomial electorate and its collapse when the race is not a dead heat; Palfrey–Rosenthal participation games; group-based and ethical-voter models (Coate–Conlin, Feddersen–Sandroni) |
| 1.3 | Rational ignorance and the swing voter's curse | Say when an uninformed voter should abstain, and when she should vote to offset partisans | Downs's rational ignorance; the value of information to one voter; conditioning on being pivotal; the swing voter's curse (Feddersen–Pesendorfer); strategic jurors cited to [`social-choice`](../social-choice/syllabus.md) 5.2 |
| 1.4 | Persuasion and the media | Design the signal that maximizes approval, and see how news markets slant | Bayesian persuasion (Kamenica–Gentzkow): splitting the prior, the threshold receiver, concavification; cheap talk as the no-commitment contrast (cited to [`grad-game-theory`](../grad-game-theory/syllabus.md) 4.5); demand-driven slant (Mullainathan–Shleifer, Gentzkow–Shapiro); media capture as a pointer to 4.4 |

**Boss problem 1:** (a) You are one of 100,001 voters; each of the other 100,000 votes for A independently with probability $p$, and your vote matters only by breaking an exact tie (worth half your benefit $B = 10{,}000$, with a coin flip otherwise). Compute your expected benefit for $p = 0.5$ and $p = 0.51$, compare each with a voting cost of 1, and say what the gap shows. *Formal.* (b) Two states of the world, A and B, with prior probability 0.6 on A. An informed independent votes for the true state; a partisan always votes B; you are an uninformed independent who wants the outcome to match the state. Majority rule, ties broken by a coin. Compute your probability of a correct outcome if you vote A, vote B, or abstain; then remove the partisan and recompute. Explain the reversal. *Formal.* (c) A ministry wants voters to approve a policy that works with prior probability 1/4; voters approve if and only if their posterior is at least 3/5. The ministry commits in advance to a test. Construct the test that maximizes the probability of approval, give that probability, and say why a test that always reveals the truth does worse for the ministry. *Formal.* (d) An invented op-ed argues that "turnout of sixty percent proves voters ignore their own interests." In 150 words, say which model of 1.2 the op-ed silently assumes, what the group-based or ethical-voter models change, and what evidence would discriminate between them. *Evaluative.*

### Module 2: Electoral competition

Candidates as strategic players in policy space: where the median-voter result lives, where it dies, and how the electoral rule shapes the game.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2.1 | The Downsian spatial model | Solve two-candidate competition on a line and prove convergence | Hotelling and Downs; vote-share vs win-probability objectives; convergence to the median; policy-motivated candidates (Wittman, Calvert) and why divergence needs both policy motivation and uncertainty about the median |
| 2.2 | Multidimensional voting and chaos | Show that a second dimension empties the core, and see what institutions put back | Plott's symmetry conditions; McKelvey's chaos theorem and agenda control (top cycle recalled from [`social-choice`](../social-choice/syllabus.md) 1.4); the uncovered set; structure-induced equilibrium with dimension-by-dimension voting (Shepsle) |
| 2.3 | Probabilistic voting | Restore equilibrium with noisy voters and read the platform as weighted welfare | ideological shocks and smooth vote shares (Lindbeck–Weibull, Coughlin); equilibrium as maximization of welfare weighted by group size times density; swing voters get targeted; core-voter vs swing-voter targeting (Dixit–Londregan) |
| 2.4 | Valence and citizen-candidates | Add non-policy advantages and endogenous entry, and let platforms separate | valence advantage and the disadvantaged candidate's incentive to differentiate (Groseclose; Aragones–Palfrey); citizen-candidate models (Osborne–Slivinski, Besley–Coate); the number of candidates as an equilibrium object |
| 2.5 | Strategic voting and Duverger's law | Derive the psychological effect as an equilibrium and count its equilibria | wasted votes; the $M+1$ rule (Cox); Duvergerian and non-Duvergerian equilibria (Palfrey, Myerson–Weber); expectations as the selection problem; runoff and district-magnitude versions; the observed regularity cited to [`political-institutions`](../political-institutions/syllabus.md) 2.3 |
| 2.6 | Electoral rules compared formally | Predict how majoritarian and proportional rules change what candidates promise | centripetal vs centrifugal incentives (Cox, Myerson's favored-minorities model); broad public goods vs targeted transfers under winner-take-all and proportional systems (Lizzeri–Persico); swing districts and spending composition (Persson–Tabellini); evidence cited to `empirical-political-economy` |

**Boss problem 2:** (a) Five voters on $[0, 10]$ have single-peaked preferences with peaks 1, 2, 4, 7 and 9. Two office-seeking candidates choose platforms simultaneously to maximize vote share. Find every Nash equilibrium and prove there are no others. *Formal.* (b) Three voters in the plane have Euclidean preferences with ideal points $(0,0)$, $(6,0)$ and $(0,6)$. Show that $(2.5, 0.5)$ beats $(2,2)$ by majority, then prove that every point is beaten by some other point, and say which of Plott's conditions fails. *Formal.* (c) Three groups with population shares 0.3, 0.5, 0.2 have ideological-shock densities 1, 2, 4. Per-capita transfers $t_g$ yield utility $\ln t_g$ and must satisfy $\sum_g n_g t_g = 1$. Find the probabilistic-voting equilibrium transfers and say which group gains most and why. *Formal.* (d) A plurality election: 35 percent rank A ≻ B ≻ C, 25 percent rank B ≻ A ≻ C, 40 percent rank C first and are indifferent between A and B. Show sincere voting elects C; show that if everyone expects B to be hopeless, the B-voters' best response elects A; exhibit the other Duvergerian equilibrium; and say what in the model picks between them. *Formal.* (e) An invented reform memo claims that moving from single-member plurality to national-list proportional representation will "shift spending from pork to public goods." In 150 words, name the model from 2.6 the memo relies on, the assumption that drives its prediction, and what the evidence would need to show. *Exegetical.*

### Module 3: Collective action and the choice of rules

Why groups fail to act on shared interests, how some succeed, and how rules for deciding are themselves chosen.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3.1 | Free riding and threshold games | Model collective action as a game and find its equilibria | the $n$-person prisoner's dilemma; step-level (threshold) public goods and their pure and mixed equilibria; assurance vs free-rider structure; participation as the political case (1.2 revisited); the Samuelson condition cited to [`public-economics`](../public-economics/syllabus.md) 1.1 |
| 3.2 | Olson's logic of collective action | Explain why small and unequal groups organize and large equal ones do not | group size and share of the benefit; privileged, intermediate and latent groups; the largest-share member as provider (contributor-set result cited to [`public-economics`](../public-economics/syllabus.md) 1.2); exploitation of the great by the small; selective incentives and the by-product theory; the rivalry objection (Hardin) |
| 3.3 | The commons and common-pool resources | Find what sustains restraint on a shared resource, and read Ostrom's principles into the incentive constraint | static tragedy recapped from [`game-theory-refresher`](../game-theory-refresher/syllabus.md) 1.4; open-access rent dissipation (Gordon); the repeated commons game and the critical discount factor; Ostrom's design principles (monitoring, graduated sanctions, clear boundaries) as terms in that constraint; where Ostrom's account goes beyond the repeated-game reading |
| 3.4 | Constitutions and the choice of rules | Pick a decision threshold as an optimization, and say why majority rule is special | Buchanan–Tullock: external costs vs decision costs, the constitutional stage; the Rae–Taylor result that majority rule maximizes a veiled voter's chance of getting her way; supermajority and status-quo bias (May's theorem cited to [`social-choice`](../social-choice/syllabus.md) 1.2) |
| 3.5 | Agenda setters and veto players | Solve for the policy a setter extracts, and for the status quos no one can move | the Romer–Rosenthal setter model and the reversion point; veto players and win-sets (Tsebelis); pivotal politics and the gridlock interval (Krehbiel); negative agenda control (institutions described in [`political-institutions`](../political-institutions/syllabus.md) 4.2) |

**Boss problem 3:** (a) Three players each choose to contribute (cost 1) or not; a public good worth 3 to every player is produced if at least two contribute. Find all pure-strategy equilibria and every symmetric mixed equilibrium. *Formal.* (b) A group values total provision $G$ at $V(G) = 20\sqrt{G}$, split by fixed shares; provision costs 1 per unit. If the largest member has share $s$ and provides alone, find $G$ and its ratio to the efficient level for $s = 0.5$ and for twenty equal members ($s = 0.05$); confirm that with $s = 0.5$ and the rest split equally among nine others, no one else wants to add. *Formal.* (c) Four fishers each catch 1 (restrain) or 2 (overfish); price per fish is $10$ minus the total catch. Show overfishing is the stage-game equilibrium, and find the discount factor above which grim trigger sustains universal restraint. *Formal.* (d) A setter at 0 proposes to a median voter at 5 (Euclidean preferences). Find the enacted policy when the status quo is 9 and when it is 4. Then with two veto pivots at 3 and 7, find the gridlock interval. *Formal.* (e) With external costs $100(1-q)^2$ and decision costs $c\,q^2$ for a required majority fraction $q$, find the optimal $q$ as a function of $c$, its value at $c = 25$, and the $c$ at which simple majority is optimal. *Formal.* (f) In 300 words: does Ostrom's evidence refute the "tragedy of the commons," or confirm the repeated-game reading of (c)? Map at least two design principles onto terms in your answer to (c), state the strongest case for the other side, and reply. *Evaluative.*

### Module 4: Accountability, interests and rents

Politicians as agents of voters, and the organized interests working around that contract.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4.1 | Elections as accountability | Compute the re-election cutoff that disciplines an incumbent, and separate sanctioning from selection | moral hazard with retrospective voting (Barro, Ferejohn); the incumbent's incentive constraint; adverse selection and choosing good types (Fearon); combining the two (Besley's *Principled Agents?*); contracts cited to [`grad-micro`](../grad-micro/syllabus.md) 5.4 |
| 4.2 | Career concerns and pandering | See how reputation disciplines effort and distorts choices | signal-jamming career concerns (Holmström); pandering to voter priors (Canes-Wrone–Herron–Shotts, Maskin–Tirole); term limits as removing the last-period incentive; signaling tools from [`grad-game-theory`](../grad-game-theory/syllabus.md) 4.5 |
| 4.3 | Lobbying: protection for sale | Solve a menu auction and compute the Grossman–Helpman tariff | menu auctions and truthful contributions (Bernheim–Whinston); the government's weight on welfare; the Grossman–Helpman tariff formula (organized vs unorganized sectors, import elasticity, output-to-imports ratio); informational lobbying as costly signaling |
| 4.4 | Regulatory capture | Explain why regulation can serve the regulated, and what limits it | the Stigler–Peltzman demand for regulation; concentrated benefits and diffuse costs (3.2 revisited); pressure-group competition (Becker); information-based capture (Laffont–Tirole); the revolving door; media capture (Besley–Prat) |
| 4.5 | Rent-seeking contests | Compute the surplus dissipated in the scramble for a politically created rent | Tullock's welfare cost of transfers and Krueger's rent-seeking; the contest success function and its exponent; symmetric equilibrium effort; partial vs full dissipation (Posner) |
| 4.6 | Political budget cycles | Model when incumbents manipulate policy before elections, and why rational voters might let them | opportunistic cycles with adaptive expectations (Nordhaus); competence signaling and equilibrium cycles (Rogoff–Sibert, Rogoff); rational partisan cycles (Alesina); evidence cited to `empirical-political-economy` |

**Boss problem 4:** (a) $n$ risk-neutral players compete for a rent $R = 100$ with win probability $x_i^r / \sum_j x_j^r$. Find the symmetric equilibrium effort, total effort and each player's payoff for $(n, r) = (2, 1)$ and $(5, 1)$, and for $(2, 2)$; say what fraction of the rent is dissipated in each and why $(2, 2)$ is a boundary case. *Formal.* (b) An infinitely-lived incumbent can take rents $r \in [0, 10]$ each term, values office at $W = 2$ per term, and discounts at $\delta = 0.8$. The voter re-elects if and only if $r \le \bar r$; a removed incumbent never returns. Find the smallest cutoff the incumbent will respect, show how it moves with $\delta$, and say what a binding term limit does in the final term. *Formal.* (c) In the Grossman–Helpman model the government weights welfare at $a = 4$, 20 percent of the population is organized, and a sector has output-to-imports ratio 2 and import-demand elasticity 1.5. Compute the equilibrium ad valorem tariff if the sector is organized and if it is not, and interpret the sign of the second. *Formal.* (d) An invented press release from a telecom regulator announces a rule that raises entry costs "to protect service quality." Say which capture model from 4.4 would predict the rule, what observable would distinguish it from a public-interest explanation, and which model of 4.3–4.5 prices the lobbying behind it. *Exegetical.* (e) In 300 words, argue whether term limits help or hurt voters, using the sanctioning logic of 4.1 and the pandering logic of 4.2; state the assumption your verdict most depends on and reply to the strongest objection. *Evaluative.*

### Module 5: Bargaining, coalitions and redistribution

Dividing the budget inside the legislature, forming governments, and the politics of who pays and who receives.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5.1 | Legislative bargaining: Baron–Ferejohn | Solve divide-the-dollar with a random proposer | closed rule, random recognition, stationary subgame-perfect equilibrium, minimum winning coalitions, proposer power; open rule contrasted; Rubinstein recalled from [`grad-game-theory`](../grad-game-theory/syllabus.md) 3.5 |
| 5.2 | Coalition and government formation | Predict which governments form and how they split office | minimal winning and minimum-size coalitions (Riker); minimal connected coalitions (Axelrod); Gamson's law vs the Baron–Ferejohn formateur premium and the empirical puzzle between them; portfolio allocation (Laver–Shepsle); core and Shapley value cited to [`grad-game-theory`](../grad-game-theory/syllabus.md) 6.1–6.2; procedures described in [`political-institutions`](../political-institutions/syllabus.md) 3.2 |
| 5.3 | The Meltzer–Richard model | Derive the equilibrium tax rate from the income distribution | linear tax with lump-sum transfer; labor-supply distortion and the revenue peak; single-crossing in productivity and the decisive median voter (representative voter theorem from [`social-choice`](../social-choice/syllabus.md) 3.4); the tax rate as a function of mean over median income |
| 5.4 | The political economy of inequality | Say why redistribution may not track inequality as the model predicts | prospect of upward mobility (Bénabou–Ok); income-skewed turnout (1.2 revisited); a second dimension of politics (Roemer); probabilistic-voting redistribution to swing groups (2.3 revisited); fairness beliefs (Alesina–Angeletos); the weak cross-country link between inequality and redistribution reported as contested, identification cited to `empirical-political-economy` |

**Boss problem 5:** (a) Five legislators divide a dollar under a closed rule; each round a proposer is recognized with equal probability, and all discount at $\delta = 0.9$. Find the stationary subgame-perfect equilibrium proposal, the share the proposer keeps, and each legislator's ex ante value; check the values are consistent. *Formal.* (b) Three parties hold 40, 35 and 25 of 100 seats (51 needed). List the minimal winning coalitions, find Riker's minimum-size coalition, and compute its Gamson's-law split and its Baron–Ferejohn split with equal recognition and $\delta = 0.9$. Say what the difference implies about seats as a source of bargaining power. *Formal (computation); exegetical (interpretation).* (c) Voters have utility $c - \ell^2/2$ with consumption $c = (1-t)\,w\,\ell + T$ and a balanced-budget transfer $T$. Five voters have $w^2 = 1, 2, 3, 6, 13$. Derive each voter's preferred tax rate, show preferences are single-crossing, and find the majority-rule rate. Then raise the top value to 23 and recompute. Express the equilibrium rate in terms of the mean-to-median income ratio and show it never exceeds 1/2. *Formal.* (d) In 300 words: the model predicts that rising inequality raises redistribution. Using 5.4, name two assumptions of (c) that an explanation of a weaker observed relationship must drop, say which one you find most promising and what evidence would test it, and reply to the strongest objection. *Evaluative.*
