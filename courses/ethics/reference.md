# Ethics · Reference Card

> Open book. This card is meant to be open while you work — including during
> quizzes. Nothing here is something you're expected to recall cold.

The course runs four normative families and then asks what lies under all of them. **Consequentialism** ranks outcomes and tells you to produce the best one (Module 1). **Kantian deontology** puts constraints and options on what you may do, whatever the ranking (Modules 2–3). **Virtue ethics and natural law** start from human nature, through the good agent or through the goods reason grasps (Module 4). **Contract theories** ground morality in agreement, whether for mutual advantage or as justifiability to each person (Module 5). **Metaethics** asks whether any of it is true, and how anyone could know (Module 6). Use the card mid-problem to look up which view says what, which premise an argument turns on, and which objection a reply is answering. The problems are about *using* these, not reciting them.

## Positions and distinctions

### Classical utilitarianism

Do whatever produces the most happiness, counting everyone equally. It is four separable claims: hedonism (theory of the good), maximizing (theory of the right), impartiality (scope), and acts as the focus. Each can be changed while the others stay fixed, so say which one an objection targets. The "classical" label here means the act version; whether Mill was an act or rule utilitarian is disputed.

*Introduced:* [1.1](lessons/01-01-classical-utilitarianism.md)

### The hedonic calculus

Bentham's dimensions fixing the value of a pleasure or pain: intensity, duration, certainty, propinquity, fecundity (chance of further pleasures), purity (chance of no pains), and extent (number reached). It assumes units of pleasure comparable across people.

*Introduced:* [1.1](lessons/01-01-classical-utilitarianism.md)

### The naturalistic fallacy

Moore's name for defining a normative notion ("good", "desirable") by a natural property. First met as Moore's attack on Mill's "desirable"; it applies even to supernatural definitions such as "what God commands". It is not the same as Hume's is-ought gap, which is about inference, not definition.

*Introduced:* [1.1](lessons/01-01-classical-utilitarianism.md), developed in [6.1](lessons/06-01-moral-realism.md)

### Hedonism

Well-being lies in experience: pleasure is good for you, pain bad. **Quantitative** (Bentham): total pleasure minus pain, whatever the source. **Qualitative** (Mill): some pleasures are higher in kind, as shown by the decided preference of competent judges who know both ("better to be Socrates dissatisfied than a fool satisfied"). Mill keeps pleasure as the only good; whether ranking quality needs a non-pleasure value is the pressure point.

*Introduced:* [1.2](lessons/01-02-what-is-good-for-a-person.md)

### Desire-satisfaction theories

Well-being lies in getting what you want, where satisfaction is a match between desire and world, not a feeling. **Actual** desire views count present desires; **informed** (ideal) views (Brandt, Railton) count what you would want with full information and clear reasoning. The stranger on the train shows the view needs a scope restriction to desires about your own life, which is hard to state without circularity.

*Introduced:* [1.2](lessons/01-02-what-is-good-for-a-person.md)

### Objective list theories

Some things (knowledge, friendship, achievement, health, autonomy, often pleasure) make a life go better for its subject whether or not they are wanted or enjoyed. The label is Parfit's. Main objection: **alienation**, a good the person does not care about at all. **Hybrid** views require both objective worth and the subject's engagement, and inherit some subjectivity.

*Introduced:* [1.2](lessons/01-02-what-is-good-for-a-person.md)

### Distribution-insensitivity and prioritarianism

A summing view ranks equal totals equally however unequally they are spread. **Prioritarianism** (Parfit) ranks by $W = \sum_i f(u_i)$ with $f$ increasing and concave, so a unit counts for more the worse off its recipient. It fixes distribution but still aggregates, so the sacrifice cases survive. Diminishing marginal utility of *resources* gives a utilitarian equality of income, not concern for who gets the well-being.

*Introduced:* [1.3](lessons/01-03-justice-and-the-separateness-of-persons.md)

### Side constraints

Rights that limit what you may do to someone in pursuit of any goal, including the goal of minimizing rights violations (Nozick). Contrast a **utilitarianism of rights**, which puts violations into the outcome and minimizes them. A constraint limits what you do; it is not a goal you advance.

*Introduced:* [1.3](lessons/01-03-justice-and-the-separateness-of-persons.md), developed in [2.4](lessons/02-04-constraints-and-options.md)

### Integrity

The fit between a person's actions and the projects and convictions they come from (not honesty; a villain can have it). Williams's charge is that act utilitarianism, by counting your projects as one person's preferences, cannot represent it, and misdescribes cases even where its verdict is right.

*Introduced:* [1.4](lessons/01-04-integrity-and-demandingness.md)

### Demandingness and supererogation

Act consequentialism requires giving until further giving costs as much as it prevents. The objection runs modus tollens on Singer's conditional: morality does not require that, because much of such giving is supererogatory (Urmson's category above duty). Replies: deny the verdict as self-interest (Singer, Kagan); an agent-centred prerogative (Scheffler); a fair-share view (Murphy).

*Introduced:* [1.4](lessons/01-04-integrity-and-demandingness.md)

### The fair-share view

You owe what you would owe if every well-off person did their part, and no more (Liam Murphy). The price critics press: when another bystander walks off from two drowning children and you save one, the view seems to say you have done enough.

*Introduced:* [1.4](lessons/01-04-integrity-and-demandingness.md)

### Options and agent-centred prerogatives

A permission not to produce the best outcome. Scheffler models it as a licence to weight your own interests $M$ times as heavily as anyone else's. Kagan's objection: a bare prerogative cannot tell *not helping* from *harming*, since the cost avoided and the benefit gained weigh the same; blocking the second requires a constraint against harming.

*Introduced:* [1.4](lessons/01-04-integrity-and-demandingness.md), [2.4](lessons/02-04-constraints-and-options.md)

### Rule consequentialism

An act is wrong if and only if forbidden by the ideal code, the one whose *internalization* by the overwhelming majority (Hooker: about 90 per cent) of each new generation has the greatest expected value, net of teaching costs, with some priority to the worst off and a rule to prevent disaster. Hooker's defence is fit with considered judgements (reflective equilibrium), not a commitment to maximizing.

*Introduced:* [1.5](lessons/01-05-modern-consequentialism.md)

### Rule worship

Smart's charge against a rule consequentialist who will not add exceptions: it is irrational to follow a rule you value only for its results in a case where you know breaking it gives better results. It is the second horn of the collapse dilemma, and the same structure as the paradox of deontology.

*Introduced:* [1.5](lessons/01-05-modern-consequentialism.md)

### Sophisticated consequentialism

Railton: **objective** consequentialism (an act is right iff it would in fact produce the most good) does not entail **subjective** consequentialism (always try to calculate), and may condemn it. Juan loves Linda without calculating, under a counterfactual condition: his commitments would shift if the relationship did serious harm. Answers alienation; does not touch the surgeon, since the criterion is still act-consequentialist.

*Introduced:* [1.5](lessons/01-05-modern-consequentialism.md)

### The two-level view

Hare: the **intuitive** level runs on firm, well-chosen principles; the **critical** level selects them and settles their conflicts. The archangel needs only critical thinking, the prole only intuitive; real people are some of each.

*Introduced:* [1.5](lessons/01-05-modern-consequentialism.md)

### Actual and expected consequences

Whether rightness depends on what happens or on what the agent's *evidence* makes expectable. Jackson's drug case: the certain partial cure is the only sensible choice and certainly not the actually best one. Actualists reply that it is wrong but blameless, at the price of making the right act unknowable. Expectation is relative to evidence, not to careless belief.

*Introduced:* [1.5](lessons/01-05-modern-consequentialism.md)

### Scalar consequentialism

Acts are only better or worse; "right" and "wrong" are not basic (Norcross). It dissolves demandingness by removing the threshold of requirement, and so stops answering "must I not?".

*Introduced:* [1.5](lessons/01-05-modern-consequentialism.md)

### The good will

The only thing good without qualification: a will acting on the right principle for the right reason. Talents, temperament and gifts of fortune are good only conditionally. Its worth survives total failure, though it must genuinely try ("the summoning of all means in our power").

*Introduced:* [2.1](lessons/02-01-the-good-will-and-acting-from-duty.md)

### Acting from duty and in accordance with duty

An act in accordance with duty is right; it has **moral worth** only if done *from* duty. Three motives: self-interest (the prudent shopkeeper), immediate inclination (the sympathetic philanthropist), and duty (the philanthropist whose sympathy is gone). Herman's reading: the sorrowful philanthropist is a test for spotting duty, not an ideal, and duty can govern inclination rather than compete with it.

*Introduced:* [2.1](lessons/02-01-the-good-will-and-acting-from-duty.md)

### Hypothetical and categorical imperatives

A hypothetical imperative commands a means to an end you have, so drop the end and the ought goes. A categorical imperative commands an action as necessary in itself. Grammar settles nothing. Foot's objection: etiquette is also non-hypothetical, so being non-hypothetical cannot by itself supply reason's authority.

*Introduced:* [2.1](lessons/02-01-the-good-will-and-acting-from-duty.md)

### The Formula of Universal Law

"Act only on that maxim whereby thou canst at the same time will that it should become a universal law" (Abbott). Its working variant imagines the maxim as a law of nature. The question is whether the maxim is self-defeating or unwillable when universal, not whether universal adoption has bad results (that is rule consequentialism's question).

*Introduced:* [2.2](lessons/02-02-the-formula-of-universal-law.md)

### Contradiction in conception and contradiction in will

Failing the conception test (the universalized world cannot coherently contain the maxim working) violates a **perfect** duty. Failing the will test (the world is conceivable but a rational agent cannot will it) violates an **imperfect** duty. Passing both makes the maxim permissible, not required. Kant's four examples: suicide and the false promise fail in conception; neglected talents and refused beneficence fail in will.

*Introduced:* [2.2](lessons/02-02-the-formula-of-universal-law.md)

### Three readings of contradiction in conception

Korsgaard's: **logical** (the universal maxim cannot be consistently described), **teleological** (it defeats the purpose of a practice or natural feeling), **practical** (in the universalized world the agent could not use the maxim to get its end). She defends the practical reading, strongest on conventional acts like promising and weaker on natural acts like violence. Kant's suicide example rests on the teleological reading, which few think works.

*Introduced:* [2.2](lessons/02-02-the-formula-of-universal-law.md)

### The maxim description problem

The universalization test misfires both ways. **False positives:** innocent maxims fail (never sell toy trains; play tennis when courts are empty). **False negatives:** gerrymandered detail (green shirt, Tuesday, a lender named Oskar) lets wrong maxims pass. Replies: test the *actual* maxim; O'Neill's underlying intentions. Critics say both presuppose an account of morally relevant features.

*Introduced:* [2.2](lessons/02-02-the-formula-of-universal-law.md)

### The Formula of Humanity

"So act as to treat humanity, whether in thine own person or in that of any other, in every case as an end withal, never as means only." Using people as means is fine; using them *merely* as means is not. Kant claimed the formulas are equivalent; Korsgaard argues they come apart on the murderer at the door.

*Introduced:* [2.3](lessons/02-03-humanity-autonomy-and-the-lie.md)

### Mere means and possible consent

O'Neill's reading: you use someone merely as a means when you act on a maxim she *could not possibly* consent to, a structural test, not "would she agree if asked?". Rivals: actual consent (too strict and too lax), shared end (strains on adversarial practices), and Parfit's scepticism (mere-means treatment is neither necessary nor sufficient for wrongness).

*Introduced:* [2.3](lessons/02-03-humanity-autonomy-and-the-lie.md)

### The kingdom of ends and autonomy

Picture a community where everyone is at once lawmaker and subject, and each law respects every member's ends. **Autonomy** is the will bound by a law it gives itself as a rational will, the law reason gives every rational agent, not a law you invent for yourself alone. Its opposite is heteronomy.

*Introduced:* [2.3](lessons/02-03-humanity-autonomy-and-the-lie.md)

### The murderer at the door

Constant: truthfulness is owed only to those with a right to the truth. Kant ("On a Supposed Right to Lie", 1797): truthfulness in statements you cannot avoid is a duty of right to everyone, and the liar owns the consequences; you may still refuse or shut the door. Korsgaard: Universal Law permits the lie, Humanity looks stricter, and in non-ideal conditions you may depart from the ideal only as far as Universal Law allows.

*Introduced:* [2.3](lessons/02-03-humanity-autonomy-and-the-lie.md)

### Agent-relative and agent-neutral reasons

A reason is agent-relative if its general statement ineliminably refers to the person who has it ("a killing *by you*"), agent-neutral if not. Terms from Parfit, developed by Nagel. Agent-relative is not selfish: "do not lie to anyone" is agent-relative and wholly other-regarding.

*Introduced:* [2.4](lessons/02-04-constraints-and-options.md)

### Inviolability

Kamm's reply to the paradox of deontology: constraints express a *status*, being a person who may not be killed for others' benefit. Status is fixed by what morality permits, so permitting the killing of one to save two would make all three less inviolable. The value is honoured, not promoted (Pettit's terms). Cost: why is higher status worth a higher chance of being killed?

*Introduced:* [2.4](lessons/02-04-constraints-and-options.md)

### Threshold deontology

A constraint holds below some threshold of harm prevented and lapses above it (Michael Moore among others). Strains: any line is arbitrary and makes the constraint maximally sensitive to marginal arithmetic at the threshold; above it, the view looks like consequentialism with an exception. Defenders say the constraint still weighs past the line.

*Introduced:* [2.4](lessons/02-04-constraints-and-options.md)

### The doctrine of doing and allowing

Other things equal, doing harm is harder to justify than allowing harm. It does not make allowing permissible; it raises the bar for doing. **Quinn:** harmful positive agency faces a stronger presumption than harmful negative agency, and holding back *because* the harm serves your plan counts as positive agency. **Foot:** negative duties (not to harm) are stricter than positive duties (to aid).

*Introduced:* [3.1](lessons/03-01-doing-and-allowing.md)

### Withdrawing aid

McMahan: withdrawing aid *you* are providing, before it is self-sustaining, is allowing; removing someone else's aid or protection, or your own aid that now runs on its own, is doing. The doctor switching off his own ventilator lets die; a stranger doing it kills. The climber's partner letting go of the rope allows; unscrewing a self-sustaining anchor does.

*Introduced:* [3.1](lessons/03-01-doing-and-allowing.md)

### The additive fallacy and contextual interaction

Kagan: the equal-difference argument assumes each factor adds a fixed amount of wrongness in every context. Factors can interact, so a pair where the verdict holds shows only that the factor is idle *there*. Kamm's principle of contextual interaction builds on this. Asymmetry: one pair where the verdict *moves* shows a factor matters at least sometimes.

*Introduced:* [3.1](lessons/03-01-doing-and-allowing.md)

### Intended and foreseen effects

Double effect's line. The counterfactual test: *if the harm failed to happen, would your plan have failed?* The strategic bomber says no; the terror bomber says yes, so for him the deaths are a means. Intention is fixed by the plan, not by the description the agent prefers. Rooted in Aquinas: acts "take their species according to what is intended" (*ST* II-II q.64 a.7).

*Introduced:* [3.2](lessons/03-02-the-doctrine-of-double-effect.md)

### The closeness problem

With a fine enough description almost any harm drops out of the intention: the cave explorers need an open exit, not a dead man; the terror bomber needs only that civilians *seem* dead. A line pressed by Bennett concludes the intended/foreseen line cannot bear moral weight. Replies: a closeness criterion (hard to state without the verdicts), or Quinn's shift from intention to agency.

*Introduced:* [3.2](lessons/03-02-the-doctrine-of-double-effect.md)

### The means principle

It is especially hard to justify harming someone by making his involvement serve your purpose. Quinn's **harmful direct agency**: harming a victim because you deliberately involve him in order to further your goal by way of that involvement. It looks at the victim's role in the plan, so it can drop the agent's psychology and survive the Scanlon-Thomson objection. Ancestor: the Formula of Humanity.

*Introduced:* [3.3](lessons/03-03-testing-the-principles-on-trolleys.md)

### The doctrine of triple effect

Kamm: acting *in order to* bring about an effect differs from acting *because* you believe it will occur. In Loop you turn in order to save the five and count on the one's being hit without aiming at it; double effect forbids only the first. Verdict: Loop permissible, Footbridge not.

*Introduced:* [3.3](lessons/03-03-testing-the-principles-on-trolleys.md)

### Thomson on the trolley

**1985:** the bystander may turn because he makes an existing threat threaten fewer, by means that do not themselves infringe anyone's rights; pushing infringes rights in the act itself. **2008 recantation:** if the bystander would not turn the trolley onto himself, he may not make the one pay that cost, so the bystander may not turn at all; the driver may. On that view Foot's explanation was right all along.

*Introduced:* [3.3](lessons/03-03-testing-the-principles-on-trolleys.md)

### Intention and permissibility

The deflationary view (Thomson 1991; Scanlon, *Moral Dimensions*): the agent's intention bears on blame and on the act's meaning, not on whether it is permissible. Two doctors give the same dose, one aiming at relief and one at death; the act is equally permissible for both. Double effect's defenders reply that intention shapes the act's *object*.

*Introduced:* [3.3](lessons/03-03-testing-the-principles-on-trolleys.md)

### Eudaimonia and the two tests

The chief good is **complete** (chosen always for itself, never for anything further) and **self-sufficient** (on its own makes a life choiceworthy and lacking nothing). Wealth fails completeness; honour is wanted as a sign of excellence; pleasure is complete but, for Aristotle, not self-sufficient for a human life. Flourishing is an activity over a complete life.

*Introduced:* [4.1](lessons/04-01-aristotle-the-human-good.md)

### External goods

Excellent activity needs friends, resources and standing as instruments, and some lacks (good birth, children, beauty) take the lustre from blessedness. Priam: the good person bears misfortune nobly and is never *miserable*, but may fall short of *blessed*. Forced by "in a complete life".

*Introduced:* [4.1](lessons/04-01-aristotle-the-human-good.md)

### Neo-Aristotelian naturalism

Foot, *Natural Goodness*: goodness is judged by the life form of the species, as a wolf that will not hunt with the pack is defective, with practical reason playing that role in the human life form. It answers the post-Darwinian teleology objection without cosmic purpose.

*Introduced:* [4.1](lessons/04-01-aristotle-the-human-good.md), [4.3](lessons/04-03-modern-virtue-ethics.md)

### Virtue as a settled state

A virtue is neither a feeling nor a capacity but a *hexis*: "a state of character concerned with choice, lying in a mean, i.e. the mean relative to us, this being determined by a rational principle, and by that principle by which the man of practical wisdom would determine it" (NE II.6). Acquired by habituation: we become just by doing just acts. Acting virtuously requires knowing, choosing the act for its own sake, and acting from firm character.

*Introduced:* [4.2](lessons/04-02-aristotle-virtue-and-practical-wisdom.md)

### The doctrine of the mean

For each sphere of feeling or action there is a too-much and a too-little; virtue hits the target relative to the person and situation, not the arithmetic midpoint (Milo's portion). The mean can be extreme (great anger at cruelty). Some things have no mean: spite, envy, adultery, theft, murder. Lean away from the extreme you are more prone to.

| Sphere | Deficiency | Mean | Excess |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fear and confidence | cowardice | courage | rashness |
| Bodily pleasures | insensibility | temperance | self-indulgence |
| Giving and taking money | meanness | liberality | prodigality |
| Anger | inirascibility | good temper | irascibility |
| Giving and sparing pain socially | flattery (obsequiousness) | friendliness | contentiousness |

*Introduced:* [4.2](lessons/04-02-aristotle-virtue-and-practical-wisdom.md)

### Practical wisdom

*Phronesis*: the trained perception of the ultimate particular ("the decision rests with perception"). Virtue makes the aim right; practical wisdom finds what serves it here. Without a good aim it is mere cleverness. You cannot be good in the strict sense without practical wisdom, nor practically wise without virtue (NE VI.13).

*Introduced:* [4.2](lessons/04-02-aristotle-virtue-and-practical-wisdom.md)

### Virtue, continence and incontinence

The **temperate** person has no contrary appetite; the **continent** person wants the wrong thing and holds out; the **incontinent** (*akratic*) person knows and gives in. Aristotle ranks the continent below the temperate. Against Socrates, the akratic has the knowledge but not in active use: appetite blocks the particular premise. Two kinds: weakness (deliberates, fails to stand by it) and impetuosity (never deliberates).

*Introduced:* [4.2](lessons/04-02-aristotle-virtue-and-practical-wisdom.md)

### The law conception of ethics

Anscombe, "Modern Moral Philosophy" (1958): the special moral "ought" is a leftover from belief in a divine legislator, keeping its force and losing its content. Set it aside and work from concepts with content, such as what humans need to flourish. The paper named "consequentialism".

*Introduced:* [4.3](lessons/04-03-modern-virtue-ethics.md)

### The criterion of right action

Hursthouse: an action is right iff it is what a virtuous agent would *characteristically* (acting in character) do in the circumstances. A virtuous agent has and exercises the virtues; a virtue is a trait a human needs to flourish. The chain runs agent, virtues, flourishing, so it is not circular, though whether "needed to flourish" needs prior moral judgement is the live question.

*Introduced:* [4.3](lessons/04-03-modern-virtue-ethics.md)

### The application objection

"Do what the virtuous agent would do" is empty for someone not already virtuous. Hursthouse's replies: the v-rules give a great deal of guidance; ask people you reasonably regard as better than you; rivals face application problems too. Harder: in circumstances only a non-virtuous agent gets into (two incompatible promises), the best way out is action-guiding but not a *right* action in the full sense.

*Introduced:* [4.2](lessons/04-02-aristotle-virtue-and-practical-wisdom.md), [4.3](lessons/04-03-modern-virtue-ethics.md)

### Global, local and mixed traits

**Global** traits are robust across situations, as virtue ethics conceives virtues. **Local** traits (Doris) are narrow: helpful when in a good mood and not rushed. **Mixed** traits (Christian Miller) are stable but neither virtues nor vices. Harman doubted ordinary traits exist at all. Annas's skill model makes virtue practical intelligence, not a behavioural regularity.

*Introduced:* [4.3](lessons/04-03-modern-virtue-ethics.md)

### Eternal law and natural law

Eternal law is God's reason governing creation. Natural law is "the participation of the eternal law in the rational creature" (q.91 a.2): creatures share by inclination, rational creatures also by grasping ends and directing themselves. The frame explains the ground; the epistemic claim is that the precepts are knowable by natural reason without revelation.

*Introduced:* [4.4](lessons/04-04-natural-law-aquinas.md)

### The first precept of natural law

"Good is to be done and pursued, and evil is to be avoided" (*ST* I-II q.94 a.2). Practical reason's first principle, as non-contradiction is theoretical reason's: formal on purpose, since to deliberate is to aim at something seen as good. Held through *synderesis*; self-evident.

*Introduced:* [4.4](lessons/04-04-natural-law-aquinas.md)

### The order of natural inclinations

Three levels, each yielding goods reason apprehends: (i) with all substances, preserving one's being; (ii) with other animals, "sexual intercourse, education of offspring and so forth"; (iii) proper to reason, "to know the truth about God, and to live in society", hence shun ignorance and avoid offending those one lives among. "According to the order of natural inclinations, is the order of the precepts." Whether the order implies priority in action is disputed.

*Introduced:* [4.4](lessons/04-04-natural-law-aquinas.md)

### Primary and secondary precepts

Primary precepts are the same for all and cannot be blotted out of human hearts in general. Secondary precepts are conclusions applied to particular matter, true "in the majority of cases", failing more the further into detail you go (q.94 a.4): return deposits, unless the owner will use them against his country. Knowledge can fail too (theft among the Germans), and vicious custom can erase secondary precepts (a.6).

*Introduced:* [4.4](lessons/04-04-natural-law-aquinas.md)

### Self-evident in itself and to us

A proposition is self-evident *in itself* when the predicate is contained in the notion of the subject, and *to us* when we actually grasp the terms. Some truths are self-evident only to the wise. So self-evidence never meant obvious to everyone, and disagreement alone does not refute it.

*Introduced:* [4.4](lessons/04-04-natural-law-aquinas.md), [4.5](lessons/04-05-the-new-natural-law-theory.md)

### The basic goods

NNL (Grisez, Finnis, Boyle): irreducible reasons for acting, grasped by practical understanding with inclinations as data rather than premises. Finnis's 1980 list: life, knowledge, play, aesthetic experience, sociability (friendship), practical reasonableness, religion; later statements add marriage. A version of the objective-list theory.

*Introduced:* [4.5](lessons/04-05-the-new-natural-law-theory.md)

### Incommensurability

No common unit in which a quantity of one basic good trades against another, and no hierarchy among them, so "produce the most good" has no content. It does not forbid ranking options by commitments, promises, fairness or plan of life; it denies a *pre-moral* metric. Formally, a denial of completeness.

*Introduced:* [4.5](lessons/04-05-the-new-natural-law-theory.md)

### Modes of responsibility

Grisez's term (Finnis: requirements of practical reasonableness), including no arbitrary preferences among goods or persons. The strictest: never choose to destroy, damage or impede an instance of a basic good, as end or as means. Side effects are permitted, which is why NNL depends on the intended/foreseen distinction (the courier's capsule).

*Introduced:* [4.5](lessons/04-05-the-new-natural-law-theory.md)

### Ontological and epistemic priority of nature

The crux between NNL and Thomistic naturalism (Hittinger, McInerny, Veatch, Lisska). Both agree human nature is what *makes* the goods good (ontological priority). NNL denies we must *know* the nature first in order to know the goods (epistemic priority); the neo-Thomists insist we must, or the list has no ground.

*Introduced:* [4.5](lessons/04-05-the-new-natural-law-theory.md)

### The is-ought gap

Hume, *Treatise* III.i.1: a move from *is* to *ought* expresses "some new relation", owes an explanation, and seems inconceivable as a deduction. Read closely it is a challenge, not a proof. NNL accepts the logical gap and denies natural law ever crossed it; neo-Thomists deny the gap exists for a teleological nature; Moore turned it from inference into meaning.

*Introduced:* [4.5](lessons/04-05-the-new-natural-law-theory.md), [6.1](lessons/06-01-moral-realism.md)

### Contractarianism

Morality as mutual advantage: the rules rational, non-tuistic agents *would* agree to because each does better inside the scheme than outside (Hobbes, Gauthier). The contract is hypothetical, a test of justification. Premises about the surplus give morality's content; the constrained-maximizer argument gives its authority.

*Introduced:* [5.1](lessons/05-01-contractarianism-morality-as-mutual-advantage.md)

### The compliance problem

The deal is good for everyone, but given others' compliance each does better by defecting, since breaking dominates in the prisoner's dilemma. The Foole is the agent who notices the dominance argument. Gauthier's answer is to choose a disposition, not an act; repeated interaction (the folk theorem) is a different answer.

*Introduced:* [5.1](lessons/05-01-contractarianism-morality-as-mutual-advantage.md)

### Constrained and straightforward maximizers

A **constrained** maximizer cooperates with those it expects to cooperate; a **straightforward** maximizer defects whenever defection pays. With translucent dispositions, constrained maximizers are admitted to cooperative schemes and do better overall. The cooperation is conditional, nothing like Kant's unconditional duty. See the payoff model under Formulas.

*Introduced:* [5.1](lessons/05-01-contractarianism-morality-as-mutual-advantage.md)

### The problem of the weak

Those who can neither contribute to the surplus nor threaten it (animals, people with severe cognitive disabilities, future people) have nothing to bargain with. Hume: we would owe such creatures "gentle usage" but no restraint of justice. Replies: indirect protection through what the strong care about; narrow the theory to justice; bite the bullet. It drives Scanlon's alternative.

*Introduced:* [5.1](lessons/05-01-contractarianism-morality-as-mutual-advantage.md)

### Contractualism

Scanlon: an act is wrong if disallowed by any set of principles for the general regulation of behaviour that no one could reasonably reject as a basis for informed, unforced general agreement. The motive is the value of being able to justify yourself to others, so the powerless have full standing. Complaints are weighed against the strongest complaint to each alternative principle, from **generic** standpoints, and may rest on non-welfare reasons (fairness, control, responsibility). It covers "what we owe to each other", not all of morality.

*Introduced:* [5.2](lessons/05-02-contractualism-what-no-one-could-reasonably-reject.md)

### The individualist restriction

Only individuals' complaints on their own behalf count, and they are not summed across persons (Parfit's label). The separateness-of-persons objection turned into a decision procedure. It does not ignore numbers: numbers break ties between comparable complaints, but many smaller complaints never outweigh one larger one.

*Introduced:* [5.2](lessons/05-02-contractualism-what-no-one-could-reasonably-reject.md)

### Taurek on the numbers

"Should the Numbers Count?" (1977): each loss is a loss to that person, and no one suffers five deaths, so with equal harms give each an equal chance by tossing a coin, even one against five. Scanlon's tie-breaking argument is built to save the five without summing.

*Introduced:* [5.2](lessons/05-02-contractualism-what-no-one-could-reasonably-reject.md)

### Relevance views

A repair to the strict restriction (Kamm, Voorhoeve, with Scanlon tentatively open): harms close enough in seriousness to the worst harm may aggregate against it; trivial ones never do. Paralysis may count against death; headaches never would. Its bill is saying what "close enough" means against chains of slightly worse cases.

*Introduced:* [5.2](lessons/05-02-contractualism-what-no-one-could-reasonably-reject.md)

### The redundancy objection

A principle is reasonably rejectable *because* it permits wrong acts, so wrongness does the work and the contract is idle (Pettit, Blackburn and others). Scanlon: the grounds for rejection are ordinary reasons (pain, unfairness, loss of control), and wrongness consists in failing justifiability built from them. "Reasonable" is already a moral notion, which is what the objection seizes on.

*Introduced:* [5.2](lessons/05-02-contractualism-what-no-one-could-reasonably-reject.md)

### Prima facie duties

Ross's seven: fidelity (including not lying), reparation, gratitude, justice, beneficence, self-improvement, non-injury. Each is a real, always-present consideration, self-evident to a mature mind. Your **actual duty** (all things considered) is not self-evident; it takes fallible judgement. No fixed ranking, though non-injury is generally more stringent than beneficence.

*Introduced:* [5.3](lessons/05-03-pluralism-and-particularism.md)

### Moral residue

What a conflict leaves behind. For the monist, at the level of theory, nothing. For the Rossian, the overridden duty still grounds regret, apology or compensation. For the particularist, residue only where a reason was outweighed, not where it was disabled. The operational form of the Ross-Dancy crux.

*Introduced:* [5.3](lessons/05-03-pluralism-and-particularism.md)

### Holism of reasons

Dancy: a feature that is a reason in one case may be no reason, or a reason on the other side, in another. For Ross context changes *how much* a consideration weighs; for Dancy it can change *whether* it counts. Vocabulary: favourers, enablers, disablers, intensifiers, attenuators; **default reasons** count one way unless disabled.

*Introduced:* [5.3](lessons/05-03-pluralism-and-particularism.md)

### Monism, pluralism and particularism

| | Monism | Rossian pluralism | Particularism |
|---|---|---|---|
| Basic principles | One (utility, the categorical imperative, reasonable rejection) | About seven, irreducible | None needed; perhaps none true |
| Does a relevant feature always count the same way? | Yes, via the master principle | Yes; only its weight varies | No: valence can shift, vanish or reverse |
| Role of judgement | Applying the principle, estimating facts | Weighing duties to reach actual duty | Discerning what matters here at all |

*From* [5.3](lessons/05-03-pluralism-and-particularism.md)

### The guidance objection

If no principle holds, how is morality learned, taught or predicted? McKeever and Ridge: holism is compatible with principles whose antecedents list enablers and disablers, so it does not entail particularism. Dancy: competence is like perceptual skill, structured by default reasons and remembered cases. Also pressed against virtue ethics.

*Introduced:* [5.3](lessons/05-03-pluralism-and-particularism.md), [4.3](lessons/04-03-modern-virtue-ethics.md)

### Moral realism

Three claims: **cognitivism** (moral judgments are beliefs), **success** (some are true), **stance-independence** (their truth does not depend on anyone's attitudes). Deny the first and you are an expressivist, the second an error theorist, the third a constructivist. Realists then split on whether wrongness is a natural property.

*Introduced:* [6.1](lessons/06-01-moral-realism.md)

### Naturalist and non-naturalist realism

**Naturalists** hold moral properties are natural, the kind the sciences could study. **Non-naturalists** (Moore, Parfit, Enoch) hold they are real but of their own kind; Parfit compares moral knowledge to knowledge of mathematics and logic.

*Introduced:* [6.1](lessons/06-01-moral-realism.md)

### Cornell realism

Boyd, Sturgeon, Brink: moral properties are natural properties, likely clusters of the features that make lives go well or badly, identified by *synthetic a posteriori* identities discovered through reflective equilibrium, as water = H2O was. Immune to the open question argument, which reaches only analytic naturalism. Pressed by **moral twin earth** (Horgan and Timmons): speakers whose "wrong" tracks a different property seem to disagree with us, not change the subject.

*Introduced:* [6.1](lessons/06-01-moral-realism.md)

### The paradox of analysis

A trivial analysis is closed; an informative one feels open. So openness may show only that an analysis is not obvious, and step 2 of the open question argument proves too much.

*Introduced:* [6.1](lessons/06-01-moral-realism.md)

### Deliberative indispensability

Enoch: explanatory indispensability is one way to earn belief; being indispensable to deliberation is another, since a deliberator cannot proceed without treating some answers as correct. Critic: indispensability to a project shows the project needs the belief, not that it is true.

*Introduced:* [6.1](lessons/06-01-moral-realism.md)

### Speaker subjectivism

"Lying is wrong" *reports* that the speaker disapproves. Then two speakers who disagree both speak truly and do not disagree at all. Expressivism is not this view: it says the sentence *expresses* the attitude, as "ouch" expresses pain.

*Introduced:* [6.2](lessons/06-02-expressivism.md)

### Emotivism, norm-expressivism and quasi-realism

**Emotivism** (Ayer; Stevenson): moral sentences add no factual content and express feeling ("a peculiar tone of horror"); Stevenson adds that they aim to influence. **Norm-expressivism** (Gibbard): to judge an act wrong is to accept norms on which guilt for it and resentment of it make sense. **Quasi-realism** (Blackburn): with a minimal notion of truth, the expressivist earns talk of moral truth, error and facts from within a moral outlook.

*Introduced:* [6.2](lessons/06-02-expressivism.md)

### Disagreement in attitude

Stevenson: two attitudes that cannot both be acted on make a real disagreement even with agreement on every fact, and new facts end it only by changing an attitude. Expressivism's advantage over speaker subjectivism.

*Introduced:* [6.2](lessons/06-02-expressivism.md), [6.6](lessons/06-06-moral-knowledge-and-disagreement.md)

### Internalism and externalism about moral motivation

**Internalism:** sincerely judging you ought to do something is necessarily connected to being motivated to do it. **Externalism:** a person could judge something wrong and not care. The motivation argument for expressivism needs internalism plus the Humean claim that beliefs motivate only with a separate desire.

*Introduced:* [6.2](lessons/06-02-expressivism.md)

### Replies to Frege-Geach

**Blackburn (1984):** the conditional expresses a higher-order attitude against holding the antecedent attitude without the consequent one; rejecting the conclusion is a "fractured sensibility". Hale and others: an attitude clash is not a logical inconsistency. **Gibbard (1990):** content is the set of fact-and-norm combinations a judgment rules out. **Schroeder (2008):** the negation problem, since disapproval has one object and so no second place for negation to go.

*Introduced:* [6.2](lessons/06-02-expressivism.md)

### Projection

Hume's taste "gilding or staining all natural objects with the colours, borrowed from internal sentiment". Mackie adds that we forget we did the staining, which explains why a whole species makes the same error.

*Introduced:* [6.3](lessons/06-03-error-theory.md)

### Error theory

Moral talk is meaningful, sincere and systematically false: ordinary moral judgments purport to report objective, categorically prescriptive facts, and there are none (Mackie). Not first-order permissivism: "permissible" and "anything goes" are false too. Unlike expressivism, it says moral judgments aimed at facts and missed.

*Introduced:* [6.3](lessons/06-03-error-theory.md)

### Abolitionism, fictionalism, conservationism

What to do with moral talk if error theory is true. **Abolitionism** (Hinckfuss, Garner): stop. **Fictionalism** (Joyce): accept rather than believe it, as with a story, because it strengthens resolve. **Conservationism** (Olson): keep believing in everyday life, set the theory aside outside the seminar.

*Introduced:* [6.3](lessons/06-03-error-theory.md)

### Companions in guilt

Cuneo: epistemic reasons are also categorically authoritative (believing against overwhelming evidence is irrational whatever you want), so if queerness rules out moral facts it rules out epistemic ones, and then no one has reason to believe the error theory. Error theorist routes: reduce the companion, distinguish the authority, or bite the bullet. The parity premise is the crux.

*Introduced:* [6.3](lessons/06-03-error-theory.md)

### Constructivism

A normative claim is true when it would be endorsed from the practical standpoint worked out properly. Korsgaard's **procedural realism** (right answers because there is a correct procedure) against **substantive realism** (the procedure is correct because it tracks answers already there). Not relativism: even Humean constructivism allows error about what your values commit you to. Scanlon's contractualism is often classed as a restricted constructivism.

*Introduced:* [6.4](lessons/06-04-constructivism-and-debunking.md)

### Kantian and Humean constructivism

**Kantian** (Korsgaard): the practical standpoint contains a lot; valuing anything commits you to valuing humanity, so morality binds every reflective agent. **Humean** (Street): the standpoint of valuing brings only formal standards (consistency, means-end coherence), so an ideally coherent Caligula is possible. Street accepts that and condemns Caligula from *our* standpoint. The cost is contingency: different evolved starting values, different reasons.

*Introduced:* [6.4](lessons/06-04-constructivism-and-debunking.md)

### Third-factor replies

Enoch (2010): survival and flourishing are by and large good, selection pushed us toward judgments favouring survival, so our judgments correlate with value truth through a common cause, with no tracking. Wielenberg gives a related account. Strains: scope (says nothing for judgments selection did not favour, like concern for distant strangers) and circularity (assumes an evolved evaluative judgment). At most it shows realism is consistent with reliability.

*Introduced:* [6.4](lessons/06-04-constructivism-and-debunking.md)

### Divine command theory

Unmodified: an act is obligatory because, and only because, God commands it. Faces the Euthyphro dilemma. Can be built stance-dependent (truth fixed by commands) or realist about the good (Adams). Not an argument for or against theism.

*Introduced:* [6.5](lessons/06-05-god-and-morality-the-euthyphro-dilemma.md), placed on the map in [6.1](lessons/06-01-moral-realism.md)

### Modified divine command theory

Adams: the Good is God, by a synthetic identity like water = H2O, so the open question argument misses; finite things are excellent as they resemble God. Only obligation comes from commands, as real demands within a relationship. God is essentially loving and just, so would not command gratuitous cruelty. Abraham: a seeming command to kill should be judged not to come from God, which critics say concedes independence for knowledge.

*Introduced:* [6.5](lessons/06-05-god-and-morality-the-euthyphro-dilemma.md)

### Paradigm and principle

Alston: God is good as a paradigm, not by satisfying a principle, as the old standard metre bar was the standard of length rather than measured against one. Asking "in virtue of what is God good?" assumes a prior principle. The rejoinder one level up (Wielenberg): is God's nature the standard because it is loving and just?

*Introduced:* [6.5](lessons/06-05-god-and-morality-the-euthyphro-dilemma.md)

### Voluntarism and intellectualism

**Voluntarism** (usually associated with Ockham, on a disputed reading): morality rests on God's will, so God could have commanded even hatred of God. **Intellectualism** (Aquinas): law is "something pertaining to reason"; eternal law is God's reason, so the standard is in God yet not arbitrary. Aquinas on Abraham: God as lord of life and death authorizes a death that is then not murder.

*Introduced:* [6.5](lessons/06-05-god-and-morality-the-euthyphro-dilemma.md)

### Intuitionism and self-evidence

Some moral truths are known without inference (Ross, Sidgwick, Moore). Audi: self-evident is not obvious, not indefeasible, and not unprovable; it means knowable through adequate understanding alone. A related line treats some moral knowledge as **perception** shaped by background understanding, not a sixth sense.

*Introduced:* [6.6](lessons/06-06-moral-knowledge-and-disagreement.md), building on [5.3](lessons/05-03-pluralism-and-particularism.md)

### Narrow and wide reflective equilibrium

**Narrow:** fit your principles to your own judgments. **Wide** (Rawls, Daniels): also test them against rival theories and background knowledge. As moral epistemology: coherence alone can be coherently false, so the method seems to need intuitionism's claim that considered judgments carry some initial credibility.

*Introduced:* [6.6](lessons/06-06-moral-knowledge-and-disagreement.md)

### Three relativisms

**Descriptive:** moral codes vary (empirical, in some form true). **Metaethical:** moral truth is relative to a framework (Harman 1975; Wong's pluralistic relativism). **Normative:** we ought to tolerate other codes (a first-order moral claim). None entails the next; metaethical relativism gives no framework-neutral ground for demanding tolerance.

*Introduced:* [6.6](lessons/06-06-moral-knowledge-and-disagreement.md)

### Conciliationism

When you learn that an epistemic peer disagrees, move substantially toward their view or suspend judgment (Elga's equal weight view; Christensen). Its conclusion is sceptical, not anti-realist. Pressures: spinelessness (but credence and action come apart), the first-person standpoint (Enoch, "Not Just a Truthometer"), and self-undermining. **Ideal-conditions convergence:** disagreement that would vanish under full information and careful reasoning is no evidence against a truth.

*Introduced:* [6.6](lessons/06-06-moral-knowledge-and-disagreement.md)

## Arguments

Skeletons as the lessons state them. "In words" glosses are in the lessons.

### The proof of the principle of utility

*Stage 1*
1. The only evidence that something is visible is that people see it.
2. By parity, the only evidence that something is desirable is that people desire it.
3. Each person desires his own happiness.
4. So each person's happiness is a good to that person. (2, 3)
5. ∴ The general happiness is a good to the aggregate of all persons. (4)

*Stage 2*
6. People desire other things for their own sake (virtue, money, fame).
7. But these are desired as *parts* of happiness.
8. ∴ Nothing is desired except happiness or its parts, so by 2 nothing is desirable except happiness.

**Attacked at:** step 2 (Moore: "desirable" means *ought to be* desired; charitable reading: evidence, not meaning) and step 5 (composition, pressed by Sidgwick; charitable reading via Mill's 1868 letter: additivity of goods, which assumes goods of different people can be added). Step 7 can make "happiness" absorb anything desired for itself. Mill claims evidence, not deduction.

### The experience machine

1. If hedonism is true, only the felt quality of your experiences affects how well your life goes for you.
2. A machine could give you, for life, experiences as good as or better than the best real life, and you would never know.
3. So if hedonism is true, plugging in makes your life go at least as well for you as the real life. (1, 2)
4. On reflection, the plugged-in life goes worse *for you*.
5. ∴ Hedonism is false.

Premise 4 carries the weight. Nozick: we want to *do* things, to *be* a certain kind of person, and contact with reality. Hedonist reply: the verdict is confounded by status quo bias; the reversed machine (De Brigard) is the test, with small samples and disputed readings.

*From* [1.2](lessons/01-02-what-is-good-for-a-person.md)

### The separateness of persons argument

1. A cost to one person can be justified by a benefit to that same person, because she is compensated.
2. Between persons there is no such compensation; nobody lives the sum.
3. A principle that permits imposing a cost whenever others' benefits outweigh it treats interpersonal trade-offs as intrapersonal.
4. Treating interpersonal trade-offs as intrapersonal ignores a morally significant fact: persons are separate.
5. Act utilitarianism ranks outcomes by summed well-being and requires the best.
6. ∴ Act utilitarianism ignores the separateness of persons.

Principle claimed violated: a serious burden must be justifiable *to* the person, not merely outweighed. Pressure falls on premise 4; Parfit's reductionism about personal identity is one way to deny it. Utilitarian replies: long-run effects (changes the case), bite the bullet (Smart).

*From* [1.3](lessons/01-03-justice-and-the-separateness-of-persons.md)

### The drowning child argument

1. Suffering and death from lack of food, shelter and medical care are bad.
2. *Strong:* if we can prevent something bad without sacrificing anything of comparable moral importance, we ought to. *Moderate:* "anything morally significant" in place of "comparable".
3. Distance, and the number of others who could help, make no moral difference.
4. The affluent can prevent such deaths by giving, at the cost of things like clothes, meals out and tickets.
5. ∴ They ought to give; on the strong version, until further giving would cost about as much as it prevents.

**The demandingness objection** runs modus tollens on the same conditional: D1. Act consequentialism requires at least the strong conclusion. D2. Morality does not require that; most of such giving is supererogatory. ∴ Act consequentialism is false.

*From* [1.4](lessons/01-04-integrity-and-demandingness.md)

### The integrity argument

1. Act utilitarianism ranks acts only by the states of affairs they produce, counted impartially.
2. So I am responsible for what I allow or fail to prevent just as for what I do.
3. So my projects, including my moral convictions, enter only as one person's preferences, overridden whenever others' add up to more.
4. A person's deepest projects are what his life is organized around; requiring him to drop them whenever the sum tips separates him from his actions and convictions.
5. ∴ Utilitarianism cannot represent integrity, and misdescribes cases like Jim's and George's even where its verdict is right.

Williams grants Jim probably should shoot. The target is the theory finding it *obvious*.

*From* [1.4](lessons/01-04-integrity-and-demandingness.md)

### The collapse objection

1. Rule consequentialism selects the rules whose general compliance has the best consequences.
2. For any rule $R$ and situation where breaking $R$ does more good, "$R$, unless breaking it here does more good" performs at least as well under general compliance.
3. So the best code contains every such exception.
4. ∴ The best code requires exactly what act utilitarianism requires (extensional equivalence).

Lyons's argument targets *compliance* formulations. Hooker attacks premise 2 for *internalization* formulations: an exception-laden rule, internalized, licenses everyone to calculate, loses assurance and costs more to teach. Refusing exceptions meets the other horn, rule worship.

*From* [1.5](lessons/01-05-modern-consequentialism.md)

### The false promise as a practical contradiction

1. **Maxim:** when I need money, I will promise to repay, intending not to, in order to get the money.
2. **Universalize:** every promise from someone in need is known to be empty.
3. In that world lenders do not lend on such promises; people "would ridicule all such statements as vain pretences".
4. So acting on the maxim there, the agent promises and gets no money: the act fails to get its end in the world his willing would create.
5. ∴ He cannot consistently will both the maxim and its universality: a contradiction in conception, violating a perfect duty.

On the logical reading, the practice of promising would not exist; on the teleological, universal false promising defeats promising's purpose. The possible-consent reading of the Formula of Humanity condemns the same act: the lender cannot consent to a maxim she cannot see.

*From* [2.2](lessons/02-02-the-formula-of-universal-law.md), [2.3](lessons/02-03-humanity-autonomy-and-the-lie.md)

### Refused beneficence as a contradiction in will

1. **Maxim:** being well off, I will give nothing to anyone in need, though I take nothing from them either.
2. A world where no one helps anyone can be coherently conceived, so there is no contradiction in conception.
3. A rational agent wills the means to its ends.
4. Situations may arise in which help from others is the only means to one's ends.
5. Willing the universal law would deprive oneself of all hope of that help.
6. ∴ One cannot will the maxim as universal law: a contradiction in will, violating an imperfect duty (adopt the end of others' happiness, with latitude over when and how).

*From* [2.2](lessons/02-02-the-formula-of-universal-law.md)

### Deception and coercion as mere means

1. Humanity is the capacity to set and pursue ends by reason.
2. To treat a person as an end is, at minimum, not to make her agency the unwitting or unwilling instrument of your ends.
3. You use a person merely as a means when you act on a maxim she could not possibly consent to.
4. Deception makes consent impossible: she cannot consent to a maxim she does not know.
5. Coercion makes consent impossible: her refusal is overridden in advance.
6. ∴ Deceiving and coercing use persons merely as means, and the Formula of Humanity forbids them.

Premise 3 is where objections land: benign deceptions, punishment, and Parfit's gangster.

*From* [2.3](lessons/02-03-humanity-autonomy-and-the-lie.md)

### The paradox of deontology

1. A constraint forbids killing one innocent even when that is the only way to prevent two killings of innocents by others.
2. What grounds the constraint is the badness of killings of innocents, which everyone has the same reason to care about.
3. The rational response to something bad is to minimize how much of it there is.
4. Killing one to prevent two minimizes killings of innocents.
5. ∴ The constraint forbids the rational response to what grounds it, so it is irrational.

Valid, so a premise must go; 1 is the deontologist's view and 4 is arithmetic. **Nagel denies 2:** the ground is the agent's relation to harm, since intending a death is having your action guided by evil ("you are aiming at me"). **Kamm denies 3:** inviolability is a status to be honoured, not promoted.

*From* [2.4](lessons/02-04-constraints-and-options.md)

### The equal-difference argument

1. The Smith and Jones cases differ only in that Smith kills and Jones lets die.
2. If a factor has moral weight in itself, two cases differing only in that factor differ in how wrong they are.
3. Smith and Jones are equally wrong.
4. ∴ Killing versus letting die has no moral weight in itself.

Replies: against 2, additivity (Kagan; Kamm's contextual interaction); against 3, the ceiling (both are maximally bad for the scale); against 1, Jones is not a clean allower on Quinn's refinement.

*From* [3.1](lessons/03-01-doing-and-allowing.md)

### The four conditions of double effect

An act with a good and a bad effect is permissible only if all four hold (the manualist form; Mangan 1949 is the usual historical reference):

1. **The act is not wrong in itself**, described apart from its effects.
2. **Only the good effect is intended**; the bad is foreseen, perhaps certain, but not aimed at as end or means.
3. **The bad effect is not a means to the good effect.**
4. **There is proportionate reason**: the good is serious enough to justify the harm, and no less harmful route exists.

Conditions 2 and 3 are the line (3 is a special case of 2, listed separately because most cases are decided there); 4 is the scale. Failing the doctrine means *this* principle cannot license the act, not that the act is wrong. How much of this *ST* II-II q.64 a.7 contains is disputed.

*From* [3.2](lessons/03-02-the-doctrine-of-double-effect.md)

### The Loop argument

1. If double effect's intention condition explains the Switch/Footbridge contrast, turning is impermissible wherever the one's being hit is intended as a means.
2. In Loop the side track rejoins the main line, so turning saves the five only because the trolley hits the one.
3. Loop differs from Switch only by an extra piece of track, which cannot make a major moral difference.
4. So turning in Loop is permissible even though the hit is a means.
5. ∴ The intention condition does not explain the contrast.

Defenders deny 3 (many lay readers judge Loop impermissible) or deny the inference from "is a means" to "is intended" (Kamm's triple effect).

*From* [3.3](lessons/03-03-testing-the-principles-on-trolleys.md)

### The function argument

1. For anything with a function, its good lies in performing that function.
2. Human beings have a function.
3. The human function is what is peculiar to humans: not nutrition (shared with plants) or perception (shared with animals), but the active life of the part of the soul that has reason.
4. Doing a function well is doing it in accordance with the thing's excellence (*aretē*).
5. So the human good is activity of soul in accordance with excellence, the best and most complete if there are several.
6. ∴ And this over a complete life: "one swallow does not make a summer".

Objections: good-qua-kind is not good-for (against 1); post-Darwinian teleology (against 2; Foot's reply); torture is also peculiar to humans (against 3; read "peculiar" as *characteristic*).

*From* [4.1](lessons/04-01-aristotle-the-human-good.md)

### The situationist argument

1. Virtues, as virtue ethics conceives them, are global traits, robust across situations including ones that pressure them.
2. If many people had global traits, their behaviour would show cross-situational consistency.
3. Controlled studies find that morally trivial features of the situation (being in a hurry, finding a coin) predict helping far better than character.
4. So few people, perhaps none, have the virtues.
5. ∴ An ethics directing us to acquire and act from global traits rests on false moral psychology.

Replies attack different boxes and combine: rarity (accept 4, deny 5), skill model (against 1), mixed traits (against the inference to 4), replication worries (weaken 3).

*From* [4.3](lessons/04-03-modern-virtue-ethics.md)

### From the first precept to the secondary precepts

1. Practical reason has a first principle: "good" is the first thing practical reason grasps, since every agent acts for an end under the aspect of good.
2. So the first precept is: good is to be done and pursued, evil avoided. It is self-evident.
3. We hold it through *synderesis*.
4. Reason apprehends the objects of our natural inclinations as goods.
5. There are three levels of inclination: with all substances, with other animals, and proper to rational nature.
6. So the order of precepts follows the order of inclinations.
7. ∴ Secondary precepts are conclusions from the primary ones applied to particular matter, holding in the majority of cases and failing in some (q.94 a.4).

The pressure point is step 4, the move from inclination to good; critics who call the first precept empty are really pressing step 4, not step 2.

### The new natural law reply to Hume

1. No valid inference runs from purely theoretical premises to a practical conclusion.
2. Aquinas's first practical principles are not inferred at all; they are self-evident, as first principles of demonstration are (q.94 a.2).
3. The basic goods are grasped by practical understanding; inclinations supply data, not premises.
4. Finnis's 1980 list has seven basic goods.
5. The basic goods are incommensurable.
6. Morality comes from the requirements of practical reasonableness (Grisez: modes of responsibility).
7. ∴ A natural law ethic can be built without deriving *ought* from *is*.

**The neo-Thomist counter:** N1. For Aquinas "good" means what perfects a thing according to its nature. N2. So what is good for humans depends on what humans are. N3. A theory that cannot say why these goods and not others has no ground for its list. ∴ NNL misreads Aquinas and cuts ethics off from metaphysics; Hume's gap assumes a fact-value split teleology never accepted. **NNL reply:** nature is ontologically, not epistemically, prior.

### The contractarian route to morality

1. Agents are rational maximizers who take no interest in each other's interests (non-tuism).
2. Without constraint, interaction has the structure of a prisoner's dilemma.
3. Rules of mutual restraint would leave each better off than no rules.
4. So rational agents would agree to such rules.
5. Compliance problem: given others' compliance, each does better by defecting.
6. Reply: choose a disposition, not an act. With translucent dispositions, constrained maximizers are admitted to cooperative schemes that straightforward maximizers are shut out of, and do better overall.
7. ∴ Moral requirements are the rules of mutual restraint rational agents would agree to, and a rational agent is disposed to comply with them.

Premises 1–4 give content; 5–6 give authority.

*From* [5.1](lessons/05-01-contractarianism-morality-as-mutual-advantage.md)

### The Foole and the reply

The outline as 5.1 gives it; the full standard-form reconstruction is Module 5's boss problem.

**The Foole**
1. Every man's conservation and contentment is committed to his own care.
2. So there can be no reason why every man might not do what he thinks conduces to it.
3. ∴ Making or breaking covenants is not against reason when it conduces to one's benefit.

**Hobbes**
1. No one can secure himself without being received into some society formed for defence.
2. One who breaks covenants, and declares it reasonable to do so, cannot be received into such a society except by the mistake of those who receive him.
3. No one can reasonably rely on other people's mistakes for his security.
4. ∴ Breaking covenant is against reason, even when it happens to pay.

The reply never appeals to justice as a value; it appeals to what happens to you afterwards.

### The transmitter room

1. Jones is receiving extremely painful shocks; freeing him means switching off the World Cup broadcast for fifteen minutes; waiting an hour leaves him no worse injured but in agony.
2. The strongest complaint against permitting waiting comes from Jones's standpoint: an hour of electrocution.
3. The strongest complaint against requiring rescue comes from one viewer's standpoint: fifteen minutes missed.
4. Complaints are not summed (individualist restriction), and every viewer's complaint is far weaker than Jones's.
5. ∴ Jones can reasonably reject the waiting principle and no viewer can reasonably reject the rescue principle: switch it off.

A summing utilitarian must say that at some audience size waiting becomes right.

*From* [5.2](lessons/05-02-contractualism-what-no-one-could-reasonably-reject.md)

### The tie-breaking argument

1. A alone faces death; B and C together face death; you can save only one side.
2. A's claim and B's claim balance, since each faces the same harm.
3. If you then treat the choice as a coin toss, you act exactly as you would if C were not there.
4. C can reasonably reject a principle that makes his presence count for nothing.
5. ∴ You must save B and C; repeated, the numbers count without adding complaints into a bigger one.

Kamm gives a similar balancing argument. Otsuka: letting C's claim tip the balance *is* counting two against one.

*From* [5.2](lessons/05-02-contractualism-what-no-one-could-reasonably-reject.md)

### The holism argument for particularism

1. Holism of reasons: a feature that is a reason in one case may be no reason, or a reason on the opposite side, in another.
2. Holism is true of theoretical reasons (the drug that makes blue things look red), and nothing marks practical reasons off as different.
3. A moral principle worth the name says some feature *always* makes the same contribution.
4. ∴ Morality does not depend on a supply of true principles, and moral competence cannot consist in knowing them.

McKeever and Ridge: holism is compatible with principles whose antecedents list enablers and disablers, so 1 does not deliver 4.

*From* [5.3](lessons/05-03-pluralism-and-particularism.md)

### The open question argument

1. Suppose "good" means some natural property N.
2. Then "Is what is N good?" would be a closed question for anyone who understands the words, like "Is a vixen a female fox?".
3. But for every N the question is open.
4. So "good" does not mean N, for any natural N.
5. ∴ Goodness is a simple, indefinable, non-natural property; identifying it with a natural property is the naturalistic fallacy.

Replies: the paradox of analysis (against 2); synthetic identity, since water = H2O was an open question (against the step from 4 to 5). By itself the argument refutes only analytic naturalism.

*From* [6.1](lessons/06-01-moral-realism.md)

### The explanation challenge

1. We are entitled to believe in a fact only if it figures in the best explanation of our observations.
2. Seeing hoodlums set fire to a cat, you judge at once that what they do is wrong.
3. The best explanation of your judgment cites your upbringing and psychology, not a fact of wrongness.
4. ∴ We have no observational warrant for believing in moral facts.

The conclusion is epistemic. Cornell reply (Sturgeon): moral facts do figure in good explanations (Hitler's depravity), and if wrongness is natural it explains. Non-naturalist reply (Enoch): deny 1; deliberative indispensability also warrants belief.

*From* [6.1](lessons/06-01-moral-realism.md)

### The motivation argument for expressivism

1. Sincerely judging that you ought to do something is necessarily connected to being motivated to do it.
2. A belief about how things are motivates only with the help of a separate desire.
3. So a moral judgment is not simply a belief; it is, or includes, a desire-like attitude.
4. The meaning of a sentence is fixed by the mental state it conventionally expresses.
5. ∴ "Lying is wrong" means what it does by expressing an attitude against lying.

Realists deny 1 (externalism) or 2 (some beliefs motivate on their own).

*From* [6.2](lessons/06-02-expressivism.md)

### The Frege-Geach problem

Test argument: (P1) Lying is wrong. (P2) If lying is wrong, then getting your little brother to lie is wrong. (C) So getting your little brother to lie is wrong. Plainly valid modus ponens.

1. On expressivism, "lying is wrong" gets its meaning from the attitude it expresses.
2. In P2 "lying is wrong" is not asserted, so expresses no attitude.
3. So in P2 the clause means something different from what it means in P1.
4. An argument whose key phrase changes meaning between premises equivocates, and is invalid.
5. But the argument is valid.
6. ∴ Expressivism's account of meaning is at best incomplete; it owes a meaning constant across asserted and unasserted contexts that explains the validity.

It applies to every unasserted embedding: negation, disjunction, questions, attitude reports. A demand for compositional semantics, not about "wrong" in particular.

*From* [6.2](lessons/06-02-expressivism.md)

### The argument from queerness

Mackie's error theory, with its supports:

1. **Conceptual claim:** ordinary moral judgments purport to report objective facts that are categorically prescriptive.
2. **Relativity:** moral variation is better explained by participation in different ways of life than by some perceiving objective values others miss.
3. **Queerness, metaphysical:** such facts would be utterly unlike anything else, with a baffling link to the natural facts they depend on.
4. **Queerness, epistemological:** knowing them would need a special faculty unlike every ordinary way of knowing.
5. **Substantive claim:** so there are no objective, categorically prescriptive facts. (2, 3, 4)
6. ∴ All positive moral judgments are false. (1, 5)

Premise 1 turns the non-existence of an odd entity into the falsity of our moral claims. Premise 2 is an inference to the best explanation, not "people disagree, so there is no truth".

*From* [6.3](lessons/06-03-error-theory.md)

### The argument from practical identity

1. Reflective creatures can step back from their impulses, so an impulse is not yet a reason.
2. A reason is an impulse that survives reflective endorsement from a practical identity.
3. Obligations come from identities; acting against one is losing integrity under that description.
4. Most identities are contingent, but needing one is not: it comes from your humanity, so valuing anything commits you to valuing your humanity.
5. Reasons are public, so valuing your own humanity commits you to valuing it in others.
6. ∴ Moral obligation binds every reflective agent, constructed from the standpoint of agency.

Step 5 is the one critics press hardest. Street's Humean constructivism denies that the standpoint of valuing has content beyond coherence.

*From* [6.4](lessons/06-04-constructivism-and-debunking.md)

### The Darwinian dilemma

1. Natural selection has heavily shaped the content of our evaluative attitudes.
2. Either there is a relation between those selective pressures and the independent evaluative truths, or there is not.
3. **Horn 1, no relation:** any match between our values and the truth would be luck, so scepticism follows.
4. **Horn 2, a relation:** the realist's natural candidate is the tracking account, that grasping evaluative truths was adaptive.
5. But the adaptive-link account (tendencies selected because they linked circumstances to responses that paid off) explains the same data better.
6. ∴ Either way realism leaves evaluative knowledge unexplained; constructivism escapes, since evolution shaping the standpoint cannot put it off-track.

Realism here means value truths independent of all our evaluative attitudes; bites hardest on non-naturalism. Replies: third factors (Enoch, Wielenberg). Turned on itself: step 5 uses an epistemic norm plausibly shaped by selection too.

*From* [6.4](lessons/06-04-constructivism-and-debunking.md)

### The Euthyphro dilemma

Plato's question (*Euthyphro* 10a) was about piety and many gods; this is the later moral reconstruction. Against unmodified DCT:

1. Either God commands right acts because they are right, or they are right because God commands them.
2. **Independence horn:** if God commands them because they are right, their rightness does not depend on God's commands, and DCT is false.
3. **Arbitrariness horn:** if they are right because God commands them, then had God commanded gratuitous cruelty, it would have been obligatory.
4. **Emptiness horn:** on the same horn, "God's commands are good" says only that God commands what God commands (Leibniz).
5. Arbitrariness and emptiness are unacceptable, for the theist most of all.
6. ∴ DCT is either false or unacceptable.

Replies: Adams (split goodness from obligation), Alston (paradigm, not principle), natural law (eternal law is God's reason, not bare will). Rejoinder: the dilemma one level up.

*From* [6.5](lessons/06-05-god-and-morality-the-euthyphro-dilemma.md)

### The popular argument from diversity

1. Moral codes vary across cultures.
2. If there were an objective moral truth, cultures would agree about it.
3. So there is no objective moral truth; moral claims are true only relative to a culture.
4. ∴ No culture may judge another.

Premise 2 is false for every other domain (disease, the shape of the earth). Step 4 does not follow from 3: if all moral truth is relative, "tolerate other cultures" is too. Darius and the Callatiae: one shared value applied through different non-moral beliefs.

*From* [6.6](lessons/06-06-moral-knowledge-and-disagreement.md)

### Disagreement as evidence

1. An epistemic peer is as informed and reliable on the question as you, judged independently of the dispute.
2. When you learn that a peer disagrees, you should move substantially toward their view, or suspend judgment.
3. Many moral disputes are between peers.
4. ∴ We should suspend judgment on many moral claims.

Sceptical, not anti-realist: suspending judgment presupposes an answer you do not know. Anti-realism needs Mackie's further explanatory step.

## Formulas

### Welfare functions

$$W = \sum_i u_i \qquad\qquad W_{\text{prior}} = \sum_i f(u_i),\ f \text{ increasing and concave}$$

$u_i$ is person $i$'s well-being. Worked in 1.3 with $f(u) = \sqrt{u}$: distribution A $(10,10,10)$ scores $3\sqrt{10} \approx 9.49$, distribution B $(28,1,1)$ scores $\sqrt{28} + 2 \approx 7.29$; both sum to 30.

*From* [1.3](lessons/01-03-justice-and-the-separateness-of-persons.md), [1.1](lessons/01-01-classical-utilitarianism.md)

### Expected value

$$EV(a) = \sum_i p_i v_i$$

$p_i$ is the probability of outcome $i$ on the agent's evidence, $v_i$ its value. Jackson's drug case, with cure 100, partial cure 90, death 0: drug A gives $1 \times 90 = 90$; B and C each give $0.5 \times 100 + 0.5 \times 0 = 50$. The expected-value view makes A right; the actual-consequences view makes A certainly wrong. The same arithmetic prices demandingness in 1.4: 250 dollars at about 5,000 dollars per expected life is $250/5000 = 0.05$ of a life.

*From* [1.5](lessons/01-05-modern-consequentialism.md), [1.4](lessons/01-04-integrity-and-demandingness.md)

### Constrained maximizer payoffs

Prisoner's dilemma with covenants, payoffs (you, other): $T = 5$, $R = 3$, $P = 1$, $S = 0$.

| | Other keeps | Other breaks |
|---|---|---|
| You keep | 3, 3 | 0, 5 |
| You break | 5, 0 | 1, 1 |

Breaking dominates. In the simplified model, a fraction $r$ of the population are constrained maximizers (CM), the rest straightforward maximizers (SM); $p$ is the probability two CMs recognize each other and both keep (otherwise both break); $q$ is the probability a CM mistakes an SM for a CM and keeps. SMs always break.

$$EU_{CM} = r\,[pR + (1-p)P] + (1-r)\,[qS + (1-q)P]$$
$$EU_{SM} = r\,[qT + (1-q)P] + (1-r)\,P$$

With these payoffs:

$$EU_{CM} - EU_{SM} = 2rp - (1-r)q - 4rq > 0 \iff \frac{p}{q} > \frac{1+3r}{2r}$$

At $r = 0.5$, $p = 0.8$, $q = 0.1$: $EU_{CM} = 1.75$, $EU_{SM} = 1.2$, threshold $2.5$. Even at $r = 1$ the threshold is 2, so if detection is no better than chance ($p/q \le 1$), the Foole wins.

*From* [5.1](lessons/05-01-contractarianism-morality-as-mutual-advantage.md)

### The prerogative multiplier

An agent-centred prerogative lets you weight your own interests $M$ times as heavily as anyone else's, for some $M > 1$ (Scheffler). The number needs a principled value, and a bare multiplier cannot distinguish not-helping from harming (Kagan).

*From* [2.4](lessons/02-04-constraints-and-options.md), [1.4](lessons/01-04-integrity-and-demandingness.md)

## Pitfalls

### Module 1: Consequentialism

- Utilitarianism does not tell each person to pursue happiness; it tells each agent to promote the *general* happiness, counting her own as one share. *([1.1](lessons/01-01-classical-utilitarianism.md))*
- Impartiality does not entail maximizing: an impartial view can refuse to trade one person's ruin for many small gains. *([1.1](lessons/01-01-classical-utilitarianism.md))*
- Mill does not claim to deduce the principle; reconstruct ch. 4 as evidence or you refute a stronger argument than he gave. *([1.1](lessons/01-01-classical-utilitarianism.md))*
- Moore's objection is not verbal: the gap between desired and ought-to-be-desired survives any wording. *([1.1](lessons/01-01-classical-utilitarianism.md))*
- "Good for a person" is not "good": a theory of well-being says nothing about whose well-being to promote. *([1.2](lessons/01-02-what-is-good-for-a-person.md))*
- Mill's higher pleasures keep hedonism; saying he has left it is an evaluative conclusion, not a reading. *([1.2](lessons/01-02-what-is-good-for-a-person.md))*
- Desire-satisfaction is a match between desire and world, not a feeling of satisfaction. *([1.2](lessons/01-02-what-is-good-for-a-person.md))*
- The objective list is not arbitrary; its defenders ground it in human nature or self-evidence. *([1.2](lessons/01-02-what-is-good-for-a-person.md), [4.5](lessons/04-05-the-new-natural-law-theory.md))*
- The separateness objection is not that individuals are ignored; everyone counts, then gets merged into a total. *([1.3](lessons/01-03-justice-and-the-separateness-of-persons.md))*
- Prioritarianism answers distribution-insensitivity, not separateness: it still aggregates. *([1.3](lessons/01-03-justice-and-the-separateness-of-persons.md))*
- "It would get found out" changes the stipulated case; say whether you concede or bite the bullet. *([1.3](lessons/01-03-justice-and-the-separateness-of-persons.md))*
- Williams does not say Jim must not shoot; the target is the theory finding the answer obvious. *([1.4](lessons/01-04-integrity-and-demandingness.md))*
- Integrity is not honesty; it is the fit between actions and the projects they come from. *([1.4](lessons/01-04-integrity-and-demandingness.md))*
- Demandingness is not only a consequentialist problem: any duty of easy rescue without a limit on iteration faces it. *([1.4](lessons/01-04-integrity-and-demandingness.md))*
- The moderate version of Singer's principle looks modest only because it is vague. *([1.4](lessons/01-04-integrity-and-demandingness.md))*
- Railton and Hare are not rule consequentialists; they keep an act criterion and change the procedure. *([1.5](lessons/01-05-modern-consequentialism.md))*
- The collapse objection targets compliance formulations; against internalization with teaching costs, its premise 2 is what is in dispute. *([1.5](lessons/01-05-modern-consequentialism.md))*
- Expected-value rightness is relative to evidence, not to whatever the agent happens to believe. *([1.5](lessons/01-05-modern-consequentialism.md))*
- Scalar consequentialism gives up a threshold of requirement, not the ranking. *([1.5](lessons/01-05-modern-consequentialism.md))*

### Module 2: Kantian deontology

- Acts from inclination are not wrong; they lack moral worth, a separate question from rightness. *([2.1](lessons/02-01-the-good-will-and-acting-from-duty.md))*
- An imperative is not categorical because it lacks "if"; the question is whether its force depends on an end the agent has. *([2.1](lessons/02-01-the-good-will-and-acting-from-duty.md))*
- Kant does not make consequences irrelevant to deliberation; the good will must genuinely try. *([2.1](lessons/02-01-the-good-will-and-acting-from-duty.md))*
- Acting from duty is not mechanical rule-following; the maxim is the agent's own principle. *([2.1](lessons/02-01-the-good-will-and-acting-from-duty.md))*
- The universalization test is not "would universal adoption be bad?"; that is rule consequentialism. *([2.2](lessons/02-02-the-formula-of-universal-law.md))*
- Failing the will test does not mean "never do that"; it means you may not give up the end. *([2.2](lessons/02-02-the-formula-of-universal-law.md))*
- Passing the test makes a maxim permissible, not required. *([2.2](lessons/02-02-the-formula-of-universal-law.md))*
- The maxim is not whatever description you like; most arguments about the test are arguments about the maxim. *([2.2](lessons/02-02-the-formula-of-universal-law.md))*
- The Formula of Humanity forbids using people *merely* as means, not using them. *([2.3](lessons/02-03-humanity-autonomy-and-the-lie.md))*
- Possible consent is not "would she agree if asked?"; it asks whether the maxim's structure leaves room for consent. *([2.3](lessons/02-03-humanity-autonomy-and-the-lie.md))*
- Kant's verdict on the murderer does not follow from Universal Law; Korsgaard argues the lie passes it. *([2.3](lessons/02-03-humanity-autonomy-and-the-lie.md))*
- Autonomy is not choosing your own values; it is being bound by the law reason gives every rational agent. *([2.3](lessons/02-03-humanity-autonomy-and-the-lie.md))*
- Agent-relative does not mean selfish. *([2.4](lessons/02-04-constraints-and-options.md))*
- The paradox of deontology reaches every view with near-exceptionless prohibitions, not only Kant. *([2.4](lessons/02-04-constraints-and-options.md))*
- Weighting *my* killings more gives up the impartial ranking that made "minimize" compelling. *([2.4](lessons/02-04-constraints-and-options.md))*
- Constraints and options do not come as a package. *([2.4](lessons/02-04-constraints-and-options.md))*

### Module 3: Doing, allowing and intending

- The doctrine of doing and allowing does not make allowing permissible; it sets a higher bar for doing. *([3.1](lessons/03-01-doing-and-allowing.md))*
- Rachels's pair shows at most that the distinction is idle in that pair; generalizing needs the additive assumption. *([3.1](lessons/03-01-doing-and-allowing.md))*
- "Doing" is not "moving your body": letting go of the rope is allowing, and Jones's stillness may be doing. *([3.1](lessons/03-01-doing-and-allowing.md))*
- Doing/allowing and double effect are different principles: *how* you contribute versus *what* you aim at. *([3.1](lessons/03-01-doing-and-allowing.md), [3.2](lessons/03-02-the-doctrine-of-double-effect.md))*
- "Foreseen, not intended" makes a harm eligible for justification, not justified; proportionality still applies. *([3.2](lessons/03-02-the-doctrine-of-double-effect.md))*
- Intention in double effect is fixed by the plan, not by the agent's preferred description. *([3.2](lessons/03-02-the-doctrine-of-double-effect.md))*
- Double effect is taught here as a secular principle; its theological use belongs to moral theology. *([3.2](lessons/03-02-the-doctrine-of-double-effect.md))*
- Loop refutes double effect only for readers who share Thomson's premise 3, which experimental work shows is contested. *([3.3](lessons/03-03-testing-the-principles-on-trolleys.md))*
- The means principle is not double effect renamed; stated in terms of the victim's role, it survives the Scanlon-Thomson objection. *([3.3](lessons/03-03-testing-the-principles-on-trolleys.md))*
- For a bystander, doing/allowing classifies every trolley variant alike. *([3.3](lessons/03-03-testing-the-principles-on-trolleys.md))*
- Thomson's 2008 view is a principled cost-bearing argument, not fringe revisionism. *([3.3](lessons/03-03-testing-the-principles-on-trolleys.md))*

### Module 4: Virtue and natural law

- *Eudaimonia* is not a feeling of happiness; it is activity assessed over a whole life. *([4.1](lessons/04-01-aristotle-the-human-good.md))*
- "Peculiar to humans" does not banish nutrition and perception; it names the capacity that organizes a human life. *([4.1](lessons/04-01-aristotle-the-human-good.md))*
- "Virtue" in the function argument's conclusion is excellence in general, not yet moral virtue. *([4.1](lessons/04-01-aristotle-the-human-good.md))*
- An Aristotelian function needs no designer. *([4.1](lessons/04-01-aristotle-the-human-good.md))*
- The mean is not moderation; it can be extreme. *([4.2](lessons/04-02-aristotle-virtue-and-practical-wisdom.md))*
- "Relative to us" makes virtue agent- and situation-indexed, not subjective. *([4.2](lessons/04-02-aristotle-virtue-and-practical-wisdom.md))*
- Aristotle ranks the continent person below the temperate one on purpose. *([4.2](lessons/04-02-aristotle-virtue-and-practical-wisdom.md))*
- Habituation is not mere conditioning; the end state includes choosing the act for its own sake. *([4.2](lessons/04-02-aristotle-virtue-and-practical-wisdom.md))*
- Virtue ethics issues rules (v-rules); it denies they ground rightness or codify into a procedure. *([4.3](lessons/04-03-modern-virtue-ethics.md))*
- The criterion is what a virtuous agent would do *in character*, not a poll of nice people. *([4.3](lessons/04-03-modern-virtue-ethics.md))*
- Situationism is a claim about consistency, not about how good people are on average. *([4.3](lessons/04-03-modern-virtue-ethics.md))*
- One failed replication weakens situationism's premise 3 without removing it; one striking study never established it. *([4.3](lessons/04-03-modern-virtue-ethics.md))*
- Natural law's epistemic claim does not require settling theism first. *([4.4](lessons/04-04-natural-law-aquinas.md))*
- "Natural" does not mean statistically normal or "animals do it"; inclinations matter because reason grasps their objects as good. *([4.4](lessons/04-04-natural-law-aquinas.md))*
- The first precept is formal on purpose; emptiness complaints really target the step from inclination to good. *([4.4](lessons/04-04-natural-law-aquinas.md))*
- A natural lawyer need not hold every rule exceptionless: q.94 a.4 says the reverse about secondary precepts. *([4.4](lessons/04-04-natural-law-aquinas.md))*
- NNL does not deny that human nature matters; it denies nature is *epistemically* prior. *([4.5](lessons/04-05-the-new-natural-law-theory.md))*
- Incommensurability does not forbid ranking options; it denies a pre-moral metric. *([4.5](lessons/04-05-the-new-natural-law-theory.md))*
- The neo-Thomists are not defending the inference Hume attacked; most deny the gap exists in Hume's form. *([4.5](lessons/04-05-the-new-natural-law-theory.md))*
- Self-evident does not mean obvious to everyone. *([4.4](lessons/04-04-natural-law-aquinas.md), [4.5](lessons/04-05-the-new-natural-law-theory.md), [6.6](lessons/06-06-moral-knowledge-and-disagreement.md))*

### Module 5: Contract and plurality

- The social contract is a test of justification, not a historical claim. *([5.1](lessons/05-01-contractarianism-morality-as-mutual-advantage.md))*
- Non-tuism is a methodological assumption, not a claim that people are selfish. *([5.1](lessons/05-01-contractarianism-morality-as-mutual-advantage.md))*
- A constrained maximizer's cooperation is conditional, nothing like an unconditional duty. *([5.1](lessons/05-01-contractarianism-morality-as-mutual-advantage.md))*
- Contractarianism and contractualism differ at the root, advantage versus justifiability, and come apart on the weak. *([5.1](lessons/05-01-contractarianism-morality-as-mutual-advantage.md), [5.2](lessons/05-02-contractualism-what-no-one-could-reasonably-reject.md))*
- The redundancy objection says reasonable rejection presupposes wrongness; Scanlon's reply is that its grounds are ordinary reasons. *([5.2](lessons/05-02-contractualism-what-no-one-could-reasonably-reject.md))*
- "Reasonable" is not "rational": a principle can serve my interests and still be one I could not reasonably insist on. *([5.2](lessons/05-02-contractualism-what-no-one-could-reasonably-reject.md))*
- Refusing to aggregate is not ignoring numbers; in Scanlon numbers break ties. *([5.2](lessons/05-02-contractualism-what-no-one-could-reasonably-reject.md))*
- A prima facie duty is not a duty that only seems to hold. *([5.3](lessons/05-03-pluralism-and-particularism.md))*
- Ross is not a particularist: he is a generalist about contributors and leaves only the verdict to judgement. *([5.3](lessons/05-03-pluralism-and-particularism.md))*
- Holism does not mean anything goes; particularists grant stable default reasons. *([5.3](lessons/05-03-pluralism-and-particularism.md))*
- Pluralism is not monism with a longer list; it denies a common currency. *([5.3](lessons/05-03-pluralism-and-particularism.md))*

### Module 6: Metaethics

- The naturalistic fallacy is not the is-ought gap: one is about definition, the other about inference. *([6.1](lessons/06-01-moral-realism.md))*
- The open question argument refutes only analytic naturalism, not Cornell realism. *([6.1](lessons/06-01-moral-realism.md))*
- Realism does not require a special faculty; Cornell realists use theory-building, and Parfit compares moral to mathematical knowledge. *([6.1](lessons/06-01-moral-realism.md))*
- Harman's conclusion is epistemic, not that nothing is wrong. *([6.1](lessons/06-01-moral-realism.md))*
- Expressivism is not speaker subjectivism: expressing an attitude is not describing one. *([6.2](lessons/06-02-expressivism.md))*
- Expressivism does not make morality "just opinion"; the quasi-realist can call cruelty truly wrong. *([6.2](lessons/06-02-expressivism.md))*
- Frege-Geach is about any unasserted sentence, a demand for compositional semantics, not about the word "wrong". *([6.2](lessons/06-02-expressivism.md))*
- Whether quasi-realism collapses into realism is a live worry, not settled either way. *([6.2](lessons/06-02-expressivism.md))*
- Error theory is not expressivism: it says moral judgments aimed at facts and missed. *([6.3](lessons/06-03-error-theory.md))*
- An error theorist need not behave badly, and does not say "anything goes"; that is a moral claim too. *([6.3](lessons/06-03-error-theory.md))*
- Mackie's relativity argument is an inference to the best explanation, not "people disagree, so no truth". *([6.3](lessons/06-03-error-theory.md), [6.6](lessons/06-06-moral-knowledge-and-disagreement.md))*
- "Queer" is specific: objective *and* intrinsically action-guiding. *([6.3](lessons/06-03-error-theory.md))*
- Constructivism is not relativism. *([6.4](lessons/06-04-constructivism-and-debunking.md))*
- The Darwinian dilemma needs only a broad evolutionary influence, not precise adaptationist stories. *([6.4](lessons/06-04-constructivism-and-debunking.md))*
- Constructivism escapes the reliability worry but pays in contingency. *([6.4](lessons/06-04-constructivism-and-debunking.md))*
- A third-factor reply shows realism is consistent with reliability, not that realism is true. *([6.4](lessons/06-04-constructivism-and-debunking.md))*
- The Euthyphro dilemma is not an argument against theism; a theist can take the independence horn. *([6.5](lessons/06-05-god-and-morality-the-euthyphro-dilemma.md))*
- Grounding goodness in God's nature relocates the question one level up; the paradigm view must be defended. *([6.5](lessons/06-05-god-and-morality-the-euthyphro-dilemma.md))*
- Adams offers a synthetic identity, not a definition, so an open question test misses it. *([6.5](lessons/06-05-god-and-morality-the-euthyphro-dilemma.md))*
- Natural law is not a divine command theory: precepts bind through reason's grasp of goods, not case-by-case commands. *([6.5](lessons/06-05-god-and-morality-the-euthyphro-dilemma.md))*
- Metaethical relativism does not imply tolerance. *([6.6](lessons/06-06-moral-knowledge-and-disagreement.md))*
- Conciliationism by itself supports suspending judgment, not anti-realism. *([6.6](lessons/06-06-moral-knowledge-and-disagreement.md))*
