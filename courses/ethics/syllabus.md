# Ethics — Syllabus

> Philosophy · Tier 1 · ~26 lessons · Prereqs: [`philosophical-method`](../philosophical-method/syllabus.md) · Roadmap id: `ethics`

## Goal

This course owns normative ethical theory and metaethics for the whole library. You will learn the major theories at the strength their best defenders would recognize: consequentialism (classical and modern), Kantian deontology with its constraints and options, virtue ethics, natural law (Aquinas and the new natural law theorists, treated as philosophy), and the contract tradition. Then you will ask what, if anything, makes moral claims true and how anyone could know them. You will finish able to run each theory on a hard case, say exactly where it strains, and name the premise a rival theory denies. You will also be able to test the doing/allowing distinction and the doctrine of double effect as secular principles, and place any metaethical view (realism, expressivism, error theory, constructivism, divine command theory) against its strongest objection. Religious and secular theories get equal footing, and the course takes no side. It deliberately skips theories of distributive justice and the state (`political-philosophy`), applied-ethics surveys (hard cases appear only to test theories), Catholic moral theology (`moral-theology`), and formal decision theory (`decision-theory`).

## How practice works in this course

Problems follow [HUMANITIES-BUILD-BRIEF.md](../../HUMANITIES-BUILD-BRIEF.md): every part declares its kind, and every solution is a rubric (must-hit moves, wrong turns) plus a model answer. Here that plays out in three ways:

- **Exegetical parts are strict.** What Mill's proof actually infers, which formula of the categorical imperative a maxim fails, what Aquinas says the first precept of natural law is, what the Frege–Geach problem demands of an expressivist. These have right answers.
- **Evaluative parts are verdict-neutral.** No rubric rewards concluding that consequentialism, Kant, Aristotle, Aquinas, Scanlon or any metaethical view is correct. Must-hit moves are ones either side has to make: state the view precisely, run it on the case, name the premise the objection attacks, and say whether the objection generalizes.
- **Counterexample and apply-to-a-hard-case are the workhorses.** Ethical theory is tested by cases. Each module also uses reconstruct, find the crux and steelman-and-reply, and every module uses at least three archetypes. Close reading uses public-domain texts only: Plato, Aristotle (Ross), Aquinas (English Dominican Province), Hobbes, Hume, Bentham, Kant (Abbott), Mill, Sidgwick, Moore. Modern authors (Foot, Thomson, Williams, Anscombe, Hursthouse, Finnis, Scanlon, Mackie, Korsgaard, Street, Adams) are paraphrased and cited, never quoted at length.
- **Formal parts are rare and light:** expected-value arithmetic in 1.5, and counting in the aggregation cases of 5.2. The real machinery lives in `decision-theory`.

## Dangerous Checklist

When you finish, you can:

- [ ] State act utilitarianism precisely (theory of the good, maximizing, impartiality) and reconstruct Mill's proof of the principle of utility, flagging the two inferences its critics attack
- [ ] Compare hedonist, desire-satisfaction and objective-list theories of well-being, and run the experience machine against the first
- [ ] Press the separateness-of-persons, integrity and demandingness objections against a consequentialist, and say which ones rule or indirect consequentialism answers and at what cost
- [ ] Run a maxim through the Formula of Universal Law and the Formula of Humanity, tell a contradiction in conception from a contradiction in will, and build a maxim that the universalization test misjudges
- [ ] Explain why agent-relative constraints look paradoxical to a consequentialist, and give the deontologist's best reply
- [ ] Apply the doctrine of doing and allowing and the doctrine of double effect to a new case, say where they diverge, and test double effect on the closeness problem and the Loop case
- [ ] Reconstruct Aristotle's function argument, and use the doctrine of the mean and practical wisdom to say what a virtuous agent would do, and where that account runs out of guidance
- [ ] State Aquinas's first principle of practical reason and his ordering of the natural inclinations, and name the crux between Thomistic natural law and the new natural law theory on deriving goods from human nature
- [ ] Tell Hobbesian contractarianism from Scanlonian contractualism, and test each on those who have no bargaining power and on whether the numbers count
- [ ] Explain Ross's prima facie duties and Dancy's holism of reasons, and say what is lost if moral principles cannot be codified
- [ ] Place a moral claim within realism, expressivism, error theory, constructivism and divine command theory, and state each view's hardest objection (explanation and debunking, Frege–Geach, queerness, the Darwinian dilemma, the Euthyphro dilemma)
- [ ] Assess whether moral disagreement is evidence against moral realism, separating descriptive from metaethical relativism

## Modules

### Module 1: Consequentialism

The view that the right act is the one with the best outcome: what counts as best, why that is so compelling, the objections that shaped every rival, and the modern versions built to survive them.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1.1 | Classical utilitarianism | State the theory's parts separately and reconstruct Mill's proof | theory of the good vs theory of the right, maximizing, impartiality, Bentham's calculus, Mill's proof (*Utilitarianism* ch. 4) |
| 1.2 | What is good for a person? | Compare the three theories of well-being and test each on a case | hedonism, Mill's higher and lower pleasures, the experience machine, desire-satisfaction (informed desires), objective list |
| 1.3 | Objections I: justice and the separateness of persons | Build the cases where maximizing sacrifices someone, and state the principle they violate | punishing the innocent, rights as side constraints (preview of 2.4), separateness of persons, distribution-insensitivity |
| 1.4 | Objections II: integrity and demandingness | Say whether consequentialism alienates agents from their own projects or asks too much | Williams's Jim and George cases, integrity, negative responsibility, demandingness, the drowning-child analogy, supererogation |
| 1.5 | Modern consequentialism | Decide whether rules, motives or expectations rescue the theory without collapsing back into act utilitarianism | rule consequentialism and the collapse objection, sophisticated/indirect consequentialism (Railton), two-level view (Hare), actual vs expected consequences, scalar consequentialism |

**Boss problem 1:** Take Mill's proof of the principle of utility from *Utilitarianism* ch. 4 (public domain). (a) Reconstruct it in standard form, including the step from each person's happiness being a good to that person to the general happiness being a good to the aggregate of all persons. (b) Name the two inferences critics since Sidgwick and Moore have attacked, and state each objection precisely. (c) Build a case in which act utilitarianism requires an act most people judge seriously wrong. Then decide whether rule consequentialism gives a better verdict *without* collapsing into act utilitarianism or into rule worship, in 300–500 words. Parts (a)–(b) are exegetical; (c) is evaluative.

### Module 2: Kantian deontology

Morality as the demands of reason on any rational agent: the good will, the categorical imperative in its main formulas, and the structure of constraints and options any deontology must defend.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2.1 | The good will and acting from duty | Tell acting from duty from acting in accordance with it, and a categorical from a hypothetical imperative | the good will, the prudent shopkeeper, moral worth, maxims, hypothetical vs categorical imperatives |
| 2.2 | The Formula of Universal Law | Run a maxim through the universalization test and find where the test misfires | contradiction in conception vs in will, the four examples (*Groundwork* II), false positives and false negatives, maxim description |
| 2.3 | Humanity, autonomy, and the lie | Apply "never merely as a means" to a case, and assess Kant on lying to a murderer | Formula of Humanity, mere means and possible consent, kingdom of ends, autonomy, Kant's reply to Constant |
| 2.4 | Constraints and options | Explain why deontological constraints look paradoxical and why options are needed | agent-relative vs agent-neutral reasons, side constraints (Nozick), the paradox of deontology, agent-centred prerogatives (Scheffler), threshold deontology |

**Boss problem 2:** Take the four examples in Section II of Kant's *Groundwork* (Abbott translation, public domain): suicide, the false promise, neglected talents and refused beneficence. (a) For the false promise and refused beneficence, state the maxim, run the Formula of Universal Law, and say which kind of contradiction each produces and why that sorts them into perfect and imperfect duties. (b) Build one maxim that passes the universalization test but is plainly wrong, or fails it but is plainly innocent, and show that it survives the obvious redescription reply. (c) Decide whether the Formula of Humanity handles your case better than the Formula of Universal Law, stating the strongest objection to your answer and replying to it, in 150 words or fewer. Part (a) is exegetical; (b)–(c) are evaluative.

### Module 3: Doing, allowing, and intending

Two secular principles, both older than the trolley problem, that try to explain why some ways of bringing about a harm are worse than others. The trolley–transplant contrast is taken as given from [`philosophical-method`](../philosophical-method/syllabus.md) 3.3. The work here is stating the principles precisely and breaking them.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3.1 | Doing and allowing | State the doctrine of doing and allowing and test it on matched cases | Rachels's bathtub cases, Foot's negative vs positive duties, Quinn's formulation, withdrawing aid and removing barriers, equal-difference arguments |
| 3.2 | The doctrine of double effect | State double effect's conditions and apply them to a case | Aquinas on self-defence (ST II-II q.64 a.7), intended vs foreseen effects, proportionality, strategic vs terror bomber, the closeness problem |
| 3.3 | Testing the principles on trolleys | Use Loop-style variants to decide whether intention, agency or something else does the work | Thomson's Loop, the means principle, Foot's and Thomson's rival explanations, the claim that intention bears on blame rather than permissibility (Scanlon, Thomson), where the principles diverge |

**Boss problem 3:** Take Aquinas, *Summa Theologiae* II-II q.64 a.7, "Whether it is lawful to kill a man in self-defense?" (English Dominican translation, public domain). (a) Close-read the *respondeo*: which words ground the distinction between an intended effect and one beside the intention, and which ground a proportionality requirement? (b) State the modern doctrine of double effect as a list of conditions, and mark which conditions the article states, which it implies, and which it does not contain. (c) Apply double effect and the doctrine of doing and allowing to a fresh case (a hospital or disaster-response case written for the problem) where the two principles give different verdicts. Then say which verdict survives a one-factor variant, in 300–500 words. Parts (a)–(b) are exegetical; (c) is evaluative.

### Module 4: Virtue and natural law

Two ethics of human nature. Aristotle's asks what kind of person to be. Aquinas's natural law asks what reason grasps as good to pursue. Both are followed into their modern revivals and the objections those revivals face.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4.1 | Aristotle: the human good | Reconstruct the function argument and say what *eudaimonia* is and is not | final ends, the function (*ergon*) argument (NE I.7), *eudaimonia* vs pleasure, external goods |
| 4.2 | Aristotle: virtue and practical wisdom | Use the mean and *phronesis* to say what a virtuous agent would do | virtue as a settled state, the doctrine of the mean (NE II.6), habituation, *phronesis*, continence vs virtue, *akrasia* |
| 4.3 | Modern virtue ethics | Assess whether virtue ethics can guide action and survive the situationist challenge | Anscombe's "Modern Moral Philosophy", Hursthouse's criterion of right action, v-rules, the application objection, situationism (Doris, Harman) and its replication worries |
| 4.4 | Natural law I: Aquinas | State the first principle of practical reason and derive precepts from the natural inclinations | eternal and natural law, *synderesis*, ST I-II q.94 a.2, the order of inclinations, primary vs secondary precepts, q.94 a.4 on variation |
| 4.5 | Natural law II: the new natural law theory | Compare the basic-goods account with Thomistic naturalism and test both on the is–ought gap | Grisez, Finnis and Boyle, self-evident basic goods, incommensurability, modes of responsibility, the neo-Thomist critique (Hittinger, McInerny), Hume's is–ought passage |

**Boss problem 4:** Take Aristotle, *Nicomachean Ethics* I.7 (Ross translation) and Aquinas, *Summa Theologiae* I-II q.94 a.2 (English Dominican translation), both public domain. (a) Reconstruct the function argument in standard form and flag its weakest premise. (b) Close-read q.94 a.2: state the first precept of natural law, and the three levels of natural inclination with an example Aquinas gives for each. (c) Find the crux between a Thomistic reading, on which goods are known through facts about human nature, and the new natural law theory, on which basic goods are self-evident practical starting points not derived from those facts. Name the premise each side denies. (d) Say whether the new theory's way around Hume's is–ought gap costs it anything the older view keeps, in 150 words or fewer. Parts (a)–(c) are exegetical; (d) is evaluative.

### Module 5: Contract and plurality

Morality as what people could agree to, in its two rival forms. Then the view that no single master principle exists at all.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5.1 | Contractarianism: morality as mutual advantage | Reconstruct the Hobbesian route to morality and test it on those with no bargaining power | Hobbes's reply to the Foole (*Leviathan* ch. 15), Gauthier, the compliance problem, the problem of the weak (animals, future people) |
| 5.2 | Contractualism: what no one could reasonably reject | Apply Scanlon's test to a case and decide whether it lets the numbers count | reasonable rejection, the individualist restriction, the transmitter-room case, Taurek on numbers, tie-breaking arguments, the redundancy objection |
| 5.3 | Pluralism and particularism | Weigh prima facie duties in a conflict and assess whether moral principles can be codified | Ross's prima facie duties, all-things-considered duty, Dancy's holism of reasons, defaults, the guidance objection, reflective equilibrium revisited |

**Boss problem 5:** Take Hobbes's reply to the Foole in *Leviathan* ch. 15 (public domain). (a) Reconstruct the Foole's challenge and Hobbes's reply in standard form, and name the premise about other people's responses that the reply relies on. (b) Steelman a critic who says a mutual-advantage theory leaves the powerless outside morality altogether. Then reply on behalf of a Scanlonian contractualist, and say whether the reply is still a contract theory or has quietly become something else, in 150 words or fewer. (c) Take a two-duty conflict written for the problem, such as a promise against a rescue. Show what a Rossian pluralist and a Scanlonian contractualist each say, and name the crux between them. Part (a) is exegetical; (b) is evaluative; (c) is exegetical for the two verdicts and evaluative for the crux.

### Module 6: Metaethics

The questions underneath all of the above. Are there moral facts? If so, what are they, and how could we know them? If not, what are we doing when we make moral claims?

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 6.1 | Moral realism | Distinguish naturalist from non-naturalist realism and state the explanation challenge to both | Hume's is–ought passage, Moore's open question argument, Cornell naturalism, non-naturalism (Parfit, Enoch), Harman's explanation argument |
| 6.2 | Expressivism | State what moral judgments express on the expressivist view and why embedding is a problem | emotivism (Ayer, Stevenson), norm-expressivism (Gibbard), quasi-realism (Blackburn), the Frege–Geach problem |
| 6.3 | Error theory | Reconstruct Mackie's arguments and say what an error theorist should do next | the argument from queerness, the argument from relativity, categorical authority, abolitionism vs fictionalism, the companions-in-guilt reply |
| 6.4 | Constructivism and debunking | State Kantian and Humean constructivism and run the Darwinian dilemma against realism | Korsgaard, Street, the practical standpoint, the Darwinian dilemma, evolutionary debunking and third-factor replies |
| 6.5 | God and morality: the Euthyphro dilemma | Reconstruct the dilemma against divine command theory and assess the modified replies | Plato's *Euthyphro*, arbitrariness and emptiness horns, Adams's modified divine command theory, grounding the good in God's nature, the natural law reply |
| 6.6 | Moral knowledge and disagreement | Assess whether we can know moral truths and whether disagreement undermines realism | intuitionism and self-evidence, reflective equilibrium as moral epistemology, descriptive vs metaethical relativism, disagreement as evidence (conciliationism cited from `epistemology`) |

**Boss problem 6:** Take the dilemma Socrates poses in Plato's *Euthyphro* (Jowett translation, public domain): is the pious loved by the gods because it is pious, or pious because it is loved? (a) Restate it as a dilemma against a divine command theory of moral obligation, in standard form, with both horns. (b) For the claim "gratuitous cruelty is wrong", say what non-naturalist realism, expressivism, error theory, Kantian constructivism and Adams's modified divine command theory each take to make it true, if anything, and how each thinks we come to know it. (c) Choose one of the five views. State its strongest objection from Module 6 at full strength, then give the best reply its defenders offer and judge whether the reply works, in 300–500 words. Parts (a)–(b) are exegetical; (c) is evaluative.

## Notes

- **political-philosophy must cite this course.** The current [`political-philosophy`](../political-philosophy/syllabus.md) syllabus teaches utilitarianism and its objections in Module 2 (2.1 "Utilitarianism as a theory of justice", 2.2 "What's wrong with utilitarianism"). That syllabus is written to an older model: no problem kinds, header prereqs "none", while the roadmap now lists `ethics` as a prerequisite. When it is rewritten, 2.1–2.2 should cite ethics Module 1 (1.1, 1.3) for the moral theory and the separateness-of-persons objection. They should keep only what is specific to justice: utilitarianism as a principle for institutions, and the comparison with Rawls. Its Hobbes lessons should likewise cite 5.1 for the contractarian account of morality.
- **Where this course goes next.** `moral-theology` takes natural law (4.4–4.5), virtue (4.2) and double effect (3.2) into a theological setting. `philosophy-of-law` takes natural law into jurisprudence. `philosophy-of-debt` builds on promising and the contract tradition. `philosophy-of-economics` builds on well-being (1.2). `decision-theory` owns the formal treatment of expected value, aggregation and population ethics that 1.5 and 5.2 only point to.
- **Lesson count.** The roadmap estimated ~22; this runs 26 (+18%, inside the ±25% band). The steering adds two owned topics beyond the roadmap blurb: doing/allowing and double effect (Module 3), and natural law as a two-lesson treatment covering both Aquinas and the new natural law theory. Metaethics needs six lessons to give divine command theory and moral knowledge their own space rather than footnotes. Nothing is crammed: the aggregation material that would have overloaded 1.5 moves to 5.2, where contractualism makes it a live question.
- **Particularism earns its place** as half of 5.3, paired with Ross. It is the standing challenge to every theory in Modules 1–5, and it closes the normative half of the course by asking whether any of them could be complete.
