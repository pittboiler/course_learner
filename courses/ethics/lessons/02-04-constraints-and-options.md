# Ethics · Lesson 2.4: Constraints and options

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 2: Kantian deontology · Builds on: [1.3 Justice and the separateness of persons](01-03-justice-and-the-separateness-of-persons.md), [2.3 Humanity, autonomy, and the lie](02-03-humanity-autonomy-and-the-lie.md) · Unlocks: [3.1 Doing and allowing](03-01-doing-and-allowing.md) (Module 3: Doing, allowing, and intending)

## Why this matters

Strip away the Kantian machinery and ordinary morality still differs from act consequentialism in two structural ways. It *forbids* some acts that would make things go best: you may not kill one innocent to prevent two killings. And it *permits* some acts that don't: you may spend the weekend on your own novel rather than on the most good you could do. The first is a **constraint**, the second an **option**. Every rival to consequentialism in this course (Kant, double effect, natural law, Scanlon, Ross) must say whether it has them and why. This lesson supplies the vocabulary, and the hardest argument against constraints: that it is irrational to forbid a wrong when committing it would prevent more of the same wrong.

## The idea

Start with who a reason belongs to. "The drowning child should be saved" gives every onlooker the same aim: that the child be saved, by anyone. "I must not break *my* promise" gives each person a different aim: that *she* not break *hers*. The second kind of reason has the agent written into it.

That difference generates both structures. A consequentialist ranks outcomes from no one's point of view in particular, and tells everyone to bring about the top one. A constraint says: whatever the ranking, *you* are not to do this. An option says: *you* need not bring about the top outcome, when doing so would cost you enough.

Now the trouble. Suppose killing innocents is forbidden because killings of innocents are terrible. Then two of them are more terrible than one. If your killing one would prevent two, the very thing that grounds the rule seems to call for breaking it. A deontologist who still says "don't" owes an account of what the rule protects, if not fewer killings.

## The argument

**Three definitions.**

1. **Agent-relative vs agent-neutral.** A reason is *agent-relative* if its general statement ineliminably refers to the person who has it ("that it would be a killing *by you*"), and *agent-neutral* if it does not ("that it would be a killing"). The terms are Parfit's (*Reasons and Persons*, 1984), and Nagel develops the distinction at length (*The View from Nowhere*, 1986, ch. 9). *In words:* does the reason give everyone one shared aim, or give each person her own version of it?
2. **Constraint (side constraint).** An agent-relative prohibition: you may not do $A$ even to minimize the total number of $A$-acts done by others. Nozick (*Anarchy, State, and Utopia*, 1974, ch. 3) contrasts side constraints with a "utilitarianism of rights", which puts rights violations into the outcome and tells you to minimize them. *In words:* a constraint limits what you do; it is not a goal you advance.
3. **Option (agent-centred prerogative).** A permission not to produce the best outcome. Scheffler (*The Rejection of Consequentialism*, 1982) models it as a licence to weight your own interests $M$ times as heavily as anyone else's, for some $M > 1$. *In words:* morality leaves you room to live your own life.

**The paradox of deontology.** Nozick raises the question in chapter 3; Scheffler (1982) presses it hardest.

- **P1.** A constraint forbids killing one innocent even when that is the only way to prevent two killings of innocents by others. *In words:* that is what makes it a constraint rather than a goal.
- **P2.** What grounds the constraint is the badness of killings of innocents, a badness everyone has the same reason to care about. *In words:* the rule exists because such killings are terrible, full stop.
- **P3.** The rational response to something bad is to minimize how much of it there is. *In words:* values are to be promoted.
- **P4.** Killing one to prevent two minimizes killings of innocents. *In words:* arithmetic.
- **∴ C.** The constraint forbids the rational response to the very thing that grounds it, so it is irrational. *In words:* the rule defeats its own point.

The argument is valid, so the deontologist must deny a premise. P1 is her own view, and P4 is arithmetic. The live replies deny P2 or P3.

**Nagel: the agent's will (denies P2).** Nagel grants that constraints are puzzling from the impersonal standpoint. His explanation locates their ground in the agent's relation to the harm, not in the harm as an event. To *intend* someone's death, even as a means, is to have your action guided by evil, steering toward what should repel you. The victim can say: *you are aiming at me*. The two killings by others involve no such aim of yours, so there is no neutral quantity of "aiming at evil" for you to minimize. The reason was agent-relative from the start. *Cost:* the consequentialist asks why the character of your will should outweigh an extra death, and calls the answer a concern for clean hands.

**Kamm: inviolability (denies P3).** Kamm (*Morality, Mortality*, vol. II, 1996; *Intricate Ethics*, 2007) grants that constraints express a value, but it is a *status*: being a person who may not be killed for others' benefit. The status is fixed by what morality permits, not by what happens, and everyone has it, the two potential victims included. If killing one to save two were permitted, no one would gain inviolability; all three would be less inviolable. So the value is not promoted by breaking the constraint. It is *respected* by keeping it. In Philip Pettit's terms (1991), some values are to be honoured rather than promoted. *Cost:* why is higher status worth more than a lower chance of being killed? A status that costs a life can look like a luxury.

## Map of positions

Two yes/no questions give four structures.

| | **No options:** always do the best | **Options:** you may do less than the best |
|---|---|---|
| **No constraints:** any act may be done for the best | **Act consequentialism.** Maximize the impartial good, with no exception in your favour and no limit on means. | **Scheffler's hybrid.** Rejects constraints as paradoxical, keeps a prerogative to favour your own projects. |
| **Constraints** | **Demanding deontology.** Never kill the innocent, *and* give until giving stops helping. A coherent structure, rarely defended as a whole. | **Common-sense deontology.** Don't kill the innocent; your weekend is yours. Where ordinary morality and most deontologists sit. |

The upper-right cell hides a problem, pressed by Kagan (*The Limits of Morality*, 1989), who argues that neither constraints nor options can be justified. A bare prerogative to weight your interests $M$ times draws no line between *not helping* and *harming*. It is triggered by the cost to you, and a benefit you could obtain by harming someone weighs the same as a cost you avoid by not helping. If it lets you keep 20,000 dollars rather than give it to save a stranger's life, it seems also to let you harm a stranger to gain 20,000 dollars. The obvious fix, allowing the first but not the second, is a constraint against harming. So options seem to need constraints, which pulls the hybrid toward common-sense deontology and back into the paradox.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean): a constraint vs a utilitarianism of rights.** An investigator can stop a colleague from planting evidence against two innocent suspects only by planting evidence against a third innocent person herself. Stipulate certainty, no discovery, no further effects.

- *Utilitarianism of rights:* planted evidence violates rights; one violation is fewer than two; plant it.
- *Act consequentialism:* plant it, for the same arithmetic plus nothing, since side effects are stipulated away.
- *Side constraint:* the prohibition is on what *this investigator* does. The two frame-ups are her colleague's. Don't plant it.
- *Scheffler's hybrid:* no constraint, and refraining protects none of her own interests, so the prerogative is idle. Plant it.

Only the agent-relative structure says no. Your verdict on this case therefore tells you whether you believe in constraints, and P2 and P3 are what you must defend if you do.

**Example 2 (hard): threshold deontology.** Many who hold constraints balk at absolutism. May you not frame one innocent to stop a riot that will kill ten thousand? Nozick left open whether constraints may be violated to avoid "catastrophic moral horror". *Threshold deontology*, defended by Michael Moore among others, answers yes: a constraint holds below some threshold of harm prevented and lapses above it.

Run it with a threshold of 1,000 lives. Framing one to prevent 2 deaths: forbidden, as P1 requires. Framing one to prevent 10,000: permitted. That matches the reactions many people report, which is the view's appeal. It strains in two places.

- **Where is the line?** If preventing 1,000 deaths is not enough and 1,001 is, a single life converts a prohibition into a permission. Constraints were meant to be insensitive to marginal arithmetic, and at the threshold this one is maximally sensitive. Any particular number looks arbitrary, and a vague threshold doesn't remove the problem, only blurs where it bites.
- **Past the line, is it consequentialism?** Above the threshold the view says to minimize harm. A critic says it is consequentialism with a large exception, and so owes both a defence of the constraint below the line and a defence of maximizing above it. The defender replies that the constraint still weighs past the line: breaking it needs far more than a surplus of good, and the agent still owes something to the one she wronged.

Neither strain refutes the view. Each marks a cost its defenders accept in exchange for escaping absolutism.

## Watch out

- **You might think agent-relative means selfish, but** constraints bind you for others' sake. "Do not lie to anyone" is agent-relative and wholly other-regarding. The relativity is in the form of the reason (it names its owner), not in who benefits.
- **You might think the paradox is only a problem for Kant, but** it reaches any view with exceptionless or near-exceptionless prohibitions: rights theories, double effect, natural law's absolute norms. Each must deny P2 or P3.
- **You might think a consequentialist can absorb constraints by ranking outcomes relative to the agent, so that *my* killing counts ten times, but** that gives up the impartial ranking that made P3 compelling. Whether the result still deserves the name "consequentialism" is itself disputed.
- **You might think constraints and options come as a package, but** they come apart. Scheffler takes options without constraints; the demanding deontologist takes constraints without options.

## One-liner

> A constraint says don't, even to prevent more of the same; an option says you needn't do the best; and the paradox asks what a constraint protects if not fewer violations.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢) *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)*** Lena faces two choices in the same week. Stipulate certainty, no discovery and no side effects.

> **Choice 1.** Spend 3,000 dollars she has saved for a writing retreat on the retreat, or give it to a charity where it will save one stranger's life.
>
> **Choice 2.** A colleague is about to give false sworn testimony against two innocent coworkers. Lena can prevent it only by giving false sworn testimony against a third innocent coworker herself. Her own interests are unaffected either way.

(a) For each of the four positions in the Map of positions, say whether Lena's lying in Choice 2 is required, permitted or forbidden, and whether spending the money on the retreat in Choice 1 is. For Scheffler's hybrid on Choice 1, say what the verdict depends on. (b) Change Choice 2: nothing is prevented, but lying against the coworker wins Lena a promotion worth a great deal to her. Does Scheffler's hybrid forbid the lie? Say whether this is a counterexample to the hybrid or a bullet it can bite. 150 words or fewer for (b).

**P2 (🟡) *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)*** Find the crux in this invented exchange.

> **Ana:** "Your rule against kidnapping is there because kidnapping is an atrocity. If kidnapping one person prevents two kidnappings, refusing leaves more atrocity in the world. You are putting your clean hands above the victims."
>
> **Ben:** "No one, the two included, is safer in a morality that lets people be seized whenever the numbers work. Keeping the rule is what treats all three as people who may not be used."

(a) Which premise of the paradox does Ben deny, and which reply in the lesson is he giving? Which premise must Ana hold fixed for her charge to stick? Ana's "clean hands" jab targets a different reply: which, and what premise does *that* reply deny? One sentence each. (b) Ana answers: "The two will actually be kidnapped. Your inviolability is a fact about permissions, and it doesn't keep anyone out of a car trunk." Say what argument would move one side off its premise, and whether Ana's answer lands. 150 words or fewer.

**P3 (🔴, optional) *(Evaluative.)*** A threshold deontologist holds: *killing an innocent person is permitted only if the act prevents at least 100 deaths.* Build a case where, applied act by act, this rule gives inconsistent or plainly wrong verdicts on outcomes that are identical. Hint: ask what counts as one act. Then propose one repair and name its cost. Show your arithmetic; 150 words or fewer.

<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)*

**Must hit, strict (a):**

| Position | Choice 1: spend on the retreat | Choice 2: lie against the one |
|---|---|---|
| Act consequentialism | Forbidden (a life outweighs the retreat) | Required (one false testimony beats two) |
| Scheffler's hybrid | Permitted if and only if $M$ times her loss from giving up the retreat outweighs the stranger's loss of life | Required: no constraint, and her interests are unaffected, so the prerogative is idle |
| Demanding deontology | Forbidden (no options) | Forbidden (constraint) |
| Common-sense deontology | Permitted (option) | Forbidden (constraint) |

The hybrid's Choice 1 verdict depends on the size of $M$ against the ratio of the stranger's loss to Lena's. For a life against a retreat, $M$ has to be very large.

**Must hit, any verdict (b):**
- Run the prerogative correctly: it is triggered by the weight of Lena's own interests, not by the difference between harming and not helping. So if $M$ times the promotion's value exceeds the coworker's loss, the hybrid *permits* the lie.
- Note that forbidding it requires limiting the prerogative to not-helping, which is a doing/allowing asymmetry, and in effect a constraint.
- A verdict, either way: counterexample, or bite the bullet (e.g. by arguing $M$ is too small for the promotion to outweigh a ruined career).

**Wrong turns:** saying the hybrid forbids the lie "because lying is wrong", which smuggles in the constraint the hybrid rejects; marking common-sense deontology's retreat verdict as *required*, when an option permits either choice.

**Model answer (b), one of several acceptable:** The hybrid has no constraint, so the only question is whether Lena's gain, weighted $M$ times, exceeds her coworker's loss. With a large enough promotion and $M$, it does, and the hybrid permits framing an innocent for a career move. To block this, the prerogative would have to cover not helping but not harming, and that asymmetry is exactly a constraint against doing harm. So I count it as a counterexample: the hybrid cannot keep its options decent without the constraints it rejected. A defender can bite the bullet by keeping $M$ modest, but then the prerogative shrinks in Choice 1 as well, since the same $M$ governs both.

---

**P2** *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)*

**Must hit, strict (a):**
- Ben denies **P3**: the value behind the rule (not being usable) is respected by keeping it, not promoted by breaking it. This is **Kamm's inviolability reply**.
- Ana holds **P3** fixed (and reads the ground agent-neutrally, as in P2): badness is to be minimized.
- "Clean hands" targets **Nagel's** reply, which denies **P2**: the constraint is grounded in the agent's will aiming at harm, an agent-relative ground.

**Must hit, any verdict (b):**
- Identify the crux as P3: are all values to be promoted, or are some to be honoured?
- Name what would move each side. Ana moves if shown a value she herself honours without promoting (for instance, whether she would break a promise to prevent two others' broken promises). Ben moves if shown that inviolability has no value beyond what it does for people's actual safety.
- Judge Ana's answer: does it show status is worthless, or only that it is a different good from safety?

**Wrong turns:** saying Ben denies P4 (the arithmetic is not in dispute); treating Ben's reply as Nagel's, since Ben speaks about the three victims' status, not about the kidnapper's will.

**Model answer (b), one of several acceptable:** The crux is P3. Ana would move if she found a value she treats as honoured, not promoted, such as refusing to betray one friend to prevent two other people's betrayals of theirs; then "values are to be promoted" is not a principle she holds universally. Ben would move if inviolability turned out to matter only instrumentally, for safety, because then fewer kidnappings would serve it better. Ana's answer lands partly. It shows that inviolability does not protect anyone physically. It does not show that the status has no value of its own, and that is the claim Ben's reply needs.

---

**P3** *(Evaluative.)*

**Must hit, any verdict:**
- A case with **identical outcomes** that the rule treats differently depending on how acts are counted.
- Correct arithmetic against the 100 threshold.
- One repair and its specific cost.

**Wrong turns:** a case that only shows the threshold number is arbitrary (1,000 vs 1,001), which the lesson already made and which does not involve identical outcomes; forgetting to check that both descriptions contain the same deaths.

**Model answer, one of several acceptable:** A dam operator must divert floodwater twice in one night. Each diversion drowns one farmhouse occupant and prevents 60 deaths downstream. Decided separately, each act kills one to prevent $60 < 100$: both forbidden, so 120 die. Decided as one plan, a single act kills two to prevent $2 \times 60 = 120 \ge 100$: permitted. Same deaths, opposite verdicts, depending only on whether she deliberates once or twice. *Repair:* make the threshold per victim, 100 deaths prevented per person killed. Now both descriptions give $120 / 2 = 60 < 100$, forbidden, consistently. *Cost:* a fixed exchange rate of 100 lives per killing is a weighted sum. The view now looks like consequentialism that counts your killings a hundredfold, and a critic will say that is exactly the agent-relative ranking the lesson warned about.

## Flashback

**From Lesson 1.5 (Modern consequentialism):** A coast-guard dispatcher has one boat. At the reef, her evidence gives a 0.9 chance of finding two swimmers alive; at the cove, a 0.5 chance of finding five. She sends it to the cove. The five had already drowned, and the two at the reef would have been saved. (a) *(Formal.)* Compute expected lives saved for each choice. What does the expected-value view say about her act, and what does the actual-consequences view say? (b) *(Exegetical.)* A rule consequentialist's code says: "Answer the call with the highest expected rescues." A critic proposes adding "unless answering another call would be expected to save more lives in this case." State the collapse objection using this rule, and Hooker's reply. 100 words or fewer for (b).

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):** $EV(\text{reef}) = 0.9 \times 2 = 1.8$; $EV(\text{cove}) = 0.5 \times 5 = 2.5$. The expected-value view says sending the boat to the cove was *right*, since $2.5 > 1.8$. The actual-consequences view says it was *wrong*, since the reef would have saved 2 and the cove saved 0, though she is blameless.

**Must hit, strict (b):**
- **Collapse:** under perfect compliance, the amended rule does at least as well as the plain one, so rule consequentialism's own criterion selects it; the amended code then gives the act-consequentialist verdict in every case, and the two theories become equivalent.
- **Hooker's reply:** the code is assessed by the consequences of its being *internalized* by nearly everyone, counting teaching costs and misapplication. Dispatchers told to override protocol whenever they judge it better will misjudge, in the heat of a rescue and on poor information, so the simpler rule does better, and the theories come apart.

**Wrong turns:** computing the cove as $5$ because "that's where the five were"; saying the actual view calls her blameworthy (rightness and blame come apart); stating collapse as "rules sometimes give bad results", which is the rule-worship charge, not collapse.

**Model answer:** (a) Reef 1.8, cove 2.5. Expected view: right. Actual view: wrong, since the reef would have saved two, but blameless. (b) Collapse: the exception-laden rule does at least as well under full compliance, so it wins, and then the code just says "do whatever saves most", which is act consequentialism. Hooker: judge codes by their internalization, not perfect compliance. Real dispatchers licensed to override will misjudge under pressure, so the plain rule does more good and the two theories diverge.

</details>
