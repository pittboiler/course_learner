# Ethics · Lesson 2.3: Humanity, autonomy, and the lie

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 2: Kantian deontology · Builds on: [2.2 The Formula of Universal Law](02-02-the-formula-of-universal-law.md) · Unlocks: [2.4 Constraints and options](02-04-constraints-and-options.md)

## Why this matters

"You're just using me" is the most common moral complaint there is, and the second formula of the categorical imperative is the attempt to say exactly what it means. It gives deontology its most quoted principle, the ground of most talk about human dignity, and its most notorious verdict: that you may not lie to a murderer asking where your friend is hiding. Whether that verdict is a reductio or a hard truth is a test of how the formula works.

## The idea

Every day you use people. The cab driver is a means to getting home and the pharmacist is a means to your prescription. Kant knew this. The formula, in Abbott's translation of the *Groundwork* (1785):

> "So act as to treat humanity, whether in thine own person or in that of any other, in every case as an end withal, never as means only."

The weight sits on two words: **withal** (at the same time) and **only**. Using someone as a means is fine. Using them *merely* as a means is not. What separates the two?

Compare the cab driver with a passenger who plans to run off without paying. Both use the driver to get home. The honest passenger acts on terms the driver knows and has agreed to; the driver's own end (a fare) is part of the deal. The runner's plan works only because the driver doesn't know what is going on. If he knew, the scheme would collapse. The driver is still an agent in the first case. In the second he has been turned into a tool, like the car.

**Humanity** here means rational nature: the capacity to set ends for yourself and pursue them by reason. Kant's claim is that "rational nature exists as an end in itself." It is the source of every other value, not one value among many. Two ideas follow, briefly:

- **The kingdom of ends.** Things have a *price*, which means an equivalent could replace them. Persons have *dignity*, which admits no equivalent. Picture a community where everyone is at once a lawmaker and a subject, and every law respects each member's ends. The moral law is the constitution of that community.
- **Autonomy.** The will is bound by a law it gives itself, as a rational will, not by desire, custom or command (*heteronomy*). In words: morality isn't imposed on you from outside; it is what your own reason legislates for any rational agent. Autonomy doesn't mean doing what you like. It means being under a law that is authentically yours.

## The argument

The most influential reconstruction of "merely as a means" is the **possible-consent reading**. Onora O'Neill developed it in "Between Consenting Adults" (1985), and Christine Korsgaard offered a close cousin. It takes its cue from Kant's own false-promise example, where the victim "cannot possibly assent to my mode of acting towards him."

1. **Humanity is the capacity to set and pursue ends by reason.** *In words:* what the formula protects is agency, not happiness.
2. **To treat a person as an end is, at minimum, not to make her agency the unwitting or unwilling instrument of your ends.** *In words:* you can use her help, but not by going around her will.
3. **You use a person *merely* as a means when you act on a maxim she could not possibly consent to.** "Could not possibly" is structural: she couldn't consent even if she wanted to. *In words:* the test isn't whether she'd say yes, but whether saying yes is even available to her.
4. **Deception makes consent impossible.** The deceived person doesn't know the real maxim, so she can't consent to it. If she knew, it would fail. *In words:* you can't agree to a trick you can't see.
5. **Coercion makes consent impossible.** The coerced person's refusal is overridden in advance, so her "consent" does no work. *In words:* a choice with no exit isn't a choice.
6. **∴ Deceiving and coercing use persons merely as means, and FH forbids them.** *In words:* lies and force are wrong not mainly for their results but because they bypass the other person's reason.

Notice what premise 3 leaves out. It makes no appeal to actual consent (the driver never signed anything) or to what she would *want*. That is its strength: it explains why ordinary exchange is fine and fraud isn't. Premise 3 is also where the objections land.

## Map of positions

| Reading of "merely as a means" | Test | Handles well | Strains on |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Actual consent** | Did she in fact agree? | Contracts, medical ethics | Too strict: you never asked the driver. Too lax: consent obtained by fraud still counts |
| **Possible consent** (O'Neill, Korsgaard) | Could she, in principle, consent to *this maxim*? | Fraud, coercion, the false promise | Benign deceptions (surprises, games); punishment, since the convict cannot consent to his sentence |
| **Shared end** (a strand in Korsgaard) | Could she hold the end of the action herself? | Why exchange is fine | Adversarial practices: the losing bidder can't share the winner's end |
| **Skeptical** (Parfit, *On What Matters*, 2011) | "Mere means" is not what makes acts wrong | Separates attitude from act | Must explain the wrongness of fraud another way |

Parfit's challenge is worth stating at strength, in rough paraphrase. Someone can treat a person merely as a means without acting wrongly: a ruthless gangster who regards the barista as a mere tool still pays for his coffee, and the purchase is not wrong. And someone can wrong a person without treating her merely as a means, by harming her as a side effect. If both hold, "mere means" is at best a partial account of wrongness. Parfit then proposes a principle about what everyone could rationally will (a descendant of the Universal Law formula) and argues it does the work better. Kantians reply that the formula targets maxims, not attitudes: the gangster's *act* is permissible even if his character is not.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean case: the false promise).**

You need money and borrow it on a promise to repay next month, knowing you won't. Run the six steps. The maxim: *to get money when I need it, promise to repay without intending to.* Could the lender possibly consent to *that*? No. The moment she knows your intention, she won't lend, because the loan exists only because she doesn't know. Her agreement is real but it's agreement to a different transaction, the one you described. So premise 4 applies: she is used merely as a means.

Now take the honest version. "I'm short, I may not be able to repay for a year, will you lend anyway?" Maybe she says no. But if she says yes, she has agreed to the real maxim and adopted your end as one she's willing to serve. Same money, same lender, same risk to her. What changed is only whether her reason was engaged or bypassed. This lines up with [2.2](02-02-the-formula-of-universal-law.md): the false promise also fails universalization, since in a world where everyone promised this way, no one would accept promises. Kant claimed the formulas are equivalent, and here they agree.

**Example 2 (hard case: the murderer at the door).**

In 1797 Benjamin Constant objected that a duty to tell the truth, held unconditionally, would make society impossible. His example: would it be a crime to lie to a murderer who asks whether your friend has taken refuge in your house? Constant's answer was that the duty of truthfulness is owed only to those with a *right* to the truth, and the murderer has none.

Kant's reply, "On a Supposed Right to Lie from Philanthropy" (1797), refuses the exception. Paraphrasing: truthfulness in statements you cannot avoid making is a duty to everyone, however great the cost. A lie always wrongs someone: if not the person lied to, then humanity generally, because it undermines the credibility of statements on which all right and contract depend. And the liar owns the consequences. Suppose you lie and say your friend has gone out. Unknown to you, he really has slipped out, and the murderer meets him on the street. Then you can justly be held responsible for his death. Had you told the truth, neighbours might have caught the murderer while he searched the house. The truth-teller leaves the outcome to fate. The liar makes it his own.

Kant's defenders stress two things. First, the essay argues from *right*, about the conditions of any lawful society, not only from the *Groundwork* formulas. Second, it condemns a lie, not silence or refusal. You may shut the door.

Christine Korsgaard's "The Right to Lie: Kant on Dealing with Evil" (1986) argues that Kant's own formulas come apart on this case. Paraphrased:

- **Universal Law permits the lie.** The murderer doesn't announce his purpose. He relies on your *not knowing* he is a murderer. Universalize "lie to murderers who deceive you about their intent" and the lie still works, because the murderer still assumes you don't know. No contradiction in conception.
- **Humanity looks stricter.** Deceiving him still bypasses his reason, whatever he is doing to you. The possible-consent test condemns the lie.
- **A two-level resolution.** Humanity and the kingdom of ends describe the *ideal*: how to act when others do their part. In non-ideal conditions, faced with evil, you may depart from the ideal, but only as far as Universal Law allows. So the lie to the murderer is permitted as a response to evil, not as an ordinary option.

This is where the principle strains. Kant's verdict follows from an unconditional reading of truthfulness plus an argument from right. Korsgaard's follows from ranking the formulas for non-ideal conditions. Constant's follows from tying the duty to a right to the truth. Each gets the murderer case its own way. They disagree on whether a moral law can be indexed to how well others are behaving.

## Watch out

- **You might think the formula forbids using people.** It forbids using them *merely* as means. Nearly all cooperation uses others. The test is whether their agency is engaged or bypassed, not whether you benefit.
- **You might think possible consent means "would she agree if asked?"** That is a hypothetical-preference test, and O'Neill rejects it. Possible consent asks whether the maxim's structure leaves room for consent. Someone might happily agree to be tricked for fun, and the trick still hides its maxim at the moment of action.
- **You might think Kant's verdict on the murderer follows from the Universal Law test.** Korsgaard argues the opposite: the lie passes that test. Kant's own essay leans on truthfulness as a duty of right, and critics and defenders alike should name which premise they are attacking.
- **You might think autonomy means choosing your own values.** For Kant it is the will's being bound by the law reason gives to every rational agent. That is the opposite of a law you invent for yourself alone.

## One-liner

> Use people all you like, but never around their reason: if the scheme only works because they can't see it or can't refuse it, you have made a person into a tool.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢) *(Exegetical.)*** On O'Neill's possible-consent reading, does each agent use someone *merely* as a means? Name the feature that decides it. One sentence each.

(a) A shopper pays the listed price for a sandwich.
(b) A landlord hides a known mould problem so that a tenant will sign a lease.
(c) An employer interviews five candidates under announced criteria, hires one, and rejects the other four.

**P2 (🟡) *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)*** Dana tells her friend Sam that they are going to a quiet dinner, so that Sam will walk into his own surprise birthday party. Sam loves surprise parties and would say, if asked in general, that he is glad his friends do this.

(a) What does the possible-consent reading say about Dana's lie, and which premise of the argument delivers that result? (b) Is this a counterexample to the Formula of Humanity so read, or can a defender absorb it? Say exactly where the principle stops determining a verdict. Any verdict; 150 words or fewer for (b).

**P3 (🔴, optional) *(Evaluative.)*** State the strongest objection to Kant's verdict that you may not lie to the murderer at the door, then give the best reply available to a Kantian (Kant's own, or Korsgaard's two-level view). Say in one line whether the reply succeeds. Any verdict; 150 words or fewer.

<details>
<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical — strict.)*

**Must hit, strict:**
- (a) **Not merely as a means.** The seller knows and agrees to the terms, and the sale serves his own end. Nothing is hidden and he is free to refuse.
- (b) **Merely as a means.** Deception: the lease is signed only because the tenant doesn't know about the mould. Disclosing it would defeat the scheme, so she cannot consent to the real maxim.
- (c) **Not merely as a means.** The rejected candidates entered a procedure whose terms were announced. They are disappointed, not deceived or coerced. Being on the losing end of a practice you could knowingly join is not being bypassed.

**Wrong turns:** calling (c) mere means because the rejected candidates didn't *want* to lose. Possible consent concerns the maxim, not the outcome, and the shared-end reading has its trouble exactly here. Calling (b) wrong "because the tenant is harmed": the reading locates the wrong in the deception, not the harm.

**Model answer:** (a) No: open terms the seller accepts. (b) Yes: the lease depends on concealment the tenant cannot consent to. (c) No: an announced procedure, joined knowingly, bypasses no one's reason.

---

**P2** *(a) exegetical — strict; (b) evaluative — any verdict.*

**Must hit, strict (a):** the lie is a deception whose success depends on Sam not knowing the real plan, so he cannot consent to *that maxim* at the time of action. Premise 4 (deception makes consent impossible) delivers the verdict: Dana uses Sam merely as a means, so the reading condemns it. His general approval of surprises is a hypothetical preference, which premise 3 excludes.

**Must hit, any verdict (b):**
- State the tension: most people judge the lie innocent, yet the reading classifies it with fraud.
- Either (i) accept it as a counterexample and name the premise to revise (usually 3, e.g. adding standing or advance consent to a *type* of deception), or (ii) absorb it: Sam's standing consent to surprises is consent to the maxim at a higher level of description, or the lie is a minor wrong.
- Name where the principle goes silent: how specific the described maxim must be. "Deceive Sam about tonight" and "run the kind of surprise Sam has endorsed" get different verdicts, and the formula doesn't say which description counts.

**Wrong turns:** answering (a) by asking whether Sam *would* agree, which is the hypothetical test the reading rejects. Treating (b) as settled by how pleasant the party is: a consequence, not a move about consent.

**Model answer (b), one of several:** A defender can absorb it. Sam has given standing consent to a *practice* of benign surprises, and the maxim, described at that level, is one he has in fact endorsed. But the absorption costs something. Everything now depends on the level at which the maxim is described, and the formula supplies no rule for that. The landlord could claim the tenant endorses "the ordinary practice of landlords presenting properties favourably." What blocks him seems to be the harm and the tenant's actual unawareness of *this* concealment. So the principle stops determining a verdict at the choice of maxim description, the same place [2.2](02-02-the-formula-of-universal-law.md)'s test misfired.

---

**P3** *(Evaluative — any verdict.)*

**Must hit, any verdict:**
- The objection at full strength, not "Kant is too rigid." For example: the murderer is using you merely as a means to murder, and a principle meant to protect rational agency cannot require you to become his instrument. Or: forbidding the lie makes the formula serve the aggressor over the victim, which inverts its point.
- A named Kantian reply at its strength: Kant's (truthfulness as a duty of right; the liar owns the consequences; silence or refusal remains available) or Korsgaard's (Universal Law permits the lie against a deceiver; the Formula of Humanity is an ideal that non-ideal conditions may override within Universal Law's limits).
- A one-line verdict on whether the reply meets the objection as stated.

**Wrong turns:** citing Korsgaard as *agreeing* with Kant's verdict. Answering only with consequences (the friend dies) without engaging Kant's point that the liar owns the consequences too. Forgetting the refusal option, which weakens either side's framing.

**Model answer (one of several acceptable):** Objection: the murderer has already made you his instrument by deceiving you about his purpose. Refusing to lie completes his use of you, so the formula meant to protect agency ends up serving its violator. Korsgaard's reply: that's right, and Kant's own Universal Law formula agrees. A maxim of lying to deceivers who rely on your ignorance is universalizable, because the lie still works. Humanity states the ideal for a world of people who do their part. Facing evil, you may depart from it, but only as Universal Law permits. Verdict: the reply meets the objection but concedes that the moral law is indexed to others' conduct, which Kant's essay denies. A Kantian loyal to the essay would reject it.

</details>

## Flashback

**From [philosophical-method 1.3 (Reconstruction and charity)](../../philosophical-method/lessons/01-03-reconstruction-and-charity.md):** An invented letter to a gym's members reads: *"Not one of the members who signed our new contract read clause 14. So the automatic-renewal term in clause 14 cannot bind them."*

(a) Supply the weakest premise that makes the argument valid. (b) A reader supplies instead: *"No person is bound by any contract term he did not personally read."* Say why this violates the weakest-premise rule, and what an opponent gains from it. (c) Name the load-bearing premise. Two sentences or fewer per part.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

*(Exegetical — strict.)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** a premise tied to what the text says, *no* signer read the clause. For example: *a contract term that none of its signers read does not bind them.*

**Must hit, strict (b):** the reader's premise claims more than the inference uses. It covers any single unread term, even when every other signer read it, and it covers every contract. An opponent can knock it down with one case (the diner who didn't read the menu's fine print is still bound to pay), and it will look as though the letter was refuted when its narrower premise is untouched.

**Must hit (c):** the supplied premise is load-bearing. The text's factual claim that nobody read clause 14 is empirical and checkable, but the argument stands or falls on whether universal non-reading voids a term.

**Wrong turns:** supplying "the gym acted in bad faith," which is a different argument. Rejecting (a)'s premise as false: truth isn't asked here. Calling the reader's version invalid: it is valid, just stronger than needed.

**Model answer:** (a) A contract term that none of its signers read does not bind them. (b) The reader's premise extends to terms only some signers skipped, so a single counterexample refutes it while leaving the letter's actual argument standing. (c) The supplied premise: everything turns on whether universal non-reading voids a term, not on the head-count.

</details>
