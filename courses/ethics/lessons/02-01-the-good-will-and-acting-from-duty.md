# Ethics · Lesson 2.1: The good will and acting from duty

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 2: Kantian deontology · Builds on: [1.5 Modern consequentialism](01-05-modern-consequentialism.md) · Unlocks: [2.2 The Formula of Universal Law](02-02-the-formula-of-universal-law.md)

## Why this matters

Module 1 judged everything, motives included, by what it brings about: [1.5](01-05-modern-consequentialism.md)'s sophisticated consequentialist cultivates love and honesty *because* they pay off. Kant starts at the other end. Before he asks what makes an act right, he asks what makes a *person* good in acting, and his answer is that results add nothing to that at all. Everything else in Module 2 builds on the two distinctions this lesson draws: acting *from* duty versus merely *in accordance with* it, and a categorical versus a hypothetical imperative.

## The idea

Two shopkeepers both give a child correct change. One does it because cheating children is bad for business. The other does it because short-changing a child is wrong, and would do so even if nobody would ever find out. Their acts are identical. Kant thinks only the second has *moral worth*, and that there is a real difference between them.

It helps to see what is not being claimed. The first shopkeeper does nothing wrong: his act is right, and Kant says so. The claim is that rightness and worth are different questions. Rightness belongs to the act. Worth belongs to the will behind it, meaning the principle the agent was acting on. A will that is good would stay good if bad luck blocked everything it tried to do. A clever, brave or cheerful person can use those gifts for cruelty. So the only thing good *without qualification* is a will that acts on the right principle for the right reason.

The second distinction concerns the "ought" itself. "If you want the bread to rise, keep the dough warm" binds you only if you want the result. Drop the goal and the "ought" goes with it. Kant thinks a moral "ought" is not like that. "Don't short-change the child" does not end with "...if you want repeat customers." It applies whatever you happen to want.

## Source

Kant, *Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals* (1785), Section I, opening paragraphs, trans. T. K. Abbott (lightly abridged):

> Nothing can possibly be conceived in the world, or even out of it, which can be called good without qualification, except a Good Will. [...] Even if it should happen that, owing to special disfavour of fortune, or the niggardly provision of a step-motherly nature, this will should wholly lack power to accomplish its purpose, if with its greatest efforts it should yet achieve nothing, and there should remain only the good will (not, to be sure, a mere wish, but the summoning of all means in our power), then, like a jewel, it would still shine by its own light, as a thing which has its whole value in itself. Its usefulness or fruitlessness can neither add nor take away anything from this value.

The move is to isolate. Talents, temperament and "gifts of fortune" such as power, wealth and health are good only *conditionally*, since each can make a bad will worse. Take away every result and something still has value. That is what Kant calls the good will.

## The argument

**Groundwork I: the good will and the three propositions.**

1. **Only a good will is good without qualification.** Everything else is good only on condition that a good will uses it. *In words:* intelligence in a swindler makes things worse, so its goodness is conditional.
2. **The good will is not good because of what it achieves.** Its worth survives complete failure. *In words:* luck can block your results, but it cannot take away the worth of your willing.
3. **First proposition: an action has moral worth only if it is done from duty.** Kant never states this one as a numbered thesis. Readers reconstruct it from his examples. *In words:* worth depends on doing the act *because* it is required, not merely in a way that happens to match what is required.
4. **Second proposition: the worth of an act done from duty comes from its maxim, not its purpose.** A *maxim* is "the subjective principle of volition" (Kant's footnote), the policy you are actually acting on, e.g. "when a customer can't check the price, charge the fixed price anyway." *In words:* what makes the act worthy is the principle you act on, not whether the aim is achieved.
5. **Third proposition: duty is the necessity of an action from respect for law.** *In words:* acting from duty means being moved by the recognition that a principle binds you, not by a desire for its effects.
6. **Section II: imperatives.** A *hypothetical* imperative commands an action as a means to an end. It is either *problematic*, commanding skill toward an end you might or might not have ("to fix the leak, shut the valve"), or *assertoric*, commanding prudence toward an end all humans actually have, happiness ("save, if you want a secure old age"). A *categorical* imperative commands an action as necessary in itself, without reference to any further end. *In words:* hypothetical "oughts" borrow their force from your desires, while a categorical one has force of its own.
7. **Therefore morality, if it binds every rational agent whatever they want, must take the form of a categorical imperative.** *In words:* this is the conclusion that sets up [2.2](02-02-the-formula-of-universal-law.md), which asks what that imperative says.

## The case

Kant's Section I examples, sorted. The columns keep apart two questions a reader tends to merge: *is the act right?* and *what moved the agent?*

| Agent (Groundwork I) | In accordance with duty? | What moves them | Moral worth on Kant's account |
|---|---|---|---|
| **The prudent shopkeeper**: keeps a fixed price, so a child is served as honestly as anyone | Yes | Self-interest: honesty is good for trade | None. Right, but done from a selfish purpose |
| **The ordinary person who preserves their life** | Yes | Immediate inclination: they want to live | None. Kant says their maxim "has no moral import" |
| **The person weary of life** who preserves it anyway, wishing for death | Yes | Duty | Yes |
| **The sympathetic philanthropist**: takes pleasure in spreading joy | Yes | Immediate inclination: sympathy | None. The act deserves praise and encouragement, not esteem |
| **The same philanthropist, sympathy extinguished by his own sorrow**, who helps anyway | Yes | Duty | Yes. Only now, Kant says, does the action have "genuine moral worth" |

Three motives, then: *self-interest* (an indirect inclination, where the act is a means to something else you want), *immediate inclination* (you want the act itself), and *duty*. The shopkeeper is the easy case because his motive is visibly separate from the act. The philanthropist is the hard one, and Kant knows it: sympathy and duty point the same way, so you cannot tell from outside which is doing the work.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean): the hardware store.** A store owner lets a first-time buyer return a drill that failed on first use, a day after the return window closed, because the store's reputation for fairness is its main asset.

- *In accordance with duty?* Assume honest dealing with the customer is required, so yes.
- *Motive?* Reputation, which makes the act a means to profit. That is self-interest.
- *Moral worth?* None, on Kant's view, and *no blame* either. He does nothing wrong.
- *The maxim* is roughly "treat customers fairly when that protects the business." The test that exposes it is counterfactual: change the incentive and see whether the act survives. If the buyer were a tourist who would never return and never post a review, this maxim no longer recommends fairness. That is how you know it was never a maxim of honesty.
- *The imperative* behind it: "if you want repeat business, deal fairly." That is hypothetical, and assertoric, since it serves the owner's happiness.

**Example 2 (where it strains): the friend who helps gladly.** A friend drives you to the hospital at 3 a.m. She wants to help because she loves you. She also believes she ought to, and would have come even if she hadn't wanted to.

Read quickly, Section I seems to deny her act moral worth, because inclination is present. A verse jibe from the *Xenien* (1796), usually credited to Schiller, makes that reading sting. In paraphrase: *I gladly serve my friends, but I do it with inclination, so I worry I am not virtuous. The advice I get: try to despise them, then do with disgust what duty commands.* On this reading Kant ranks the cold helper above the loving one, which looks like the wrong way round.

The objection at full strength is not just "Kant dislikes feelings." Aristotle and much ordinary thought hold that a good person *enjoys* acting well, and that doing it grudgingly shows the virtue is incomplete ([4.2](04-02-aristotle-virtue-and-practical-wisdom.md)). If Kant's test of worth rewards the absence of that enjoyment, it has mistaken a symptom (you can see duty at work only when inclination is gone) for the thing itself.

The best-known defence is Barbara Herman's ("On the Value of Acting from the Motive of Duty", 1981, collected in *The Practice of Moral Judgment*, 1993). She is paraphrased here, and others such as Marcia Baron press similar readings.

- **The sorrowful philanthropist is a test for spotting duty, not an ideal.** Kant removes inclination from the example so that we can see duty working alone. He does not say the agent should remove it from their life. The friend's love does not cancel her act's worth. What matters is whether duty is what her helping *rests on*.
- **Duty can govern inclination rather than compete with it.** An agent can act *from* love while a standing commitment to duty sets the limits: she acts on that love only where acting is permissible, and would not do otherwise. On Herman's account that limiting role guarantees the act is right. Whether it also confers worth depends on whether duty is part of her actual reason. A plain overdetermination reading, where the act has worth whenever duty *would* have sufficed, is a view Herman criticizes.

The replies relocate the dispute; they do not end it. The critic can still ask why, if both motives are present, the moral credit goes to the one Kant approves of. Kant scholars broadly accept that the crude "no feelings allowed" reading is a misreading. Whether the corrected view keeps what the Aristotelian thinks is lost is still contested.

## Watch out

- **You might think Kant says acts done from inclination are wrong, but** he says they lack *moral worth*, which is a different question. The shopkeeper acts rightly. Worth concerns the will, and rightness concerns the act.
- **You might think an imperative is hypothetical if it contains "if" and categorical if it doesn't, but** the grammar settles nothing. "Don't lie to customers" is hypothetical when its only ground is repeat business. What matters is whether the imperative's force depends on an end the agent has. Philippa Foot ("Morality as a System of Hypothetical Imperatives", 1972) presses a further point. Etiquette and club rules are *also* non-hypothetical, since "members should wear jackets" applies whatever you want, yet nobody thinks they bind with reason's authority. So being non-hypothetical cannot by itself be what gives morality the special authority Kant claims for it. The question then becomes what does give it that authority, which Module 6 takes up ([6.3](06-03-error-theory.md)).
- **You might think Kant makes consequences irrelevant to deliberation, but** the good will is "the summoning of all means in our power." It must genuinely try to bring about its ends. What results cannot change is the will's *worth*, not what it aims at.
- **You might think "from duty" means following rules mechanically, but** the maxim is the agent's own principle, and acting from respect for law is supposed to be what rational autonomy *is* ([2.3](02-03-humanity-autonomy-and-the-lie.md)).

## One-liner

> Two people can do the same right thing, but only the one who does it because it is required, rather than because it pays or pleases, acts with moral worth. Morality's "ought" is supposed to bind you whatever you want.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢) *(Exegetical.)*** (a) A reader says the jewel passage in the Source section shows that Kant thinks outcomes don't matter to morality. Which words in the passage support that reading? Which words limit it? State exactly what the passage claims results cannot affect. Three sentences or fewer. (b) Classify each imperative as hypothetical-problematic (skill), hypothetical-assertoric (prudence), or categorical, with a reason of one clause each:
(i) "Bleed the radiator before you refill the system."
(ii) "Don't eat so late. You'll sleep badly and regret it."
(iii) "Return the money you were overpaid, even though no one would ever notice."
(iv) A sales manager to a new hire: "Never lie to a customer. Liars lose accounts."

**P2 (🟡) *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b)).*** (a) For each agent, say whether the act is in accordance with duty, what moves them (self-interest, immediate inclination, or duty), and whether it has moral worth on Kant's Section I account. One sentence each.
- **A.** A landlord returns a departing tenant's deposit in full because he knows a dispute would cost him more in legal fees.
- **B.** A hospice volunteer sits with a frightened patient through the night because she finds comforting people deeply satisfying.
- **C.** A man numbed by a recent bereavement, and indifferent to everyone, finds a wallet holding 300 dollars and mails it back to its owner. His only thought is that it isn't his to keep.

(b) **D.** A teacher stays late to tutor a struggling student. She is fond of him, and she also believes she owes it to him. Either reason alone would have kept her there. Say what Section I settles about D, where it stops giving a verdict, and how one defensible reading resolves it. 100 words or fewer; any reading passes if the moves are made.

**P3 (🔴, optional) *(Evaluative.)*** Steelman and reply. A critic says: "On Kant's view, a father who nurses his sick daughter out of love does something less morally worthy than a father who nurses her coldly, only because he must. Any theory that ranks them that way has misunderstood moral goodness." (a) State the objection at its strongest, naming the premise about moral worth that it attacks. (b) Give the best Kantian reply. (c) Say whether the reply fully answers the objection. 150 words or fewer; any verdict passes.

**P1** *(Exegetical, strict, both parts.)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** Supporting words: "wholly lack power to accomplish its purpose", "achieve nothing", and "Its usefulness or fruitlessness can neither add nor take away anything from this value." Limiting words: "(not, to be sure, a mere wish, but the summoning of all means in our power)". The good will must really try to bring about its ends. What the passage claims results cannot affect is the good will's **value** ("its whole value in itself"). It makes no claim that outcomes are irrelevant to what one should aim at or do.

**Must hit, strict (b):** (i) problematic/skill: the end, a working heating system, is one you may or may not have. (ii) assertoric/prudence: the end is your own well-being, which Kant says all humans actually seek. (iii) categorical: the command is given regardless of any end, and explicitly regardless of consequences to you. (iv) hypothetical and assertoric, though phrased without "if": its whole ground is a desired end, keeping accounts, which serves the hire's happiness. Accept "problematic" for (iv) only if the student argues that keeping accounts is an optional end rather than part of happiness, and gives the reason.

**Wrong turns:** In (a), reading "fruitlessness" as a claim about the moral *rightness* of acts. The passage concerns the will's value. Ignoring the parenthesis, which is the textual limit. In (b), calling (iv) categorical because it says "never" and contains no "if".

**Model answer:** (a) "Wholly lack power... achieve nothing" and "usefulness or fruitlessness can neither add nor take away" support the reader. But "not a mere wish, but the summoning of all means in our power" requires the will to aim seriously at results. So what results cannot touch is the good will's *value*, not the importance of trying to bring them about. (b) (i) skill; (ii) prudence; (iii) categorical; (iv) prudence, since the reason given is lost accounts.

**P2** *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)*

**Must hit, strict (a):**
- **A:** in accordance with duty; self-interest (avoiding fees); no moral worth, and not wrong. This is the shopkeeper structure.
- **B:** in accordance with duty (assuming comforting him is permissible and beneficent); immediate inclination; no moral worth on the Section I account, though praiseworthy. This is the sympathetic philanthropist.
- **C:** in accordance with duty; duty, since inclination is absent and he acts because it is required; moral worth. This is the sorrowful philanthropist and the weary man preserving his life.

**Must hit, any verdict (b):** (1) Section I settles that her act is right and that inclination is present. (2) It does not settle cases where duty and inclination are *both* sufficient, because Kant's examples remove inclination in order to show duty at work. (3) A reading that answers the question, e.g. Herman: worth depends on whether duty is part of what she actually acts on, so the fondness does not cancel it. Or the strict reading: worth only if duty alone moved her, which in a mixed case is unknowable. Or the counterfactual-sufficiency reading, flagged as one Herman criticizes.

**Wrong turns:** Calling B's act wrong, or blameworthy. Saying A has worth "because the deposit was returned", which confuses rightness with worth. Treating Kant's text as clearly denying D worth.

**Model answer (b), one of several:** Section I settles that D acts rightly and that inclination is among her motives. It is silent on overdetermined acts, because its examples strip inclination out specifically to make duty visible. On Herman's reading, worth turns on whether duty actually governs what she does, not on whether fondness is absent. If she would stay only where staying is permitted, and her reason includes that she owes it, the act has worth, fondness and all.

---

**P3** *(Evaluative; graded on the moves, not the verdict.)*

**Must hit, any verdict:**
- (a) The objection, stated strongly: the premise under attack is that *moral worth requires acting from duty rather than inclination*. Its strength is that a good person's emotions are *part of* their goodness, which is the Aristotelian point that the virtuous enjoy virtuous acts. So a theory that finds worth only in dutiful coldness misplaces goodness. Credit for naming Schiller's jibe as the historical form.
- (b) The best reply: the cold case is a *diagnostic* for when duty is visibly at work, not an ideal. Worth can belong to the loving father if duty governs his care (Herman's limiting-condition and primary-motive account). Also: Kant nowhere says the loving father acts wrongly or is less good as a person. Worth is a specific assessment.
- (c) A verdict with a reason. For example, whether the reply still gives the moral credit to duty rather than to the love, and whether that concedes what the objector cares about.

**Wrong turns:** Stating the objection as "Kant says love is bad", which is a strawman. Replying only "the loving father still acts rightly". That concedes the objection, which is about worth, not rightness. Giving (c) with no reason.

**Model answer, one of several:** (a) The objection targets the thesis that only acting from duty gives moral worth. At its strongest it says love is not a rival to goodness but part of it. The father who nurses gladly is *better*, as Aristotle held. (b) Kantian reply: the cold father is the case where duty is easy to see, not the ideal. If duty governs the loving father's care, so that he acts because it is owed and within its limits, his act has worth and his love takes nothing away. (c) The reply removes the ranking of coldness *above* love. It still places the source of worth in duty rather than in the love. An Aristotelian can grant everything else and deny exactly that, so the crux survives.

## Flashback

**From Lesson 1.3 (Objections I: justice and the separateness of persons):** The paragraph below is from an invented school-board memo. (a) Name the misreading of the separateness-of-persons objection that the memo's third sentence relies on. (b) The memo's last sentence answers one strand of the objection. Say which strand, and which strand the plan still faces. 150 words or fewer in total. *(Exegetical.)*

> "Closing Hollin Creek, our smallest school, will pay for new labs and smaller classes for 2,000 pupils in town. Its 40 pupils, already the district's poorest, will ride a bus 90 minutes each way to Riverside. Critics say the plan ignores the separateness of persons, but every pupil in the district, Hollin Creek's included, counted equally in our assessment. Nor did we simply add up gains: we weighted each pupil's gain or loss more heavily the worse off that pupil already is, and the plan still comes out ahead."

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):** the objection never said utilitarianism leaves anyone out. Everyone counts, equally. Its charge is about what happens *after* counting: individual gains and losses are merged into one total, so a burden on some is justified by gains to others, as though the district were one person balancing its books. Counting everyone equally is the step the objection grants, so it cannot be the reply.

**Must hit, strict (b):** the weighting is prioritarian (a unit counts for more the worse off its recipient), so it answers **distribution-insensitivity**: the poorest pupils' losses now count for more. It still *sums* across pupils, so it leaves the **sacrifice** strand standing. A serious burden on 40 pupils is accepted because weighted gains to 2,000 outweigh it, not because it has been shown to be justifiable to those 40.

**Wrong turns:** saying the weighting answers the whole objection; arguing about whether the closure is actually unjust, which is not asked; reading (a) as "the memo forgot to count Hollin Creek," when it says it counted them.

**Model answer:** (a) It treats the objection as the charge that some people go uncounted. The objection grants that everyone counts. Its target is the sum, which lets gains to some cancel losses to others as though they were one person. (b) The weighting answers distribution-insensitivity, since the poorest pupils' losses now count for more. But it still aggregates, so the sacrifice strand survives: the 40 bear a real burden because enough weighted gain elsewhere outweighs it, and nobody has shown it is justifiable to them.
