# Ethics · Lesson 1.5: Modern consequentialism

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 1: Consequentialism · Builds on: [1.3 Justice and the separateness of persons](01-03-justice-and-the-separateness-of-persons.md), [1.4 Integrity and demandingness](01-04-integrity-and-demandingness.md) · Unlocks: [2.1 The good will and acting from duty](02-01-the-good-will-and-acting-from-duty.md) (Module 2: Kantian deontology)

## Why this matters

Lessons 1.3 and 1.4 built the cases that make act utilitarianism look monstrous (the framed innocent), alienating (Jim) and crushing (the drowning child, every day). Almost nobody who calls themselves a consequentialist today defends the naive theory those cases hit. They defend a repaired version. This lesson asks whether the repairs work, or whether each one either quietly becomes act utilitarianism again or stops being consequentialist. The distinctions the repairs introduce also turn up in every rival theory: *criterion versus procedure*, *actual versus expected*, *rules versus acts*.

## The idea

Act utilitarianism packs three claims into one sentence. Consequences settle rightness. The consequences that count are those of *this act*. And the agent should decide by *calculating* them. The objections from 1.3–1.4 bite hardest on the second and third. So each modern version keeps the first claim and moves the consequences somewhere else.

- **Onto rules.** Evaluate codes, not acts. An act is right if the best *code* permits it. A code forbidding the framing of innocents does enormous good even if one framing, taken alone, would do a little.
- **Onto motives and decision procedures.** Keep the act-consequentialist *criterion* of rightness but deny it is a *method* for deciding. The best-motivated person, by consequentialist lights, may almost never calculate.
- **Onto expectations.** Rightness depends on the outcomes an act *can be expected* to have on the agent's evidence, not on what actually happens.
- **Off the binary.** Drop "right" and "wrong" as basic. Acts are only *better* or *worse*.

The test for each repair is the same pair of questions. Does it give better verdicts on the hard cases? And does it survive on consequentialist grounds, or has it quietly given those grounds up?

## The argument

**Rule consequentialism (Hooker's formulation, *Ideal Code, Real World*, 2000; building on Brandt).**

1. An act is wrong if and only if it is forbidden by the ideal code. *In words:* you don't weigh this act's effects; you ask what the best rulebook says.
2. The ideal code is the one whose *internalization* by the overwhelming majority of each new generation has the greatest expected value in well-being, with some priority to the worst off. Hooker uses a figure like 90 per cent. *In words:* picture nearly everyone raised to believe these rules and feel their pull, and ask which rulebook makes that world go best.
3. The code's value is counted *net of the costs of teaching it*. *In words:* a rule too complicated to learn, or too demanding to stick, is expensive, and that expense counts against it.
4. The code includes a rule to prevent disaster, even by breaking other rules. *In words:* the rulebook is not a suicide pact.

Hooker's case for the view is *not* that the good must be maximized. It is that this theory fits our considered moral convictions, after reflection, better than its rivals do, and gives them an impartial justification. That is reflective equilibrium, from [philosophical-method 3.4](../../philosophical-method/lessons/03-04-intuitions-and-reflective-equilibrium.md).

**The collapse objection (Lyons, *Forms and Limits of Utilitarianism*, 1965), aimed at compliance versions.**

- P1. Rule consequentialism selects the rules whose general compliance has the best consequences. *In words:* the best rulebook, if everyone follows it.
- P2. For any rule $R$ and any situation where breaking $R$ does more good, the rule "$R$, unless breaking it here does more good" performs at least as well under general compliance. *In words:* adding the exception can only help if everyone applies it correctly.
- P3. So the best code contains every such exception. *In words:* keep adding them.
- ∴ C. The best code requires exactly what act utilitarianism requires. *In words:* the rule theory and the act theory give the same verdicts on every act (they are *extensionally equivalent*).

The other horn, if the rule consequentialist refuses the exceptions, is J. J. C. Smart's charge of **rule worship** ("Extreme and Restricted Utilitarianism", 1956; *Utilitarianism: For and Against*, 1973). It is irrational to follow a rule you value only for its results in a case where you know breaking it gives better results.

**Sophisticated consequentialism (Railton, "Alienation, Consequentialism, and the Demands of Morality", 1984).**

1. *Objective* consequentialism: an act is right if and only if it would in fact produce the most good of the acts available. *In words:* this is the criterion.
2. *Subjective* consequentialism: whenever you choose, you should try to work out which act produces the most good and do it. *In words:* this is one possible decision procedure.
3. Objective consequentialism does not entail subjective consequentialism, and may condemn it, if calculating agents do less good than loving, loyal, rule-following ones. *In words:* the theory can tell you not to think in its own terms.

Railton's Juan visits his wife Linda, in a long-distance marriage, out of love, not out of a calculation. What makes him a sophisticated consequentialist is a *counterfactual condition*. If the relationship came to do serious harm to the world, his commitments would shift. Hare's two-level view (*Moral Thinking*, 1981) gives the same structure a psychology. The *intuitive* level runs on firm, well-chosen principles. The *critical* level selects those principles and settles their conflicts. Hare's "archangel" would need only critical thinking; his "prole" only intuitive. Actual people are some of each.

## The case

Frank Jackson's drug case ("Decision-Theoretic Consequentialism and the Nearest and Dearest Objection", 1991), with values assigned for this lesson: complete cure 100, partial cure 90, death 0. The doctor knows drug A gives a partial cure for certain. Of B and C, one cures completely and the other kills. Her evidence gives each an even chance of being the cure. The expected value of an act $a$ is $EV(a) = \sum_i p_i v_i$, where $p_i$ is the probability of outcome $i$ and $v_i$ its value. The formal machinery belongs to [`decision-theory`](../../decision-theory/syllabus.md).

| Drug | Outcomes (probability × value) | $EV$ | Actual-consequences view | Expected-value view |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | $1 \times 90$ | $90$ | Wrong: one of B or C would have done better | **Right** |
| B | $0.5 \times 100 + 0.5 \times 0$ | $50$ | Right if B is in fact the cure | Wrong |
| C | $0.5 \times 100 + 0.5 \times 0$ | $50$ | Right if C is in fact the cure | Wrong |

Nearly everyone judges that she should give A, and she *knows* A is not the act with the best actual outcome. So on the actual-consequences view the one act she can identify as a sensible choice is certain to be wrong. Jackson concluded that rightness is expectation-relative. Actualists reply by separating the *right* act from the *blameless* one: giving A is wrong but excused. The price of that reply is that the "right" act becomes something no agent in her position could ever knowingly perform.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean): the surgeon from 1.3.** A surgeon can kill one patient and use his organs to save five, undetected. Act utilitarianism, stipulating no leak, says do it. Hooker's rule consequentialism doesn't ask about this operation. It asks which code for surgeons, internalized by nearly everyone, does most good. A code permitting secret harvesting whenever the surgeon judges the numbers favourable fails at once. Surgeons misjudge, patients who know the code avoid hospitals, and the rule is easy to rationalize. A near-absolute prohibition wins, so the operation is wrong. The rule theory reaches the common verdict, and its reason is not "the numbers happened to go the other way this time." It is a reason about *practices*.

**Example 2 (hard): collapse, and Hooker's reply.** Run Lyons's argument on the surgeon. Why not "never harvest, unless harvesting here really would do more good"? Hooker's reply attacks P2, and does so because his view evaluates *internalization*, not compliance. A population that has internalized the exception-laden rule is not a population that applies it perfectly. It is one in which every surgeon is licensed to make the calculation, and every patient knows it. The assurance the prohibition provides is gone, and teaching the fine-grained exception costs something too. So the best *internalized* code is simpler than the best *complied-with* code, and the two theories really do come apart.

But now the second horn. In a case where the stipulations hold and the surgeon knows that breaking the rule does more good, rule consequentialism still says: don't. Smart calls that rule worship. Hooker's answer is that the charge assumes his theory rests on a commitment to maximizing, which it does not. It rests on fit with considered judgments. Critics then ask what is left that is *consequentialist*. If the foundation is reflective equilibrium, why not accept a pluralist deontology like Ross's ([5.3](05-03-pluralism-and-particularism.md)), which may fit the same judgments without the detour through ideal codes? Whether that question has a good answer is the crux of the whole programme.

**Two more exits, briefly.** Railton's sophisticated consequentialist gets through Williams's integrity objection from [1.4](01-04-integrity-and-demandingness.md) at the level of *motive*: Juan loves Linda without calculating. It does not help with the surgeon, since his criterion is still act-consequentialist, so the harvesting is still right even if he hopes no one has the disposition to do it. **Scalar consequentialism** (Norcross, "The Scalar Approach to Utilitarianism", 2006) dissolves demandingness differently. There is no line above which giving is "required" and below which it is "wrong", only more and less good. The cost is that a theory which never says "you must not" has stopped answering the question the objections in 1.3 were asking.

## Watch out

- **You might think every "indirect" consequentialist is a rule consequentialist, but** Railton and Hare keep an *act*-consequentialist criterion. What they change is the decision procedure and the motives. The surgeon counterexample still reaches them, and the collapse objection doesn't apply to them.
- **You might think the collapse objection refutes rule consequentialism, but** Lyons's argument targets *compliance* formulations. Against *internalization* formulations with teaching costs, P2 is exactly what is in dispute. Say which formulation you are attacking.
- **You might think the expected-value view makes rightness depend on what the agent happens to believe, but** it is relative to the *evidence* available. A doctor who ignores the chart doesn't get to count her careless guess as the probability.
- **You might think scalar consequentialism says anything goes, but** it ranks every option. What it gives up is a *threshold* of requirement, not the difference between better and worse.

## One-liner

> Every modern consequentialism keeps "outcomes matter" and moves them onto rules, motives or expectations, and each has to show that the move is still justified by outcomes.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢) *(Formal (a), (c) · Exegetical (b).)*** A variant of Jackson's case. Drug A certainly gives a partial cure, worth 60. Of B and C, exactly one cures completely (100) and the other kills (0). The doctor's evidence gives B a 0.7 chance of being the cure. (a) Compute $EV$ for A, B and C. (b) She gives B and the patient dies. What does the expected-value view say about her act, and what does the actual-consequences view say? One sentence each. (c) Holding the other values fixed, above what probability $p$ that B is the cure does B have higher expected value than A?

**P2 (🟡) *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)*** You promised to help your neighbour move on Saturday. A better use of the day turns up. Stipulate that, counting every effect (including his disappointment and any loss of trust), breaking the promise produces slightly more well-being than keeping it, and that nothing like a disaster is at stake. (a) State what act utilitarianism and Hooker's rule consequentialism each say, and state the collapse objection as it applies to the promise rule. (b) Does Hooker's internalization reply avoid collapse here *without* falling onto the rule-worship horn? Any verdict. 150 words or fewer for (b).

**P3 (🔴, optional) *(Exegetical.)*** Find the crux. Position H: rule consequentialism is to be accepted because its verdicts best fit our considered convictions and give them an impartial justification. Position A: what ultimately matters morally is well-being, so a rule has value only as a means to it, and following a rule when you know breaking it does more good is irrational. Name the single premise that one side must deny, and say what argument or evidence would move each side off its position. 150 words or fewer.

<details>
<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Formal (a), (c) · Exegetical (b).)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** $EV(A) = 1 \times 60 = 60$. $EV(B) = 0.7 \times 100 + 0.3 \times 0 = 70$. Since exactly one of B and C cures, $P(\text{C cures}) = 1 - 0.7 = 0.3$, so $EV(C) = 0.3 \times 100 + 0.7 \times 0 = 30$. Check: B and C's expected values sum to 100, as they must when exactly one of them cures.
**Must hit, strict (b):** the expected-value view says giving B was *right*, because it had the highest expected value on her evidence ($70 > 60 > 30$), and the bad outcome doesn't change that. The actual-consequences view says giving B was *wrong*, because C, which in fact was the cure, would have produced 100. The best an actualist can add is that she is blameless.
**Must hit, strict (c):** $EV(B) = 100p + 0 \cdot (1-p) = 100p$. We need $100p > 60$, so $p > 0.6$.
**Wrong turns:** giving C the same 0.7 chance, forgetting that the two probabilities are linked; saying the actual view counts A as right because it was "safe". The actual view ranks only what happened or would have happened, and A's 60 is beaten by whichever drug was the cure.
**Model answer:** (a) 60, 70, 30. (b) Expected-value view: right, since it maximized expected value on her evidence. Actual view: wrong, since C would have cured, though she is not to blame. (c) $p > 0.6$.

---

**P2** *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** act utilitarianism says break the promise, since that act has the best consequences by stipulation. Hooker's rule consequentialism says keep it: the ideal code contains a promise-keeping rule whose exceptions cover disaster and serious harm, and this case triggers neither. Collapse: the amended rule "keep promises unless breaking does more good" would, under perfect general compliance, do at least as well as the plain rule. So by rule consequentialism's own criterion it belongs in the code, and then the code says break, just as act utilitarianism does.
**Must hit, any verdict (b):**
- State the reply precisely. Evaluating *internalization*: a population that has internalized "unless breaking does more good" cannot rely on promises. That destroys the assurance promising exists to provide, invites self-serving miscalculation, and costs something to teach. So the amended rule does *worse* than the plain one.
- Say whether the reply generalizes or only moves the line. The code already allows exceptions for disaster and serious harm, so where exactly the exceptions stop has to be defended.
- Face the second horn. In *this* case you keep a promise you know is worse to keep. Either accept Hooker's claim that the theory rests on fit with considered judgments, not on maximizing, or press what that concession costs the theory's claim to be consequentialist.
**Wrong turns:** reading rule consequentialism as asking about the consequences of *this* promise-breaking; running the collapse argument against internalization without noticing that P2 is exactly what the internalization reply denies; changing the stipulated numbers ("but the loss of trust would make it worse"). The stipulation already counts that loss.
**Model answer (b), one of several acceptable:** The internalization reply blocks collapse. A code that licenses breaking promises whenever the breaker judges it better, internalized by real people, removes the reliability that makes promises worth having, so its expected value is lower than the plain rule's, and the two theories really do diverge here. But this doesn't dispose of rule worship: I know that keeping *this* promise is worse, and the theory still tells me to keep it. Hooker's answer, that his view is justified by fit with considered judgments and not by a maximizing ideal, is consistent. It does, though, make the consequentialist structure look optional: a Rossian duty of fidelity fits the same verdict directly. So collapse is avoided, but at the cost of the theory's claim that outcomes are the ground of rightness.

---

**P3** *(Exegetical.)*

**Must hit, strict:**
- **The crux:** whether a moral theory's authority comes from a foundational value claim that well-being is what ultimately matters and is to be promoted, or from coherence with considered judgments. A asserts the foundational premise and uses it to judge every rule-following act. H denies that rule consequentialism is justified *by* that premise.
- **What would move A:** showing that the maximizing premise is not itself self-evident, and that its verdicts on 1.3's cases (framing the innocent, the surgeon) are real evidence against it. That is, showing that A's foundation has to answer to considered judgments too.
- **What would move H:** showing that a non-consequentialist theory, such as Ross's pluralism, fits the same considered judgments at least as well. Then fit gives no reason to prefer *rule consequentialism*, and H's choice of that theory rested on the maximizing idea after all.
**Wrong turns:** naming "whether rules matter" as the crux, since both agree rules are useful; naming the collapse objection, which is a symptom of the disagreement, not the premise at issue; taking a side on who wins.
**Model answer:** The crux is whether consequentialism's justification comes from a foundational premise that well-being is to be promoted, or from fit with considered judgments. A holds the premise and so treats any rule as a mere instrument. H denies that rule consequentialism rests on it. A would move if shown that the premise must itself answer to considered judgments, which the cases in 1.3 contradict. H would move if a non-consequentialist theory, like Ross's, fit the same judgments equally well, since then fit alone would not favour rule consequentialism.

## Flashback

**From Lesson 1.2 (What is good for a person?):** Maren, a local historian, spends her last decade writing a book she hopes will change how her region remembers a forgotten village. She dies believing it failed. Twenty years later it succeeds, and she never learns of it. (a) *(Exegetical.)* Did the posthumous success make Maren's life go better for her, according to hedonism, an informed-desire theory, and an objective-list theory that includes achievement? One sentence each. (b) *(Evaluative.)* Is it plausible that something a person never experiences can affect how well her life went? Any verdict; 3 sentences or fewer.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):**
- **Hedonism:** no. Well-being is pleasure and the absence of pain, and nothing in her experience changed.
- **Informed-desire theory:** yes, on the standard unrestricted version, since her desire that the book succeed is satisfied whether or not she knows it. Credit also for noting that versions restricted to desires about one's own experiences say no.
- **Objective list with achievement:** yes, if achievement counts regardless of awareness. The book's success is her accomplishment. Credit for noting that some list theorists require that the goods be recognized or endorsed by the person.
**Must hit, any verdict (b):** connect the case to the experience machine from 1.2. That thought experiment pushes against hedonism by suggesting we care about how things actually are, not only how they seem, and Maren's case pushes the same way. Then face the cost on the other side: when did the benefit occur, and can someone who no longer exists be benefited?
**Wrong turns:** saying the desire theory says no "because she's dead", which confuses the satisfaction of a desire with the experience of satisfaction; treating (b) as settled by (a).
**Model answer (b), one of several acceptable:** It seems plausible: if we would refuse the experience machine because we want our projects really to succeed, then Maren's real success matters to her life, not just to the region. The cost is a puzzle about timing, because the benefit arrives twenty years after the life it improves. A hedonist can reply that this intuition is really about the value of her work to others, not about her well-being.
