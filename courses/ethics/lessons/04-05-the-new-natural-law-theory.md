# Ethics · Lesson 4.5: Natural law II: the new natural law theory

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 4: Virtue and natural law · Builds on: [4.4 Natural law I: Aquinas](04-04-natural-law-aquinas.md) · Unlocks: [5.1 Contractarianism: morality as mutual advantage](05-01-contractarianism-morality-as-mutual-advantage.md)

## Why this matters

[4.4](04-04-natural-law-aquinas.md) left natural law with a question it cannot dodge. If the precepts follow the order of our natural inclinations, is natural law just reading *oughts* off facts about human nature? Since Hume, many philosophers have taken that inference to be the textbook mistake. In 1965 Germain Grisez answered no: read properly, Aquinas never makes that inference. John Finnis built that reading into a full theory in 1980. The result is the most influential natural law theory in contemporary philosophy and jurisprudence, and it has been attacked hardest by fellow Thomists. Their quarrel is the cleanest case in ethics of one question: can you know what is good for humans without first knowing what humans are?

## The idea

Take two ways of learning that friendship is good.

**Route one.** Study what a human being is: a rational animal whose nature is fulfilled in shared life. Conclude that friendship perfects that nature, and so is good. Knowledge of the good *comes after* knowledge of the nature.

**Route two.** Don't start with a theory of human nature. Reflect on your own reaching toward other people, and you *understand*, directly and practically, that friendship is worth having for its own sake. The understanding is not proved from anything. It is a starting point, the way "the whole is greater than its part" is a starting point in theoretical reasoning.

The **new natural law theory** (NNL: Grisez, Finnis, Joseph Boyle) takes route two. Its **Thomistic naturalist** critics (Russell Hittinger, Ralph McInerny, Henry Veatch, Anthony Lisska) take route one, or insist that route two quietly depends on it.

Route two carries two further theses. First, the basic goods are a *plurality*: no one good is the master good that the others serve. Second, they are **incommensurable**: there is no common unit in which a quantity of knowledge trades against a quantity of friendship. That second thesis is what turns NNL into a rival of consequentialism rather than a footnote to Aquinas. You cannot "maximize the good" if goods have no common measure.

## Source

Hume, *A Treatise of Human Nature* III.i.1 (1739–40):

> In every system of morality, which I have hitherto met with, I have always remark'd, that the author proceeds for some time in the ordinary way of reasoning, and establishes the being of a God, or makes observations concerning human affairs; when of a sudden I am surpriz'd to find, that instead of the usual copulations of propositions, *is*, and *is not*, I meet with no proposition that is not connected with an *ought*, or an *ought not*. This change is imperceptible; but is, however, of the last consequence. For as this *ought*, or *ought not*, expresses some new relation or affirmation, 'tis necessary that it shou'd be observ'd and explain'd; and at the same time that a reason should be given, for what seems altogether inconceivable, how this new relation can be a deduction from others, which are entirely different from it.

Read the demand before the verdict. Hume says the *ought* expresses something "new". He says an explanation is owed. And he says a deduction from premises "entirely different" *seems* inconceivable. Whether that amounts to a proof that no such deduction exists is itself disputed (P1).

## The argument

**The new natural law reply to Hume** (Grisez 1965; Finnis, *Natural Law and Natural Rights*, 1980, ch. II–IV).

1. **No valid inference runs from purely theoretical premises to a practical conclusion.** *In words:* NNL accepts Hume's logical point. Facts alone, however rich, do not entail an *ought*.
2. **Aquinas's first practical principles are not inferred at all.** In ST I-II q.94 a.2 he says the precepts of natural law stand to practical reason as the first principles of demonstration stand to speculative reason: both are self-evident (*per se nota*). *In words:* on Aquinas's own account the starting points are not derived from anything, facts about nature included.
3. **The basic goods are grasped by practical understanding.** Knowing our inclinations supplies the data. The insight that life, knowledge or friendship is *to be pursued* is a fresh act of understanding, not a conclusion from those data. *In words:* you notice that you want to know; you *see* that knowing is worthwhile. The second is not deduced from the first.
4. **Finnis's 1980 list has seven basic goods:** life, knowledge, play, aesthetic experience, sociability (friendship), practical reasonableness, and religion. Later statements add marriage. *In words:* these are the irreducible reasons for acting, and every intelligible action pursues at least one of them.
5. **The basic goods are incommensurable.** None is a means to another, and none is simply more of what another is. *In words:* no scale weighs them, so "do whatever produces the most good" has no content.
6. **Morality comes from the requirements of practical reasonableness.** Finnis lists these as requirements (among them no arbitrary preferences among goods or among persons, and respect for every basic value in every act). Grisez lists them as *modes of responsibility*. The strictest: never choose to destroy, damage or impede an instance of a basic good. *In words:* you may pursue one good at the cost of another as a side effect, but never target a good as your end or your means.
7. **So** a natural law ethic can be built without deriving *ought* from *is*. *In words:* on this view Hume's gap marks a mistake Aquinas never made.

**The neo-Thomist counter** (Hittinger, *A Critique of the New Natural Law Theory*, 1987; McInerny, *Aquinas on Human Action*, 1992; Lisska, *Aquinas's Theory of Natural Law*, 1996).

1. **For Aquinas, "good" means what perfects a thing according to its nature.** *In words:* goodness is a fact about fulfillment, not a separate practical quality.
2. **So what is good *for humans* depends on what humans are.** *In words:* change the nature and you change the goods.
3. **A theory that cannot say why these goods and not others has no ground for its list.** *In words:* without a philosophy of nature, "self-evident" hides the work instead of doing it.
4. **So** NNL misreads Aquinas and cuts ethics off from metaphysics. For its part, Hume's gap assumes a fact–value split that Aristotelian teleology never accepted.

**The NNL reply.** Nature is *ontologically* prior: the goods are good *because* of what we are. It is not *epistemically* prior: we do not have to know that first in order to know the goods. Finnis grants the first and denies the second.

**The crux.** Is our *knowledge* of what is good for humans derived from our knowledge of human nature? Both sides agree that human nature is what *makes* the goods good.

## Map of positions

| | Thomistic naturalism | New natural law | Humean |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Is–ought** | The gap is an artifact of a nature stripped of ends. For a teleological nature, "fulfills its nature" is already evaluative | Accepts the logical gap. Denies that natural law ever needed to cross it | No *ought* is deducible from *is*. Moral distinctions are not perceived by reason alone (III.i.1) |
| **How goods are known** | Through knowledge of human nature and its ends (dispositions, perfection) | Self-evident to practical understanding, with inclinations as data rather than premises | Through sentiment: approval felt from a general point of view (III.iii.1) |
| **Commensurability** | Goods are ordered to a final end, so there is a hierarchy though not a numerical scale | Incommensurable basic goods with no hierarchy among them. Each can reasonably be treated as most important in its turn | No rational metric needed. Weighing is done by sentiment |

What separates the two natural law columns is the *order of knowing*. They agree on what is ultimately *real*.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean case: a free Saturday).** Maya can spend Saturday on a hard translation of Dante or on a pickup football game with old friends.

- *NNL:* knowledge sits on one side; play and friendship sit on the other. Every option instantiates basic goods, and they are incommensurable, so reason does not name one option as *the* best. The requirements rule some things out: she can't break a promise to the team, and she can't abandon a translation she committed to. Beyond that, both choices are reasonable. Finnis treats this underdetermination as a feature: much of a moral life is choice among options that are all reasonable.
- *Thomistic naturalist:* the goods are ordered toward a single final end, so in principle there is a better answer, though prudence may not detect it in a case this small.
- *Humean:* whichever she does, the question is what an impartial spectator would approve. Most would approve either.

The theories diverge in *structure* even where their verdicts converge: permission among equals, a hidden ranking, or approval.

**Example 2 (hard case: the courier's capsule).** A resistance courier is captured. She knows that under torture she will reveal names, and she carries a capsule that will kill her.

- *NNL:* taking it means choosing her own death as the *means* of keeping the names secret. That is choosing to destroy an instance of the good of life, and the mode of responsibility forbids it without exception. The lives saved can't be weighed against hers, because incommensurability rules out the weighing. A variant would be permitted: a courier who swallows the paper list, foreseeing she may choke, does not intend her death.
- *Where it strains.* Many readers judge the courier admirable, not wrong. That leaves NNL two options. It can hold firm against the intuition. Or it can argue that her act is really "preventing disclosure" with death beside the intention, and then it needs a principled account of how narrowly intention may be described (compare [3.2](03-02-the-doctrine-of-double-effect.md)). A Thomist critic presses a different point: even if the verdict is right, NNL owes an explanation of *why* life is a good that may never be targeted. "It is self-evident" answers that for the good but not for the absoluteness.
- *Thomistic naturalist:* reaches the same prohibition through a different route: the act turns against the natural inclination to preserve oneself, which q.94 a.2 lists first. The verdict can agree while the grounding differs.

## Watch out

- **You might think NNL denies that human nature matters, but** Finnis explicitly accepts that the goods are good because of what humans are. What he denies is that we must *know* the metaphysics before we can know the goods. Conflating ontological and epistemic priority is the commonest misreport of the debate.
- **You might think incommensurability means we can never rank options, but** NNL allows rankings that come from commitments, promises, fairness and one's plan of life. What it denies is a *pre-moral* metric that would settle a choice by adding up goods. "Life matters more than a game" can still be true for a given agent's choice. It is not a conversion rate.
- **You might think the neo-Thomists are defending the inference Hume attacked, but** most deny that the gap exists in the form Hume assumes. If "good" means "what perfects a nature," then a factual description of perfection is not "entirely different" from an evaluation.
- **You might think self-evident means obvious to everyone, but** *per se nota* means knowable without a middle term *once the terms are understood*. Aquinas notes that some things are self-evident only to the wise. So disagreement about the list does not refute self-evidence by itself.

## One-liner

> Both natural law schools agree that human nature makes the goods good; they split over whether you must know the nature to know the goods, and that split decides how each meets Hume.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢) *(Exegetical.)*** Using the Hume passage in **Source**: (a) Quote the three words or short phrases that carry the claim that an *ought* cannot simply be read off an *is*, and say in one sentence what each contributes. (b) Does the passage *as quoted* assert that no *ought* can ever be derived from an *is*, or something weaker? Name the words that decide it. 100 words or fewer for (b).

**P2 (🟡) *(Exegetical.)*** A Thomistic naturalist and a new natural law theorist agree that knowledge is a basic human good and that it is good because humans are rational animals. (a) State the single premise one of them asserts and the other denies. (b) Say what each would count as a mistake in the other's account of how a child comes to know that learning is worthwhile. (c) Name one argument or piece of evidence that would move one side, and say which side it would move. 150 words or fewer in total.

**P3 (🔴, optional) *(Evaluative.)*** Build a counterexample to *one* of these NNL theses: (i) the basic goods are incommensurable; or (ii) one may never choose to destroy, damage or impede an instance of a basic good. Your case must survive the reply NNL is most likely to make: for (i), that the ranking comes from commitments or fairness rather than from a metric; for (ii), that the harm is a side effect rather than intended. State that reply, then say whether your case beats it. Any verdict. 150 words or fewer.

<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical — strict.)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** any three of: "new relation or affirmation" (the *ought* adds content the *is*-premises lack); "observ'd and explain'd" plus "a reason should be given" (the author owes a justification for the step); "deduction" (the step claims to be a logical derivation); "entirely different" (the premises and the conclusion are of different kinds, so nothing in the premises is there to be drawn out). "Imperceptible" earns partial credit: it shows the step slips in unnoticed, which is the charge of carelessness, not the gap itself.

**Must hit, strict (b):** as quoted, the passage is weaker than "no *ought* from *is*." It demands an explanation, and it says the deduction "*seems* altogether inconceivable." Those are the deciding words. A strong reading (Hume's next sentences say the point would "subvert all the vulgar systems of morality") is defensible from the wider context. What is graded is noticing that the quoted text itself stops at a demand plus an appearance.

**Wrong turns:** citing "is, and is not" as the gap, when it only names the copulas; saying Hume argues that moral claims are meaningless, which the passage does not say.

**Model answer (b):** Weaker. Hume says the *ought* "expresses some new relation," that "a reason should be given," and that a deduction from different premises "seems altogether inconceivable." *Seems* and *a reason should be given* stop short of a proof of impossibility. They issue a challenge. The strong slogan is a gloss, though one his surrounding remarks invite.

---

**P2** *(Exegetical — strict.)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** the disputed premise is epistemic: *knowledge that knowledge is good for humans is derived from (depends on prior) knowledge of human nature.* The Thomistic naturalist asserts it and NNL denies it. Both accept the ontological claim that the goodness is grounded in the nature, and a strong answer says so.

**Must hit, strict (b):** NNL says the naturalist confuses the *order of being* with the *order of knowing*. No child first learns a metaphysics of rational animality. The naturalist says the NNL "insight" is either a disguised grasp of what perfects a nature, or ungrounded. Either way it cannot explain why this good is on the list and some other good is not.

**Must hit (c):** a genuine mover, with the side named. Examples: textual work on whether Aquinas treats *per se nota* precepts as independent of knowledge of nature (moves whichever side's *exegesis* it cuts against); a case of someone who reliably grasps a basic good while holding a false theory of human nature (pressure on the naturalist); or a candidate "self-evident" good that NNL cannot exclude without appeal to nature (pressure on NNL).

**Wrong turns:** naming the ontological claim as the crux, when both sides accept it; making the crux "whether Hume is right," which is downstream of it.

**Model answer:** (a) Whether knowing that knowledge is good *depends on* first knowing human nature. The naturalist says yes, NNL says no, and both agree that the nature is what makes it good. (b) NNL: the naturalist mistakes the order of being for the order of knowing, since a child sees that learning is worthwhile without any metaphysics. Naturalist: that "seeing" is either implicit knowledge of what perfects a rational nature or a groundless intuition that can't fix the list. (c) A clear case of a good NNL counts as self-evident that it can only rule in or out by appeal to what humans are would move NNL. Evidence that people with wildly false views of human nature still reliably grasp the same goods would move the naturalist.

---

**P3** *(Evaluative — graded on construction, not on the verdict.)*

**Must hit, any verdict:**
- A case naming which thesis is targeted and which basic goods are involved.
- For (i): a choice where one option seems *rationally* better for anyone, without any commitment, promise or fairness consideration in play. For (ii): a choice where damaging a good is the *means*, not a side effect, and the choice still seems permissible.
- The NNL reply stated accurately, and applied to the case.
- An explicit judgment on whether the case survives the reply, with a reason.

**Wrong turns:** a (ii) case where the harm is plainly a side effect (burning books for heat in a siege: the destruction of knowledge is not the means); a (i) case where the ranking is supplied by a prior commitment, which NNL already accepts; treating "most people would choose X" as showing a metric exists.

**Model answer (one of several acceptable), thesis (ii):** A witness to an atrocity takes a drug to erase the memory, so that she can function. Erasing the knowledge *is* the means: the memory's loss is what relieves her. NNL reply: what is targeted is a traumatic state, not the good of knowledge, which is understanding worth having. And even if knowledge is damaged, the choice is forbidden, and the intuition should yield. Verdict: the first reply is strong if knowledge as a basic good is understanding rather than stored information. On that reading, the case targets a memory rather than knowledge and does not refute (ii). A version that erases something she needs to understand (who harmed her) blocks that reply and forces the second, harder one.

## Flashback

**From Lesson 4.1 (Aristotle: the human good):** (a) Run Aristotle's completeness test on *health*, and say where the result leaves health as a candidate for the chief good. (b) Nadia, a gifted and upright physician, is imprisoned for ten years by a regime she criticised: a solitary cell, no books, no patients, no letters. Her character holds throughout. Was she flourishing during those years? Give the Stoic answer, then Aristotle's answer and the concept that drives it, and say whether his position is stable. Two sentences for (a); 150 words or fewer for (b). *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)*

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):** health is chosen for itself *and* for the sake of other things, namely the activities it makes possible and, through them, flourishing. So, like honour and pleasure, it fails completeness and cannot be the chief good, though it can be a part or a condition of a flourishing life.

**Must hit, any verdict (b):**
- **Stoic:** yes. Virtue suffices for happiness, and hers never failed.
- **Aristotle:** not during those years. Flourishing is virtuous *activity*, not a state, and activity needs **external goods** as instruments: friends, resources, a field of action (NE I.8–10). The cell removes nearly all of them. But she is not *miserable*, since she does nothing base, and the verdict belongs to a **complete life**, so ten years do not settle her life as a whole.
- A judgment on whether that middle position is stable, with a reason.

**Wrong turns:** saying Aristotle counts her as flourishing because her character is intact, when a state of character without its exercise is the sleeper's case; saying Aristotle calls her miserable; making her lack of pleasure the deciding factor.

**Model answer, (b) one of several:** The Stoic says she flourished: virtue is enough, and hers held. Aristotle says no. Flourishing is virtuous activity, and activity needs external goods as instruments: patients to heal, friends to be just and generous toward, something to think with. The cell strips these away, so her excellence survives mostly as a state, like the sleeper's. Still, she is not miserable, since she never acts basely, and ten years are not a whole life. Is the middle stable? This case helps it. The Stoic must call nearly idle virtue a flourishing life, which strains the idea that virtue is something you *do*. Aristotle's price is that her flourishing lay partly in a regime's hands, and he pays it openly rather than sliding to either extreme.
