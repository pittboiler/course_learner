# Ethics · Lesson 5.2: Contractualism: what no one could reasonably reject

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 5: Contract and plurality · Builds on: [5.1 Contractarianism: morality as mutual advantage](05-01-contractarianism-morality-as-mutual-advantage.md), [1.3 Justice and the separateness of persons](01-03-justice-and-the-separateness-of-persons.md) · Unlocks: [5.3 Pluralism and particularism](05-03-pluralism-and-particularism.md)

## Why this matters

The Hobbesian contract of [5.1](05-01-contractarianism-morality-as-mutual-advantage.md) left the powerless outside morality, because they have nothing to bargain with. T. M. Scanlon's *What We Owe to Each Other* (1998) keeps the idea of agreement and throws out the bargaining: morality is what you could *justify to each person*, whatever their power. That one change produces the most developed rival to consequentialism in modern ethics. It also raises a question every theory has to face and most dodge: when you can help one person or many, do the numbers count, and why?

## The idea

Imagine proposing a rule for everyone's conduct, then walking round the room and asking each affected person, one at a time, whether they have a reasonable objection. Not "would you gain from this?" but "could you, reasonably, refuse to accept it?" A rule someone could reasonably refuse is out. An act is wrong when the rules that survive that walk forbid it.

Three features make this different from both rivals.

- **Against Hobbes:** the parties are not bargaining. What moves them is wanting to live on terms others could accept, so a person with no power still has full standing to object. Scanlon puts the motive of morality there, in the value of being able to justify yourself to others.
- **Against the utilitarian:** objections are *personal*. You compare what this rule would cost *one person* with what the alternative would cost *one person*. You never add up small costs across many people into one large complaint. That is the lesson's hinge, and [1.3](01-03-justice-and-the-separateness-of-persons.md)'s separateness-of-persons objection built into the structure of the test.
- **Against a pure welfare view:** a complaint need not be about well-being. You can object to a rule because it is unfair to you, strips you of control over your own life, or treats you as not responsible for your choices.

## The argument

**Scanlon's contractualist formula** (paraphrased from *What We Owe to Each Other*, ch. 4–5).

1. **The standard of wrongness.** An act is wrong if the circumstances are ones in which it would be disallowed by any set of principles for the general regulation of behaviour that no one could "reasonably reject" as a basis for informed, unforced general agreement. *In words:* wrong means ruled out by principles nobody could reasonably turn down.
2. **The comparison.** Whether an objection to principle $P$ is reasonable depends on the alternatives: a person's complaint against $P$ is weighed against the strongest complaint someone would have against each rival principle. *In words:* no rule is objection-free, so ask whose burden is heavier under each option.
3. **The individualist restriction.** Only the complaints of individuals, on their own behalf, count as grounds for rejection; complaints are not summed across persons. (The label is Parfit's.) *In words:* ten thousand small complaints are ten thousand small complaints, not one big one.
4. **Generic reasons.** Complaints are assessed from standpoints ("someone trapped in an accident," "a viewer of a broadcast"), not from facts about particular named people. *In words:* you ask what anyone in that position would have reason to say, since rules are for types of situation.
5. **Non-welfare reasons.** Grounds for rejection include fairness, responsibility, control and being treated as an equal, not only harm and benefit. *In words:* a rule can wrong you without making you worse off.
6. **Scope.** The formula characterizes "what we owe to each other," which Scanlon presents as a central part of morality rather than all of it. *In words:* the test covers our duties to other people, so other moral questions (animals, the environment) may need something else.

## The case

The number cases, run through three theories. "Same harm" means each person faces the same loss (say, death) unless you help them. Assume you can only help one side.

| Case | Utilitarian (sum the harms) | Taurek (no aggregation, no tie-break) | Scanlon (individualist restriction plus tie-break) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 v 1, same harm | Indifferent | Toss a coin | Toss a coin, or either is permissible |
| 1 v 5, same harm | Save the five: $5 > 1$ | Toss a coin | Save the five, by the tie-breaking argument |
| 1 severe harm v 1,000,000 trivial harms (the transmitter room) | Depends on the sum; with enough people, the many | Not his case; read as refusing to sum, the one | Help the one: no individual complaint comes close |
| 1 death v 1,000 paralyses | The many, easily | Not his case; read as refusing to sum, the one | Disputed: strictly the one; on "relevance" views, possibly the many |

Taurek's paper is about equal losses, so his column in the last two rows is an extrapolation. Scanlon's verdicts in rows 2 and 3 are his own. Row 4 is where contractualism is still under construction, and Example 2 ends there.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean: the transmitter room).**

Scanlon's case, lightly paraphrased. Jones has had an accident in the transmitter room of a television station during a World Cup match. Equipment has fallen on his arm and he is receiving extremely painful electrical shocks. Freeing him means switching off the transmitter for fifteen minutes. If you wait for the match to end, in an hour, he will be no worse injured, just in agony the whole time. A very large number of people are watching.

Run the test. Take two candidate principles.

- $P_{\text{wait}}$: you may leave someone in severe pain to avoid interrupting entertainment enjoyed by many.
- $P_{\text{rescue}}$: you must relieve such pain even at the cost of interrupting the entertainment.

The strongest complaint against $P_{\text{wait}}$ comes from Jones's standpoint: an hour of electrocution. The strongest complaint against $P_{\text{rescue}}$ comes from one viewer's standpoint: fifteen minutes of a match missed. Condition 3 forbids adding the viewers together, and *every* viewer's complaint is far weaker than Jones's. So Jones can reasonably reject $P_{\text{wait}}$ and no viewer can reasonably reject $P_{\text{rescue}}$. Verdict: switch it off.

A summing utilitarian has to say that at *some* audience size, the lost enjoyment outweighs Jones's hour, and waiting becomes right. Scanlon takes the transmitter room as a fixed point any acceptable theory must respect. Utilitarians reply either by biting the bullet or by doubting that trivial pleasures really sum like that.

**Example 2 (hard: when the numbers are equal harms).**

Now two rocks, one person on the first and five on the second, the tide rising, one boat. The same harm, death, faces everyone.

The individualist restriction seems to leave the one and each of the five with *equal* complaints, which suggests no reason to prefer the five. John Taurek ("Should the Numbers Count?", 1977) accepted exactly that. Each person's loss is a loss *to that person*; no one suffers five deaths. So give each person an equal chance and toss a coin.

Scanlon wants to save the five without summing. His **tie-breaking argument**, shown with one versus two. Put A alone, B and C together. A's claim and B's claim balance: each faces death. If you then treat the choice as a coin toss, you act exactly as you would if C were not there. C can reasonably reject a principle that makes his presence count for nothing. So you must save B and C. Repeat, and the numbers count, supposedly without ever adding complaints into a bigger one. F. M. Kamm gives a similar "balancing" argument (*Morality, Mortality*, vol. 1, 1993). Critics such as Michael Otsuka (2000) argue that the move smuggles aggregation back in: letting C's claim tip the balance *is* counting two against one.

Now the case that strains the restriction. One person will die unless you help him; a thousand others will each be permanently paralysed unless you help them. Death is the worse individual complaint, so the strict restriction says save the one, for any number of paralysed people. Many find that hard to accept. The best-known repair is a **relevance view**, developed by Kamm and later by Alex Voorhoeve and others, with Scanlon himself tentatively open to it. On this view harms that are *close enough* in seriousness to the worst harm may aggregate against it, and trivial ones may not. Paralysis would count against death; headaches never would. The repair's bill is the line. Someone has to say what "close enough" means, and chains of cases each only slightly worse than the last put pressure on any threshold.

## Watch out

- **You might think contractualism is contractarianism with a nicer name.** They differ on what grounds agreement. Gauthier's parties accept what advances their interests; Scanlon's accept what no one could reasonably reject. Hence the powerless are in by construction here and out by construction in [5.1](05-01-contractarianism-morality-as-mutual-advantage.md). Nor is the agreement actual. It is a test you run on principles, not a meeting anyone attended.
- **You might think the test explains why acts are wrong.** The redundancy objection, pressed in early reviews by Philip Pettit, Simon Blackburn and others, says a principle is reasonably rejectable *because* it permits wrong acts, so wrongness is doing the work and the contract is idle. Scanlon's reply is that the grounds for rejection are not wrongness but ordinary reasons such as pain, unfairness and loss of control. Wrongness *consists in* failing justifiability built from those reasons. Whether that reply succeeds is a live dispute.
- **You might think "reasonable" means "rational."** A principle can serve my interests perfectly and still be one I could not reasonably insist on, because it asks too much of you. Reasonableness is a moral notion already, which is exactly what the redundancy objection seizes on.
- **You might think refusing to aggregate means ignoring numbers.** It does in Taurek. In Scanlon, numbers break ties between comparable complaints. What the restriction rules out is letting many *smaller* complaints outweigh one larger one.

## One-liner

> Contractualism asks whether you could justify a rule to each person separately, so a million small losses never outweigh one great one, and the whole fight is over whether the numbers can still count.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢) *(Exegetical.)*** One lifeboat can reach either a sandbar with one stranded swimmer or a reef with four. It can only make one trip before the tide covers both, and everyone left behind will drown. A utilitarian, a Taurekian and a Scanlonian advise you. (a) Give each adviser's verdict and the reason in one sentence. (b) Name the premise the Taurekian denies that the other two accept, and the premise the Scanlonian denies that the utilitarian accepts. 150 words or fewer.

**P2 (🟡) *(Formal (a) · Exegetical (b))*** A hospital has one shipment of a scarce drug. It can go to one patient, who will otherwise die, or be split among $n$ patients, each of whom will otherwise lose all sight in one eye. Stipulate welfare units: a death costs 40, loss of sight in one eye costs 2. (a) For $n = 50$, what does a summing utilitarian choose? What is the smallest $n$ at which the utilitarian *strictly* prefers the $n$ patients? (b) What does a Scanlonian who holds the strict individualist restriction choose at $n = 50$ and at $n = 5{,}000{,}000$? Say why the tie-breaking argument does not apply here. Two sentences for (b).

**P3 (🔴, optional) *(Evaluative.)*** (a) Build a case, different from any in this lesson, where the strict individualist restriction gives a verdict you judge wrong, *or* argue that the verdict is right despite appearances. (b) Say whether a relevance view repairs your case, and name the cost it pays for doing so. Any verdict; you are graded on the moves. 150 words or fewer.

<details>
<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical — strict.)*

**Must hit, strict (a):**
- **Utilitarian:** go to the reef, because four deaths are a worse outcome than one and you should minimize the total harm.
- **Taurekian:** toss a coin (or otherwise give each person an equal chance). Each loss is a loss to one person, no one suffers four deaths, so no group has a stronger claim.
- **Scanlonian:** go to the reef, by tie-breaking. The swimmer's claim balances one reef claim. A coin toss would treat the other three as if absent, which they can reasonably reject.

**Must hit, strict (b):**
- The Taurekian denies that the *number* of people with equal claims gives a reason to favour one group. Both others accept that it does.
- The Scanlonian denies that the reason is that harms *sum* across persons into a larger total. The utilitarian accepts this.

**Wrong turns:** saying the Scanlonian tosses a coin because of the individualist restriction. That is Taurek's position; tie-breaking is Scanlon's way out of it. Saying the Scanlonian and utilitarian disagree about the verdict here: they agree on the verdict and differ on the ground.

**Model answer:** (a) Utilitarian: the reef, since four deaths are worse than one. Taurekian: toss a coin, since each death is one person's loss and nobody suffers four. Scanlonian: the reef, since after balancing the swimmer against one reef-dweller, ignoring the other three fails to count their claims at all. (b) Taurek denies that numbers among equal claims matter to what you should do. Scanlon denies that they matter by summing harms into a total.

---

**P2** *(Formal (a) — strict · Exegetical (b) — strict.)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** at $n = 50$ the many cost $50 \times 2 = 100$ units, the one costs $40$. Since $100 > 40$, the utilitarian sends the drug to the fifty. Strict preference needs $2n > 40$, so $n > 20$. The smallest such $n$ is **21** ($42 > 40$). At $n = 20$ the two options tie ($40 = 40$).

**Must hit, strict (b):** at both $n = 50$ and $n = 5{,}000{,}000$ the strict Scanlonian gives the drug to the one. The strongest individual complaint is death, which exceeds any single person's loss of one eye's sight, and complaints may not be summed. Tie-breaking applies only when the complaints being balanced are comparable (equal), and here they are not, so there is no tie for the extra patients to break.

**Wrong turns:** answering 20 for the threshold, which is the tie, not strict preference. Letting the strict Scanlonian switch at some large $n$, which is a relevance view, not the strict restriction.

**Model answer (b):** At both 50 and five million the strict Scanlonian saves the one, because death is a stronger individual complaint than any single loss of an eye and the restriction forbids adding the eye losses together. The tie-breaking argument starts only after two *equal* claims balance, and no one-eye claim balances a claim against death.

---

**P3** *(Evaluative — graded on moves, not verdict.)*

**Must hit, any verdict:**
- A **new case** with one person facing a serious harm and many facing a lesser but non-trivial harm, stated precisely enough that the strict restriction clearly delivers a verdict.
- The **strict verdict** stated, with your judgment on it and a reason, or a defence of the verdict (e.g. that no individual is wronged by it).
- **Relevance view** applied: does the lesser harm count as relevant to the greater? Say whether the verdict changes.
- **The cost** named: where the threshold of relevance lies and why, or the problem of chains of slightly different harms (possible intransitivity), or the charge that this is aggregation after all.

**Wrong turns:** using a trivial harm (headaches, a missed match). The restriction's verdict there is the one Scanlon wants, so it is no counterexample. Naming "relevance" without applying it to your case.

**Model answer (one of several):** A bridge collapse traps one worker, who will lose both legs unless the crane frees him now. The same crane is needed immediately to stop floodwater that will permanently destroy the hearing of 300 residents. Strictly, losing both legs is the worse individual loss, so the crane goes to the worker whatever the number of residents. I judge that wrong at 300. On a relevance view, deafness is close enough to losing both legs to count against it, so the residents may be preferred. The cost is saying *why* deafness is relevant here when a sprained ankle is not. Any threshold faces a chain of harms, each barely lighter than the last. Either somewhere a tiny step flips relevance to irrelevance, or the chain lets trivial harms outweigh grave ones after all.

## Flashback

**From Lesson 4.3 (Modern virtue ethics):** Marisol coaches an under-14 basketball team and must cut one player from the squad for the regional final. Tariq has never missed a practice and is the weakest player on court. Ruben is far more skilled and has skipped half the practices. (a) Name the v-rules that bear on the choice, and say where they pull apart. (b) Marisol says: *"I'm not a virtuous agent, so 'do what a virtuous agent would do' tells me nothing."* Give Hursthouse's reply in full, and say whether it gets Marisol to a verdict in this case. 150 words or fewer for (b). *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)*

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):** at least three v-rules with their bearing. *Do what is just:* commitment earns a place, which favours Tariq, but fairness to the squad's shared effort toward the final can favour Ruben. *Do what is honest:* tell whichever boy is cut the real reason. *Do what is kind, not callous:* attend to a thirteen-year-old's dignity and development. The pull-apart point is that justice itself points two ways, toward rewarding commitment and toward the team's aim, while honesty and kindness mostly govern *how* she cuts, not *whom*.

**Must hit, any verdict (b):**
- All three parts of the reply: the v-rules give a great deal of guidance; where they conflict, ask people you reasonably regard as better than yourself; the rival theories also need judgment to apply.
- Apply them: say what the v-rules settle here and what they leave open.
- A verdict on whether the reply reaches a decision, or only makes the gap no worse than the rivals', with a reason.

**Wrong turns:** listing v-rules without saying where they conflict; asserting that "a virtuous coach would obviously keep Tariq" as though the criterion settled it; treating this as a situationism question, when the issue is guidance, not whether traits exist.

**Model answer (b), one of several:** Hursthouse makes three replies. The v-rules guide a lot; where they conflict, ask someone you reasonably judge wiser than yourself; and the rival theories need judgment too. Here the v-rules settle *how*: tell the cut player the true reason, face to face, with no pretence that it was close to random. They do not settle *who*. That turns on what an under-14 team is for, rewarding commitment or giving the squad its best chance, and no v-rule ranks those. An experienced coach she respects might see it at once, and asking one is good advice, but then the answer comes from that coach's judgment, not from the criterion. The parity point holds, though: "maximize welfare" needs the same judgment about what matters to thirteen-year-olds. The gap is real, but it is not special to virtue ethics.
