# Ethics · Lesson 3.3: Testing the principles on trolleys

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 3: Doing, allowing, and intending · Builds on: [3.1 Doing and allowing](03-01-doing-and-allowing.md) · [3.2 The doctrine of double effect](03-02-the-doctrine-of-double-effect.md) · [Philosophical Method 3.3 Thought experiments](../../philosophical-method/lessons/03-03-thought-experiments.md) · Unlocks: [4.1 Aristotle: the human good](04-01-aristotle-the-human-good.md)

## Why this matters

You already know the trolley family from [Philosophical Method 3.3](../../philosophical-method/lessons/03-03-thought-experiments.md): Switch, Footbridge, Loop, Transplant, and why Loop was built. That lesson used them to teach a method. This one uses them to put principles on trial. The doctrine of double effect, the doctrine of doing and allowing, and the Kantian ban on using people each claim to explain why diverting the trolley differs from pushing the man. They cannot all be the explanation, because on a few carefully built cases they give different verdicts. Those cases are where the principles can be tested.

## The idea

Most readers accept two fixed points: turning the trolley in Switch is permissible, and pushing the man in Footbridge is not. The question is **what does the work**. There are four families of answer.

**Intention.** Double effect ([3.2](03-02-the-doctrine-of-double-effect.md)): in Switch the one's death is foreseen but not intended, since an empty side track would serve you just as well. In Footbridge his being hit is the means you intend.

**Agency.** Foot (1967) explained her original case with negative and positive duties ([3.1](03-01-doing-and-allowing.md)). Her agent was the *driver*. His choice is between killing five and killing one, two negative duties, so he should kill fewer. The surgeon in Transplant chooses between killing one and letting five die, and the negative duty wins. Thomson (1985) replied by moving the agent off the trolley. A *bystander* at the switch chooses between killing one and letting five die, exactly the surgeon's structure, yet most readers still let him turn.

**Using.** The *means principle*: it is especially hard to justify harming someone by making his involvement serve your purpose. Kant's Formula of Humanity ([2.3](02-03-humanity-autonomy-and-the-lie.md)) is its ancestor. Quinn (1989) gave it a precise form: *harmful direct agency*, where the victim is harmed because you deliberately involve him in order to further your goal by way of that involvement. The means principle looks at the victim's role in the plan, not only at the agent's state of mind.

**Something else.** Thomson (1985) proposed that the bystander may turn because he makes an *existing* threat threaten fewer people, by means that do not themselves infringe anyone's rights. She described this as something like a distributive exemption. Pushing the man infringes his rights in the act itself. Kamm (*Intricate Ethics*, 2007) refines double effect instead. And a deflationary camp says intention was never about permissibility at all.

Loop is where these part company.

## The argument

**Thomson's Loop argument against double effect (1985).**

1. If double effect's intention condition explains the Switch/Footbridge contrast, then turning is impermissible wherever the one's being hit is intended as a means. *In words:* the explanation must hold in every case, not only the two it was built for.
2. In Loop the side track rejoins the main line, so turning saves the five only because the trolley hits the one. *In words:* he is a means, just as in Footbridge.
3. Loop differs from Switch only by an extra piece of track, and a piece of track cannot make a major moral difference. *In words:* if Switch is fine, Loop is fine.
4. So turning in Loop is permissible even though the hit is a means. *In words:* here using someone does not rule out the act.
5. ∴ The intention condition does not explain the contrast. *In words:* whatever separates Switch from Footbridge, it is not intending the harm as a means.

A defender can deny premise 3, and many lay readers do: they judge Loop impermissible. Or she can deny premise 1's inference from "is a means" to "is intended". That is Kamm's move.

**Kamm's Doctrine of Triple Effect (as reconstructed here).**

1. Acting *in order to* bring about an effect differs from acting *because* you believe the effect will occur. *In words:* an effect can be your reason for acting without being your aim.
2. In Loop you turn in order to save the five from the oncoming trolley. The one's being hit is what makes turning *worth doing*, since without it the trolley comes back, but you do not aim at it. *In words:* you count on his being hit without aiming at it.
3. Double effect forbids aiming at harm, not counting on it. *In words:* only "in order to" is ruled out.
4. ∴ Loop is permissible, and Footbridge, where you push *in order that* he be hit, is not. *In words:* triple effect keeps the intention idea and gives Thomson her verdict.

**Thomson's recantation (2008).** In "Turning the Trolley", Thomson rejected her own starting point. Suppose the bystander has a third option: turn the trolley onto a track where he himself stands. She argued that if he is unwilling to pay that cost himself, he may not make the one pay it. Few would volunteer, so the bystander may not turn at all. The driver may still turn, because the driver otherwise kills the five. On this view Foot's explanation was right all along, and the "problem" came from a mistaken verdict.

## The case

Five cases, all with a bystander. **Loop-with-weight**: as Loop, but a heavy weight also sits on the loop behind the one, enough to stop the trolley alone. The one is hit and dies, but his being hit is not needed. **Trapdoor**: the one stands on a trapdoor over the main line, and a lever drops him in front of the trolley. That is Footbridge without contact.

| Case | Double effect | Doing/allowing (Foot) | Means principle | Triple effect | Thomson 1985 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Switch | Permissible | Kills one to avoid letting five die: forbidden | Permissible | Permissible | Permissible |
| Loop | Impermissible on the standard reading (hit intended as means); **disputed** | Same as Switch | Impermissible (direct agency); **disputed** | Permissible | Permissible |
| Loop-with-weight | Permissible | Same as Switch | Permissible | Permissible | Permissible |
| Footbridge | Impermissible | Same as Switch | Impermissible | Impermissible | Impermissible |
| Trapdoor | Impermissible | Same as Switch | Impermissible | Impermissible | Impermissible |

Read the Doing/allowing column carefully. For a bystander, every row is a killing to prevent a letting-die, so the doctrine gives one verdict everywhere. It can separate the cases only if the agent changes (driver versus bystander), never the track. "Disputed" marks real splits. Some double-effect theorists say you intend only the trolley's contact with the one, not his death, and that raises the closeness problem from 3.2. And whether Loop is *direct* agency on Quinn's definition is argued both ways.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean): Loop against Loop-with-weight.** One factor changes: whether the one's being hit is needed. The track, the numbers and the death are the same. Double effect and the means principle both treat this factor as decisive, so they must say Loop is worse. Triple effect and Thomson (1985) treat it as idle, so they must say the two are alike. Now check your own verdicts. If they match, the case supports the second pair. If Loop feels worse, it supports the first. Either way, you have learned something no two-case contrast could show you. Trapdoor adds one more control. It removes physical contact and leaves every principle's verdict unchanged, so if Trapdoor feels better than Footbridge, the principles are not tracking whatever moved you.

**Example 2 (hard): the bad-intent turner.** A bystander turns the trolley in Switch, and his reason is that the one on the side track is his rival. Saving the five is cover. Everything else is identical. Read strictly as a condition on the agent's actual intention, double effect condemns the act. Thomson ("Self-Defense", 1991) and Scanlon (*Moral Dimensions*, 2008) argue that this is a confusion. Scanlon's illustration has this shape: two doctors give the same dose of painkiller to a dying patient. One aims to relieve pain. The other aims at the death the dose will also hasten. On his view the act is equally permissible for both. What differs is what the act shows about the agent, which bears on blame and on the *meaning* of the act. Permissibility, he says, depends on the considerations that could justify acting, not on which of them moved this agent.

This is where the principles strain. Accept the deflationary view and the intention family explains Switch/Footbridge only if it is recast in non-mental terms: the victim's causal role, which is roughly the means principle. Reject it and you must say why a hidden motive changes what the rival may resist or what a witness may prevent. Defenders of double effect have replies, for instance that intention shapes the act's *object*, so the two doctors perform different acts. The dispute is live.

## Watch out

- You might think Loop refutes double effect outright, but it does so only for readers who share Thomson's verdict in premise 3. Experimental work on Loop reports split and order-sensitive responses, and replication status varies. Premise 3 is contested, not given.
- You might think the means principle is just double effect under another name, but it can drop the agent's psychology entirely. A means principle stated in terms of the victim's role survives the Scanlon–Thomson objection, while an intention-based double effect has to answer it.
- You might think doing/allowing rules on the trolley cases, but for a bystander it classifies every variant alike. Only changing the *agent* (driver, surgeon, bystander) moves its verdict.
- You might think Thomson's 2008 view is fringe revisionism, but it is a principled cost-bearing argument. Its price is overriding a verdict most readers hold, and whether that price is acceptable is the evaluative question.

## One-liner

> Loop and its weight variant ask whether being *used* matters or only being *harmed*. Double effect, the means principle, triple effect and Thomson each answer differently, and no answer comes free.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢) *(Exegetical.)*** **Turntable.** Five people stand on a rotating platform in the trolley's path. You rotate the platform, which carries the five clear. Its swinging edge crushes one person standing beside it. Give the verdict of (i) double effect, (ii) the means principle, and (iii) Thomson's 1985 redirection account, each with a one-sentence reason. Then say which one parts company with the others, and why. 150 words or fewer.

**P2 (🟡) *(Exegetical.)*** A double-effect defender says Loop is impermissible. A triple-effect defender says it is permissible. (a) Name the single premise about intention that one of them must deny. (b) Describe a case outside the trolley family that would put pressure on that premise, and say which way it pushes. 150 words or fewer.

**P3 (🔴, optional) *(Evaluative.)*** Build a one-factor variant of **Loop-with-weight** on which intention-based double effect and the Scanlon–Thomson view (intention bears on blame, not permissibility) give different verdicts. Say which verdict you hold and what holding it costs. Any verdict passes; you are graded on the construction. 150 words or fewer.

<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical — strict.)*

**Must hit, strict:**
- **(i) Double effect: permissible.** The crushing is a foreseen side effect. The good end is moving the five, the platform's motion is not itself bad, and five against one is proportionate.
- **(ii) Means principle: permissible.** The one plays no role in saving the five. If he were absent, the rotation would work just as well.
- **(iii) Thomson 1985: no licence.** You do not redirect the existing threat, since the trolley still runs where it was going. You create a *new* threat, the swinging edge, to the one. Her exemption covers making an existing threat threaten fewer, so it does not cover this case.
- **Parts company:** (iii), because it tracks the *source* of the threat to the one, not his role in your plan.

**Wrong turns:** saying the means principle forbids it because "he dies so the five can be saved". He dies *as* they are saved, not *by way of* it. Calling (iii) permissible because the numbers improve.

**Model answer:** Double effect permits it, since the death is foreseen, not intended, and proportionate. The means principle permits it, since the one is not used and the rotation works without him. Thomson's 1985 account gives no licence, since the threat to the one is newly created rather than an existing threat redirected. So the redirection account is the odd one out: it cares where the threat comes from, and the other two care about his role.

---

**P2** *(Exegetical — strict.)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** the premise *if the effect's occurring is part of what makes the act worth doing, the agent intends that effect.* Double effect, on the standard reading, needs it to count the hit in Loop as intended. Triple effect denies it by separating "in order to" from "because".

**Must hit, strict (b):** a case where someone acts only because they expect an effect but plainly does not aim at it, and the recognition that such a case supports the denial. A case where the distinction feels empty supports the premise.

**Wrong turns:** naming the verdict on Loop as the crux; the crux is the premise that generates the verdict. Offering another trolley case, which just reruns the disputed intuition.

**Model answer:** (a) The premise is that whatever makes your act worth doing is something you intend. Double effect needs it; triple effect denies it. (b) I throw a party to enjoy myself, and I would not throw it unless I expected friends to stay and help clean up. Still, I do not throw the party *in order that* they clean. If that description sounds right, it pressures the premise and helps triple effect. If "because" collapses into "in order to" on reflection, the premise stands. (A case of this shape is associated with Kamm.)

---

**P3** *(Evaluative — graded on construction, not on the verdict.)*

**Must hit, any verdict:**
- The base is Loop-with-weight, and **exactly one** factor changes: the agent's intention. The causal structure stays fixed.
- Stated verdicts: strict double effect says impermissible, because the agent intends the one's death. The Scanlon–Thomson view says permissible, with the agent blameworthy.
- Your verdict, and its named cost.

**Wrong turns:** changing the track as well as the motive, for instance by removing the weight, so two factors move. Assuming double effect already has a "blame" category that settles the case.

**Model answer (one of several acceptable):** Variant: the same Loop-with-weight, but the bystander turns *so that* the one, his creditor, is killed, and he is glad the five are saved as cover. The weight still stops the trolley, and nothing causal changes. Double effect read as a test of actual intention says impermissible. Scanlon and Thomson say the act is permissible and the agent is contemptible. I hold the second verdict. Its cost: double effect must be restated in terms of the victim's causal role, not the agent's mind, so intention does no independent work in permissibility and the Switch/Footbridge contrast must be carried by that role alone.

## Flashback

**From Lesson 2.3 (Humanity, autonomy, and the lie):** A graduate applies for a job. To push the salary up, she tells the employer she holds a competing offer that does not exist. (a) Does she treat the employer as a means, and does she treat him *merely* as a means? Use the possible-consent reading of the Formula of Humanity. (b) Contrast an honest negotiation over the same salary. (c) In one sentence, say whether the one in Loop is treated merely as a means on this reading. Three sentences or fewer per part.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):** yes to both. Every hiring uses the other party as a means, which Kant permits. It becomes *mere* means because the employer could not consent to the way he is being used: the lie works only if he does not know the maxim, so consent to it is impossible in principle.

**Must hit, strict (b):** honest bargaining still uses the employer as a means, but he can know exactly what is happening and still agree. So it is not *mere* means. The formula forbids the deception, not the self-interest.

**Must hit (c):** a recognition that possible consent is hard to apply here. The one cannot consent to his being hit, and no deception is involved, so the reading turns on whether "could not consent" covers being harmed in someone's plan without deception. Accept either answer if that point is stated.

**Wrong turns:** saying any use of another for your own benefit violates the formula, which drops "merely". Testing *actual* consent, which the reading does not ask about.

**Model answer:** (a) She uses him as a means, and merely so: her lie succeeds only if he cannot see and share her maxim, so he could not consent to it. (b) Hard but honest bargaining uses him in a way he can know and accept, so it is permitted. (c) On possible consent, Loop is unclear, because it involves no deception, only a harm the one could not agree to. Whether that is enough to make him a mere means is exactly what the Kantian must settle.

</details>
