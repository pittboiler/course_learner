# Ethics · Lesson 1.2: What is good for a person?

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 1: Consequentialism · Builds on: [1.1 Classical utilitarianism](01-01-classical-utilitarianism.md) · Unlocks: [1.3 Objections I: justice and the separateness of persons](01-03-justice-and-the-separateness-of-persons.md)

## Why this matters

Lesson 1.1 split utilitarianism into a theory of the right (maximize) and a theory of the good (what gets maximized). Bentham and Mill filled the second slot with pleasure, but that was a separate choice, and it is the one this lesson opens up. The question is not only a utilitarian's problem. Every moral theory that tells you to benefit people, avoid harming them, or count their interests needs some account of what makes a life go well *for the one living it*. A Kantian's duty of beneficence and a natural lawyer's basic goods both assume an answer. Economics assumes one too, usually without saying so.

## The idea

Separate two questions. *Is this life good?* could mean admirable, useful or morally excellent. *Is this life good for the person?* asks only how well it is going for them. A saint who dies young for others may have lived a very good life that went badly for her. The theories here answer only the second question. Philosophers call their target **well-being** (or welfare, or prudential value).

Three families of answer, sorted by where they locate the good:

- **Hedonism: in your experience.** What is good for you is pleasure; what is bad for you is pain. Anything else matters only as a cause of these.
- **Desire-satisfaction: in your wants.** What is good for you is getting what you want. It depends on you, but not on your feelings: the want is satisfied when the world goes the way you wanted, whether or not you find out.
- **Objective list: in the world, whatever you think.** Some things are good for any human being whether or not they are enjoyed or wanted. Typical lists include knowledge, friendship, achievement, health and autonomy, and some add pleasure itself.

A quick test: imagine two lives identical from the inside. Hedonism must rank them equal. The other two can tell them apart.

## The argument

**The three theories, stated precisely.**

1. **Quantitative hedonism (Bentham).** How good a life is for a person is its total of pleasure minus pain, weighted by intensity and duration. The source does not matter: Bentham held, roughly, that a simple game gives as much value as poetry when the pleasure is equal (*The Rationale of Reward*, 1825). *In words:* add up how good it felt; nothing else counts.
2. **Qualitative hedonism (Mill, *Utilitarianism* ch. 2).** Pleasures differ in quality as well as quantity. One pleasure is higher than another if nearly everyone who has experienced both decidedly prefers it, even when it brings more discontent. Mill calls these people *competent judges*. His verdict: "It is better to be a human being dissatisfied than a pig satisfied; better to be Socrates dissatisfied than a fool satisfied." *In words:* only pleasure counts, but some kinds count for more, and experienced people are the test of which.
3. **Actual desire-satisfaction.** A person's life goes better as more of their desires are satisfied, weighted by how strongly they want them. *In words:* you are doing well if the world matches what you want.
4. **Informed (ideal) desire-satisfaction.** Only the desires you *would* have with full information and clear reasoning count. This version was developed by Brandt and Railton among others. *In words:* what is good for you is what a fully informed version of you would want for you.
5. **Objective list.** A set of goods whose presence makes a life go better for its subject, whether or not the subject enjoys or wants them. The label is Parfit's (*Reasons and Persons*, 1984, Appendix I). *In words:* some things are good for you whether or not you care about them.
6. **Hybrid views.** A good benefits you only when two conditions hold together: it is objectively worthwhile, and you are positively engaged with it (enjoying it, endorsing it, caring). Parfit floated such a view in the same appendix. *In words:* the good must be real and it must be yours.

**The experience machine against hedonism** (Nozick, *Anarchy, State, and Utopia*, 1974, ch. 3, paraphrased).

- **P1.** If hedonism is true, only the felt quality of your experiences affects how well your life goes for you. *In words:* this is just what hedonism says.
- **P2.** A machine could give you, for life, experiences exactly as good as or better than the best real life you could have. You would believe it was real, and you would never know otherwise. *In words:* stipulated, to hold the experiences fixed.
- **P3.** So if hedonism is true, plugging in makes your life go at least as well for you as living the real life. *In words:* from P1 and P2.
- **P4.** On reflection, the plugged-in life goes worse *for you*, not just worse morally or for others. *In words:* the verdict premise, and the one that carries the weight.
- **C.** Hedonism is false.

Nozick names three things we seem to want beyond experiences. We want to actually *do* things, not only to have the experience of doing them. We want to *be* a certain kind of person, where someone floating in a tank is no particular kind of person at all. And we want *contact with reality*, not a world that someone else built.

## Map of positions

| Test case | Hedonism | Actual desire | Informed desire | Objective list |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Experience machine**: a lifetime of simulated, better-than-real experience | As good or better than real life | Worse, if you want real achievement and real friends, since those wants are unmet though you believe them met | Worse, for the same reason, as long as full information keeps those wants | Worse: no real achievement, friendship or knowledge |
| **Deceived businessman** (a case pressed by Kagan and others): loved, respected, successful in his own eyes, but his family and colleagues secretly despise and cheat him | Equal to his undeceived twin | Worse | Worse | Worse: the friendships and achievement are not real |
| **Grass-counter** (Rawls, *A Theory of Justice*, 1971, sec. 65): a brilliant person whose only wish is to count blades of grass on lawns | Good, if he enjoys it | Good | Good, *if* the desire survives full information | Poor, unless counting counts as achievement or knowledge |
| **Adaptive preference** (Elster, *Sour Grapes*, 1983; Sen): someone denied schooling who has stopped wanting it and is content | Fine, if content | Fine | Probably not: fully informed, she would want what was denied | Deprived of knowledge and autonomy |

Read the columns, not the rows. The first two rows split hedonism from everything else, which is the work the experience machine is built to do. The last two split objective list from desire, and in the grass-counter row it is the informed-desire theorist who is left without a clear verdict.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (a clean case: the stranger on the train).**
Parfit's case (1984, Appendix I). You meet a stranger on a train who tells you he has a serious illness. You sincerely want him to recover. You part and never hear of him again. Years later, he is cured.

Run the theories. Hedonism says your life has gone no better, because nothing entered your experience. Objective list agrees, since nothing on the list changed. Actual desire-satisfaction says your life went better, because a desire of yours was satisfied. That verdict is plainly wrong, and it shows the theory needs a scope restriction. The usual repair counts only desires *about your own life*. Parfit, among others, noted how hard this is to state without circularity. "Desires about your own life" cannot mean "desires whose satisfaction is good for you," since that is the thing being defined. Note what the case does: it attacks the view's *range*, not its core.

**Example 2 (a hard case: the reversed machine).**
The hedonist can reply that P4 is contaminated. People may refuse to plug in for reasons unrelated to well-being: fear that the machine will break, a sense of duty to others, or plain status quo bias, meaning a preference for whatever you already have.

The test for the last worry is a reversal. Suppose you learn you are *already* in a machine and can unplug into a real life that is much less pleasant. Would you? De Brigard (2010) reported that many participants given reversed versions chose to stay, with the choice varying by what the real life was said to be like. The reading hedonists draw is that the original intuition tracks the status quo, not reality. Take this with caution: the samples were small, and philosophers dispute what the results show. An objective-list theorist can say the reversed case reintroduces a confound, since unplugging means losing a whole life of relationships you believe in. What matters is the structure. The experience machine is a thought experiment ([philosophical-method 3.3](../../philosophical-method/lessons/03-03-thought-experiments.md)), so the fight is over whether P4 survives controlling for the framing.

The objective-list theorist faces a problem too: **alienation**. Suppose knowledge is on the list, but a particular person, fully informed, does not care about some piece of knowledge at all. Is it still good *for her*? Railton (1986) pressed a line of thought along these lines: what is intrinsically good for someone must connect with what she would, under some conditions, find compelling or attractive. Hybrid views are built to answer this, and the cost is that they inherit part of the desire theory's subjectivity.

## Watch out

- **You might think "good for" and "good" are the same question, but** a theory of well-being ranks lives by how they go for their subjects. It says nothing about whether it is right to promote well-being, or whose well-being to count. That is the job of the theory of the right, which is where [1.3](01-03-justice-and-the-separateness-of-persons.md) picks up.
- **You might think Mill's higher pleasures abandon hedonism, but** Mill keeps pleasure as the only good and ranks its kinds. Whether quality can be ranked without appealing to some non-pleasure value is the pressure point critics since Sidgwick have pressed. The claim that Mill has already left hedonism is an evaluative conclusion, not a reading of the text.
- **You might think desire-satisfaction requires you to *feel* satisfied, but** satisfaction here is a match between the desire and the world. That is why the deceived businessman fares badly on it and why the stranger on the train is a problem for it.
- **You might think the objective list is arbitrary, but** its defenders offer a reason for what goes on it: the goods tied to human nature (Aristotle, [4.1](04-01-aristotle-the-human-good.md)) or goods self-evident to practical reason (the new natural law theorists, [4.5](04-05-the-new-natural-law-theory.md)). Whether those grounds work is a separate question.

## One-liner

> Well-being lives in your experiences, your wants or the world: the experience machine splits the first from the rest, and the grass-counter splits the last two.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢) *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b))*** Tomas spends forty years translating a medieval chronicle. The work is mostly tedious, and his days hold more frustration than pleasure. What he wants above all is to finish, and he does, a week before he dies, believing he has preserved a real record. Unknown to him, the manuscript was a nineteenth-century forgery. (a) Give the verdict on how well Tomas's life went for him according to quantitative hedonism, actual desire-satisfaction and an objective list containing knowledge and achievement, and name the feature of the case that decides each verdict. (b) Pick one of those theories and say exactly where, as stated, it stops determining a verdict here, and what it would need to settle one. 150 words or fewer for (b).

**P2 (🟡) *(Evaluative.)*** Build a case in which **informed** desire-satisfaction and an objective-list theory give opposite verdicts on whether something makes a person's life go better for them. Your case must survive the informed-desire theorist's standard escape, "with full information she would want it." Then say which verdict you accept and what the other theory's defender would say in reply. Any verdict passes; you are graded on the construction. 150 words or fewer.

**P3 (🔴, optional) *(Exegetical.)*** A hedonist and an objective-list theorist disagree about the experience machine. (a) Using P1–P4 of the lesson's reconstruction, name the premise the hedonist must deny, and name the further claim the objective-list theorist rejects that the hedonist accepts. (b) Describe one variant case that, if its verdict came out a certain way, should move the hedonist, and say which way it would have to come out. 150 words or fewer in total.

<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b))*

**Must hit, strict (a):**
- **Quantitative hedonism:** the life went poorly, or at least not well, because it held more frustration than pleasure. The forgery is *irrelevant*, since it never entered his experience. The deciding feature is the balance of felt pleasure over pain.
- **Actual desire-satisfaction:** the verdict depends on the desire's content. If he wanted "to finish this translation," the desire was satisfied and his life went well in its central respect. If he wanted "to preserve a real record," it was frustrated even though he never knew. The deciding feature is whether the world matched the desire, not whether he felt satisfied.
- **Objective list:** knowledge is compromised, since his central belief about his life's work is false. Achievement is partly present (a difficult, skilled task carried through) and partly undercut (it achieves nothing real). The deciding feature is what was actually true of the work.

**Must hit, any verdict (b):** name a specific indeterminacy and what would fix it. Desire theory needs a rule for individuating desire content (finish versus preserve). The list needs a rule for weighing goods (achievement against false belief) and for whether an achievement built on an error still counts. Hedonism needs a way to count his satisfaction at finishing against forty tedious years.

**Wrong turns:** letting the forgery lower the hedonist verdict; treating desire-satisfaction as feeling satisfied, which gives "went well" automatically; a (b) that simply picks a verdict instead of locating the gap.

**Model answer (b), one of several:** Actual desire-satisfaction goes silent on the content of his desire. If his desire was to finish, it was satisfied; if it was to preserve a real chronicle, it was frustrated. Nothing in the theory says which it was, because Tomas himself never separated the two while he believed they coincided. To settle it, the theory needs a counterfactual test: what would he have wanted had he known about the forgery? That test already moves toward the informed-desire version, which then owes an account of how much idealization is allowed.

---

**P2** *(Evaluative, graded on construction.)*

**Must hit, any verdict:**
- A case where the two theories really diverge, with each verdict stated. Either (i) something *off* the list that the person would still want under full information (a grass-counter type), or (ii) something *on* the list that the person would not want even when fully informed (an alienation case).
- Explicit handling of the escape. The case must stipulate that full information and clear reasoning leave the desire, or its absence, unchanged, and must say why that stipulation is coherent: nothing she is ignorant of would change it.
- A stated verdict, plus the reply from the other side: the list theorist calls the desire misplaced or says the good benefits her regardless, or the desire theorist calls the list paternalistic.

**Wrong turns:** a case that only splits *actual* from informed desire, such as adaptive preference, which the informed version is built to handle; leaving the escape unaddressed; choosing a list item and a desire that do not concern the same thing.

**Model answer, one of several:** Ines, a historian, is offered a DNA test that would reveal true facts about her ancestry. She understands exactly what she would learn, has no false beliefs about it, reasons clearly, and does not want to know. The indifference is stable, not a fear or a bias. Informed desire: learning would not benefit her, because no idealized version of her wants it. Objective list with knowledge on it: learning would benefit her, since it is significant knowledge about herself. My verdict: it does not benefit her. The list theorist would reply that knowledge of one's origins is good for a person whether or not she cares, and that her indifference is a flaw in her caring, not evidence about her good. A hybrid theorist would agree with my verdict for a different reason: without engagement, the good is not hers.

---

**P3** *(Exegetical.)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** P1 is hedonism's own content and P2 is a stipulation, so P3 follows from them and the hedonist cannot reject any of the three. The hedonist must deny **P4**, or reinterpret it so that the machine life is worse in some respect (less admirable, bad for others) but not worse *for the person*. The objective-list theorist accepts P4, and what they reject is the **experience requirement**, the claim inside P1 that only what enters experience can affect how a life goes for you. That claim is the real crux.

**Must hit (b):** a variant that controls for a named rival explanation of P4, together with the verdict that would move the hedonist. The control could be for status quo bias (a reversal), fear of malfunction (the machine is certain to work), or duties to others (you have none, or they are all plugged in too). What would move the hedonist is the reluctance to plug in *persisting* once that factor is removed.

**Wrong turns:** saying the hedonist denies P2 ("no machine could do that"), which fights the stipulation instead of the argument; saying the objective-list theorist denies P4.

**Model answer:** (a) The hedonist denies P4, or reads it as about something other than well-being. The objective-list theorist accepts P4 and denies the experience requirement built into hedonism. (b) Run a reversal that also balances the losses: you are already in a machine, and unplugging leads to a real life *exactly as pleasant* as your simulated one, with every relationship carried over. If people still prefer to unplug, status quo bias and loss of attachments cannot explain it, since the status quo favours staying and nothing is lost. That leaves contact with reality as the explanation, which is what the hedonist denies.
