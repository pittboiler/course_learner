# Ethics · Lesson 5.3: Pluralism and particularism

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 5: Contract and plurality · Builds on: [5.1 Contractarianism](05-01-contractarianism-morality-as-mutual-advantage.md), [5.2 Contractualism](05-02-contractualism-what-no-one-could-reasonably-reject.md), [philosophical-method 3.4 Intuitions and reflective equilibrium](../../philosophical-method/lessons/03-04-intuitions-and-reflective-equilibrium.md) · Unlocks: [6.1 Moral realism](06-01-moral-realism.md)

## Why this matters

Every theory so far has been a bid for one master principle: maximize the good, act only on universalizable maxims, do what no one could reasonably reject. Each ran into cases where the master principle said something hard to accept. Two responses stop looking for the master. W. D. Ross says there are several basic duties, none reducible to another, and no formula for ranking them. Jonathan Dancy goes further: there may be no true moral principles at all, only reasons whose force depends on the case. If Dancy is right, the project of Modules 1–5 was misconceived from the start. If he is wrong, the precise way he is wrong tells you what a moral principle has to look like.

## The idea

Think about what you actually do when two obligations collide. You owe a friend a favour; a stranger has a stronger claim on the same afternoon. You don't compute. You feel both pulls, you notice the favour doesn't simply vanish when you decide against it (you still owe an explanation), and you judge.

**Ross's pluralism** takes that experience at face value. There are several distinct kinds of moral consideration, each of which always counts. When they conflict, no rule tells you which wins; practical judgement does. The considerations are fixed; their *weights* in a particular case are not.

**Dancy's particularism** questions the first half. Take the plainest reason there is: *I borrowed this book from you*, so I should return it. Now suppose you stole it from the library. The fact that I borrowed it from you isn't outweighed. It is no longer a reason to give it back to you at all. If even that feature can switch off, maybe no feature carries a fixed moral charge from case to case, and so no principle of the form "features of type F always count in favour" is true.

Here is the difference in one line. For Ross, context changes **how much** a consideration weighs. For Dancy, context can change **whether** it counts, and in which direction.

## The argument

**A. Ross's pluralism** (*The Right and the Good*, 1930, ch. 2, paraphrased).

1. **There are several prima facie duties, usually counted as seven:** fidelity (keeping promises, including the implicit promise not to lie), reparation (for wrongs you have done), gratitude (to benefactors), justice (distributing goods in line with merit), beneficence (improving others' condition), self-improvement, and non-injury (not harming others). *In words:* morality has several irreducible sources, not one.
2. **"Prima facie" does not mean "apparent."** A prima facie duty is a real feature of the situation that tends to make an act right. Ross himself regretted the phrase, called it a *conditional duty*, and in later work preferred the word "claim". *In words:* the promise really counts even when it loses; it is not an illusion of a duty.
3. **The prima facie duties are self-evident**, in the way a mathematical axiom is evident to a mind mature enough to grasp it. *In words:* you don't infer that promises matter; you see it once you understand what a promise is.
4. **Your actual duty (all things considered) is not self-evident.** It is the act whose prima facie rightness most outweighs its prima facie wrongness, and finding it takes judgement, which Ross calls fallible. *In words:* the ingredients are certain, but the recipe for any given case isn't.
5. **There is no fixed ranking.** Ross holds that non-injury is generally more stringent than beneficence (not harming weighs more than helping) but gives no lexical order beyond rough tendencies like that. *In words:* no duty always trumps another.

**B. Dancy's route to particularism** (*Ethics Without Principles*, 2004, paraphrased).

1. **Holism of reasons:** a feature that is a reason in one case may be no reason, or a reason on the opposite side, in another. *In words:* reasons don't carry fixed charges.
2. Holism is true of theoretical reasons (that something looks red is normally a reason to believe it is red, but not if I know I've taken a drug that makes blue things look red), and nothing marks practical reasons off as different. *In words:* if reasons work this way for belief, they probably do for action too.
3. A moral principle worth the name says that some feature *always* makes the same contribution. *In words:* a principle is a promise of invariance.
4. **∴** Morality does not depend on a supply of true principles, and moral competence cannot consist in knowing them. *In words:* a good judge sees what matters here, rather than looking it up.

Dancy's vocabulary for how context works: a **favourer** is the reason itself (that I borrowed it); an **enabler** is a condition needed for it to count without being a reason itself (that it wasn't stolen); a **disabler** switches it off (that it was stolen); **intensifiers** and **attenuators** turn its strength up or down. **Default reasons** are features that count one way *unless* something disables them, so causing pain is a default reason against, even if not an invariant one.

## Map of positions

| | Monism | Rossian pluralism | Particularism |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Number of basic principles** | One (utility; the categorical imperative; reasonable rejection) | Several (about seven), irreducible to one another | None needed; there may be no true ones |
| **Does a morally relevant feature always count the same way?** | Yes, via the master principle | Yes: each prima facie duty always counts in its direction; only its weight varies | No: a feature's valence can shift, vanish or reverse |
| **Role of judgement** | Applying the principle and estimating facts | Weighing conflicting duties to reach actual duty | Discerning which features matter at all here, and how |
| **What a conflict leaves behind** | At the level of theory, nothing: the losing option was simply not required (two-level views allow for fitting regret) | Moral residue: the overridden duty still grounds regret, apology or compensation | Residue only where a reason was outweighed, not where it was disabled |

The bottom row is the crux in operational form. Keep it in view for Problem 3.

Where reflective equilibrium fits ([philosophical-method 3.4](../../philosophical-method/lessons/03-04-intuitions-and-reflective-equilibrium.md)): Ross treats the considered convictions of thoughtful people as the data of ethics, like perceptions in natural science, and that is the method's starting point. The three views then part over its output. The monist expects equilibrium to converge on one principle, the pluralist on a short list with weights left to judgement, and the particularist expects no general principle to come out of it at all.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean case: reparation against beneficence).** Backing out of a parking space, you dent a neighbour's car, and the repair costs 400 dollars. You could instead give the 400 dollars to a famine charity, where it would do more good.

- *Ross:* two prima facie duties are engaged. Reparation rests on your own past act; beneficence rests on the bare fact that you can help. This is Ross's central charge against utilitarianism ([1.1](01-01-classical-utilitarianism.md)): it flattens every duty into beneficence and so misses that your relation to the neighbour, as the person who wronged him, generates a special claim. Most people judge you should pay for the repair, and Ross's list explains why without requiring beneficence to be worth nothing.
- *Dancy:* nothing strains. "That I damaged his car" favours paying; no enabler is missing and no disabler is present. Particularists don't deny that clean cases exist, only that their cleanness is guaranteed by a principle.

**Example 2 (hard case: the honest verdict).** A friend asks for your honest opinion of the novel she has worked on for twenty years. You think it is poor.

- Normally, *that she asked for my honest opinion* favours giving it (fidelity, on Ross's reading of truthfulness as an implicit promise). *That she is about to send it to publishers* intensifies it: honesty now saves her months of rejection. *That she asked lightly, at a party* attenuates it.
- Now add one fact: she asks in her last week in hospice, and the novel will never be sent anywhere. Most people judge that you should not give the verdict.

The two theories agree on that verdict and disagree on its structure. The Rossian says fidelity still counts, outweighed by non-injury. So something is lost, and a pang is fitting. The particularist can say her request no longer gives any reason for a critical verdict, since the point of honest assessment (improving the book, deciding to submit it) is gone. On that view a *purpose* was an enabler, and it has been removed. So nothing is lost and nothing is owed. The case doesn't decide between them, but it shows where to look: at whether a remainder is fitting. And it shows the Rossian's best move, which is to build the enabler into the duty ("honest assessment when it can serve the asker"). Dancy's question in reply is whether that list of built-in conditions ever ends.

**The objection particularism must answer: guidance and learnability.** If no principle holds, how does anyone learn morality, teach a child, or predict what a decent person will do? Finite learners generalize from finitely many cases. Critics, notably McKeever and Ridge (*Principled Ethics*, 2006), press that holism is compatible with principles whose antecedents list enablers and disablers, so holism doesn't entail particularism, and that codifiability is at least a goal worth pursuing. Dancy replies that competence is like perceptual skill. Default reasons and remembered cases give it structure, and what the learner acquires is a sense of *what can matter and how*, not a rulebook. What would be lost if he is right: moral argument by principle, uniform public justification, and the hope of a theory that settles hard cases in advance. What would be kept: reasons, better and worse judgement, and cases as precedents.

## Watch out

- **You might think "prima facie duty" means a duty that only seems to hold.** Ross means a genuine, always-present consideration that can be overridden. He admitted the phrase invites the misreading.
- **You might think Ross is a particularist because he leaves conflicts to judgement.** Ross is a generalist about the *contributors*: promising always counts for keeping. Only the *verdict* is left to judgement. Dancy denies the invariance of the contributors themselves.
- **You might think holism means anything goes.** Particularists grant stable default reasons, and they grant that cruelty is almost never a reason in favour. The claim is that no feature's contribution is guaranteed by a true exceptionless principle, not that moral reasons are random.
- **You might think pluralism is just monism with a longer list.** Monism can resolve any conflict by appeal to its one principle; Rossian pluralism denies there is any common currency in which to do that. That missing currency is the source of both its realism about hard cases and its thinness as a guide.

## One-liner

> Ross: the ingredients are fixed and the recipe is judgement. Dancy: even the ingredients change with the dish.

## Problems

**P1 (🟡) *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)*** Years ago a senior colleague, Dr. Okafor, went out of her way to get you your first research job. You now sit on a hiring panel. Her former student is one of two finalists; the other candidate is, on the panel's own criteria, clearly stronger. Okafor has written to you asking you to "remember who helped you." (a) Name every Rossian prima facie duty engaged, saying for each what grounds it in this case, and state what Ross says determines your actual duty. (b) Weigh them and give a one-line verdict, then say where Ross's theory stops determining an answer and what, if anything, is owed afterward. 150 words or fewer for (b).

**P2 (🟡) *(Evaluative.)*** Build a counterexample to the invariance of one reason-giving feature. Pick a feature that ordinarily counts **in favour** of an act, and describe a case in which that same feature counts **against** it, not merely too weakly to win. Then give the Rossian's best redescription of your case, and say whether it succeeds or is ad hoc. Do not use a promise or a rescue. 150 words or fewer.

**P3 (🔴, optional) *(Exegetical.)*** A tutor once spent a month of evenings coaching you through an exam, unpaid. You later learn she did it to make you feel obliged to keep quiet about her falsifying lab results, which you have now discovered. A Rossian and a Dancyan agree you should report her. Find the crux: (a) state what each says about the tutor's past help, (b) name the single premise one side must deny, and (c) say what observation about the case would count as evidence for one side. 150 words or fewer.

<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b))*

**Must hit, strict (a):**
- **Gratitude** to Okafor, grounded in her past benefit to you.
- **Justice**, in Ross's sense of distribution according to merit: the stronger candidate has a merit-based claim on the job.
- **Fidelity**: in joining the panel you implicitly undertook to judge on its stated criteria, and Ross reads implicit undertakings as promises.
- Credit also **non-injury** to the stronger candidate (being passed over unfairly) or **beneficence** toward either candidate, if grounded.
- Actual duty: no fixed ranking. It is the act whose prima facie rightness most outweighs its prima facie wrongness, found by fallible judgement, not derived.

**Must hit, any verdict (b):** an explicit weighing that names which duties you take to prevail and why; a place where the theory underdetermines (for example, how much merit gap gratitude could offset, since no scale exists); and a statement of residue under Ross's view (a remainder of gratitude owed in some other form, such as an explanation or other help).

**Wrong turns:** treating gratitude as a duty to do whatever the benefactor asks, since Ross's gratitude is a duty to return benefit, not to obey; saying Ross ranks justice lexically above gratitude, which he doesn't; omitting fidelity to the panel, which is the duty most readers miss.

**Model answer (b), one of several:** Verdict: vote for the stronger candidate. Justice and my implicit undertaking to the panel both point that way. Gratitude is a duty to return benefit, and doing it by skewing a job decision at a third party's expense is a poor fit for it. But Ross's theory does not say this. Had the gap been narrow, nothing in the list says whether gratitude could tip a close call, and my judgement is all I have. Something is still owed: gratitude isn't cancelled by losing. I owe Okafor a candid explanation, and some other return of her kindness if I can find one.

---

**P2** *(Evaluative)*

**Must hit, any verdict:**
- A named feature with a clear ordinary positive valence (for example, that a remark is funny, that it would please her, that it is what he wants).
- A second case where that feature counts *against*, with a test showing it is a reversal and not mere outweighing. Best test: increasing the feature makes the act *worse* ("the funnier, the worse").
- The Rossian redescription stated at strength: the feature keeps its positive contribution, and a separate consideration (non-injury, justice) grows alongside it.
- A judgement on whether the redescription is independently motivated or introduced only to save invariance, using the ad hoc test from [philosophical-method 3.4](../../philosophical-method/lessons/03-04-intuitions-and-reflective-equilibrium.md).

**Wrong turns:** a case where the feature is merely outweighed (that shows pluralism, not holism); using a feature that was never a reason in favour to begin with; Dancy's own sadistic-pleasure example repeated without a fresh case.

**Model answer, one of several:** That a joke is funny is normally a reason to tell it. At the funeral of a child, the joke's funniness counts against telling it, and the funnier it is, the worse telling it would be. That is a reversal, not outweighing, since more of the supposedly positive feature makes the act worse. Rossian redescription: funniness still gives a small beneficence-reason, but it also intensifies the injury to the mourners, and the injury grows faster. That is not obviously ad hoc, because non-injury is independently on Ross's list. But it has a cost: the Rossian must say something good remains in the joke's funniness at the funeral, and nothing about the funniness seems worth regretting losing. I judge the redescription strained but not refuted.

---

**P3** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict:**
- (a) **Rossian:** the coaching still grounds a prima facie duty of gratitude, outweighed by justice and non-injury (to those harmed by the falsified results). **Dancyan:** the motive disables the benefit as a reason for gratitude, or even makes it a reason for resentment, so there is nothing to outweigh.
- (b) The crux is **invariance**: whether a feature that grounds a prima facie duty (here, past benefit) always contributes in the same direction. Ross affirms it; Dancy denies it.
- (c) Evidence: **residue**. If some compunction, thanks or compensation is fitting after you report her, that supports outweighing (Ross). If nothing is owed and regret would be misplaced, that supports disabling (Dancy). A reader's stable judgement about fitting residue is the observable.

**Wrong turns:** locating the crux in the verdict (both agree); saying Ross holds that gratitude always wins; making the crux "whether judgement is needed," since both views need it.

**Model answer:** (a) Ross: her help grounds a real but outweighed duty of gratitude. Dancy: its purpose switches that off, so the help gives no reason for gratitude at all. (b) Invariance: does past benefit always count in favour of returning it? Ross must affirm it; Dancy denies it. (c) Ask whether anything is owed to her after you report. If a residue of thanks is fitting, the reason survived and was outweighed; if not, it was disabled.

## Flashback

**From Lesson 4.4 (Natural law I: Aquinas):** (a) State the first precept of natural law as Aquinas gives it in ST I-II q.94 a.2. (b) Aquinas distinguishes a secondary precept that fails *in rectitude* (it does not rightly apply in this case) from one that fails *in knowledge* (people do not grasp it). Classify each case, and say in which one the precept still binds the people involved: (i) *Do not enter a neighbour's house without permission*, when smoke is pouring from next door and a child is crying inside. (ii) In a port town, custom holds that short-weighting grain sold to foreign merchants is shrewd dealing rather than cheating, and those who do it are admired. (c) In q.94 a.4 Aquinas holds that the general principles are the same for all, but the more particular conclusions drawn from them hold only in most cases and can fail. Say in two sentences whether that makes him a Rossian, a particularist, or neither. *(Exegetical (a)–(b) · Evaluative (c).)*

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):** good is to be done and pursued, and evil is to be avoided. All other precepts are founded on this one.

**Must hit, strict (b):** (i) **failure in rectitude.** The precept is a conclusion serving life in society and the security of each household. Here keeping it would defeat the very goods it serves, the child's life and the neighbour's own good, so it does not bind in this case. (ii) **failure in knowledge.** The precept against defrauding others in exchange is still true and still binds the merchants. Custom has obscured it from them, which q.94 a.6 says vicious customs can do to secondary precepts, though not to the primary precepts in general. So the precept still binds in (ii), not in (i).

**Must hit, any verdict (c):** the Rossian point of contact is that general principles are fixed while particular applications admit exceptions. The difference is that Aquinas has a single first principle with an ordering of goods beneath it, whereas Ross's duties are several and irreducible. Against particularism, Aquinas holds the common principles invariant; the variation is only in conclusions drawn from them. Any classification passes if both contrasts are drawn.

**Wrong turns:** calling (ii) a failure in rectitude because "the rule doesn't hold there", when local approval does not make a precept inapplicable, only unknown; calling (i) a failure in knowledge, when everyone in the case grasps the precept perfectly well; reading q.94 a.4 as saying the first principles themselves vary.

**Model answer:** (b) (i) Rectitude: entering serves the very goods the precept protects, so here it does not bind. (ii) Knowledge: the precept still binds the merchants, and custom has hidden it from them. (c), one of several: Neither, quite. Like Ross, Aquinas holds fixed principles with defeasible applications, but his principles descend from one first precept and an ordering of inclinations, not an irreducible list. He is further still from Dancy, since invariance at the level of the common principles is exactly what he affirms.

</details>
