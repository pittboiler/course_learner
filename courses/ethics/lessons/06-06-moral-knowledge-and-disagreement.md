# Ethics · Lesson 6.6: Moral knowledge and disagreement

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 6: Metaethics · Builds on: [6.1 Moral realism](06-01-moral-realism.md), [6.3 Error theory](06-03-error-theory.md), [6.4 Constructivism and debunking](06-04-constructivism-and-debunking.md), [6.5 God and morality](06-05-god-and-morality-the-euthyphro-dilemma.md), [philosophical-method 3.4 Intuitions and reflective equilibrium](../../philosophical-method/lessons/03-04-intuitions-and-reflective-equilibrium.md) · Unlocks: Module 6 boss problem; [`epistemology`](../../epistemology/syllabus.md)

## Why this matters

Every theory in this course was tested against judgments like "gratuitous cruelty is wrong." The last two questions are about those judgments themselves. First, if there are moral truths, how could anyone *know* one? You cannot see wrongness or measure it. Second, thoughtful people across cultures and in the same seminar room disagree about morality, deeply and persistently. Does that show there is nothing to know, or only that knowing it is hard? Every metaethical view from [6.1](06-01-moral-realism.md) to [6.5](06-05-god-and-morality-the-euthyphro-dilemma.md) owes answers to both.

## The idea

**Knowing.** Some truths you grasp without inferring them from anything: that if A is taller than B and B taller than C, A is taller than C. **Intuitionism** says some moral truths are like that. W. D. Ross (*The Right and the Good*, 1930, ch. 2) held that the prima facie duties ([5.3](05-03-pluralism-and-particularism.md)) are self-evident to a mind mature enough to attend to them, as mathematical axioms are. Sidgwick and Moore said similar things about their own first principles. Robert Audi's modern version (*The Good in the Right*, 2004) makes three clarifications:

- **Self-evident is not obvious.** Understanding the proposition may take reflection.
- **Self-evident is not indefeasible.** You can be justified and still be wrong, or come to have a reason to doubt.
- **Self-evident is not unprovable.** A self-evident proposition may also be supported by argument; the point is that it doesn't *need* to be.

A related line holds that some moral knowledge is **perceptual**: that seeing the hoodlums set fire to the cat ([6.1](06-01-moral-realism.md)) is seeing wrongness, the way a trained ear hears a wrong note. Defenders (Audi among them) treat this as perception shaped by background understanding, not a sixth sense.

The third answer is **reflective equilibrium**, which you met as a method in [philosophical-method 3.4](../../philosophical-method/lessons/03-04-intuitions-and-reflective-equilibrium.md). Adjust principles and case verdicts against each other until they fit. *Narrow* equilibrium fits your principles to your own judgments; *wide* equilibrium, the distinction Rawls drew and Norman Daniels developed ("Wide Reflective Equilibrium and Theory Acceptance in Ethics", 1979), also tests them against rival theories and background knowledge. The epistemic question here is new: **does coherence justify?** A coherent set of judgments can be coherently false. If the starting judgments carry no evidential weight at all, adjusting them against each other produces a tidy output from worthless input: garbage in, garbage out. So reflective equilibrium seems to need intuitionism's claim that considered judgments carry some initial credibility. Wide equilibrium adds external checks but does not remove that need.

**Disagreeing.** Three different claims all go by the name **relativism**:

- **Descriptive relativism**: moral codes vary across societies and persons. An empirical claim, and in some form plainly true.
- **Metaethical relativism**: the truth of a moral claim is relative to a framework, so "slavery is wrong" can be true relative to one moral code and not another. Gilbert Harman ("Moral Relativism Defended", 1975) ties certain moral judgments to implicit agreements among those involved; David Wong (*Natural Moralities*, 2006) defends, roughly, a *pluralistic* relativism on which more than one morality is true, though human nature and the needs of social life limit which.
- **Normative relativism**: we ought to tolerate, or not judge, other moral codes. A first-order moral claim.

## The argument

**A. The popular argument from diversity.**

1. **Moral codes vary across cultures.** *In words:* descriptive relativism.
2. **If there were an objective moral truth, cultures would agree about it.** *In words:* truth would produce convergence.
3. **So there is no objective moral truth; moral claims are true only relative to a culture.** *In words:* metaethical relativism.
4. **So no culture may judge another.** *In words:* normative relativism.

Premise 2 is false for every other domain: cultures disagreed about disease and the shape of the earth. And step 4 does not follow from 3. If all moral truth is relative, "tolerate other cultures" is true only relative to cultures that accept it, so the relativist cannot demand it of an intolerant one.

**B. The serious version: disagreement as evidence.** Mackie's relativity argument ([6.3](06-03-error-theory.md)) is an inference to the best explanation: variation is better explained by differing ways of life than by some people perceiving objective values others miss. A second, purely epistemic version borrows **conciliationism** from [`epistemology`](../../epistemology/syllabus.md) (Adam Elga's equal weight view, 2007; David Christensen, 2007):

1. **An epistemic peer** is someone as well informed and as reliable on the question as you are, judged independently of the dispute itself. *In words:* you cannot demote someone to non-peer just because they disagree with you.
2. **When you learn that a peer disagrees, you should move substantially toward their view**, or suspend judgment. *In words:* you have no non-question-begging reason to think you are the one who got it right.
3. **Many moral disputes are between peers.** *In words:* philosophers who know the same arguments still split on abortion, eating meat and the doctrine of double effect.
4. **So we should suspend judgment on many moral claims.** *In words:* we lack moral knowledge wherever informed peers disagree.

Note the conclusion. B is **skeptical, not anti-realist**: suspending judgment on a question presupposes it has an answer you don't know. The relativist and the error theorist need the further explanatory step Mackie supplies.

## Map of positions

| Claim | Kind of claim | Main challenge | Does it entail the next row? |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Descriptive relativism**: codes vary | Empirical | Not its truth but its depth: much variation may rest on shared values plus different non-moral beliefs | No: variation is compatible with one truth that some miss, or with one value applied to different facts |
| **Metaethical relativism**: moral truth is framework-relative | Metaethical | An argument that some moral claims hold across frameworks, or that relativized "wrong" cannot capture disagreement | No: a relativist about truth may still condemn intolerance from within her own framework |
| **Normative relativism**: tolerate other codes | First-order moral | A case where tolerance is plainly wrong, e.g. of a practice harming non-consenting members | It is itself a non-relative moral claim, which is its tension |

Realists, expressivists, error theorists, constructivists and divine command theorists can all accept the first row. Where they part is the second.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean case: Darius and the Callatiae).** Herodotus (*Histories* III.38) tells how the Persian king Darius asked some Greeks at his court what price would induce them to eat their dead fathers; they said nothing would. He then asked the Callatiae, a people of India who did eat their dead parents, what would induce them to burn their fathers, as the Greeks did; they cried out and begged him not to speak of it. Herodotus concludes with Pindar that custom is king of all.

Descriptive relativism: confirmed, vividly. But look at what each side is doing. Both treat the disposal of a parent's body as a matter of grave duty; both are horrified at disrespect to the dead. The disagreement is over which *practice* honours the dead, given different beliefs and customs about bodies. One shared value, applied through different non-moral beliefs and conventions, yields opposite practices. So the story, far from showing that moral truth is relative, is consistent with a single principle ("honour your dead parents") holding everywhere. This is the standard reply to Mackie: much moral diversity is disagreement over facts or over how a shared value applies.

**Example 2 (hard case: a peer dispute).** Ana and Ben are palliative-care physicians of equal training and experience. They know the same studies and have heard each other's arguments. A dying patient asks whether his scans are worse; his family asks the doctors to say no. Ana judges the lie impermissible; Ben judges it permissible. Run argument B.

*Are they peers?* By independent criteria, yes. Ben cannot say "Ana misjudged this case, so she isn't my peer here": that uses the disagreement to decide who is reliable, which premise 1 forbids.

*Conciliationism* then says both should lower their confidence sharply. Here the case strains in three places.

- **Spinelessness.** Suspension seems to leave Ben with no view at the bedside, but he must act. Conciliationists reply that credence and action come apart: you can act on 55 percent confidence.
- **The first-person standpoint.** David Enoch ("Not Just a Truthometer", published in *Mind* around 2010) argues, roughly, that you cannot treat your own judgment as just one reading among instruments, because weighing the readings is itself done from your standpoint. Some think this has special force in ethics, where deferring on a moral verdict seems to leave something missing even if the other person is reliable.
- **Self-undermining.** Conciliationism is itself disputed among epistemic peers, so by its own lights its defenders should lower their confidence in it.

**Ideal-conditions convergence** is the realist's further reply: disagreement that would vanish under full information, impartiality and careful reasoning is no evidence against a truth. Whether Ana and Ben's dispute is of that kind is exactly what is contested. Empirical work on persistent moral disagreement exists but its interpretation is disputed.

## Watch out

- **You might think self-evident means obvious or certain, but** for Ross and Audi it means knowable through adequate understanding alone. That can take reflection, and it can be defeated.
- **You might think "cultures disagree, so there's no moral truth" is Mackie's argument, but** Mackie's is an inference to the best explanation, and it is only as strong as the rival explanation of the variation.
- **You might think metaethical relativism implies tolerance, but** tolerance is a first-order moral claim. A relativist has no framework-neutral ground for demanding it.
- **You might think conciliationism supports anti-realism, but** by itself it only recommends suspending judgment on questions it assumes have answers.

## One-liner

> Diversity is a fact, relativism is a theory, tolerance is a moral view, and peer disagreement threatens our moral knowledge long before it threatens moral truth.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢) *(Exegetical.)*** The paragraph below is from an invented op-ed. (a) Identify each of the three kinds of relativism it asserts, quoting the phrase. (b) Name the inference that does the most work and say why it is invalid as stated. 150 words or fewer.

> "Anthropologists have catalogued thousands of societies, and no two agree on marriage, punishment, or what is owed to strangers. The lesson is simple: there is no moral truth out there, only what each people has decided is true for itself. Missionaries and human-rights lawyers who export 'universal' values are therefore guilty of arrogance, and we should stop judging practices we were not raised in."

**P2 (🟡) *(Evaluative.)*** Build a counterexample to the inference *from* "people in different societies disagree deeply and persistently about whether p" *to* "whether p is true only relative to a society." Take your case from a non-moral domain. Then state the strongest disanalogy a metaethical relativist would claim between your case and moral disagreement, and say whether it holds. Any verdict. 150 words or fewer.

**P3 (🔴, optional) *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)*** Two philosophers of equal standing, who have read the same literature, disagree about whether it is permissible to eat factory-farmed meat. (a) State what the equal weight view recommends and what would have to be true for one of them to justifiably discount the other. (b) Decide whether either may keep her view with undiminished confidence, meeting either the spinelessness objection or the self-undermining objection. 150 words or fewer for (b).

**P1** *(Exegetical — strict.)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** descriptive: "no two agree on marriage, punishment, or what is owed to strangers"; metaethical: "no moral truth out there, only what each people has decided is true for itself"; normative: "guilty of arrogance … we should stop judging practices."
**Must hit, strict (b):** the step from variation to "no moral truth" (argument A, premise 2 suppressed): disagreement does not entail absence of truth, as disagreement over medicine shows. Credit also for noting the second invalid step: the normative conclusion is a non-relative moral claim the metaethical premise cannot support.
**Wrong turns:** classing "arrogance" as descriptive; saying the argument fails because the anthropology is false (the premise may well be true).
**Model answer:** (a) Descriptive: "no two agree on marriage, punishment…"; metaethical: "no moral truth… only what each people has decided is true for itself"; normative: "we should stop judging." (b) The load-bearing step runs from diversity to no truth, and needs the hidden premise that truth would produce agreement, which fails for astronomy and medicine. The last step also fails: if all moral truth is relative, "don't judge" binds only cultures that accept it, so the lawyers' home culture may rightly judge.

**P2** *(Evaluative.)*

**Must hit, any verdict:**
- A non-moral domain with deep, persistent, cross-cultural disagreement where the truth is not relative: causes of disease, the age of the earth, whether a dietary practice is safe. State why no one infers relativity there.
- The best disanalogy: e.g. moral disagreement persists *after* agreement on the facts and among the fully informed, whereas the science disputes yield to evidence; or there is no agreed method for resolving moral disputes.
- An assessment of whether the disanalogy holds (e.g. some scientific disputes persisted for centuries among the informed; or much apparent moral disagreement rests on non-moral beliefs, as with the Callatiae).

**Wrong turns:** using a matter of taste (which may really be relative) as the counterexample; giving a moral case, which begs the question.
**Model answer (one of several):** For centuries, informed physicians across cultures disagreed about whether disease spreads by miasma or contagion. The truth was not relative to Galenic or Chinese medicine; some were wrong. So persistent disagreement does not entail relativity. The relativist's best disanalogy: medical disputes ended when evidence arrived, while moral disputes persist among people who agree on every fact, so no evidence of the relevant kind seems available. That partly holds. But much moral disagreement does turn on non-moral beliefs (about souls, bodies, consequences), and the disanalogy then shows that moral knowledge is harder to get, not that there is nothing to get.

---

**P3** *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** the equal weight view: give the peer's judgment the same weight as your own, so each should move substantially toward the other or suspend judgment. Discounting requires a reason independent of the disagreement itself: evidence that the other is tired, biased, missing information, or has erred in reasoning on this occasion.
**Must hit, any verdict (b):** a verdict; one objection stated fairly; the conciliationist's best reply to it; a judgment on whether the reply works.
**Wrong turns:** discounting the peer because her conclusion is wrong (question-begging); treating suspension as a claim that there is no fact about meat-eating.
**Model answer (b), one of several:** No, not with undiminished confidence. Self-undermining objection: conciliationism is itself contested among peers, so it recommends doubting itself. Reply: a principle about how to respond to evidence can coherently exempt itself, as a rule for updating need not be revisable by its own application (Elga has argued along these lines). That reply looks ad hoc unless the exemption is independently motivated. Still, even if conciliationism fails as a universal rule, disagreement from a known peer is some evidence I may be wrong. So some reduction of confidence is warranted, though not necessarily full suspension.

## Flashback

**From Lesson 6.2 (Expressivism):** Consider this argument.

> (P1) If gambling is wrong, then running the state lottery is wrong.
> (P2) Running the state lottery is not wrong.
> (C) So gambling is not wrong.

(a) It is an instance of modus tollens. Say which occurrences of a moral clause are unasserted, and what attitude Blackburn's higher-order account takes P1 to express. (b) State, in two sentences, why the negations in P2 and C cause a further problem for simple expressivism, and name the author who pressed it.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):** both clauses in P1 ("gambling is wrong," "running the state lottery is wrong") are unasserted, since the speaker commits only to the conditional. On Blackburn's account P1 expresses a higher-order attitude: disapproval of the combination of disapproving of gambling while not disapproving of running the lottery.
**Must hit, strict (b):** P2 and C must express *some* attitude toward lotteries and gambling, and the expressivist must explain why "running the lottery is not wrong" is inconsistent with "running the lottery is wrong" as a logical matter, not merely as a clash of attitudes. Mark Schroeder (*Being For*, 2008) pressed this as the negation problem: disapproval has one place for "not," while a belief's structured content has several.
**Wrong turns:** calling P2 unasserted (it is asserted, though negated); answering (b) with Hale's point that a clash of attitudes is not a logical inconsistency, which concerns the conditional, not negation.
**Model answer:** (a) Both clauses in P1 are unasserted; Blackburn reads P1 as disapproving of anyone who disapproves of gambling but tolerates the lottery. (b) "Not wrong" has to express tolerance, and the view must explain why tolerating and disapproving of the same thing are logically inconsistent rather than just in tension. Schroeder argued that a single attitude like disapproval has too little structure to place the negation, since "thinks not-wrong" and "thinks wrong not to" need different homes.

</details>

## Where the course leaves you

You now have five normative families and five metaethical pictures, each stated at the strength its defenders would recognize. None has won, and the course has not tried to make one win. What you should have is sharper: for any moral claim you hold, you can say which theory would justify it, what that theory's best objection is, and what it would take for you to know the claim is true.
