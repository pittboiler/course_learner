# Immunology · Lesson 2.5: Antigen processing & presentation

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 2: Antigen Recognition · Builds on: [2.4](02-04-mhc-molecules.md), [2.3](02-03-t-cell-receptor.md) · Unlocks: [3.5](03-05-helper-t-cells-polarization.md) (helper T cells & polarization)

## Why this matters

[2.4](02-04-mhc-molecules.md) gave you two display platforms and said class I reports the cytosol to CD8 cells and class II reports the endosome to CD4 cells. This lesson supplies the machinery that makes that true — and once you have it, **Module 2 stops being descriptive and becomes predictive.**

The payoff is a chain of inferences you can run in one direction, every time:

$$\textbf{where the antigen is} \;\Rightarrow\; \textbf{which pathway degrades it} \;\Rightarrow\; \textbf{which MHC class carries it} \;\Rightarrow\; \textbf{which T cell responds}$$

That chain is exactly what boss problem 2 in the [syllabus](../syllabus.md) asks you to run, and it is the reason a virus that hides inside a cell still gets caught. The second half of the lesson is quantitative: **how many copies of one peptide does an infected cell actually put on its surface, and how does that compare with the handful a T cell needs?** The answer — a margin of roughly two orders of magnitude — is what makes surveillance work, and closing that margin is what viral immune evasion is trying to do.

## The idea

**The compartment is the address.** A cell has two protein populations that mean completely different things:

- Proteins it is **making** — in the cytosol, on free ribosomes. If one of these is foreign, *this cell is compromised.* The correct response is to kill it. Report to CD8.
- Proteins it has **eaten** — taken in from outside by endocytosis or phagocytosis. If one of these is foreign, *something is loose in the tissue.* The correct response is to organize help: antibody, macrophage activation, inflammation. Report to CD4.

Nothing about a peptide's sequence tells you which situation produced it. **The only information available is which compartment the protein was degraded in** — so the system builds two physically separate conveyor belts, each ending on a different MHC class, and takes the compartment as the message.

**Class I samples the synthetic flux, not the steady-state proteome.** This is the sharpest point in the lesson. The proteasome is fed heavily by **DRiPs** — *defective ribosomal products*, the 10–30 percent of newly translated polypeptides that misfold or terminate early and are ubiquitinated within minutes. So the class I display is weighted toward what the cell is making *right now*. **A virus cannot avoid it while replicating**: translation is the one thing a virus must do, and translation is precisely what class I samples.

**The separation has to be enforced, and one protein does it.** Class I and class II are both assembled in the ER — the compartment stuffed with TAP-delivered cytosolic peptides. Left alone, class II would load cytosolic peptides there and the whole compartmental logic would collapse. It does not, because class II is born with its groove already plugged by the **invariant chain (Ii)**, which is not removed until the molecule reaches an acidified endosome. *Ii exists to keep the two pathways from talking to each other* — that is its job description, and everything else about it follows.

**Then the deliberate exception.** A virus that infects keratinocytes but never infects a dendritic cell would, on the logic above, never prime a CD8 response — the only cells licensed to prime naive T cells would have nothing viral in their cytosol to present. Dendritic cells solve this with **cross-presentation**: they route *exogenous* antigen onto class I. This is not a leak in the system. **It is the mechanism without which CD8 priming would only work against viruses that happen to infect dendritic cells**, which is a small and arbitrary subset.

## The formal version

### The class I (cytosolic) pathway

1. **Substrate.** Cytosolic and nuclear proteins, ubiquitinated and degraded by the 26S proteasome — the housekeeping machine of [molecular-cell-biology 4.4](../../molecular-cell-biology/lessons/04-04-protein-quality-control-degradation.md), which the immune system taps rather than owns. DRiPs dominate the fast-turnover input.
2. **Cleavage.** IFN-γ replaces the catalytic subunits β1, β2, β5 with β1i, β2i, β5i, producing the **immunoproteasome**, which shifts cleavage toward hydrophobic and basic C-termini. *In words: interferon re-tunes the shredder so its products are the shapes TAP transports and class I binds.* The C-terminus is generated by the proteasome and is never trimmed afterwards.
3. **Transport.** **TAP** (TAP1/TAP2), an ATP-driven ABC transporter in the ER membrane, pumps 8–16-mers into the ER lumen. Cytosolic peptidases destroy most peptides within seconds, so TAP is a race against degradation.
4. **Trimming and loading.** **ERAP1** trims the N-terminus to the 8–10 residues the closed groove accepts. The **peptide-loading complex** — TAP, tapasin, calreticulin, ERp57, and MHC I with β2-microglobulin — holds the empty molecule receptive; **tapasin acts as a peptide editor, exchanging weak binders for tight ones** until a stable complex forms, which then leaves for the surface.

**Empty class I is unstable at 37 °C.** So surface class I level is a direct readout of peptide supply: block TAP and total surface class I falls to a few percent of normal.

### The class II (endocytic) pathway

1. **Uptake and acidification.** Endocytosis, phagocytosis, or receptor-mediated capture (a B cell using its BCR — see [3.2](03-02-clonal-selection-b-cell-activation.md)) delivers protein to an endosome that acidifies from pH ≈ 6 to pH ≈ 5 as it matures ([molecular-cell-biology 1.4](../../molecular-cell-biology/lessons/01-04-endomembrane-trafficking.md)). Cathepsins S, L and B become active as the pH drops.
2. **Ii and CLIP.** Class II αβ dimers assemble in the ER around an Ii trimer, forming an $(\alpha\beta\text{Ii})_3$ nonamer. Ii does two things at once: it occupies the groove, and its cytoplasmic tail carries the sorting motifs that route the complex to the endosome rather than the surface. Cathepsins progressively degrade Ii, leaving one fragment still in the groove — **CLIP** (class-II-associated invariant chain peptide).
3. **Editing.** **HLA-DM** catalyses release of CLIP and, like tapasin on the other side, edits the repertoire toward stable binders. HLA-DO, expressed in B cells and thymic epithelium, dampens DM. Loaded class II then goes to the surface.

**The open-ended groove means length is not constrained** (13–25-mers), which is why class II epitopes come as nested sets sharing a core register, while class I epitopes are sharply defined 8–10-mers.

### How many complexes does a cell display?

Let $R$ be the rate at which new copies of one specific peptide–MHC complex reach the surface (complexes per hour) and let surface complexes be lost with first-order rate $k$, so the mean surface lifetime is $\tau = 1/k = t_{1/2}/\ln 2$. Then

$$\frac{dN}{dt} = R - kN \quad\Longrightarrow\quad N(t) = R\tau\left(1 - e^{-t/\tau}\right), \qquad \boxed{\,N_{\text{ss}} = R\tau\,}$$

*In words: the steady-state display of one epitope is its production rate multiplied by how long a complex survives on the surface.* $R$ itself is a product of small numbers:

$$R = R_{\text{syn}} \times f_{\text{DRiP}} \times \varepsilon$$

where $R_{\text{syn}}$ is the synthesis rate of the source protein, $f_{\text{DRiP}} \approx 0.15$ is the fraction promptly degraded, and $\varepsilon \approx 10^{-3}$ is the overall yield of the pipeline — the joint probability that one degraded protein molecule gives a peptide with the right C-terminus, that the peptide survives cytosolic peptidases, is transported, binds the allele, and reaches the surface. **Roughly one degraded protein in a thousand yields one surface complex.** The pipeline is spectacularly wasteful, and it still works, because the threshold at the other end is tiny.

### The numbers on both ends

| Quantity | Value |
|---|---|
| Total class I molecules per nucleated cell | $\approx 10^{5}$ |
| Distinct peptide species displayed | $\approx 10^{4}$ |
| Mean copies of a typical *self* peptide | $\approx 10$ |
| Copies of an immunodominant *viral* peptide | $10^{2}$–$10^{3}$ |
| Complexes needed to trigger a CTL ([2.3](02-03-t-cell-receptor.md)) | 3–10 |
| Complexes needed to trigger a CD4 cell | $\approx 50$–100 |
| Surface half-life of a loaded class I complex | $\approx 6$ h |

**The threshold is at the same order as the background.** A typical self peptide sits at about ten copies, right at the edge of what a T cell can see — which is why the tolerance thresholds of [4.3](04-03-self-tolerance-regulation.md) have to be set as carefully as they are, and why the whole margin for a viral epitope comes from over-representation in the synthetic flux rather than from any amplification step.

### Cross-presentation

Two routes, both largely confined to conventional type 1 dendritic cells (cDC1):

- **Cytosolic route** — antigen is exported from the phagosome into the cytosol, degraded by the proteasome, then re-imported through TAP either into the ER or into the phagosome itself, which carries its own loading machinery. TAP-dependent.
- **Vacuolar route** — cathepsin S generates the peptide inside the vacuole and it is loaded onto recycling class I there. TAP-independent.

cDC1s also keep their phagosomes *deliberately inefficient* — limited acidification, NOX2-driven alkalinization — so antigen is not destroyed before it can be sampled. **A macrophage digests; a cross-presenting dendritic cell curates.**

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (mechanical — the display of one viral epitope, and the margin).** An epithelial cell is infected with influenza. At peak it synthesizes nucleoprotein (NP) at $R_{\text{syn}} = 10^{6}$ molecules per hour. Take $f_{\text{DRiP}} = 0.15$, pipeline yield $\varepsilon = 10^{-3}$, and a surface half-life of 6 h for the loaded complex. (a) Find the steady-state number of NP-peptide–class I complexes. (b) Compare with the CTL threshold. (c) How long after the onset of viral translation is the threshold crossed?

**(a)** Production rate of surface complexes:

$$R = 10^{6} \times 0.15 \times 10^{-3} = 150 \ \text{complexes per hour}.$$

$$\tau = \frac{t_{1/2}}{\ln 2} = \frac{6}{0.693} = 8.66 \ \text{h}, \qquad N_{\text{ss}} = R\tau = 150 \times 8.66 = \mathbf{1.3\times10^{3}\ \text{complexes}}.$$

That is about 1.3 percent of the cell's total class I — consistent with the measured abundance of an immunodominant epitope.

**(b)** Against a threshold of 3–10 complexes:

$$\text{margin} = \frac{1300}{10} = \mathbf{130\times} \quad\text{(and } 430\times \text{ against a threshold of 3)}.$$

**(c)** Set $N(t) = 10$ in $N(t) = N_{\text{ss}}(1 - e^{-t/\tau})$:

$$1 - e^{-t/\tau} = \frac{10}{1300} = 7.7\times10^{-3} \;\Longrightarrow\; t = -\tau\ln(0.9923) = 8.66 \times 0.00773 = 0.067\ \text{h} = \mathbf{4\ \text{minutes}}.$$

**This is the result worth keeping.** The *steady state* takes about a day to build ($\tau\ln 10 \approx 20$ h to reach 90 percent), but the *threshold* is crossed within minutes of the first viral protein being translated — long before a single virion is assembled. **Surveillance is fast not because presentation is fast but because the threshold is absurdly low**, which is only possible given the kinetic-proofreading discrimination of [2.3](02-03-t-cell-receptor.md): a T cell can afford a threshold of three complexes only because it almost never fires on the wrong ones.

**Example 2 (why you'd care — what a virus has to do to hide, and the trap that closes).** The same cell, same 1300 complexes. A virus encodes an MHC-I downregulating protein. (a) What fold reduction does it need to fall below the CTL threshold? (b) What does that cost it?

**(a)** To reach $N < 10$ from 1300:

$$\text{required reduction} > \frac{1300}{10} = 130\times, \quad\text{i.e. } > 99.2\ \text{percent of surface class I removed.}$$

**Partial downregulation buys nothing.** Cut class I by 90 percent and 130 complexes remain — more than ten times the threshold. Cut it by 99 percent and 13 remain, still detectable. **Immune evasion at class I is not a gradient; it is a cliff**, and the virus must go essentially all the way or not bother.

**(b)** Going all the way is exactly what makes the cell an NK target. From [1.4](01-04-inflammation-innate-effectors.md), NK cells integrate inhibitory receptors reading MHC class I against activating receptors reading stress ligands, and kill on **missing self**. So:

| Surface class I | CTL ([4.1](04-01-cytotoxic-t-cells.md)) | NK ([1.4](01-04-inflammation-innate-effectors.md)) | Outcome for the virus |
|---|---|---|---|
| Normal | kills | inhibited | caught |
| Reduced 10–100× | kills (13–130 complexes) | inhibited | caught |
| Reduced $>$ 1000× | blind | activated | caught |

**Two mechanisms cover each other's escape route, and the arithmetic says there is no gap between them.** This is the clearest instance of the layered design in the whole course.

**The evidence that this pincer is real is what the successful viruses had to build.** Human cytomegalovirus does not merely downregulate class I — it encodes US6 to block TAP, US2 and US11 to dislocate class I molecules to the cytosol for degradation, *and separately* UL18 (a class I decoy engaging the NK inhibitory receptor LIR-1) and UL40 (whose leader peptide loads HLA-E without TAP, keeping the NKG2A brake engaged). **The virus has to buy off both arms independently, and it spends four genes doing it.** A mechanism you can read off a table of gene functions is a mechanism you have understood.

## Watch out

- **You might state the rule as "class I presents endogenous antigen, class II exogenous."** That is a heuristic with two named exceptions built into the system: cross-presentation routes exogenous antigen onto class I, and autophagy delivers cytosolic antigen into the class II compartment. The defensible version is **the pathway is defined by the compartment of degradation, not the origin of the protein.**
- **You might think the proteasome is an immune enzyme.** It is a housekeeping machine for protein quality control ([molecular-cell-biology 4.4](../../molecular-cell-biology/lessons/04-04-protein-quality-control-degradation.md)); the immune system taps its waste stream. Only the interferon-induced subunit swap is an immunological adaptation, and it re-tunes an existing machine rather than adding a new one.
- **You might treat the invariant chain as just a chaperone.** It is a *placeholder that enforces compartmental logic*. Remove it and class II loads TAP-delivered cytosolic peptides in the ER, and the class-I/class-II division of labour — hence the CD8/CD4 division of labour — stops meaning anything.
- **You might expect abundant proteins to be the immunodominant epitopes.** Presentation is a chain of low-probability steps: correct proteasomal C-terminus, escaping cytosolic peptidases, TAP preference, allele-specific anchors ([2.4](02-04-mhc-molecules.md)), stable binding. A protein can be abundant and yield no presented epitope for a given allele. **Immunodominance is set by the pipeline, not by abundance.**
- **You might assume TAP blockade strips the surface bare.** A residue of class I persists on TAP-independent peptides, chiefly signal-sequence fragments liberated by signal peptide peptidase — which is precisely the loophole HCMV's UL40 exploits.

## One-liner

> The compartment of degradation *is* the message — cytosol to class I to CD8, endosome to class II to CD4, with the invariant chain enforcing the split and cross-presentation as the one deliberate exception; and the pipeline is so wasteful (one surface complex per thousand degraded proteins) yet so over-threshold (a thousand complexes against a threshold of three) that a virus must remove more than 99 percent of its class I to hide, at which point NK cells kill it for having none.

Predict the outcome anyway, and state what a virus would additionally need in order to make TAP blockade pay. (d) A vaccine must prime CD8 memory against a virus that never infects dendritic cells. The long surface half-life is what converts a brief antigen exposure into a window wide enough for a rare precursor to find it. For vaccine design this is one of the reasons a **depot or slow-release formulation outperforms a bolus of the same total dose** ([4.2](04-02-immunological-memory-vaccines.md)): what matters is the integral of display over the priming window, not the peak.

**P3 (a)** The **class I / cytosolic pathway** fails at the transport step. Peptides are still generated by the proteasome but cannot enter the ER, so class I molecules stay empty; empty class I is unstable at 37 °C and is not exported, so **total surface class I falls to a few percent of normal**, and the specific TAP-dependent viral epitope goes essentially to **zero** — the CTL loses its target entirely rather than seeing a reduced signal.

**(b)** **Class II presentation is unaffected.** Its peptides never pass through TAP: they are generated by cathepsins in an acidified endosome, and class II reaches that compartment with its groove already plugged by invariant chain, exchanging CLIP for peptide via HLA-DM. **The pathways are physically separate by design, so a lesion in one does not propagate to the other** — which is precisely what the invariant chain is for.

**(c)** The cell has lost more than 95 percent of its class I, far past the roughly 99 percent cliff computed in Example 2, so it now reads as **missing self** and is killed by **NK cells** ([1.4](01-04-inflammation-innate-effectors.md)).

For TAP blockade to pay, the virus needs a *second, independent* mechanism to keep NK inhibitory receptors engaged. Real solutions: a class-I decoy that binds an NK inhibitory receptor (HCMV UL18 → LIR-1), or a TAP-independent way to keep HLA-E loaded (HCMV UL40, whose leader peptide loads HLA-E without TAP, engaging CD94/NKG2A). **Evading one arm requires separately paying off the other, and that is the pincer working as designed.**

**(d)** The vaccine must exploit **cross-presentation**, or bypass the problem by putting antigen into the dendritic cell's own cytosol.

- A **purified protein** is endocytosed and goes overwhelmingly to class II → CD4 help and antibody, with little CD8 priming. It depends entirely on the inefficient cross-presentation route (and, without an adjuvant, on a dendritic cell that was never licensed to prime anything — [3.5](03-05-helper-t-cells-polarization.md)).
- **mRNA and viral-vector vaccines cause the dendritic cell to synthesize the antigen itself**, feeding it into the cytosolic pathway directly, DRiPs and all. That is why they prime CD8 memory well and a subunit protein generally does not.

**The general statement:** *a vaccine primes the arm whose compartment it loads.* Reaching CD8 memory means either arranging endogenous synthesis or engineering for cross-presentation — a design rule that falls straight out of this lesson's chain of inferences, and the argument boss problem 4 in the [syllabus](../syllabus.md) is asking for.

(This is not a toy sequence: LLFGYPVYV is the HTLV-1 Tax epitope, one of the best-characterized A\*02:01 ligands.)

**Note the second filter you cannot see from the sequence:** the proteasome must also generate that exact C-terminus. Anchor motifs give candidates; the pipeline of this lesson decides which candidates are actually displayed.

**(b)** The class II groove is **open at both ends**, so the peptide lies in an extended conformation with residues hanging out past the ends. Length is not constrained (13–25-mers), and anchors sit at relative positions P1/P4/P6/P9 of a **binding register** that can slide along the peptide. There is no fixed P2/P9 rule because there is no fixed peptide length — which is why class II epitopes are reported as nested sets sharing a core.

**(c)** MHC polymorphism is concentrated in the groove's peptide-contact residues, so **which epitopes an individual can present is allele-specific**. An A\*02-negative person is not immunodeficient; their repertoire simply covers a different slice of the pathogen's proteome. That is exactly the point of the polymorphism: a viral escape mutant that fits no allele must be invisible to *everyone*, and with hundreds of alleles segregating that is unachievable, so **polymorphism is a population-level defense** maintained by balancing selection.

At the individual level, codominant expression means a heterozygote at HLA-A, -B and -C expresses

$$2 \times 3 = \mathbf{6}\ \text{distinct class I molecules},$$

against 3 for a homozygote — a twofold broader presentation repertoire, which is the mechanistic content of heterozygote advantage at the MHC.
