# Social Choice Theory — Syllabus

> Politics & Society · Tier 2 · ~24 lessons · Prereqs: [`proofs-primer`](../proofs-primer/syllabus.md), [`game-theory-refresher`](../game-theory-refresher/syllabus.md) · Roadmap id: `social-choice`

## Goal

This is the mathematics of collective decision: what happens when many rankings, judgments, verdicts or populations must be folded into one choice, one ranking or one allocation of seats. Arrow's theorem is the starting point, not the destination; its proof is owned by [`grad-game-theory`](../grad-game-theory/syllabus.md) 5.1 and is used here as a map of escape routes. You will finish able to prove May's theorem, compute and compare the major voting rules and say which axioms each keeps, prove Gibbard–Satterthwaite by reduction to Arrow, design strategy-proof rules on restricted domains, prove Sen's liberal paradox and the List–Pettit impossibility, compute jury-theorem probabilities and say exactly which assumption a real body violates, say what grading ballots and comparable utilities do and do not escape, and apportion a legislature while naming the paradox each method risks. Where the results bear on what democracy is or should be, the course says precisely what a theorem shows and does not show, and leaves the verdict open. It deliberately skips the normative case for or against democracy ([`political-philosophy`](../political-philosophy/syllabus.md) 5.1, 5.4), spatial and electoral-competition models ([`political-economy`](../political-economy/syllabus.md)), mechanism design with money, matching and power indices ([`grad-game-theory`](../grad-game-theory/syllabus.md) Modules 5–6), Harsanyi's theorem and interpersonal comparability ([`decision-theory`](../decision-theory/syllabus.md) 5.1–5.2), computational complexity of voting, probabilistic social choice, and fair division.

## How practice works in this course

Problems follow [HUMANITIES-BUILD-BRIEF.md](../../HUMANITIES-BUILD-BRIEF.md). This is a formal course: most parts are **formal** and graded as in any math course — compute a winner, build a profile that breaks an axiom, prove a lemma. Four archetypes carry the load:

- **Formal model** — tally a profile under several rules, compute a cycle or jury probability, apportion a house. Every profile and every number in a lesson or boss problem is recomputed by script before it ships.
- **Counterexample** — build the smallest profile on which a rule violates monotonicity, consistency, participation or quota. Formal; the hardest type to fake, and the course's signature skill.
- **Diagnose** — an invented op-ed or memo says "the voters chose X" or "the panel's verdict is what the evidence supports"; name the theorem or assumption the claim silently depends on. Exegetical, graded strictly.
- **Find the crux / steelman and reply** — reserved for interpretation: is IIA a reasonable demand, should a court aggregate premises or conclusions, does a grading ballot really escape Arrow? **Evaluative**, verdict-neutral: any conclusion passes if the required moves are made, and the must-hit moves always include stating precisely what the relevant theorem does and does not assert.

Exegetical parts about what a theorem says (its hypotheses, what it forbids) are strict: misstating a hypothesis is an error even inside an evaluative answer. Modern sources (Arrow, Sen, Young, List and Pettit, Balinski and Young) are paraphrased and cited, never quoted at length.

## Dangerous Checklist

When you finish, you can:

- [ ] Prove May's theorem, and say which of its four conditions a given two-alternative rule (supermajority, weighted, status-quo default) gives up
- [ ] Compute the probability of a majority cycle under impartial culture for a small electorate, and say what the model assumes and in which direction realistic correlation moves the answer
- [ ] Tally one profile under plurality, Borda, instant runoff, Copeland and Kemeny, and build profiles on which they pick different winners
- [ ] Use Young's consistency axiom to show why no Condorcet-consistent rule is a scoring rule
- [ ] Build the smallest profile you can on which a runoff rule fails monotonicity or participation
- [ ] Exhibit a profitable manipulation of a voting rule, and prove Gibbard–Satterthwaite by reducing it to Arrow
- [ ] Test a profile for single-peakedness and single-crossing, find its Condorcet winner, and design a strategy-proof generalized median rule with phantom voters
- [ ] Prove Sen's liberal paradox and state the main escapes at full strength
- [ ] Show that proposition-wise majority voting is inconsistent on a judgment agenda, state the List–Pettit impossibility, and say which condition premise-based voting drops
- [ ] Compute jury-theorem accuracy for independent, correlated and strategic voters, and name the assumption a real committee violates
- [ ] Compute approval, range and majority-judgment outcomes, show how strategic exaggeration moves them, and say which comparability assumption lets a rule with utility inputs escape Arrow
- [ ] Apportion a house by Hamilton, Jefferson, Webster and Hill–Huntington, exhibit an Alabama paradox or a quota violation, and state the Balinski–Young impossibility

## Modules

### Module 1: The aggregation problem

What an aggregation rule is, the one clean positive theorem, and Arrow read as a map of the ways out.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1.1 | Profiles, rules and the majority relation | Say precisely what kind of object a voting rule is and what an axiom about it asserts | profile, social welfare function vs social choice function vs correspondence, anonymity, neutrality, the majority relation, Condorcet winner and loser (cycle recapped from [`grad-game-theory`](../grad-game-theory/syllabus.md) 5.1) |
| 1.2 | May's theorem | Prove that simple majority is the only two-alternative rule with May's four properties | decisiveness, anonymity, neutrality, positive responsiveness, the proof by counting, supermajority and status-quo rules as what you get by dropping one |
| 1.3 | Arrow as a map | Use Arrow's theorem to classify every later result by the axiom it relaxes | Arrow's axioms restated (proof cited to [`grad-game-theory`](../grad-game-theory/syllabus.md) 5.1), IIA as the contested axiom, quasi-transitivity and oligarchy, the escape routes: drop IIA (Module 2), restrict the domain (Module 3), change the input (Modules 4–6) |
| 1.4 | How often do cycles happen? | Compute cycle probabilities and say what they depend on | impartial culture, the 3-voter count (1/18), growth with the electorate toward Guilbaud's limit (about 8.8 percent), more alternatives, the top cycle and agenda control, what cultures with correlated preferences do to the numbers |

**Boss problem 1:** (a) A two-alternative rule changes the status quo only if at least two thirds of voters favour the change. Check it against each of May's conditions and prove that exactly one fails. *Formal.* (b) Under impartial culture with three voters and three alternatives, count the profiles with no Condorcet winner and derive the probability; then explain in two sentences why the probability rises for five voters. *Formal.* (c) "Ranked pairs" builds a social ranking by locking in pairwise majorities from strongest to weakest and skipping any that would create a cycle. Arrow guarantees it violates one of his axioms on the unrestricted domain; name the axiom and exhibit the violation on a profile of your own. *Formal.* (d) An invented op-ed cites the impartial-culture figure as proof that majority cycles are "a textbook curiosity." In 150 words, say what the model assumes, whether real electorates plausibly make cycles more or less likely than it predicts, and what evidence would settle it. *Evaluative.*

### Module 2: Voting rules and their axioms

Drop IIA and a zoo of rules appears; the axiomatic characterizations are what tell them apart.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2.1 | Scoring rules | Compute plurality, Borda and antiplurality winners and see how far they can disagree | score vectors, plurality, Borda, antiplurality, Condorcet's 81-voter example (the Condorcet winner loses under every scoring rule), Borda never elects a Condorcet loser |
| 2.2 | Consistency and Young's characterization | State which axioms single out scoring rules, and why Condorcet methods cannot satisfy them | consistency (reinforcement), anonymity, neutrality, continuity; Young's theorem (easy direction proved, hard direction sketched as a separation argument); the two-electorate counterexample for Condorcet methods; Borda's own characterization cited |
| 2.3 | Condorcet methods and Kemeny | Compute Copeland, maximin and Kemeny outcomes and say what Kemeny is optimal for | Copeland score, maximin (Simpson), Kemeny distance and the median ranking, Young–Levenglick characterization (statement), Kemeny as a maximum-likelihood estimate of a true ranking (bridge to 5.3) |
| 2.4 | Monotonicity and participation | Build profiles on which runoff rules punish support and turnout | monotonicity, the instant-runoff failure (counting cited to [`political-institutions`](../political-institutions/syllabus.md) 1.2), participation and the no-show paradox, Moulin's theorem that Condorcet methods suffer it once there are four or more alternatives and enough voters, plurality with runoff |

**Boss problem 2:** Twenty-nine voters rank four candidates: 7 B ≻ A ≻ C ≻ D, 9 D ≻ C ≻ A ≻ B, 5 C ≻ B ≻ A ≻ D, 8 A ≻ C ≻ B ≻ D. (a) Find the winner under plurality, Borda, instant runoff and Copeland, and the Kemeny ranking; show your tallies. *Formal.* (b) A different electorate of 21 voters: 6 A ≻ B ≻ C, 8 B ≻ C ≻ A, 7 C ≻ A ≻ B. Find the instant-runoff winner, then find a group of voters who, by moving that winner to the top of their ballots and changing nothing else, make it lose. *Formal (counterexample).* (c) For each rule in (a), say whether it satisfies consistency, Condorcet consistency and monotonicity, citing the lesson result that decides each. *Exegetical.* (d) An invented city council must pick one of these rules for mayoral elections. In 300 words, recommend one, stating the axiom you are willing to give up and the strongest objection from a defender of a rival rule. *Evaluative.*

### Module 3: Strategy and restricted domains

Why every reasonable rule can be gamed, the proof, and the domains where honesty and coherence come back.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3.1 | Manipulation and the first half of Gibbard–Satterthwaite | Find profitable misreports, and prove that strategy-proofness forces monotonicity and unanimity | manipulation of Borda (burying) and instant runoff, strategy-proofness (statement from [`grad-game-theory`](../grad-game-theory/syllabus.md) 5.1), strong monotonicity lemma, onto plus strategy-proof implies Pareto |
| 3.2 | Proving Gibbard–Satterthwaite | Build an Arrovian social welfare function out of a strategy-proof choice rule and invoke Arrow | the lift-to-top construction, checking completeness, transitivity, Pareto and IIA, Arrow as a black box, what the proof says about where manipulability comes from |
| 3.3 | Single-peakedness: Black and Moulin | Prove the majority relation is transitive on single-peaked profiles and characterize the strategy-proof rules there | Black's theorem for the whole majority relation (median voter theorem recapped from [`grad-game-theory`](../grad-game-theory/syllabus.md) 5.1), even electorates, peak-only rules, Moulin's generalized medians with phantom voters |
| 3.4 | Single-crossing and value restriction | Recognize the other domains where majority rule behaves, and why a second dimension destroys them | single-crossing and the representative voter theorem, a profile that is single-crossing but not single-peaked, Sen's value restriction, why multidimensional policy spaces reopen cycles (McKelvey cited to [`political-economy`](../political-economy/syllabus.md)) |

**Boss problem 3:** (a) Five voters use Borda over four candidates: 2 A ≻ B ≻ C ≻ D, 2 B ≻ A ≻ C ≻ D, 1 C ≻ B ≻ D ≻ A. Find the sincere winner, then find a misreport by the two A-voters that elects A. *Formal.* (b) Three voters have single-peaked preferences on [0, 10] with peaks 1, 3 and 9. A generalized median rule uses phantom voters at 0, 6, 7 and 10. Find the outcome, prove the voter with peak 9 cannot gain by misreporting, and find phantom positions that reproduce the ordinary median of the peaks. *Formal.* (c) Five voters, ordered 1 to 5, rank three tax levels: L ≻ M ≻ H, L ≻ H ≻ M, H ≻ L ≻ M, H ≻ M ≻ L, H ≻ M ≻ L. Show the profile is single-crossing in that order but single-peaked on no ordering of the levels, and find the Condorcet winner through the representative voter. *Formal.* (d) Explain why neither (b) nor (c) contradicts Gibbard–Satterthwaite, naming the hypothesis each one escapes. *Exegetical.*

### Module 4: Rights and reasons

Aggregation breaks in two more places: when individuals get protected spheres, and when a group must hold reasons as well as conclusions.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4.1 | Sen's liberal paradox | Prove that minimal liberalism, Pareto and unrestricted domain are incompatible | minimal liberalism (two individuals each decisive over one pair), weak Pareto, the proof, the book-reading example, why only a cycle is needed rather than Arrow's full machinery |
| 4.2 | Answers to Sen | State the main escapes at full strength and say what each gives up | meddlesome preferences and conditional Pareto (Sen, Blau), waivable rights and Gibbard's paradox of rights, rights as game forms rather than preferences over outcomes, the crux between them |
| 4.3 | The doctrinal paradox and the discursive dilemma | Show that majority voting on premises and on conclusions can disagree, and that proposition-wise majority can be inconsistent | agenda of propositions, the three-judge contract case (Kornhauser–Sager), premise-based vs conclusion-based procedures, the discursive dilemma (Pettit), consistency and completeness of a judgment set |
| 4.4 | The List–Pettit impossibility | State and prove the impossibility on the conjunctive agenda, and map the escapes | universal domain, collective rationality, anonymity, systematicity; the proof; premise-based voting as dropping systematicity; sequential priority and path-dependence; Arrow recovered as a judgment-aggregation result (Dietrich–List); group agency as an interpretation (List and Pettit, paraphrased) |

**Boss problem 4:** (a) Two people and one copy of a book. The prude ranks "nobody reads it" ≻ "the prude reads it" ≻ "the lewd reads it"; the lewd ranks "the prude reads it" ≻ "the lewd reads it" ≻ "nobody reads it". Give each person rights over the pair that involves only their own reading, add weak Pareto, and derive the cycle. *Formal.* (b) Steelman one escape from 4.2 in 150 words, then reply for a defender of a rival escape in 150. *Evaluative.* (c) Three judges decide a contract case: liability requires both a valid contract (p) and a breach (q). Judge 1 finds p and q; judge 2 finds p but not q; judge 3 finds q but not p. Compute the premise-based and the conclusion-based verdicts, and show that proposition-wise majority on {p, q, p ∧ q} yields an inconsistent set. *Formal.* (d) Say which List–Pettit condition the premise-based procedure violates, and prove it violates that one. *Formal.* (e) In 300 words, argue which procedure a multi-member court ought to use, stating what each procedure costs and replying to the strongest objection. *Evaluative.*

### Module 5: Truth-tracking

When there is a right answer, majority rule can be a very good detector of it — under assumptions that are easy to state and easy to break.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5.1 | The Condorcet jury theorem | Prove that majority accuracy rises to one with independent voters who are better than chance | binary question with a correct answer, competence p > 1/2, independence, the binomial proof of monotonicity in odd n, the limit by the law of large numbers, weighted majority with log-odds weights (Nitzan–Paroush) |
| 5.2 | When the jury theorem fails | Compute accuracy when voters are correlated or strategic, and say which assumption each case breaks | opinion leaders and correlation (Ladha, Dietrich–Spiekermann), heterogeneous competence, p < 1/2, strategic voting and pivotality (Austen-Smith–Feddersen), unanimity juries that convict the innocent (Feddersen–Pesendorfer) |
| 5.3 | Epistemic readings of aggregation | Say precisely what the truth-tracking results show about collective judgment, and what they leave open | the jury theorem as an argument form (the justification debate cited to [`political-philosophy`](../political-philosophy/syllabus.md) 5.1), Condorcet's maximum-likelihood reading of Kemeny (2.3), Rousseau's general will read through the jury theorem (Grofman–Feld; text owned by [`history-of-political-thought`](../history-of-political-thought/syllabus.md) 4.4), Hong–Page "diversity trumps ability" and its critics, the premise-based procedure as truth-tracking (4.3) |

**Boss problem 5:** (a) Voters are independent with competence 0.6. Compute majority accuracy for 3 and 11 voters, and find the smallest odd electorate whose accuracy reaches 95 percent. *Formal.* (b) Three voters with competence 0.6: voters 2 and 3 each copy voter 1 with probability 1/2 and otherwise vote independently. Compute majority accuracy and compare it with (a). *Formal.* (c) A three-member jury convicts only unanimously; the prior probability of guilt is 1/2 and each juror's private signal is correct with probability 0.7. Assuming the other two vote their signals, compute the probability of guilt a juror with an innocent signal should assign conditional on being pivotal, and show that if she convicts whenever that probability exceeds 0.6, sincere voting is not an equilibrium. *Formal.* (d) An invented memo argues that a large legislature is "almost certainly right" on any yes-or-no question. In 300 words, say which assumption of the theorem the memo most needs, whether (b) and (c) threaten it, and whether a weaker version of the argument survives. *Evaluative.*

### Module 6: Richer inputs: approvals, grades and utilities

Arrow fed on bare rankings. Give the rule more information — approvals, grades, comparable utilities — and ask what the extra information buys and what it assumes.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 6.1 | Approval voting | Compute approval outcomes, characterize approval voting, and see where it parts from Condorcet | approval ballots, sincere approval strategies, Fishburn's axiomatic characterization (statement), a Condorcet winner losing under approval, strategic thresholds |
| 6.2 | Grading ballots: range voting and majority judgment | Compare the mean-grade and median-grade rules on strategy and on intensity | range (score) voting, strategic exaggeration and the collapse to approval, majority judgment (Balinski–Laraki) and the median grade, what a common grading language assumes |
| 6.3 | Utilities in, possibility out | Say which interpersonal comparisons turn Arrow's impossibility into a possibility | social welfare functionals, Sen's restatement of Arrow for utility profiles (ordinal non-comparability), invariance classes and the rules each admits, utilitarian and leximin as possibility results (comparability concepts cited to [`decision-theory`](../decision-theory/syllabus.md) 5.2), Harsanyi's theorem as the bridge (cited to [`decision-theory`](../decision-theory/syllabus.md) 5.1) |

**Boss problem 6:** (a) Five voters: 2 rank A ≻ B ≻ C and approve only A; 2 rank C ≻ B ≻ A and approve only C; 1 ranks B ≻ A ≻ C and approves B and A. Find the approval winner and the Condorcet winner, and confirm every approval set is sincere. *Formal.* (b) Five voters grade two candidates from 0 to 10: X receives 9, 9, 3, 2, 2 and Y receives 1, 1, 5, 6, 6 (voter by voter). Find the range winner and the majority-judgment (median-grade) winner; show that voter 3 alone can flip the range outcome by exaggerating, and that no voter can change the majority-judgment outcome by changing only their own grades. *Formal.* (c) Three people and two policies: person 1 gets utility 10 from X and 0 from Y; persons 2 and 3 get 0 from X and 4 from Y. Find the utilitarian and leximin choices; then halve person 1's utility scale, show the utilitarian verdict flips, and say which invariance class permits that transformation and therefore which class the utilitarian rule cannot live in. *Formal.* (d) An invented reform pamphlet claims grading ballots "escape Arrow's theorem." In 150 words steelman the claim, then in 150 reply, saying precisely which Arrovian input assumption grading changes and what new assumption it adds. *Evaluative.*

### Module 7: Apportionment

The same aggregation problem with integers: how to round populations into seats, and why no rounding rule avoids every paradox.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 7.1 | Quotas, Hamilton, and the paradoxes | Apportion by largest remainders and build Alabama, population and new-states paradoxes | standard divisor and quota, the quota rule, Hamilton's method (Hare largest remainders, counting cited to [`political-institutions`](../political-institutions/syllabus.md) 1.3), house monotonicity and the Alabama paradox, population monotonicity, the new-states paradox |
| 7.2 | Divisor methods and Balinski–Young | Compare divisor methods by the property each keeps, and state the impossibility | Jefferson, Adams, Webster, Hill–Huntington as rounding rules (D'Hondt and Sainte-Laguë counting cited to [`political-institutions`](../political-institutions/syllabus.md) 1.4), divisor methods are population monotone but can violate quota, bias toward large or small states, the Balinski–Young theorem that no population-monotone method always satisfies quota once there are enough states |

**Boss problem 7:** Four states have populations 2,500, 1,490, 6,360 and 2,550 (thousands). (a) Compute the quotas for a 12-seat house and apportion by Hamilton's method; repeat for 13 seats and identify the paradox. *Formal.* (b) Apportion the 12-seat house by Jefferson's, Webster's and Hill–Huntington's methods, giving a divisor that works for each, and identify any quota violation. *Formal.* (c) State the Balinski–Young theorem and say which property each of the four methods gave up in (a)–(b). *Exegetical.* (d) In 300 words, argue whether a national legislature should prefer a quota method or a divisor method, stating the strongest objection to your choice and replying. *Evaluative.*
