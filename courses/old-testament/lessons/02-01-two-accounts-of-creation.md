# The Old Testament · Lesson 2.1: Two accounts of creation

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 2: The Pentateuch · Builds on: [1.2 The senses of Scripture](01-02-the-senses-of-scripture.md), [1.3 The historical-critical method and its limits](01-03-the-historical-critical-method-and-its-limits.md) · Unlocks: [2.2 Who wrote the Pentateuch?](02-02-who-wrote-the-pentateuch.md), [2.3 The primeval history](02-03-the-primeval-history.md)

## Why this matters

Read Genesis 1 and 2 straight through and the world gets made twice. First the earth begins as water and humanity comes last, male and female together. Then it begins as dry ground, the man comes first and the woman last. These are [two creation accounts](../reference.md#the-two-creation-accounts). This lesson reads each in its literal sense, sets it beside its Babylonian neighbour, and weighs what the Biblical Commission said about these chapters in 1909.

## The idea

Think of two paintings of one subject, hung side by side: a formal diagram in seven panels with a refrain in every frame, and a close-up of a gardener at work. Nobody asks which is "right" about the order of events. You ask what each shows.

Genesis 1:1–2:4a is the diagram: God (*Elohim*) speaks, and the world falls into order over six days, with a seventh of rest. Genesis 2:4b–25 is the close-up. The LORD God (*YHWH Elohim*) shapes a man from the soil like a potter, plants a garden, parades the animals past him, and builds a woman from his side. The literal sense of each is what its author meant, and neither was writing physics.

## Source

The two openings, Douay–Rheims (Genesis 1:1–3; 2:4–7):

> "In the beginning God created heaven, and earth. And the earth was void and empty, and darkness was upon the face of the deep; and the spirit of God moved over the waters. And God said: Be light made. And light was made."
>
> "These are the generations of the heaven and the earth, when they were created, in the day that the Lord God made the heaven and the earth: And every plant of the field before it sprung up in the earth, and every herb of the ground before it grew: for the Lord God had not rained upon the earth; and there was not a man to till the earth. But a spring rose out of the earth, watering all the surface of the earth. And the Lord God formed man of the slime of the earth: and breathed into his face the breath of life, and man became a living soul."

The first starting state is too much water: "void and empty" renders *tohu wa-bohu*, "formless and empty", and "the deep" is *tehom*, the primeval ocean. The second is too little water: no rain, no plants, no farmer. The divine name changes at 2:4, and so does God's way of acting. In the first account God *speaks* (*bara*, "created", a verb whose subject in this form is always God). In the second he *forms* (*yatsar*, the potter's verb) *adam*, the human, from *adamah*, the ground.

## The argument

1. **Genesis 1 is built on a pattern.** Days 1–3 *separate* the regions: light from darkness, waters above from waters below, sea from land. Days 4–6 *fill* them: lights, then fish and birds, then land animals and humans. Day 1 pairs with day 4, day 2 with day 5, day 3 with day 6. The refrain "God saw that it was good" comes seven times, and the seventh day is blessed and made holy (2:3). *In words:* days 1–3 cure the formless, days 4–6 the empty, and the week ends in Sabbath: a liturgy's rhythm, not a chronicle's.
2. **Genesis 2 runs in a different order.** The man (2:7), then the garden's trees (2:9), then the animals, brought to him because he needs a helper (2:19), then the woman (2:22). Its God comes close: he plants, breathes, and "built" the rib into a woman (2:22). *In words:* humanity is the capstone in Genesis 1 and the centre in Genesis 2.
3. **Humanity in God's [image and likeness](../reference.md#image-and-likeness).** Genesis 1:26 uses *tselem* (image, a carved or cast figure, the word used elsewhere for idols) and *demut* (likeness, resemblance). Many scholars read the pair against royal custom: a king's statue marked the land he ruled, and Genesis gives that office to every human, male and female, so dominion follows at once. Irenaeus read the two words apart. Man has the image by his making, and the likeness only "through the Spirit" (*Against Heresies* V.6.1). Most modern exegetes treat the pair as near-synonyms. *In words:* the literal sense is representation and rule; the distinction is a later theological reading.
4. **The Babylonian parallel.** In *[Enuma Elish](../reference.md#enuma-elish)*, the god Marduk kills Tiamat, the salt-water deep, and makes the world out of her corpse. In E. A. Wallis Budge's translation (1921):

   > "He slit Tiamat open like a flat (?) fish [cut into] two pieces, / The one half he raised up and shaded the heavens therewith, / He pulled the bolt, he posted a guard, / He ordered them not to let her water escape."

   Shared: a watery beginning, and a sky set up to hold back the waters above, as in Genesis 1:6–7. The differences are pointed. Genesis has no battle. *Tehom* is water, not a goddess. Hermann Gunkel (*Schöpfung und Chaos*, 1895) derived the word from Tiamat. Many Semitists now treat the two as cognates from a common Semitic root rather than a borrowing, so hedge the link. The great sea monsters are simply "created" (1:21). Sun and moon go unnamed: they are "lights", made and hung up to mark seasons (1:14–16). In *Enuma Elish* humanity is made from a slain god's blood to relieve the gods of labour. In Genesis humanity is blessed and given rule. *In words:* Genesis shares its neighbours' map of the world and denies their theology.
5. **Genre, not cosmology.** The "firmament" is *raqia*, from a verb for hammering metal thin. The Septuagint rendered it *stereoma*, "a solid thing". It holds up "the waters that were above the firmament" (1:7). *Providentissimus Deus* already held that the sacred writers described things as they appear, not their inner constitution (see [inerrancy](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/03-02-inerrancy.md)). *In words:* the account teaches that God made and ordered the dome. It does not teach that the sky is a dome.

**The spiritual senses.** The Fathers heard "in the beginning" alongside John 1:1. Augustine found the Church foreshadowed in the woman taken from the sleeping man's side (*City of God* XXII.17, NPNF):

> "that sleep of the man was the death of Christ, whose side, as He hung lifeless upon the cross, was pierced with a spear, and there flowed from it blood and water, and these we know to be the sacraments by which the Church is 'built up.'"

He hangs the typology on the literal verb "built" (2:22). As [1.2](01-02-the-senses-of-scripture.md) requires, the spiritual sense rests on the literal and does not replace it.

**Jewish readings.** Rashi, on 1:1, explains the change of name with the rabbinic pairing of *Elohim* with God's justice and the divine name with his mercy. God first meant to create under strict justice alone, but saw that the world could not endure, and joined mercy to it. The proof is 2:4, "the Lord God" (Rosenbaum–Silbermann, paraphrased). Umberto Cassuto (*The Documentary Hypothesis*, Hebrew 1941) made a modern case in the same spirit: the names differ by meaning, not by source. See [Jewish readings](../reference.md#jewish-readings).

**Mark the levels.** That God created all things is doctrine, owned by [`creation-grace-last-things`](../../creation-grace-last-things/syllabus.md). This lesson does not teach it. That two sources lie behind the two accounts (classically P for Genesis 1, dated to the exile or later, and J for Genesis 2, dated earlier) is a **scholarly hypothesis**. Its dates are **reconstructed and disputed**, and [2.2](02-02-who-wrote-the-pentateuch.md) weighs it. *Enuma Elish*'s composition date is **disputed** (often late second millennium; our copies are seventh-century). The **Biblical Commission's responses of 30 June 1909** are the hard case ([its weight](../reference.md#the-biblical-commission-and-its-weight)). Responses I–III denied that Genesis 1–3 are myths, allegories or legends without objective reality, and held that the literal historical sense may not be doubted for facts touching the foundations of the faith. They listed them: creation by God in the beginning, the special creation of man, the woman formed from the man, the unity of the race, the first parents' original state, the command, the transgression, the fall, the promise of a Redeemer. Responses IV–VIII granted freedom: where the Fathers disagreed, each may hold what commends itself; not every word must be literal; Genesis 1 does not aim at scientific precision; and *yom* ("day") may mean a natural day or a period, freely discussed. When issued, they were owed the submission Pius X required in 1907 (*Praestantia Scripturae*). The 1948 letter to Cardinal Suhard said these chapters use simple, figurative language, not modern historiography; *Humani Generis* (1950, §38) added that they still belong to history in a true sense, for exegetes to study further (authentic ordinary magisterium, [rung 3](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-04-the-ladder-of-doctrinal-authority.md)). In 1955 the Commission's secretaries freed literary and historical questions but not those touching faith and morals. So the responses' literary judgments are **free** today. Their list of faith-bearing facts is not simply void, and its doctrinal content is taught elsewhere, at that doctrine's own level ([Church teaching vs hypothesis](../reference.md#church-teaching-vs-hypothesis)).

## The synopsis

| | Genesis 1:1–2:4a | Genesis 2:4b–25 |
|---|---|---|
| Divine name | *Elohim*, "God" | *YHWH Elohim*, "the Lord God" |
| Starting state | watery deep, *tohu wa-bohu* (1:2) | dry ground, no rain, no farmer (2:5) |
| God acts by | speaking; *bara*, "created" | forming (*yatsar*), breathing, planting, building |
| Order | light, sky, land and plants, lights, fish and birds, animals, humans | man, trees, animals, woman |
| Humanity | male and female together, in the image (1:27) | man from *adamah*; woman from his side |
| Style | refrains, numbered days, Sabbath close | story, dialogue, one garden |
| Classical source label (hypothesis, 2.2) | P | J |

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean): the waters above.** Genesis 1:7 separates "the waters that were under the firmament, from those that were above the firmament". Psalm 148:4 still calls on "all the waters that are above the heavens" to praise. Literal sense: the author pictured a solid vault holding back an upper ocean, opened for rain and flood (7:11, "the floodgates of heaven"). Challoner's note makes the firmament "the whole space between the earth, and the highest stars", with the upper waters in the clouds: a harmonization with later science, not the author's meaning. The genre reading keeps what the author asserts, that the waters and the sky's order are God's doing, and lets his picture of the sky go.

**Example 2 (hard): "Let us make man" (1:26).** A plural in a chapter of singulars. The literal-sense options:

- **Plural of majesty.** A royal "we".
- **Self-deliberation.** God resolving within himself.
- **The heavenly court.** Rashi says God consulted his heavenly council, to teach that the greater should take counsel with the lesser. He adds that the very next verse, "God created" in the singular, answers anyone who would read in many creators.

The Fathers, and Challoner's note, read the plural as a hint of the Trinity: legitimate as a canonical, spiritual reading, anachronistic as the literal sense, and [`trinity-and-god` 2.1](../../trinity-and-god/lessons/02-01-the-one-god-of-israel.md) says why it proves nothing. Here the method strains. Grammar cannot decide among the three. The court reading has the best parallels (1 Kings [Douay 3 Kings] 22:19, Isaiah 6:8), but it raises the question of whether humans are made in the image of the angels too. The honest answer is a ranked list.

## Watch out

- **"The 1909 decree requires six 24-hour days."** Response VIII says the opposite: *yom* may be read either way, and exegetes may discuss it freely. Genesis 2:4 itself uses "in the day" for the whole work of creation.
- **"The early Commission decrees are dead letters."** The 1955 clarification freed literary and historical questions. It did not declare that the faith-bearing facts in Response III do not matter. Overstating their irrelevance is as much a level error as overstating their force.
- **"Two sources is Church teaching," or "forbidden."** It is a hypothesis (2.2). A Catholic may hold it, reject it, or read the final form as one deliberate diptych.
- **"The parallel proves Genesis copied Babylon."** Shared cosmology is not literary dependence, and *tehom* ~ Tiamat is contested. The comparison shows best where Genesis *differs*.

## One-liner

> Genesis gives creation twice, a seven-day liturgy and a potter's garden, and both teach through an ancient picture of the world without teaching the picture.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢)** *(Exegetical)* Genesis 1:14–19 (Douay–Rheims):

> "And God said: Let there be lights made in the firmament of heaven, to divide the day and the night, and let them be for signs, and for seasons, and for days and years: To shine in the firmament of heaven, and to give light upon the earth, and it was so done. And God made two great lights: a greater light to rule the day; and a lesser light to rule the night: and the stars. And he set them in the firmament of heaven to shine upon the earth. And to rule the day and the night, and to divide the light and the darkness. And God saw that it was good. And the evening and morning were the fourth day."

(a) List every job the passage gives the lights. (b) Name what the passage conspicuously does not say about them, the words that show it, and the most likely reason, hedged as the evidence requires. **Two sentences per part.**

**P2 (🟡)** *(Exegetical)* Aquinas, ST I q.74 a.1 (English Dominican Province), on why there are six days of work:

> "the parts of the world had first to be distinguished, and then each part adorned and filled, as it were, by the beings that inhabit it. Now the parts into which the corporeal creation is divided are three, according to some holy writers, these parts being the heaven, or highest part, the water, or middle part, and the earth, or the lowest part. ... The first part, then, is distinguished on the first day, and adorned on the fourth, the middle part distinguished on the middle day, and adorned on the fifth, and the third part distinguished on the third day, and adorned on the sixth. But Augustine, while agreeing with the above writers as to the last three days, differs as to the first three, for, according to him, spiritual creatures are formed on the first day ..."

(a) Match Aquinas's scheme to the verses of Genesis 1: for each pair of days, name the region and what fills it. (b) Name one feature of Genesis 1 that his three-region scheme fits awkwardly. (c) Say whether Augustine's first day, as reported here, is a reading of the literal sense in the sense of [1.2](01-02-the-senses-of-scripture.md)–[1.3](01-03-the-historical-critical-method-and-its-limits.md), and why. **100 words or fewer in total.**

**P3 (🔴)** *(Exegetical)* Two **invented** parish handouts:

> **A.** "In 1909 the Church's Biblical Commission ruled that Genesis 1–3 is literal history, word for word. Catholics must therefore hold six 24-hour days, and science will one day confirm that the sky has waters above it."
>
> **B.** "Scholars have shown that Genesis 1 is simply a Hebrew copy of the Babylonian *Enuma Elish*: the 'deep', *tehom*, is the goddess Tiamat. Since Vatican II the Church accepts that these chapters are myth with no connection to real events, and the old Commission decrees are dead letters."

(a) Name A's errors about what the 1909 responses said and about the genre of Genesis 1. (b) Name B's errors about the parallel and about the Church's documents. **Three sentences per part.**

<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** the lights **divide** day from night and light from darkness; they serve as **signs, seasons, days and years**, a calendar; they **shine / give light upon the earth**; and they **rule** the day and the night.

**Must hit, strict (b):** the sun and moon are **never named**. They are "lights", a "greater light" and a "lesser light", things God **made** and **set** in the firmament, with the stars added in three words ("and the stars"). The likeliest reason is **polemical**: the ordinary Hebrew words for sun and moon were also names of gods in the region (Akkadian Shamash is the sun god). Leaving them nameless makes them lamps with a job, not powers. This is the majority reading, and it should be hedged as an inference, since the text gives no reason.

**Wrong turns:** treating "to rule" as giving the lights divine power. In the text their rule is an assignment from God. Stating the anti-astral motive as proven fact. Reading "and the stars" as a cosmological claim about their size.

**Model answer:** (a) They divide day from night and light from darkness, mark signs, seasons, days and years, give light on the earth, and rule the day and the night. (b) The passage never names the sun or moon. It calls them a "greater" and "lesser light" that God "made" and "set", which most scholars read as a deliberate refusal to treat bodies worshipped as gods around Israel as anything but lamps, an inference the text itself does not state.

**P2** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** days 1/4 are **heaven**: light (1:3), then the lights (1:14–19). Days 2/5 are **water**, the middle part: the firmament separating the waters (1:6–8), then fish and birds (1:20–23). Days 3/6 are **earth**: dry land and plants (1:9–13), then land animals and humans (1:24–31).

**Must hit, strict (b):** any one of these. **Birds** fill the air "under the firmament" (1:20), not the water, yet they are day 5's work. The **lights are set in the firmament**, which is made on day 2, not day 1. **Day 1's light** is not a region at all.

**Must hit, strict (c):** **no.** As reported, Augustine reads day 1's light as the formation of **spiritual creatures** (the angels). That is not what the human author meant by light. It is a theological or spiritual reading, compatible with the literal sense but not the literal sense itself.

**Wrong turns:** calling Aquinas's scheme a modern invention. He reports it from "holy writers". Calling Augustine's reading an error. It is a legitimate reading, just not of the literal sense in the course's (post-*Divino Afflante*) usage. Credit a student who notes that Augustine himself called his reading "literal" (*De Genesi ad litteram*) in an older sense of the word.

**Model answer:** (a) Days 1/4 are heaven: light, then the lights. Days 2/5 are water: the dividing firmament, then fish and birds. Days 3/6 are earth: land and plants, then animals and humans. (b) Birds, which fill the air, are made with the fish. (c) No. Reading the first light as the angels goes beyond what the author meant by light, so it is a spiritual or theological reading that rests on the literal sense.

**P3** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** (1) The 1909 responses did **not** require every word to be literal. Response V says figurative and anthropomorphic language may be read as such. (2) Response VIII left **yom** open: a natural day or a period of time, freely discussed. (3) Response VII said Genesis 1 does not aim at **scientific** precision, and the "waters above" belong to the **ancient cosmology** the account uses, not to what it asserts.

**Must hit, strict (b):** (1) "Copy" overstates it: Genesis shares a **cosmology** with *Enuma Elish*, with **pointed differences** (no battle, sea monsters simply created, lights unnamed, humanity blessed rather than enslaved). (2) ***Tehom* ~ Tiamat** is contested; many treat the words as **cognates**, and in Genesis *tehom* is not a goddess. (3) The Church has **not** declared these chapters myth without reality. *Humani Generis* §38 (rung 3) and the 1948 Suhard letter say they belong to history in a true, non-modern sense. And the 1955 clarification freed literary and historical questions, not the faith-bearing facts of Response III, so "dead letters" overstates it.

**Wrong turns:** defending A's six days as "one permitted reading" without saying that the handout makes it *compulsory*, which is the error. Answering B by insisting Genesis is modern history, which swaps one genre error for another. Calling the Commission's documents dogma, or calling them irrelevant.

**Model answer:** (a) The Commission said the opposite of "word for word": Response V allows figurative language, Response VIII leaves the length of the "days" to free discussion, and Response VII says Genesis 1 does not aim at scientific precision. The waters above the firmament are the ancient picture of the world the account uses, not something it asserts. So nothing obliges a Catholic to 24-hour days or to an upper ocean. (b) Genesis shares Babylon's map of the world but rewrites its theology: no divine battle, no named sun and moon, humanity blessed rather than made to serve, and *tehom* may be a cognate, not a borrowed goddess. The Church has not called these chapters myth without reality; *Humani Generis* and the Suhard letter hold that they are history in a true though non-modern sense. The 1955 clarification freed literary and historical questions, but not the faith-bearing facts, so the decrees are neither dogma nor dead.

## Flashback

**From Lesson [1.3](01-03-the-historical-critical-method-and-its-limits.md) (The historical-critical method and its limits):** *(Exegetical.)* At Bethel, a man of God from Judah denounces the altar that Jeroboam I built (late tenth century; *reconstructed*). 1 Kings 13:2 (Douay 3 Kings 13:2):

> "O altar, altar, thus saith the Lord: Behold a child shall be born to the house of David, Josias by name, and he shall immolate upon thee the priests of the high places, who now burn incense upon thee, and he shall burn men's bones upon thee."

Josiah's reform came about three centuries later (conventionally 622). 2 Kings 23:15–17 (Douay 4 Kings) reports him burning bones on this altar "according to the word of the Lord, which the man of God spoke". In the same story an old prophet speaks of the high places "in the cities of Samaria" (13:32), though Samaria was built by Omri decades after Jeroboam (1 Kings 16:24). Two arguments:

- **Argument 1.** No one can know a king's name three hundred years ahead, so 13:2 was written after Josiah.
- **Argument 2.** The anachronism in 13:32 and the story's close fit with 2 Kings 23 suggest that the narrative, in its present form, was written or edited by someone who knew Josiah's reform.

(a) Which argument falls under Leo XIII's second charge in *Providentissimus Deus* §17, and why? (b) Does the other need that premise? Say what it rests on, and which later documents make such evidence the exegete's business. (c) Does accepting Argument 2's conclusion commit a Catholic to denying that the man of God prophesied? **Two sentences per part.**

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):** **Argument 1.** Its premise rules out prediction in advance, the rationalist exclusion of "all prophecy and miracle" that Leo named, so the conclusion is settled before any evidence is weighed. (This is 1.3's Form A.)

**Must hit, strict (b):** **No.** Argument 2 rests on evidence in the text, an **anachronism** and a literary fit, and argues to the likeliest setting of the story's composition. It needs no premise about what God can reveal (Form B). *Divino Afflante Spiritu* (1943) and **Dei Verbum 12** make the study of how and when ancient texts took shape a duty, and *Verbum Domini* §§34–36 forbids only the secularized premise, not the evidence. Leo's first charge, against internal evidence *alone*, was a prudential rule later documents moved past; credit an answer that says so.

**Must hit, strict (c):** **No.** Argument 2 dates the story's **present wording**, not the event behind it. Whether a man of God foretold the altar's desecration, and whether the king's name stood in the original oracle or was made explicit by a narrator who knew the fulfilment, are **scholarly questions left free**. Either answer is compatible with inspiration.

**Wrong turns:** calling Argument 2 rationalist because it concludes the passage is late; its conclusion is not the problem, its premises are what count. Calling Argument 1 acceptable because its conclusion might be true. Treating "written after Josiah" as a denial of inerrancy, when the genre and the narrator's purpose decide what the text asserts.

**Model answer:** (a) Argument 1: it assumes prophecy of a future name is impossible, which is the rationalist premise Leo condemned, and so it decides the case before looking at the text. (b) Argument 2 does not need it; it argues from an anachronism and a literary fit to the story's likeliest date, which is the kind of historical and literary study *Divino Afflante Spiritu* and Dei Verbum 12 require. (c) No: a late date for the narrative's wording leaves open whether a prophet foretold Josiah's act, and whether the name was in the oracle or supplied by the narrator is a freely disputed question.

</details>
