# The Old Testament · Lesson 5.2: Proverbs, Ecclesiastes, and the Song of Songs

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 5: Psalms and wisdom · Builds on: [5.1 The Psalms](05-01-the-psalms.md), [1.2 The senses of Scripture](01-02-the-senses-of-scripture.md) · Unlocks: [5.3 Job](05-03-job.md), [5.4 Sirach and the Wisdom of Solomon](05-04-sirach-and-the-wisdom-of-solomon.md)

## Why this matters

Three books under Solomon's name barely mention the Exodus, the covenant or the Temple. One reads like a courtier's manual, one doubts that wisdom gets you anywhere, and one is a love poem that never clearly names God. Together they show something the rest of the Old Testament does not: Israel reasoning from experience, borrowing from its neighbours, and arguing with itself.

## The idea

**[Wisdom literature](../reference.md#wisdom-literature)** asks how to live well in the world as it is. Its evidence is observation, not revelation at Sinai, and its genre was international: Egypt and Mesopotamia had instruction books long before Israel. The canon keeps three voices:

- **Confident (Proverbs).** The world is ordered. Diligence prospers, laziness starves, and "The fear of the Lord is the beginning of wisdom" (Prov 1:7).
- **Sceptical (Ecclesiastes).** Qoheleth tests that order and finds it thin. The wise man dies like the fool, and nobody knows what comes after.
- **Erotic (the Song of Songs).** Two lovers seek, find and lose each other in the countryside and the city.

Proverbs states the rule, Qoheleth presses its exceptions, and the Song celebrates a good no rule accounts for.

## Source

Proverbs 22:17–22 (Douay–Rheims), the start of a collection headed "the words of the wise":

> Incline thy ear, and hear the words of the wise: and apply thy heart to my doctrine: Which shall be beautiful for thee, if thou keep it in thy bowels, and it shall flow in thy lips: That thy trust may be in the Lord, wherefore I have also shewn it to thee this day. Behold I have described it to thee three manner of ways, in thoughts and knowledge: That I might shew thee the certainty, and the words of truth, to answer out of these to them that sent thee. Do no violence to the poor, because he is poor: and do not oppress the needy in the gate.

Look at verse 20. The Vulgate's "three manner of ways" (*tripliciter*) follows a Hebrew word that the Masoretes themselves were unsure of. The written consonants read *shilshom*, "formerly", and the marginal reading is *shalishim*, "excellent things" or "officers". In 1924 the Egyptologist Adolf Erman proposed *sheloshim*, "thirty", because an Egyptian text had just come to light that ends by telling its reader to look to its thirty chapters. Verse 19 states the collection's purpose: "that thy trust may be in the Lord".

## The argument

1. **Proverbs is an anthology.** Its headings mark separate collections: "proverbs of Solomon" (10:1), "the words of the wise" (22:17), the proverbs "which the men of Ezechias, king of Juda, copied out" (25:1), and the words of Agur (30:1) and of King Lamuel (31:1). Solomon is the patron of the genre, since 1 Kings 4:30 [Douay 3 Kings 4:30] says his wisdom surpassed Egypt's. The headings themselves say that others wrote and gathered. Chapters 1–9 are usually dated late, to the Persian period, and some sayings in 10–29 may be monarchic. **Reconstructed**, and not closely.

2. **The [Instruction of Amenemope](../reference.md#instruction-of-amenemope).** E. A. Wallis Budge acquired British Museum Papyrus 10474 at Thebes in 1888 and published it in full in 1923. In 1924 Erman listed its parallels with Proverbs, most of them in 22:17–23:11. It is usually dated to the Ramesside period (roughly 1300–1075), a **reconstructed** date. Most scholars since the mid-twentieth century hold that Proverbs depends on *Amenemope*, perhaps through an intermediate version. A minority argue for a common source or dispute the link. *In words:* an inspired writer borrowed from a pagan instruction book, edited it, and put Israel's God at its centre.

3. **[Lady Wisdom](../reference.md#lady-wisdom) (Proverbs 8).** Wisdom speaks in the first person: she was with God before the mountains, "playing before him at all times" (8:30). In its literal sense she is a poetic personification of God's own wisdom. Verse 22 turns on a verb. Hebrew *qanani* can mean "acquired", "possessed", "begot" or "created". The Vulgate chose *possedit* ("The Lord possessed me in the beginning of his ways"). The Septuagint chose *ektisen*, "created". Arius's party argued from the Greek, and the Nicene reply is told in [`trinity-and-god` 2.2](../../trinity-and-god/lessons/02-02-the-father-and-the-son-in-the-new-testament.md), [`christology` 1.4](../../christology/lessons/01-04-arius-and-the-divinity-of-the-son.md) and [`patristics` 3.1](../../patristics/lessons/03-01-athanasius.md).

4. **Qoheleth and [hebel](../reference.md#hebel).** "Qoheleth" is a participle from *qahal*, "assembly": one who gathers or addresses an assembly. The Greek rendered it *Ekklesiastes*. His key word, *hebel*, occurs more than thirty times. It means "vapour" or "breath": something real but insubstantial and quickly gone. The Vulgate's *vanitas* gave the Douay "Vanity of vanities, said Ecclesiastes: vanity of vanities, and all is vanity" (1:2). His themes are that death levels wise and foolish, man and beast (2:14–16; 3:19–20); that God's work cannot be found out (3:11; 8:17); and that the right response is to eat, drink and enjoy one's work as God's gift (3:12–13; 9:7). The epilogue (12:9–14) speaks *about* Qoheleth in the third person. Most scholars assign it to one or two editors. Whether it frames the book faithfully or domesticates it is **disputed**. The royal voice ("son of David, king of Jerusalem", 1:1) is dropped after chapter 2. The late Hebrew and Persian loanwords (*pardes*, "orchards", 2:5) lead most scholars to date the book to the Persian or Hellenistic period, often the third century. **Disputed.**

5. **The Song as love poetry.** In its literal sense the Song is a set of lyrics in which a woman and a man speak desire, absence and union. God is not clearly named. The one candidate is the "flame of Yah" (*shalhevetyah*, 8:6), which may just be a superlative. Its date is **disputed**, with proposals running from Solomon's century to the Hellenistic age. The ancient rabbis argued over its status. **Mishnah *Yadayim* 3:5** records a debate on whether the Song and Qoheleth "defile the hands", which is the rabbis' way of saying that a book is holy Scripture. Rabbi Akiva answers that no one in Israel ever disputed the Song: "all the Writings are holy, and the Song of Songs is holy of holies" (this lesson's translation). If there was a dispute, he says, it was about Qoheleth.

6. **The [allegorical reading of the Song](../reference.md#allegory-of-the-song).** Both traditions read it as a song of divine love, each in its own way. For the Targum and the midrash, the lovers are God and Israel, and the poem follows Israel's history. Rashi, on 1:2, hears the bride speaking "in her exile and her widowhood" and longing for God to love her as at first (this lesson's paraphrase of the Hebrew). His introduction also insists that no verse loses its plain sense. Among Christians, Origen made the Bridegroom the Word and the bride the Church or the soul. Jerome, writing to Damasus in 383, praised his commentary:

   > "Origen, whilst in his other books he has surpassed all others, has in the Song of Songs surpassed himself. He wrote ten volumes upon it ..." (NPNF 2nd series vol. 6)

   The Greek of the commentary is mostly lost. Its first books survive in Rufinus's Latin, and two homilies survive in Jerome's. Bernard of Clairvaux's sermons on the Song, some eighty-six of them, still unfinished at his death in 1153, carried the mystical reading into the Middle Ages. Theodore of Mopsuestia was the exception. He read the Song as a marriage poem ([`patristics` 3.5](../../patristics/lessons/03-05-allegory-and-history-alexandria-and-antioch.md)).

**Mark the levels.** That Proverbs, Ecclesiastes and the Canticle are sacred and canonical is **defined dogma**: Trent's list names them, and the point is cited from [`fundamental-theology` 3.3](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/03-03-the-canon-as-defined.md). Their inspiration ([3.1](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/03-01-inspiration.md)) covers borrowed material too. An inspired author can adopt an Egyptian saying, and what he asserts is the book's, whatever its source. As far as this lesson can find, none of the Biblical Commission's Old Testament responses ruled on these books. Solomonic authorship, the dates, the dependence on *Amenemope* and the editors of Qoheleth's epilogue are **scholarly positions with no magisterial level** ([Church teaching vs hypothesis](../reference.md#church-teaching-vs-hypothesis)). That the Song speaks of Christ and the Church is a **spiritual sense** received through the Fathers and the liturgy. It is not a definition. How far the allegory may range beyond a literal sense of human love is **freely disputed** among Catholics. For [dating discipline](../reference.md#dating-discipline): the papyrus's publication (1923) is secure, and every date given to the books themselves is reconstructed or disputed.

## The synopsis

*Amenemope* is paraphrased here from the Egyptian. Its chapter numbers follow the standard division.

| Proverbs (Douay) | *Amenemope* (paraphrase) | What changed |
|---|---|---|
| 22:17–18 "Incline thy ear ... keep it in thy bowels" | ch. 1: give your ears to the sayings and keep them in the casket of your belly | the image kept, almost word for word |
| 22:20 "three manner of ways" (emended: thirty) | ch. 30: look to these thirty chapters, which instruct | the count kept, and the Hebrew later garbled |
| 22:22 "Do no violence to the poor" | ch. 2: beware of robbing the wretched | the motive changes: "the Lord will judge his cause" (22:23) |
| 22:28 "Pass not beyond the ancient bounds" | ch. 6: do not move the boundary markers of fields | the same law of land |
| 22:29 a man swift in his work "shall stand before kings" | ch. 30: the skilled scribe is found worthy to be a courtier | a scribe's ideal made general |

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean): "vanity of vanities" (Eccl 1:2).** The Hebrew is *havel havalim*. That is the construction of "song of songs" and "holy of holies": a noun doubled into its plural makes a superlative. So the phrase means "the utmost breath", "sheer vapour". Literal sense first: Qoheleth calls the world fleeting and ungraspable, like breath on a cold morning, not worthless or sinful. The Douay's "vanity" pushes toward moral contempt for the world, and its headnote does exactly that, reading the book as meant "to withdraw the hearts and affections of men from such empty toys". That is a legitimate *moral* sense, the one the tradition drew from the book. But it is a second step, and it is not what *hebel* means.

**Example 2 (hard): Proverbs 8:22 in three Bibles.** The Hebrew *qanani* is ambiguous, the Greek says "created", and the Latin says "possessed". Literal sense first: the poet personifies God's wisdom as prior to creation and delighting in it. He is not writing about a second person in God, and nothing in the chapter asks the question Nicaea answered. The Christian reading comes second. The New Testament calls Christ the wisdom of God (1 Cor 1:24), and Colossians 1:15 echoes this chapter's language. So the Fathers read Proverbs 8 of the Son and referred "created" to the Incarnation, not to his eternal origin. The strain shows here. The Arians had a *more literal* case from their own Bible than the Nicenes had. The method's answer is that the dogma was never settled from this verse ([`trinity-and-god` 2.2](../../trinity-and-god/lessons/02-02-the-father-and-the-son-in-the-new-testament.md)). The chapter's Christological sense is a spiritual or fuller sense built on a personification, not a proof text about the Son's origin.

## Watch out

- **Hypothesis as teaching:** "The Church holds that Solomon wrote Proverbs, Ecclesiastes and the Song." The books are defined as canonical, not as Solomon's. Their own headings name Hezekiah's scribes, Agur and Lamuel.
- **Parallel as debunking:** "*Amenemope* proves Proverbs is just Egyptian wisdom." Borrowing is not a problem for inspiration, and the borrowing was edited: verse 19 adds "trust ... in the Lord", and 22:23 makes God the defender of the poor.
- **Qoheleth as nihilist, or as a disguised preacher of heaven:** *hebel* is "fleeting", not "worthless". The book neither denies God (3:14; 12:7) nor promises an afterlife (3:21, "Who knoweth").
- **Allegory against the literal sense:** the Douay's headnotes ("Christ caresses his spouse") are a spiritual reading printed as a heading. The literal sense is human love, and the spiritual sense rests on it; it does not replace it ([the senses of Scripture](../reference.md#the-senses-of-scripture)).

## One-liner

> Proverbs says the world is ordered, Qoheleth says wisdom is breath, and the Song says love is strong as death; the canon keeps all three, and the tradition reads the love song as God's without making it stop being a love song.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢)** *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b))* Ecclesiastes 3:16–22 (Douay–Rheims). "Vanity" renders Hebrew *hebel*, "vapour, breath".

> I saw under the sun in the place of judgment wickedness, and in the place of justice iniquity. And I said in my heart: God shall judge both the just and the wicked, and then shall be the time of every thing. I said in my heart concerning the sons of men, that God would prove them, and shew them to be like beasts. Therefore the death of man, and of beasts is one, and the condition of them both is equal: as man dieth, so they also die: all things breathe alike, and man hath nothing more than beast: all things are subject to vanity. And all things go to one place: of earth they were made, and into earth they return together. Who knoweth if the spirit of the children of Adam ascend upward, and if the spirit of the beasts descend downward? And I have found that nothing is better than for a man to rejoice in his work, and that this is his portion.

(a) State what the passage affirms about judgment and about death, and what it leaves open about life after death. Name the words that decide each. **Two to three sentences.** (b) Does the epilogue's "God will bring into judgment" (12:14) complete this passage or correct it? **100 words or fewer**, either verdict.

**P2 (🟡)** *(Exegetical)* Two further parallels. *Amenemope* ch. 7 (paraphrase): do not set your heart on riches; wealth gained by robbery will not stay with you but makes itself wings like geese and flies to the sky. *Amenemope* ch. 6 (paraphrase, compressed): do not move the boundary markers of fields or encroach on a widow's boundary; whoever does is caught by divine power. Proverbs 23:4–5 (Douay): "Labour not to be rich ... Lift not up thy eyes to riches which thou canst not have: because they shall make themselves wings like those of an eagle, and shall fly towards heaven." Proverbs 23:10–11: "Touch not the bounds of little ones: and enter not into the field of the fatherless: For their near kinsman is strong: and he will judge their cause against thee." (a) List two things Proverbs keeps and two it changes. (b) Say what the parallels are evidence *for* and what they do **not** settle. **120 words or fewer in all.**

**P3 (🔴)** *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b))* Song of Songs 2:8–14 (Douay):

> The voice of my beloved, behold he cometh leaping upon the mountains, skipping over the hills. My beloved is like a roe, or a young hart. Behold he standeth behind our wall, looking through the windows, looking through the lattices. Behold my beloved speaketh to me: Arise, make haste, my love, my dove, my beautiful one, and come. For winter is now past, the rain is over and gone. The flowers have appeared in our land, the time of pruning is come: the voice of the turtle is heard in our land: The fig tree hath put forth her green figs: the vines in flower yield their sweet smell. Arise, my love, my beautiful one, and come: My dove in the clefts of the rock, in the hollow places of the wall, shew me thy face, let thy voice sound in my ears: for thy voice is sweet, and thy face comely.

The Douay's note on verse 8 reads the "voice" as "the preaching of the gospel surmounting difficulties". (a) Give the literal sense of the passage (speaker, situation, imagery), and say how Rashi's tradition and Origen's would each identify the lovers. **Three sentences.** (b) Is the Douay note a legitimate spiritual sense of this passage, or a reading that has lost contact with its literal sense? **150 words or fewer**, either verdict.

<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b))*

**Must hit, strict (a):** **Judgment is affirmed:** "God shall judge both the just and the wicked" (v. 17), although injustice is seen now "in the place of justice" (v. 16). **Death is the same for all:** "the death of man, and of beasts is one ... into earth they return together" (vv. 19–20). **Life after death is left open, neither denied nor affirmed:** "**Who knoweth** if the spirit of the children of Adam ascend upward" (v. 21). The conclusion is to enjoy one's work as one's "portion" (v. 22). *Hebel* ("vanity") here means fleeting and ungraspable, not worthless.

**Must hit, any verdict (b):** read 12:14 closely: judgment of "all things that are done", including hidden things. Note that 3:17 **already** affirms judgment, so the epilogue is not introducing a new idea. State the case that the epilogue is an editor's frame (third person, 12:9–10), and the case that it continues Qoheleth's own voice. Draw a conclusion.

**Wrong turns:** reading v. 21 as a denial of the soul's survival, or as its affirmation. It is a question. Reading "man hath nothing more than beast" as metaphysics rather than as a statement about how death looks (the Douay's own note says "as to the life of the body"). Reading *hebel* as "sinful".

**Model answer (b), one of several:** Completes more than corrects. Qoheleth himself already says that God "shall judge" (3:17). What he cannot see is *when*, since the courts are corrupt and death levels everyone. The epilogue changes the voice to the third person, so an editor probably wrote it. But it presses a conviction the book already holds and does not import a foreign one. What it does add is emphasis: it makes judgment, and not *hebel*, the last word.

**P2** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** **kept:** riches that grow wings and fly to the sky (geese in *Amenemope*, an eagle in Proverbs); the ban on moving boundaries; concern for the vulnerable owner. **Changed:** the bird; the widow becomes "the fatherless" and "little ones"; the vague "divine power" becomes a named defender, the "near kinsman" (Hebrew *go'el*, the redeemer-relative) who "will judge their cause". In Israel's idiom that defender is ultimately God (compare 22:23). Proverbs also sets the riches saying under "Labour not to be rich".

**Must hit, strict (b):** evidence **for** literary contact, most likely Proverbs depending on *Amenemope* (the majority view), with Israelite editing. It does **not** settle the direction of dependence with certainty, a precise date, or anything about inspiration. Inspiration covers what the sacred author asserts, whatever his source.

**Wrong turns:** "the parallel proves Proverbs is not inspired." Assuming Egypt copied Israel without argument. Treating *go'el* as merely human, when the saying makes it a threat of divine judgment.

**Model answer:** Kept: wealth that flies off on wings, and the ban on shifting boundaries. Changed: geese become an eagle, the widow becomes the fatherless, and anonymous divine power becomes the orphans' strong kinsman who "will judge their cause", which in Proverbs points to God. The parallels show literary contact, and most scholars think Proverbs used *Amenemope*. They do not fix the direction beyond doubt, and they say nothing against inspiration.

**P3** *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b))*

**Must hit, strict (a):** **literal:** the woman speaks. She hears her lover coming "leaping upon the mountains", sees him at the wall and lattice, and reports his invitation to come out because winter is over and spring has come (flowers, the turtledove, figs, vines). He asks to see her face and hear her voice. It is a spring love lyric of courtship and invitation. **Rashi's tradition:** the lover is God and the woman is the Congregation of Israel, often read of the Exodus, God coming to redeem Israel from Egypt. Credit it without the Exodus detail if the lovers are named correctly. **Origen's:** the Bridegroom is Christ, the Word, and the bride is the Church or the individual soul.

**Must hit, any verdict (b):** state the principle that the spiritual senses are founded on the literal (1.2). Test the note against it: is there a point of contact, such as the lover's approach overcoming obstacles, or the call to "arise" at the turn of the season? Or is the gospel read into the images with no anchor? Then conclude.

**Wrong turns:** making the literal sense the Church, or denying that the passage is erotic poetry. Stating Rashi's reading as "just a love poem", which is not his reading. Treating the Douay note as a magisterial interpretation. It is an eighteenth-century editor's note.

**Model answer (b), one of several:** Legitimate, but loosely anchored. The allegory works by carrying the whole love relation over (Bridegroom to Christ, bride to Church) and not by decoding each image. At that level the approach of the beloved who overcomes mountains to call his love out of winter carries naturally into Christ seeking the Church. The note fails when it fixes "voice" as "preaching of the gospel": nothing in the image supplies that particular, and a different allegorist could as easily make it the Incarnation. So the passage's spiritual sense is sound, and this note's precision is not required by it.

## Flashback

**From Lesson [4.5](04-05-the-book-of-the-twelve.md) (The Book of the Twelve):** *(Exegetical.)* Three texts on Elijah's return (Douay–Rheims). "Anathema" in Malachi renders Hebrew *herem*, the ban: total destruction.

> **Malachi 4:4–6 (Hebrew 3:22–24), the last verses of the Twelve.** Remember the law of Moses my servant, which I commanded him in Horeb for all Israel, the precepts, and judgments. Behold, I will send you Elias the prophet, before the coming of the great and dreadful day of the Lord. And he shall turn the heart of the fathers to the children, and the heart of the children to their fathers: lest I come, and strike the earth with anathema.
>
> **Sirach 48:10 (Ben Sira, about 190–180 BC), addressed to Elijah.** Who art registered in the judgments of times to appease the wrath of the Lord, to reconcile the heart of the father to the son, and to restore the tribes of Jacob.
>
> **Luke 1:17, the angel on John the Baptist.** And he shall go before him in the spirit and power of Elias: that he may turn the hearts of the fathers unto the children and the incredulous to the wisdom of the just, to prepare unto the Lord a perfect people.

(a) In Malachi's literal sense, what is Elijah sent to do, when, and what happens if he fails? Name the two figures these closing verses put side by side. (b) Name one thing Ben Sira adds to Malachi's task, and two changes Luke makes. (c) How does the Mishnah (*Eduyot* 8:7) read Elijah's coming, and how does Luke read it? Say at what level, literal or spiritual, Luke's identification of John stands. **Two sentences per part.**

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):** Elijah is sent **before the day of the Lord** to **reconcile the generations**, turning fathers' hearts to children and children's to fathers. If he fails, God will strike the land with ***herem***, **total destruction**. The last verses of the Twelve set **Moses (the law, at Horeb)** beside **Elijah (the prophet)**.

**Must hit, strict (b):** **Ben Sira adds** that Elijah will **"restore the tribes of Jacob"**, gathering scattered Israel, and "appease the wrath of the Lord". **Luke** (any two): he **keeps only the first half** of the reconciliation and **replaces "children to their fathers"** with **"the incredulous to the wisdom of the just"**, a moral conversion. He sends John **"in the spirit and power of Elias"**, not Elijah in person. He **drops the threat** of *herem*. He makes the goal **"to prepare unto the Lord a perfect people"**, and "before him" points to the one John precedes.

**Must hit, strict (c):** **The Mishnah:** the Sages hold, citing this verse, that Elijah comes **to make peace in the world**. Stated as the Mishnah's own reading, not refuted. **Luke:** the promise is fulfilled in **John the Baptist**, who comes in Elijah's spirit and power. **Level:** Malachi's literal sense names Elijah and does not name John. Luke's identification is the **New Testament's fulfilment reading**, a **spiritual sense** resting on the literal one, not what Malachi's Persian-period hearers (a reconstructed date) understood.

**Wrong turns:** saying Malachi's literal sense predicts John. Reading "spirit and power of Elias" as Elijah returning in person, or as reincarnation. Treating Luke's changes as a careless misquotation instead of a reading. Presenting the Mishnah's view as a failed Christian reading.

**Model answer:** (a) Elijah comes before the day of the Lord to reconcile fathers and children; if he does not, God will strike the land with *herem*, total destruction. The Twelve thus end by naming Moses, whose law is to be remembered, and Elijah, the prophet to come. (b) Ben Sira adds a national task: Elijah will "restore the tribes of Jacob". Luke replaces the second half of the reconciliation with turning "the incredulous to the wisdom of the just", and sends John in Elijah's "spirit and power" rather than Elijah himself, with no threat of the ban. (c) The Mishnah's Sages read the verse to mean that Elijah comes to make peace in the world. Luke reads it as fulfilled in John, which is a New Testament spiritual reading built on a literal sense that names only Elijah.
