# The Old Testament · Lesson 6.4: The Old Testament in light of the New

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 6: Canon, deuterocanon, apocalyptic, and the Old in light of the New · Builds on: [1.2 The senses of Scripture](01-02-the-senses-of-scripture.md), [1.4 Canonical reading and the unity of the Testaments](01-04-canonical-reading-and-the-unity-of-the-testaments.md), [4.1 What a prophet is: Amos and Hosea](04-01-what-a-prophet-is-amos-and-hosea.md) · Unlocks: Boss problem 6 in the [syllabus](../syllabus.md); [`new-testament`](../../new-testament/syllabus.md)

## Why this matters

Every lesson in this course has read a text twice: in its own century, then in the Church's reading. This one asks what licenses the second reading and what limits it. Three answers go wrong: throw the Old Testament away, keep it only as a code for Christ, or treat the Jewish reading of the same books as blindness. The Church's documents reject all three, at different levels of authority. One verse, Hosea 11:1, shows how.

## The idea

Think of a novel's early chapter, reread after you know the ending. The chapter meant something the first time, and the ending does not erase that. It shows a pattern the chapter carried without announcing it. A reader who does not accept the ending can still read the chapter well; what he cannot share is the rereading.

That is the Church's picture of the Old Testament, with three refusals built in:

- **Against Marcion:** the chapter belongs in the book. The Old Testament is the word of God with permanent value, not a preface to discard.
- **Against flattening:** the chapter keeps its own meaning. Christian rereading adds to the [literal sense](../reference.md#literal-sense); it does not replace it.
- **Against contempt:** the first reading is not a misreading. The Biblical Commission calls the Jewish reading "a possible one".

**Fulfilment**, on this picture, is not a prediction ticked off but a pattern completed in a way it did not foresee.

## Source

Hosea 11:1–4, 8–9 (Douay–Rheims; its 11:1 opens with a line modern Bibles print as the end of 10:15, omitted here).

> 11:1 Because Israel was a child, and I loved him: and I called my son out of Egypt. 11:2 As they called them, they went away from before their face: they offered victims to Baalim, and sacrificed to idols. 11:3 And I was like a foster father to Ephraim, I carried them in my arms: and they knew not that I healed them. 11:4 I will draw them with the cords of Adam, with the bands of love ... 11:8 How shall I deal with thee, O Ephraim, shall I protect thee, O Israel? how shall I make thee as Adama, shall I set thee as Seboim? my heart is turned within me, my repentance is stirred up. 11:9 I will not execute the fierceness of my wrath ... because I am God, and not man: the holy one in the midst of thee ...

Challoner's note on "my son" reads: "Viz., Israel. But as the calling of Israel out of Egypt, was a figure of the calling of Christ from thence; therefore this text is also applicable to Christ". A translator's note is not Church teaching, but it states the method: the literal referent first, then the figure.

## The argument

1. **Hosea's literal sense.** The oracle belongs to the northern kingdom before its fall in **722** (secure). Hosea's career runs from about 750 to the 720s, a **reconstructed** date from the kings named in 1:1. The Hebrew reads *ki na'ar yisra'el va-ohavehu, u-mimmitsrayim qarati livni*: "when Israel was a boy, I loved him, and out of Egypt I called my son" (the lesson's translation). Every verb is past. The "son" is the people, as in Exodus 4:22: "Israel is my son, my firstborn." The poem is a father's lament: he called, the son ran after the Baals (11:2), and the father who carried him (11:3) cannot destroy him (11:8–9). *In words:* Hosea 11:1 remembers the Exodus to show how deep God's love is and how bad the betrayal. It predicts nothing.

2. **Matthew's reading.** Matthew 2:15 quotes the verse after the flight into Egypt: "Out of Egypt have I called my son." [`new-testament` 2.2](../../new-testament/lessons/02-02-matthew.md) reads the link: God's son is called out of Egypt, first Israel, now Jesus. That is [typology](../reference.md#typology) ([1.2](01-02-the-senses-of-scripture.md)). From the Old Testament side, two things matter. The typology *needs* Hosea's literal sense: without Israel's Exodus there is no pattern to repeat. And Matthew follows the Hebrew. The Septuagint reads "I called *his children*" (*ta tekna autou*), which would not serve him. In *Lives of Illustrious Men* 3 (NPNF 2nd ser. vol. 3) he says Matthew, when he quotes the Old Testament, "does not follow the authority of the translators of the Septuagint but the Hebrew", and he gives this verse as his example.

3. **Dei Verbum 14–16 (1965), paraphrased.** No. 14: God chose a people and made covenants with Abraham and through Moses, and the Old Testament books remain the true word of God and **permanently valuable**. No. 15: the old covenant was ordered above all to prepare for Christ, to announce him by prophecy and to signify him by types. The books contain some things that are "incomplete and temporary", yet they show a true divine pedagogy, and in them the mystery of salvation is present in a hidden way. No. 16 (Augustine's "hidden in the Old, disclosed in the New", read in [1.4](01-04-canonical-reading-and-the-unity-of-the-testaments.md)): the Old Testament books gain their full meaning in the New *and in turn shed light on it*. *In words:* the Old Testament keeps its value, prepares for Christ, and lights up the New in return.

4. **Marcion as the foil.** About 144, Marcion rejected the Jewish scriptures and the God they describe ([`church-history` 1.2](../../church-history/lessons/01-02-bishops-canon-and-the-parting-of-the-ways.md)). The Catechism names the error. CCC 121 says the Old Testament is an indispensable part of Scripture, its books inspired and of permanent value, "for the Old Covenant has never been revoked". CCC 123 says the Church has always opposed rejecting the Old Testament on the pretext that the New has made it void, and it calls that idea **Marcionism**.

5. **The Biblical Commission, 2001.** *The Jewish People and their Sacred Scriptures in the Christian Bible*, paraphrased:
   - **No. 19.** "Old Testament" does not mean outdated; the post-apostolic Church resisted Marcion.
   - **No. 21.** Fulfilment is "an extremely complex" notion. It is not literal, and prophecies are not photographic previews of later events. Every text had a meaning for its own hearers first. The Christian sees the movement toward Christ *retrospectively*, starting from the apostolic preaching and not from the text alone. An apologetic that overstressed prophecy as proof is to be discarded; it bred harsh judgments on Jews.
   - **No. 22.** Christians cannot read the Bible as Judaism does, because that would mean accepting Judaism's presuppositions. But they "can and ought to admit that the Jewish reading of the Bible is a possible one", in continuity with the Second Temple Scriptures and analogous to the Christian reading. Each expresses its own faith, so the two are "irreducible".

6. **The [Jewish reading](../reference.md#jewish-readings) of Hosea 11:1, as its tradition gives it.** Rashi takes the son to be Israel. He reads the calling as God's call, through his prophets, for Israel to cleave to his Torah. On 11:3 he identifies the one who carried them in his arms as Moses, citing Numbers 11:12.

7. **Nostra Aetate 4 (1965), paraphrased.** The Church received the Old Testament through the people with whom God made the ancient covenant. She draws her life from the root of the olive tree onto which the Gentiles were grafted. The Jews are not to be presented as rejected or accursed by God as if Scripture taught it. **Supersessionism** (replacement theology) is the view that the Church simply replaces Israel, so that God's covenant with the Jewish people is void. Nostra Aetate 4 and CCC 121 close off its crude form. The rest of the question belongs to [`ecclesiology-and-mariology`](../../ecclesiology-and-mariology/syllabus.md) and [`apologetics-foundations`](../../apologetics-foundations/syllabus.md).

**Mark the levels.** [Church teaching vs hypothesis](../reference.md#church-teaching-vs-hypothesis). That the Old Testament books are sacred and canonical, and that one God is the author of both Testaments, is **defined dogma** (rung 1; Trent, Session IV, via [`fundamental-theology` 3.3](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/03-03-the-canon-as-defined.md)). Marcionism contradicts it. **Dei Verbum 14–16** and **Nostra Aetate 4** are conciliar teaching that defines nothing: **authentic magisterium** (rung 3 of [the ladder](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-04-the-ladder-of-doctrinal-authority.md)). **CCC 121–123** carries the weight of its sources. **The 2001 document** comes from the post-1971 Commission. Ratzinger signed its preface as the Commission's president, but it is **not a magisterial act**, and "a possible one" is an advisory body's judgment ([the Commission's weight](../reference.md#the-biblical-commission-and-its-weight)). That Hosea 11:1 means Israel's Exodus is the **literal sense**, which no Church document disputes. That Matthew's reading is true is Scripture's own reading. *How* it works, whether as typology, as a fuller sense ([*sensus plenior*](../reference.md#sensus-plenior)) or in some other way, is **freely disputed** (rung 5). Dates: 722, 1965, 2001 secure; Hosea's career and Marcion's break reconstructed.

## Map of positions

Four readings of Hosea 11:1:

| | Marcionite | Flattening | Jewish (Rashi) | The Church's documents |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Who is the son? | Irrelevant: the book is discarded | Really Christ; Israel is incidental | Israel | Israel (literal), then Christ (typological) |
| Hosea's own meaning | Rejected with the book | Overwritten | The whole meaning | Kept and needed |
| Matthew 2:15 | Proof the old book is superseded | The verse's "real" meaning | Not accepted | A fuller meaning seen in hindsight (PBC 2001 no. 21) |
| Standing | Contradicts defined dogma | Contrary to DV 14–16 and PBC 2001 | "A possible" reading (PBC 2001 no. 22) | DV 14–16, rung 3 |

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean): the Passover lamb.** Exodus 12:46 commands of the lamb: "neither shall you break a bone thereof." John 19:36 says of the crucified Jesus: "that the scripture might be fulfilled: You shall not break a bone of him." (Some commentators also hear Psalm 34:20 [Douay 33:21].) Run the method. *Literal sense:* a ritual law about eating the Passover whole, in one house. *New Testament reading:* the lamb whose bones stay whole prefigures Christ. *Test:* the typology works only because the law was real and kept. Nothing in Exodus is emptied; the meal remains the Exodus memorial, and Christians see a further figure in it.

**Example 2 (hard): a "new covenant" in the Old Testament itself.** Jeremiah 31:31 is already in the Old Testament: "I will make a new covenant with the house of Israel, and with the house of Juda." So newness is not a Christian import, and the promise is made to Israel and Judah ([4.4](04-04-jeremiah-and-ezekiel.md)). Hebrews quotes it and draws its own inference (8:13): "in saying a new, he hath made the former old. And that which decayeth and groweth old is near its end." Here the method strains: read alone, 8:13 sounds supersessionist, and CCC 121 says the Old Covenant has never been revoked. Three moves keep both texts in play. (i) Hebrews argues about the Levitical priesthood and sacrifice, and [`new-testament` 6.2](../../new-testament/lessons/06-02-hebrews.md) notes that 8:13 does not settle how Hebrews bears on Judaism today. (ii) "Incomplete and temporary" (DV 15) is said of *some things* in the books, such as the ceremonial law ([2.6](02-06-covenant-and-law.md)), not of the covenant relationship as a whole. (iii) Nostra Aetate 4 rules out reading Scripture as a curse on the Jews. How "old" and "never revoked" fit together is live among Catholic theologians; no document settles it.

## Watch out

- **Teaching stated as hypothesis:** "Since the 2001 document isn't magisterial, a Catholic may call Jews cursed readers of their own Bible." Nostra Aetate 4 is conciliar teaching, and it forbids presenting the Jews as rejected or accursed on the strength of Scripture. The advisory status of the 2001 document does not remove that.
- **Hypothesis stated as teaching:** "The Church teaches that the Jewish reading is valid." The phrase "a possible one" comes from the Biblical Commission, which is not the Magisterium. It also says less than "valid": the two readings are irreducible.
- **Fulfilment as prediction.** Matthew does not claim Hosea foresaw Jesus; Hosea's verb is past. Read it as a forecast and you misread both texts.
- **Flattening.** "The real meaning of Hosea 11:1 is Christ" throws away the literal sense the typology needs.

## One-liner

> The Church reads the Old Testament forward from its own century and backward from Christ. Marcion discards it, flattening overwrites it, and contempt for Jewish readers mistakes a first reading for a misreading. Hosea's son is Israel, and that is why Matthew can find Jesus in him.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢)** *(Exegetical)* Read the Source (Hosea 11:1–4) and Matthew 2:14–15 (Douay–Rheims): "Who arose, and took the child and his mother by night, and retired into Egypt: and he was there until the death of Herod: That it might be fulfilled which the Lord spoke by the prophet, saying: Out of Egypt have I called my son."
(a) In Hosea, who is "my son", when does the calling happen, and which words decide both? (b) What link lets Matthew use the verse, and why would the Septuagint's wording, "I called his children", not serve him? **Two sentences per part.**

**P2 (🟡)** *(Exegetical)* Assign each statement its source and level: a rung on the ladder (name the act), "no magisterial act (advisory)", or "freely disputed". **One line each.**

1. God, the author of both Testaments, arranged that the New be hidden in the Old and the Old be made manifest in the New.
2. Christians should admit that the Jewish reading of the Bible is a possible reading.
3. Scripture may not be used to present the Jews as rejected or cursed by God.
4. The Church has always opposed rejecting the Old Testament on the pretext that the New has rendered it void.
5. Matthew 2:15 draws on a fuller sense that God intended in Hosea 11:1 but Hosea did not know.

**P3 (🔴)** *(Exegetical)* An **invented** sermon paragraph:

> "Brothers and sisters, the Old Testament is the book of an angry God, and it was only ever a placeholder. That is why we call it 'old'. Hosea 11:1 was always really about Jesus; the prophet was predicting the Holy Family's flight. Now that Christ has come, we need those books only to find him hidden in them. The Jews who still read them without Christ are reading a cancelled contract, as Hebrews 8:13 says."

Name **four** errors. For each, give the text or document that corrects it and its level. **150 words or fewer.**

<details>
<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** the son is **Israel, the people**. The words that decide it are "Israel was a child" in parallel with "my son", and the plural "they" of 11:2, who sacrificed to the Baals. Exodus 4:22 is a supporting parallel. The calling is **past**: the Exodus, remembered ("I loved him", "I called", "I carried them"). It is not a prediction.

**Must hit, strict (b):** the link is the **pattern**. God's son is called out of Egypt, first Israel and now Jesus: typology, which depends on Hosea's literal sense. The Septuagint's "his children" loses the singular **"my son"**, which carries the link to Jesus as Son. Matthew follows the Hebrew (Jerome, *Illustrious Men* 3).

**Wrong turns:** calling Hosea 11:1 a messianic prediction. Saying Matthew "misunderstood" Hosea. Typology does not claim that Hosea meant Jesus. Answering (b) by saying Matthew quotes the Septuagint.

**Model answer:** (a) "My son" is Israel: "Israel was a child" stands in parallel with it, and the next verse's "they" sacrificed to Baalim. The calling is the Exodus, told in the past tense as a memory. (b) Matthew reads a pattern: the son God called out of Egypt, Israel, is relived by Jesus, who also returns from Egypt. The Greek "his children" drops the singular "my son" on which that link hangs, and Matthew follows the Hebrew.

**P2** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict:**
1. **Dei Verbum 16**, citing Augustine: conciliar teaching, **rung 3** (authentic magisterium). Its root, that one God authors both Testaments, is defined at Trent (rung 1). Credit either answer if the two layers are named.
2. **PBC 2001, no. 22: no magisterial act (advisory).**
3. **Nostra Aetate 4: rung 3.**
4. **CCC 123**, which carries the weight of its sources. Rejecting the Old Testament also contradicts the **defined** canon (Trent, rung 1).
5. **Freely disputed (rung 5)**: the *sensus plenior* theory. Credit a note that the 1993 document keeps the term, and that document is advisory too.

**Wrong turns:** ranking 2 as Church teaching because Ratzinger signed the preface. Calling 3 "defined". Treating 5 as settled because Matthew's use is canonical: the *fact* of fulfilment is Scripture's, but the *theory* of how it works is not.

**Model answer:** (1) DV 16, rung 3, resting on a rung-1 dogma. (2) PBC 2001 no. 22, advisory. (3) NA 4, rung 3. (4) CCC 123, with the weight of DV 14–16 and Trent behind it. (5) Freely disputed.

**P3** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict:** any four, each with its corrector.
- **"Placeholder" and "angry God":** DV 14 and CCC 121, the books' **permanent value** (rung 3). CCC 123 names this **Marcionism**, contrary to the defined canon (rung 1).
- **"'Old' means cancelled":** PBC 2001 no. 19 (advisory). CCC 121: the Old Covenant "has never been revoked".
- **"Really about Jesus ... predicting":** Hosea 11:1 is past tense and its son is Israel (literal sense). Matthew's link is typology. PBC 2001 no. 21 rejects "photographic" prediction (advisory).
- **"Only to find him":** DV 15 lists the books' own goods (teaching on God, wisdom, prayers), and DV 16 says they shed light on the New.
- **The Jews and Hebrews 8:13:** Nostra Aetate 4 (rung 3) forbids presenting the Jews as rejected on the strength of Scripture. Hebrews 8:13 concerns the Levitical cult. PBC no. 22 (advisory) calls the Jewish reading "a possible one".

**Wrong turns:** correcting the preacher by denying that Christ fulfils the Old Testament, which DV 15–16 teaches. Citing the 2001 document as if it bound.

**Model answer:** (1) "Placeholder": DV 14 and CCC 121 say the books keep permanent value (rung 3). Rejecting them is the Marcionism CCC 123 names, contrary to Trent's canon (rung 1). (2) "Old" does not mean void: PBC 2001 no. 19 (advisory), and CCC 121, "never revoked". (3) Hosea looks back on Israel's Exodus. Matthew reads a type, not a forecast: PBC no. 21 (advisory). (4) Nostra Aetate 4 (rung 3) forbids calling the Jews rejected on scriptural grounds. Hebrews 8:13 is about the Levitical cult, and PBC no. 22 calls the Jewish reading possible.

## Flashback

**From Lesson [6.2](06-02-tobit-judith-baruch-and-the-maccabees.md) (Tobit, Judith, Baruch, and the Maccabees):** *(Exegetical.)* The death of Antiochus IV Epiphanes is told three times (Douay–Rheims, condensed):

- **1 Maccabees 6:1–16.** He fails to plunder a rich temple at Elymais in Persia. Then he hears that the Jews have routed Lysias and thrown down "the abomination which he had set up upon the altar". He "fell sick for grief" and says: "I remember the evils that I have done in Jerusalem ... behold I perish with great grief in a strange land". He dies "in the year one hundred and forty-nine". The altar had been rededicated in year 148 (4:52).
- **2 Maccabees 9.** Driven out of Persepolis for trying to rob its temple, he swears to make Jerusalem "a common burying place of the Jews". Then "the Lord, the God of Israel ... struck him with an incurable and an invisible plague" in his bowels, "very justly, seeing he had tormented the bowels of others" (9:5–6). He falls from his chariot, worms swarm from his body, and he promises "that he would become a Jew himself" (9:17). He dies "in a strange country, among the mountains" (9:28). Only after this (10:1–3) does Judas purify the temple.
- **2 Maccabees 1:13–16**, inside a letter from "the people that is at Jerusalem ... and Judas" (1:10) that the book copies out at its head. The king goes to the temple of the goddess Nanea in Persia to collect its treasure as a dowry. Her priests shut the doors, "cast stones and slew the leader", and cut him to pieces.

(a) Name two things all three accounts share and two points on which they conflict. (b) Say what each book is doing with the story, given its genre, and why 1:13–16 raises a different question about what the inspired author asserts. (c) An **invented** note says: "At least two of these must be false, so 2 Maccabees contains errors, contrary to the Church's teaching on inerrancy." Name the step it skips. **Two sentences per part.**

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):** *Shared* (any two): Antiochus is **in Persia after trying to take a temple's wealth**; he **dies far from home** ("a strange land", "a strange country"); his death is **tied to his crimes against Jerusalem** (his own confession in 1 Macc 6:12–13; God's stroke in 2 Macc 9; "Blessed be God ... who hath delivered up the wicked" after the letter's report, 1:17). *Conflicting* (any two): **how he dies** (sickness from grief in bed; a plague, a fall and worms; stoned and hacked apart by priests); **where** (after Elymais, heading back toward Babylonia; near Ecbatana, "among the mountains"; inside Nanea's temple); **the order of events** (1 Maccabees: death in year 149, after the rededication in 148; 2 Maccabees: death in ch. 9, before the purification in ch. 10); **his repentance** (remorse for Jerusalem vs a vow to free it and become a Jew).

**Must hit, strict (b):** **1 Maccabees** is sober dynastic history, dated by the Seleucid era. Even its moral lesson is spoken by the king himself, not added by the narrator. **2 Maccabees 9** is **theological history**, an epitome built to show God's justice: punishment fits the crime (bowels for bowels, "as himself had treated others", 9:28), the man who would "reach to the stars" is brought to the ground, and the tyrant falls before the temple is restored. **1:13–16** is a **quoted document**. The epitomist asserts that the letter said this; he does not thereby vouch for every report in it. Its account differs from his own in chapter 9, so he evidently did not make it his own.

**Must hit, strict (c):** it skips **genre and the author's assertion**. Inerrancy concerns **what the sacred writer asserts**, read in its literary form (Dei Verbum 11–12; [`fundamental-theology` 3.2](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/03-02-inerrancy.md); [literary forms](../reference.md#literary-forms)). Before calling a conflict an error, you have to ask what each account asserts, and whether a copied letter is asserted at all. Levels: 2 Maccabees' canonicity is **defined dogma** (Trent); which account is closest to the event is a **historical judgment with no magisterial level**.

**Wrong turns:** harmonizing all three into one death. Taking 1 Maccabees' Elymais and 2 Maccabees' Persepolis as proof of error without asking what each writer claims to know. Treating 2 Maccabees 9's stylized punishment as a medical report. Saying the letter must refer to Antiochus IV beyond doubt: some scholars think it confuses him with his father Antiochus III, who was killed while plundering a temple in Elymais in 187 (**secure**). Swinging the other way and calling 2 Maccabees "only theology, so not about a real king".

**Model answer:** (a) All three put Antiochus in Persia after a raid on a temple's treasure, and all three see his death far from home as judgment for what he did to Jerusalem. They conflict on how he died (grief, a plague of worms, or stoning by priests) and on the order of events: 1 Maccabees dates his death after the rededication, and 2 Maccabees narrates it before the purification. (b) 1 Maccabees reports, and lets the dying king draw the moral himself. 2 Maccabees 9 shapes the story to display God's measure-for-measure justice. In 1:13–16 the author copies a letter, so what he asserts is that the letter said this, not that its account is exact. (c) The note skips the question of what each author asserts in his genre, since inerrancy attaches to that and not to every detail as a chronicle entry or to every quoted report. That the book is canonical is defined; which death happened is history.

</details>
