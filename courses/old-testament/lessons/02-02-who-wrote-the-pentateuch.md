# The Old Testament · Lesson 2.2: Who wrote the Pentateuch?

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 2: The Pentateuch · Builds on: [1.3 The historical-critical method and its limits](01-03-the-historical-critical-method-and-its-limits.md), [2.1 Two accounts of creation](02-01-two-accounts-of-creation.md) · Unlocks: [2.3 The primeval history](02-03-the-primeval-history.md), [2.6 Covenant and law](02-06-covenant-and-law.md), [3.3 Two kingdoms and two falls](03-03-two-kingdoms-and-two-falls.md)

## Why this matters

For two thousand years Jews and Christians called Genesis to Deuteronomy "the books of Moses", and Jesus cites "the book of Moses" (Mark 12:26). For two hundred and fifty years scholars have argued that the five books were woven from several hands over several centuries. The Church once ruled on the question with binding force and later loosened that ruling. So this lesson asks two things: what the evidence is, and what a Catholic is and is not bound to say about it. Overstate either and you misreport the Church.

## The idea

Read slowly and you trip over three kinds of data.

- **Doublets.** The same kind of story is told twice, or three times, with different details. There are two creation accounts ([2.1](02-01-two-accounts-of-creation.md)). There are two [flood strands](02-03-the-primeval-history.md) interleaved in one story. A patriarch passes his wife off as his sister three times (Gen 12, 20, 26). Bethel is named twice (Gen 28:19; 35:15), and Jacob is renamed Israel twice (32:28; 35:10). See [doublets](../reference.md#doublets).
- **Divine names.** Some passages call God *Elohim* ("God"), others *YHWH* (the proper name, rendered "the Lord"). Yet Exodus 6:3, below, says the patriarchs did not know the name YHWH.
- **Post-Mosaic notices.** "The Chanaanite was at that time in the land" (Gen 12:6) looks back on a vanished age. Edom's kings reigned "before the children of Israel had a king" (Gen 36:31), which assumes Israel has one. And Deuteronomy 34 narrates Moses's death and burial, adding that "there arose no more a prophet in Israel like unto Moses" (34:10), a long retrospect.

The tradition noticed the last category first. A baraita (an early rabbinic teaching) in the Babylonian Talmud (Bava Batra 14b–15a) records a dispute. Rabbi Yehuda held that Joshua wrote the Torah's last eight verses, because Moses could not write "Moses died there". Rabbi Shimon held that God dictated them and Moses wrote them with tears. Josephus has Moses record his own death in advance so that no one would say he had gone to God (*Antiquities* IV.8.48). Challoner's note at Deut 34:5 assigns the chapter to "Josue, or ... some of the prophets". So even the traditional view allowed some writing after Moses. The modern question is how much, and how the rest was put together.

## Source

Exodus 6:2–3, with Genesis 4:26 and 15:7 (Douay–Rheims):

> "And the Lord spoke to Moses, saying: I am the Lord That appeared to Abraham, to Isaac, and to Jacob, by the name of God Almighty: and my name ADONAI I did not shew them." (Ex 6:2–3)
>
> "But to Seth also was born a son, whom he called Enos: this man began to call upon the name of the Lord." (Gen 4:26)
>
> "And he said to him: I am the Lord who brought thee out from Ur of the Chaldees ..." (Gen 15:7)

The Douay's "ADONAI" follows the Vulgate. The Hebrew of Ex 6:3 has the four-letter name, which Jewish readers pronounce *Adonai*, "my Lord", as Challoner's own note explains. The lesson's translation of the Hebrew: "I appeared to Abraham, Isaac and Jacob as *El Shaddai*, but by my name YHWH I did not make myself known to them." Now set that beside Gen 4:26, where people call on the name YHWH before the flood, and Gen 15:7, where God introduces himself to Abraham as YHWH. Read flatly, the texts disagree about when the name was revealed. That tension is the pivot of the whole debate.

## The argument

1. **The founders.** The medieval Jewish commentator Abraham ibn Ezra hinted at post-Mosaic verses. Baruch Spinoza (*Theological-Political Treatise*, 1670, ch. 8) argued that Moses did not write the Pentateuch. The French Oratorian priest Richard Simon (*Critical History of the Old Testament*, 1678) proposed that Moses's material was compiled and edited by later public scribes. Bossuet had the book suppressed. Jean Astruc, a physician (*Conjectures*, 1753), sorted Genesis into columns by divine name and argued that Moses had combined earlier memoirs.

2. **The documentary hypothesis.** W. M. L. de Wette (1805) linked Deuteronomy to the law book found under Josiah (2 Kings 22). Julius Wellhausen gave the theory its classic form (*Geschichte Israels* I, 1878, retitled *Prolegomena to the History of Israel* in 1883). He posited four continuous [sources](../reference.md#documentary-hypothesis): **J** (Yahwist, Judah), **E** (Elohist, north), **D** (Deuteronomy) and **P** (Priestly). A redactor combined them. His decisive argument concerned worship. The early historical books show many altars. Deuteronomy demands one chosen place. P simply assumes a single sanctuary. So P comes last, and the law as a finished system comes after the prophets. *In words:* the order is J, E, D, P, and the Priestly law is the latest layer, not the earliest.

3. **Its dates.** The classical dates are J in the tenth or ninth century BC, E in the ninth or eighth, D in the seventh (Josiah's reform, conventionally 622), and P exilic or after. All of them are **reconstructed**, and every one has been [disputed](../reference.md#dating-discipline). Only the anchors outside the Pentateuch (722, 587/586, 539) are secure.

4. **The collapse.** In the 1970s the consensus broke. John Van Seters (*Abraham in History and Tradition*, 1975) dated the Yahwist to the exile and treated him as an author who supplemented earlier material. H. H. Schmid (*Der sogenannte Jahwist*, 1976) made a similar move. Rolf Rendtorff (*Das überlieferungsgeschichtliche Problem des Pentateuch*, 1977) argued that the large blocks grew separately and were joined late: primeval history, patriarchs, exodus, Sinai. On his view no continuous J ran through them. Erhard Blum (1984; 1990) described two post-exilic compositions, a Deuteronomistic one and a Priestly one. *In words:* the "sources" dissolved into blocks and later layers of editing.

5. **Where the field stands.** In Europe most scholars now work with a [Priestly/non-Priestly model](../reference.md#supplementary-hypothesis). P is still identified, but the non-Priestly material is not divided into J and E. Many, such as Konrad Schmid, argue that Genesis and the exodus story were first joined in a single narrative by P. In North America and Israel the [neo-documentarians](../reference.md#neo-documentary-hypothesis) revived four continuous sources, redefined. They are Baruch Schwartz, Joel Baden (*The Composition of the Pentateuch*, 2012) and Jeffrey Stackert. Their test is plot: contradictory accounts of *what happened* mark separate documents. Style and divine names are secondary. A third group reads the final form as a deliberate literary unity and leaves sources open. Umberto Cassuto, a Jewish scholar, argued (lectures published in Hebrew in 1941) that the names were chosen for their meaning: *Elohim* for God as creator and ruler of all, YHWH for Israel's covenant God. **There is no consensus.**

**Mark the levels.** ([Church teaching vs hypothesis](../reference.md#church-teaching-vs-hypothesis).) **Defined dogma:** the five books are sacred, canonical and inspired, with God as their author ([`fundamental-theology` 3.1](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/03-01-inspiration.md), [3.3](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/03-03-the-canon-as-defined.md)). Trent's canon lists them as "the five books of Moses". That is the traditional title of the books it defines as canonical. It is not a definition of who wrote them. **The [Biblical Commission](../reference.md#the-biblical-commission-and-its-weight):** its response of 27 June 1906 said the critics' arguments did not justify denying Mosaic authorship. In the same response it allowed that Moses may have entrusted the writing to secretaries and used written or oral sources, and that later inspired additions, glosses and scribal errors crept in. Under Pius X's 1907 motu proprio, Catholics owed it submission then. On 16 January 1948 the Commission's secretary, J.-M. Vosté, O.P., wrote to Cardinal Suhard of Paris. His letter said the 1906 and later replies were no hindrance to further scientific study. It also said that no one now doubted the existence of sources or a gradual growth of the Mosaic laws. The 1955 Miller–Kleinhans clarification is covered in [1.3](01-03-the-historical-critical-method-and-its-limits.md). After 1971 the Commission became consultative. Neither extreme is accurate: 1906 was never formally revoked, and it does not bind today's exegete to a composition model. **Freely disputed:** every model in steps 2–5, and how much of the Pentateuch goes back to Moses. **Dates:** composition dates are reconstructed or disputed. 1906 and 1948 are secure.

## Map of positions

| Model | Units | How joined | Ex 6:3 is ... | Names |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mosaic, substantial | Moses's work plus sources and later additions | Moses and scribes; later glosses | a claim about the name's meaning | tradition; Rashi (below) |
| Documentary | J, E, D, P, continuous | a redactor combines them | P's independent claim, contradicting J | Wellhausen |
| Supplementary / blocks | blocks, then compositions | later authors extend earlier text | a problem: P wrote it knowing non-P texts | Van Seters, Rendtorff, Blum |
| Neo-documentary | J, E, D, P, redefined by plot | a compiler preserves each | the textbook case of a source contradiction | Schwartz, Baden, Stackert |
| Final form | left open | read as designed | a deliberate reinterpretation | Cassuto |

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean): Bethel and "Israel", twice.** In Gen 28:10–19 Jacob, fleeing to Haran, dreams of the ladder and names the place Bethel. In Gen 35:9–15, on his return, God appears, renames him Israel, introduces himself as "God almighty" (35:11, *El Shaddai*), and Jacob names the place Bethel again. Run the method. (1) Identify the doublet: the same place and the same naming. The renaming also repeats 32:28. (2) Note the markers. Chapter 35 uses *El Shaddai*, the name Ex 6:3 assigns to the patriarchal age. (3) State the rival explanations. Documentarians assign 35:9–15 to P, a separate account in which the naming happens once. Supplementary models make it a later Priestly layer that restates the earlier story. A final-form reader points to the text's own "again" (35:9) and reads a solemn reaffirmation. All three explain the data; they differ on whether the repetition was *made* or *found*.

**Example 2 (hard): "He wrote of me."** Jesus says, "if you did believe Moses, you would perhaps believe me also: for he wrote of me" (John 5:46). Does inerrancy ([`fundamental-theology` 3.2](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/03-02-inerrancy.md)) then settle Mosaic authorship? The 1906 response's first question cited the testimony of both Testaments. Catholic exegetes since have argued that such sayings name the Torah by its received title and appeal to its authority. They were not meant to teach literary history. What is guaranteed is what the speaker asserts, and his point here is that the Law bears witness to him. The strain is real. John 5:46 says "wrote", not just "the book of Moses". A Catholic may read it as asserting a substantial Mosaic origin behind the Torah, or as the common designation. That choice is **freely disputed**, and neither side may call the other's reading a denial of inerrancy.

**The Jewish reading of Ex 6:3.** [Rashi](../reference.md#jewish-readings) notes that the verb is "I was not *known*", not "I did not make known". He reads it this way: the patriarchs heard the name, but they did not experience God under the aspect it signifies. That aspect is keeping faith, fulfilling promises. God promised them the land, and they died before seeing it fulfilled. On this reading no contradiction arises, because the verse is about the name's meaning, not the name's first use.

## Watch out

- **"The Church teaches that Moses wrote the Pentateuch."** No definition exists. Trent defines the books, not their author, and the 1906 response is not dogma. The opposite overstatement also fails: "1906 was repealed." It was clarified (1948) and relativized (1955), never formally revoked.
- **"Scholars have proved JEDP."** Wellhausen's four sources are one model among several, and his J and E have lost their European majority. "Scholars agree it's composite" is close to true. "Scholars agree how" is false.
- **"Two names means two authors."** The divine-name criterion is the weakest one. The Douay's "Lord" hides which Hebrew word is meant, and neo-documentarians rest their case on plot, not names.
- **"If Moses didn't write it, it isn't inspired."** Inspiration attaches to the books and their true human authors, whoever they were ([`fundamental-theology` 3.1](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/03-01-inspiration.md)). Anonymous and composite books are inspired too.

## One-liner

> The Pentateuch is canonical, inspired Scripture by defined dogma; how it was composed, from Moses's hand to Wellhausen's four sources to today's Priestly/non-Priestly layers, is a freely disputed question with no consensus.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢)** *(Exegetical)* Genesis 12 and 20, excerpts (Douay–Rheims). The Hebrew word behind each divine name is in brackets.

> "But the Lord [YHWH] scourged Pharao and his house with most grievous stripes for Sarai, Abram's wife. And Pharao called Abram, and said to him: What is this that thou hast done to me? Why didst thou not tell me that she was thy wife?" (12:17–18)
>
> "And God [Elohim] came to Abimelech in a dream by night, and he said to him: Lo thou shalt die for the woman that thou hast taken: for she hath a husband. Now Abimelech had not touched her, and he said: Lord [Adonai], wilt thou slay a nation that is ignorant and just? ... For the Lord [YHWH] had closed up every womb of the house of Abimelech, on account of Sara, Abraham's wife." (20:3–4, 18)

(a) Name three things the stories share and three ways chapter 20 differs from chapter 12. (b) Say why a divine-name sort of chapter 20 is not clean, naming the two verses that complicate it. **Two sentences for (b).**

**P2 (🟡)** *(Exegetical)* Give each claim its status: **defined dogma**, **scholarly hypothesis** (say majority, minority or unsettled), or **tradition's report** (name the witness). One line each.

1. "Genesis to Deuteronomy are sacred and canonical, with God as their author."
2. "Joshua wrote the last eight verses of the Torah."
3. "Deuteronomy's core is connected with the law book found in Josiah's reign."
4. "No continuous Yahwist source ever ran from Genesis into Exodus."
5. "Because Trent calls them 'the five books of Moses', Mosaic authorship is defined."

**P3 (🔴)** *(Exegetical)* Two positions on Ex 6:2–3, paraphrased:

- **A neo-documentarian:** Ex 6:3 belongs to P, which says the name YHWH was first revealed to Moses. Gen 15:7 belongs to J, in which Abraham already knew it. Two documents told contradictory histories, and a compiler kept both.
- **A supplementary-model scholar:** P was composed as a layer that expanded an existing non-Priestly text. Ex 6:3 is P's theological reframing of the patriarchal period, written with that text in view.

(a) Name the single premise about P that the first needs and the second must deny. (b) Name one kind of evidence that would move it, and say which way. **100 words or fewer in total.**

<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** *Shared:* the patriarch calls his wife his sister, a foreign king takes her, God intervenes on her behalf, the king rebukes the patriarch, and she is restored with wealth or gifts. Any three. *Different:* the king is Pharao in Egypt in chapter 12 and Abimelech in Gerara in chapter 20. God strikes with plagues in chapter 12 but speaks in a dream in chapter 20. Chapter 20 insists the king never touched her (20:4, 6). Abimelech protests his innocence and Abraham justifies himself as a half-brother (20:12), while in chapter 12 Abram says nothing. Abraham is called a prophet who will pray (20:7). Any three.
**Must hit, strict (b):** chapter 20 mostly uses *Elohim* (20:3, 6, 17), the classic E marker. But **20:18 uses YHWH**, and **20:4's "Lord" is *Adonai***, a title and not the name. So an English "Lord" cannot be sorted as YHWH, and a single verse breaks the pattern. Documentarians usually give 20:18 to a redactor, which is the kind of adjustment their critics call *ad hoc*.
**Wrong turns:** counting 20:4's "Lord" as YHWH from the Douay's English. Treating the doublet as proof of separate sources rather than as data several models explain.
**Model answer (b):** Chapter 20 names God *Elohim* in its dream scenes, but 20:18 uses YHWH and 20:4 addresses God with the title *Adonai*, which the English "Lord" hides. So the names alone do not yield a clean "E" story; a redactor must be posited for 20:18.

**P2** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict:** (1) **Defined dogma**: canonicity and divine authorship (Trent, Vatican I). (2) **Tradition's report**: the baraita in Bava Batra 14b–15a, on Rabbi Yehuda's view, disputed there by Rabbi Shimon. (3) **Hypothesis, majority**: de Wette's link, still widely held. It is not doctrine. (4) **Hypothesis, unsettled**: widespread in Europe (Rendtorff, Blum, Schmid) and denied by the neo-documentarians. (5) **False as a level claim**: Trent defines the canon and uses the traditional title. Mosaic authorship was never defined.
**Wrong turns:** calling (3) or (4) Church teaching or "consensus". Calling (2) a Christian tradition, or giving it as Talmudic doctrine without the internal dispute. Marking (5) as defined.
**Model answer:** (1) Dogma. (2) Report, Bava Batra 15a (R. Yehuda; R. Shimon disagrees). (3) Majority hypothesis. (4) Hypothesis, split by region and school. (5) Wrong: the title is not a definition.

**P3** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** the crux is **whether P was composed independently of, or without regard to, the non-Priestly narrative**. The documentarian needs P to be a free-standing document. The supplementarian denies it: P was written onto the earlier text. Both can grant that Ex 6:3 and Gen 15:7 are in tension.
**Must hit, strict (b):** evidence of P's continuity or dependence. If the P passages, extracted, form a continuous story that needs nothing from the non-P text, that favours independence. If P passages presuppose non-P episodes, cross-reference them, or are unintelligible alone, that favours supplementation. Either direction counts if stated.
**Wrong turns:** making the crux "whether Moses wrote it" or "whether the texts contradict", which both scholars accept. Offering the divine names as the decisive evidence, since both models already account for them.
**Model answer:** (a) Whether P was an independent document; the supplementarian denies it. (b) If extracted P passages read as a complete, self-standing narrative, independence gains; if they presuppose non-P episodes they could not tell alone, supplementation gains.

## Flashback

**From Lesson [1.4](01-04-canonical-reading-and-the-unity-of-the-testaments.md) (Canonical reading and the unity of the Testaments):** *(Exegetical.)* Anointed at a prophet's command to "cut off the house of Achab" (2 Kings 9:7), Jehu kills Ahab's son, Jezebel and Ahab's house at Jezreel (2 Kings 9–10, mid-ninth century). Two verdicts (Douay–Rheims):

> "And the Lord said to Jehu: because thou hast diligently executed that which was right and pleasing in my eyes, and hast done to the house of Achab according to all that was in my heart: thy children shall sit upon the throne of Israel to the fourth generation." (2 Kings 10:30 [Douay 4 Kings 10:30])
>
> "Call his name Jezrahel: for yet a little while, and I will visit the blood of Jezrahel upon the house of Jehu, and I will cause to cease the kingdom of the house of Israel." (Hosea [Douay Osee] 1:4, under Jehu's descendant Jeroboam II)

"Visit" renders Hebrew *paqad*, "call to account". Label each reader's move **harmonizing**, **canonical reading** or **flattening**, one line each. Then answer (b).

1. "Kings approves the deed and Hosea condemns the man. Jehu did what he was told, but for his own ends, as 10:31 shows: he 'took no heed to walk in the law of the Lord'."
2. "Kings approves the extermination and Hosea, a century later, holds the dynasty guilty for the blood. The canon keeps both verdicts, and the reader of the whole learns that a prophet could call to account a deed an earlier word had approved."
3. "Christ said, 'Put up again thy sword into its place' (Matthew 26:52), so neither text is really about Jehu: both teach that violence never pleases God."

(b) Move 1 has real support in 2 Kings 10. Name what it leans on there, and say what it has to do to Hosea 1:4. **Two sentences.**

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict:** (1) **Harmonizing**: it removes the tension with a distinction between the deed and the doer's spirit. (2) **Canonical reading**: each verdict keeps its literal sense, and the meaning is read from the arrangement that holds both. (3) **Flattening**: a later text replaces both passages' meaning, and 10:30's explicit approval simply disappears.
**Must hit, strict (b):** it leans on **10:29 and 10:31**, which already fault Jehu for keeping Jeroboam's golden calves and not walking in the law with all his heart. It must **narrow Hosea's "blood of Jezrahel"** to guilt for Jehu's motive or his later sins, when Hosea's words name the blood shed at Jezreel itself.
**Wrong turns:** calling move 1 illegitimate. Harmonizing is a real operation and may be right; the task is to name it. Calling move 2 harmonizing because it draws a lesson; it removes neither text's claim. Calling move 3 a spiritual sense: a spiritual sense rests on the literal, and "neither text is really about Jehu" discards it.
**Model answer:** (1) Harmonizing. (2) Canonical reading. (3) Flattening. (b) Move 1 draws on 10:29–31, where Kings itself says Jehu kept the calves of Bethel and Dan and did not walk in the law with all his heart. To reconcile the two, it has to read Hosea's "blood of Jezrahel" as guilt for Jehu's motives rather than for the bloodshed Hosea actually names.
