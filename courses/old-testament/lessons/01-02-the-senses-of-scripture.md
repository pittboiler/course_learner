# The Old Testament · Lesson 1.2: The senses of Scripture

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 1: How the Church reads the Old Testament · Builds on: [1.1 What the Old Testament is](01-01-what-the-old-testament-is.md) · Unlocks: [1.3 The historical-critical method and its limits](01-03-the-historical-critical-method-and-its-limits.md), [1.4 Canonical reading and the unity of the Testaments](01-04-canonical-reading-and-the-unity-of-the-testaments.md)

## Why this matters

Abraham takes Isaac up a mountain to sacrifice him, and is stopped (Genesis 22). A modern commentator reads a story about a test, or about ending child sacrifice. The Letter to the Hebrews says Abraham received his son back "for a parable". Augustine sees the cross in the wood Isaac carries. Rival answers? The Catholic answer is no: they answer different questions, and one level governs the rest. This lesson gives the four senses of Scripture, Aquinas's reason for them, and the rule that keeps them honest: literal sense first, then the spiritual, kept distinct.

## The idea

In an ordinary book, words point to things and stop: "Jerusalem" means a city. The Christian claim is that in Scripture the *things* can point further, because the one who wrote the book also runs the history it describes. God can make events mean.

John Cassian's *Conferences* (XIV.8, early fifth century) gives the classic miniature. One Jerusalem, four ways: historically as the city of the Jews, allegorically as the Church of Christ, anagogically as the heavenly city, and tropologically (morally) as the human soul. These are the [four senses](../reference.md#the-senses-of-scripture). A medieval couplet, which the Biblical Commission's 1993 document attributes to the thirteenth-century Augustine of Dacia (Denmark), fixed them; the Catechism cites it (CCC 118):

> *Littera gesta docet, quid credas allegoria, / moralis quid agas, quo tendas anagogia.*

The lesson's translation: "The letter teaches what happened; allegory, what to believe; the moral sense, what to do; anagogy, where you are heading."

Its history (Origen's threefold reading, the Antiochene *theoria*, Gregory the Great's Latin version) is [`patristics` 3.5](../../patristics/lessons/03-05-allegory-and-history-alexandria-and-antioch.md) and [5.3](../../patristics/lessons/05-03-gregory-the-great.md). **[Typology](../reference.md#typology)** reads a real person or event as prefiguring another real one: Jonah in the fish, Christ in the tomb. **Allegory**, in the narrow sense, reads the details of a narrative as a code for truths of another order. Typology keeps the history; allegory can spend it.

## Source

Thomas Aquinas, *Summa Theologiae* I q.1 a.10, "Whether in Holy Scripture a word may have several senses?" (English Dominican Province, 1920). From the body of the article, then the reply to the first objection:

> The author of Holy Writ is God, in whose power it is to signify His meaning, not by words only (as man also can do), but also by things themselves. So, whereas in every other science things are signified by words, this science has the property, that the things signified by the words have themselves also a signification. Therefore that first signification whereby words signify things belongs to the first sense, the historical or literal. That signification whereby things signified by words have themselves also a signification is called the spiritual sense, which is based on the literal, and presupposes it. ...
>
> ... all the senses are founded on one — the literal — from which alone can any argument be drawn, and not from those intended in allegory, as Augustine says (Epis. 48). Nevertheless, nothing of Holy Scripture perishes on account of this, since nothing necessary to faith is contained under the spiritual sense which is not elsewhere put forward by the Scripture in its literal sense.

Three phrases decide it. "**By things themselves**": the spiritual sense belongs to events, not words, so it is not a pun. "**Presupposes it**": no literal sense, no *thing* to signify further. "**Nothing necessary to faith**": the rule costs nothing, since no doctrine hangs on a figure alone.

## The argument

1. **God signifies by words and by things.** Scripture's author is also the Lord of history, so persons, events and institutions can signify. *In words:* the events told were arranged to be signs.
2. **The literal sense is what the words signify, including figures of speech.** Aquinas's reply to the third objection: when Scripture speaks of God's arm, the literal sense is not a limb but God's power. So "literal" is not "literalist". The Biblical Commission's *The Interpretation of the Bible in the Church* (1993) adds that the literal sense is what the human author expressed, read by his genre's conventions, and that a story's literal sense need not be a report of fact. Genre as the exegete's tool goes back to *Divino Afflante Spiritu* (1943; [`fundamental-theology` 3.2](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/03-02-inerrancy.md)). *In words:* the [literal sense](../reference.md#literal-sense) is what the author meant, in the kind of writing he chose.
3. **The spiritual sense is what the things signify, and it has three kinds.** The Old Law is a figure of the New (Hebrews 10:1), which gives the **allegorical** sense. What Christ did is a pattern for what we should do, which gives the **moral** sense. The New Law figures future glory (Aquinas cites Pseudo-Dionysius): the **anagogical** sense. *In words:* a thing in the story can point to Christ and the Church, to how to live, or to the end.
4. **This is not equivocation.** One word still has one meaning. The *referent* means further. *In words:* "Jerusalem" means the city; the city means the Church.
5. **Argue from the literal sense alone.** A doctrine cannot rest on a spiritual reading by itself, and none needs to, because whatever faith requires is stated literally somewhere in Scripture. *In words:* the spiritual senses feed faith; they do not prove it.
6. **The Church adds a control.** *Divino Afflante Spiritu* 26–27 (paraphrased) tells exegetes to expound the spiritual sense only where God clearly intended it. Its signs are Christ's teaching, the apostles, tradition and the liturgy. Figurative applications in preaching are allowed with restraint, but Pius XII calls them "extrinsic" and "accidental" to the text: the **accommodated sense**, which is not a sense of Scripture at all. The 1993 document adds that the spiritual sense is read in light of Christ's death and resurrection, never cut loose from the literal, and that finding a figure in every ritual detail has no real interpretive value today. *In words:* not every edifying reading is a spiritual sense.

**Mark the levels.** That Scripture has a spiritual sense intended by God and founded on the literal is **authentic ordinary magisterium** (*Divino Afflante Spiritu* 26–27; restated in CCC 115–117). The fourfold division is an **ancient tradition** the Catechism reports as such. Aquinas's argument is theology, weighty but not a magisterial act (levels from [`fundamental-theology` 4.4](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-04-the-ladder-of-doctrinal-authority.md)). Typologies the New Testament itself draws belong to the **literal sense of an inspired New Testament text**. A Father's allegory carries that Father's weight. The 1993 document comes from the Commission after 1971, which is **consultative, not magisterial** ([the Commission and its weight](../reference.md#the-biblical-commission-and-its-weight), taken up in [1.3](01-03-the-historical-critical-method-and-its-limits.md)). Whether Scripture also has a *sensus plenior*, a fuller sense God intended beyond the human author's grasp, is **freely disputed** ([4.3](04-03-the-servant-of-the-lord.md)). Every literal reading of Genesis 22 below is a scholarly position, and the dates of Abraham and of the chapter's composition are **disputed** ([2.2](02-02-who-wrote-the-pentateuch.md), [2.4](02-04-the-patriarchs.md)).

## The case: Genesis 22 in four senses

**The literal sense, with its rivals.** Three readings are live among scholars.

- **A test of trust.** The narrator tells you at 22:1 what Abraham does not know: "God tempted Abraham". The Hebrew *nissah* means "tested", as the Douay's own note explains. The command seems to cancel the promise, which runs through Isaac; the point is Abraham's fear of God (22:12). Gerhard von Rad's commentary is usually cited here.
- **A polemic or aetiology.** The story explains why Israel offers a ram, not a child (22:13). Hermann Gunkel is usually credited with finding a sanctuary legend of that substitution behind it.
- **The beloved son given and returned.** Jon D. Levenson (*The Death and Resurrection of the Beloved Son*, 1993) argues that the story does not condemn offering the son: it assumes God may ask, praises Abraham's willingness, and tells of the son's return.

The words that decide are *yachid*, "only one", at 22:2 (Douay "only begotten"). There is *wayya'aqod*, "and he bound" (22:9), from which Jewish tradition names the whole episode the ***akedah***, "the binding". And there is *yir'eh*, "will see" or "will provide" (22:8, 22:14).

**The Jewish reading, as its tradition states it.** The *akedah* is Israel's great memory of self-offering. The Aramaic Targum known as Pseudo-Jonathan has Isaac speak at 22:10 (Etheridge's 1862 translation): "Bind me properly (aright), lest I tremble from the affliction of my soul". In the Talmud (Rosh Hashanah 16a), Rabbi Abbahu explains the ram's-horn shofar of the New Year: God asks for it so as to remember the binding of Isaac for Israel, as if they had bound themselves. Rashi notes that God said "bring him up", not "slay him". Moriah (Douay "the land of vision") is the Temple mount (2 Chronicles 3:1 [Douay 2 Paralipomenon 3:1]). This is a tradition in its own right, not a Christian reading gone wrong.

**The spiritual senses.**

| Sense | Genesis 22 | Warrant |
|---|---|---|
| Literal | See the rivals above | The text, in its genre |
| Allegorical | Isaac, the only and beloved son, is Christ; the wood is the cross; the ram caught in thorns is Christ crowned with thorns | Hebrews 11:19 ("for a parable"); Romans 8:32 echoes "hast not spared" (22:12, 16); Augustine, *City of God* XVI.32 |
| Moral | Take up the cross as Isaac took up the wood, and follow | Irenaeus, *Against Heresies* IV.5.4 |
| Anagogical | Abraham reckoned that God could raise the dead; the son returned from the mountain figures the resurrection to come | Hebrews 11:19 |

Augustine's line (NPNF 1st series vol. 2): "Isaac also himself carried to the place of sacrifice the wood on which he was to be offered up, just as the Lord Himself carried His own cross." It rests on a *thing* the literal sense supplies, the wood on the boy's back (22:6): typology, not allegory.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean): the crossing of the sea** (Exodus 14). *Literal:* Israel's deliverance from Egypt, the founding saving act (what lies behind it historically is [2.5](02-05-exodus-and-sinai.md)'s question). *Allegorical:* baptism. The fathers "were baptized, in the cloud and in the sea" (1 Corinthians 10:2); CCC 117 uses this example. *Moral:* leave the slavery behind for good; the wilderness events are figures "of us" (1 Corinthians 10:6). *Anagogical:* the victors in Revelation 15:2–3 stand on a sea of glass and sing "the canticle of Moses". Each spiritual reading has a New Testament warrant and rests on an event the literal sense narrates: the method without strain.

**Example 2 (hard): Hagar and Sarah** (Galatians 4:21–31). Paul reads Abraham's two wives as two covenants: "Which things are said by an allegory" (4:24, Greek *allēgoroumena*). Typology or allegory? Both, in part. Two real women prefigure two real covenants, which is typology; but Hagar is also "Mount Sina", which reads a detail as code. Paul's word is not our category. Now the argument rule. You *can* argue doctrine from Galatians 4, because Paul's reading is the **literal sense of Galatians**. You cannot argue it from Genesis 16 and 21 alone, whose literal sense is a family story of promise and inheritance. A preacher who extends Paul's figure detail by detail has moved into accommodation. The strain: one passage is both a spiritual reading of Genesis and a literal statement of Paul's.

## Watch out

- **"Literal" means what the author meant, not a wooden reading.** The literal sense of a parable is its point. Historicity is a separate question of genre ([1.3](01-03-the-historical-critical-method-and-its-limits.md)).
- **Level errors both ways.** "The four senses are defined dogma" overstates: the fourfold scheme is a tradition. So does treating the 1993 document as the Church's teaching: it is a consultative body's. "The spiritual sense is pious decoration" understates *Divino Afflante Spiritu*. And no literal reading of Genesis 22 above is Church teaching; each is a hypothesis.
- **Not every edifying reading is a spiritual sense.** A clever application with no warrant from Christ, the apostles, tradition or the liturgy is accommodation: preachable with restraint, proof of nothing.
- **The *akedah* is not a failed Christian reading.** Arguing Christian against Jewish readings belongs to [`apologetics-foundations`](../../apologetics-foundations/syllabus.md).
- **Marian typology** (Eve, the Ark, the Queen Mother) is taught in [`ecclesiology-and-mariology`](../../ecclesiology-and-mariology/syllabus.md).

## One-liner

> Words signify things, and in Scripture the things signify again; so read the letter first, argue from the letter alone, and let the figures feed faith without proving it.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢)** *(Exegetical)* Exodus 12:3, 5–7, 46 (Douay–Rheims):

> Speak ye to the whole assembly of the children of Israel, and say to them: On the tenth day of this month let every man take a lamb by their families and houses. ... And it shall be a lamb without blemish, a male, of one year ... And you shall keep it until the fourteenth day of this month; and the whole multitude of the children of Israel shall sacrifice it in the evening. And they shall take of the blood thereof, and put it upon both the side posts, and on the upper door posts of the houses, wherein they shall eat it. ... In one house shall it be eaten, neither shall you carry forth of the flesh thereof out of the house, neither shall you break a bone thereof.

Give the literal sense in one sentence. Then give the allegorical, moral and anagogical senses in one line each, and name for each a New Testament text or a reason that grounds it. **100 words or fewer.**

**P2 (🟡)** *(Exegetical)* For each reading, say (i) whether it is **typology** or **allegory** in the narrow sense, and (ii) which spiritual sense it gives (allegorical, moral, anagogical), or whether it is only **accommodation**. One line each.

1. "For as Jonas was in the whale's belly three days and three nights: so shall the Son of man be in the heart of the earth" (Matthew 12:40).
2. "As Moses lifted up the serpent in the desert, so must the Son of man be lifted up" (John 3:14, on Numbers 21:8–9).
3. The wilderness rebellions "were done in a figure of us, that we should not covet evil things" (1 Corinthians 10:6).
4. Joshua (Greek *Iēsous*) did not give the people their final rest, so "There remaineth therefore a day of rest for the people of God" (Hebrews 4:8–9).
5. An **invented** retreat talk: David's "five smooth stones" (1 Samuel 17:40 [Douay 1 Kings 17:40]) are the five books of Moses, with which the Christian slays temptation.

**P3 (🔴)** *(Exegetical)* An **invented** sermon paragraph:

> "The manna could not be kept overnight: whoever saved it found it full of worms (Exodus 16:19–20). The manna is the Eucharist. Scripture therefore teaches that the faithful must receive Communion every day, and whoever lets a day pass without it sins against the word of God."

(a) Name the principle of interpretation the sermon breaks, and give its source. (b) Say what in the sermon is a legitimate spiritual reading, and what warrants it. (c) Name the level error, and say what the Church actually requires. **Two sentences per part.**

<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict:** a **literal** sense that stays in Exodus: the rite of the Passover lamb, with its blood on the doorposts, instituted for Israel's deliverance from Egypt and kept as a memorial. An **allegorical** sense pointing to **Christ** and his death, grounded in the New Testament: "Christ our pasch is sacrificed" (1 Corinthians 5:7), or John 19:36 applying "You shall not break a bone of him" to the crucified Jesus. A **moral** sense about the Christian's conduct, for example 1 Corinthians 5:7–8 (purge the old leaven, keep the feast in sincerity), or being marked by and sheltered under Christ's blood. An **anagogical** sense pointing to the **end**, for example the Lamb "standing, as it were slain" (Revelation 5:6) or "the marriage supper of the Lamb" (Revelation 19:9).

**Wrong turns:** putting Christ into the literal sense of Exodus 12. Giving a moral sense with no link to Christ or to a New Testament warrant (Aquinas ties the moral sense to what Christ did, or to what signifies him). Swapping the allegorical and anagogical senses.

**Model answer:** Literal: God commands Israel's Passover, a spotless lamb whose blood marks the houses spared at the Exodus, eaten whole with no bone broken. Allegorical: the lamb is Christ, sacrificed as "our pasch" (1 Cor 5:7), no bone of his broken (John 19:36). Moral: purge the old leaven and live in sincerity and truth (1 Cor 5:8). Anagogical: the slain and standing Lamb and his wedding feast (Rev 5:6; 19:9).

**P2** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict:** (1) **Typology**, **allegorical** (Christ's death and resurrection), warranted by Christ's own word. (2) **Typology**, **allegorical** (the crucifixion), warranted by John. (3) **Typology**, **moral**: the events are figures "of us", for our conduct. (4) **Typology**, **anagogical**: the land's rest points to the final rest. Credit a note that Hebrews also makes Joshua a type of Jesus, which is allegorical. (5) **Allegory** in the narrow sense: a detail (five) read as a code, with no warrant from Christ, the apostles, tradition or the liturgy, so it is **accommodation**, *Divino Afflante Spiritu* 27's "extrinsic" use.

**Wrong turns:** calling (3) allegory because it is a lesson rather than a prefigured event. The rebellions are real events read as figures, which is typology. Calling (5) a moral sense because it gives moral advice. A moral sense has to rest on a thing that signifies; this rests on a number. Treating (1)–(4) as merely the reader's opinions, when each is in the literal sense of a New Testament text.

**Model answer:** (1) Typology, allegorical. (2) Typology, allegorical. (3) Typology, moral. (4) Typology, anagogical. (5) Allegory, only accommodation.

**P3** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** the rule that **argument is drawn from the literal sense alone**, not from what is meant in allegory (ST I q.1 a.10 ad 1, citing Augustine). Credit also *Divino Afflante Spiritu* 27: extending the figure to a precept is **accommodation**, "extrinsic" to the text, from which nothing binding follows.

**Must hit, strict (b):** **manna → Eucharist** is a warranted **typology** (allegorical sense). Its warrant is **John 6:31–35 and 49–51** (the true bread from heaven) and 1 Corinthians 10:3 ("spiritual food"). What is not warranted is reading a *precept* off the detail that the manna spoiled overnight.

**Must hit, strict (c):** the sermon turns a preacher's figure into **"what Scripture teaches" with the force of an obligation**, so that missing it is sin. That is a level error. The Church's precept requires Communion **at least once a year, in the Easter season** (Lateran IV, 1215, chapter 21). Daily Communion is encouraged, not commanded.

**Wrong turns:** rejecting the manna–Eucharist link as well. It is the Gospel's own typology. Blaming the literal sense of Exodus 16, which is about trusting God for daily bread and keeping the sabbath, and says nothing about the Eucharist. Answering (c) with "the Church allows it", which misses that the sermon *commands*.

**Model answer:** (a) It argues from an allegorical detail to a binding conclusion, but Aquinas holds that argument is drawn from the literal sense alone (ST I q.1 a.10 ad 1). A precept read off the spoiling manna is at best accommodation, which *Divino Afflante Spiritu* calls extrinsic to the text. (b) Reading the manna as a figure of the Eucharist is legitimate typology, since Jesus himself contrasts the manna with the true bread from heaven in John 6. What has no warrant is making the overnight spoiling mean a daily duty. (c) It presents a preacher's figure as a divine command whose neglect is sin, which invents an obligation. The Church requires Communion at least once a year in the Easter season (Lateran IV) and only encourages frequent or daily Communion.
