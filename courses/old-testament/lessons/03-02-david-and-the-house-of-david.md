# The Old Testament · Lesson 3.2: David and the house of David

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 3: From conquest to return · Builds on: [3.1 Joshua, Judges, and the land](03-01-joshua-judges-and-the-land.md), [1.3 The historical-critical method and its limits](01-03-the-historical-critical-method-and-its-limits.md) · Unlocks: [3.3 Two kingdoms and two falls](03-03-two-kingdoms-and-two-falls.md), [5.1 The Psalms](05-01-the-psalms.md), [4.2 Isaiah and Immanuel](04-02-isaiah-and-immanuel.md)

## Why this matters

God promises David that his house and throne will stand for ever. The royal psalms, Isaiah's child-king, the hope for a messiah, the angel's words to Mary and the title "son of David" all hang on it. The same chapters raise the hardest historical question in the Old Testament. Was David the king of a real state, a hill-country chieftain, or a literary figure? This lesson reads the promise, reads the story around it as literature, and lays out the historical debate without letting it do theology's work.

## The idea

The books of Samuel (Douay: 1–2 Kings) tell three stories at once.

- **A story of rise and fall.** Israel asks for a king "as all nations have" (1 Samuel 8:5). Saul is chosen and rejected. The shepherd boy David is anointed, rises at court, becomes king, and then sins and watches his family tear itself apart.
- **A promise.** When David wants to build God a *house* (a temple), God answers that He will build David a *house* (a dynasty). The oracle turns on one Hebrew word, *bayit*, which means a building, a household and a royal line.
- **A historical problem.** No object from the tenth century BC names David or Solomon. What we have is a text finished centuries later and a mound of disputed archaeology.

Keep the three apart. The literary reading needs no verdict on the history. The theology rests on the promise and on how later Scripture reads it, not on how many square miles David ruled.

## Source

Nathan's oracle, 2 Samuel 7:5, 11b–13, 15–16 (Douay 2 Kings 7), Douay–Rheims:

> "Go, and say to my servant David: Thus saith the Lord: Shalt thou build me a house to dwell in? ... And the Lord foretelleth to thee, that the Lord will make thee a house. And when thy days shall be fulfilled, and thou shalt sleep with thy fathers, I will raise up thy seed after thee, which shall proceed out of thy bowels, and I will establish his kingdom. He shall build a house to my name, and I will establish the throne of his kingdom for ever. ... But my mercy I will not take away from him, as I took it from Saul, whom I removed from before my face. And thy house shall be faithful, and thy kingdom for ever before thy face, and thy throne shall be firm for ever."

Three words carry it. **"House"** (*bayit*) shifts meaning. In verse 5 it is a temple David will not build. In verse 11 it is the dynasty God will build ("YHWH will make you a house"). In verse 13 it is the temple again, which the son will build. **"Mercy"** is *hesed*, covenant loyalty: God's steady commitment, which, unlike Saul's case, will not be withdrawn. **"Faithful"** renders *ne'eman*, from the root of *amen*: made firm, reliable. The son can be punished "with the rod of men" (v. 14), but the line is not revoked. That contrast between the conditional and the unconditional is the [Davidic covenant](../reference.md#the-davidic-covenant).

## The argument

1. **The monarchy is told with two minds.** In 1 Samuel 8 the request for a king is a rejection of God ("they have not rejected thee, but me", 8:7), and Samuel warns what kings take. Yet the same book presents the anointed king as God's gift. Scholars have long split 1 Samuel 8–12 into pro- and anti-monarchic strands; the final text holds both in tension. *In words:* kingship is a concession that God then adopts.

2. **The story of David shows its seams.** David joins Saul's court twice: as a harpist Saul loves (1 Sam 16:21), and after Goliath as a youth Saul cannot place (17:55). 2 Samuel 21:19 credits Goliath's death to Elhanan. The Douay, following the Vulgate, turns the name into "Adeodatus". 1 Chronicles 20:5 (Douay 1 Paralipomenon) has Elhanan kill Goliath's *brother*. Codex Vaticanus of the Septuagint gives a shorter 1 Samuel 17 that lacks 17:12–31 and 17:55–18:5, the verses that produce the second introduction. *In words:* Samuel was built from traditions that did not always agree, and the ancients noticed.

3. **The Deuteronomistic History.** Martin Noth (*Überlieferungsgeschichtliche Studien*, 1943) argued that Deuteronomy through Kings (excluding Ruth) forms one work. A single exilic author compiled it to explain the fall of Jerusalem by Deuteronomy's standard. Its last event, Jehoiachin's release in exile (2 Kings 25:27), falls around 561 BC. Frank Moore Cross proposed two editions instead, one under Josiah and one in exile, and that is probably the most widely held model. The Göttingen school (Rudolf Smend and his students) proposes successive exilic layers. See [the Deuteronomistic History](../reference.md#the-deuteronomistic-history). *In words:* whatever its sources, the oracle reached its readers as part of a history whose kings had ended. That is why the promise had to be read as hope.

4. **The Succession Narrative as literature.** Leonhard Rost (1926) isolated 2 Samuel 9–20 and 1 Kings 1–2 (Douay 3 Kings) as a single "succession" story: who will sit on David's throne? It has no miracles, and God speaks through Nathan only once. The characters' motives are left to the reader, and the story ends with the ruthless Solomon enthroned. God works in three short notes. David's act "was displeasing to the Lord" (11:27), "the Lord loved" the infant Solomon (12:24), and "by the will of the Lord" Absalom's best counsel failed (17:14). Rost read the story as an apology for Solomon. Other scholars read it as a critique of the dynasty. Rost's boundaries are still the starting point, but fewer scholars now accept them whole. See [the Succession Narrative](../reference.md#the-succession-narrative).

5. **The historical debate.** Positions sit on a spectrum ([maximalists and minimalists](../reference.md#maximalists-and-minimalists)).
   - **Minimalists.** Niels Peter Lemche and Thomas L. Thompson (the "Copenhagen school") and Philip Davies (*In Search of "Ancient Israel"*, 1992) treat the united monarchy as a later literary creation. On their view these texts are too late to serve as sources for the tenth century.
   - **Low chronology.** Israel Finkelstein (from the 1990s) moves the pottery and monuments usually dated to Solomon down to the ninth-century Omride kings. His David is historical but rules a small highland territory around Jerusalem.
   - **Modified conventional chronology.** Amihai Mazar's framework, now the most widely used, allows a modest but real tenth-century polity. Yosef Garfinkel's fortified town at Khirbet Qeiyafa, which he dates by radiocarbon to around 1000 BC, is claimed as Judahite. Finkelstein reads it as early northern Israelite, and others read it as Canaanite, so its identity is disputed.
   - **Maximalists.** Kenneth Kitchen (*On the Reliability of the Old Testament*) defends the biblical outline as broadly reliable.

   The **Tel Dan inscription**, three Aramaic fragments found in 1993–94, is usually attributed to Hazael of Damascus (who is not named) and dated to the ninth century. Its *bytdwd* is read by most scholars as "house of David". *In words:* a dynasty named after a David was known abroad within a century or two of him, and that tells us nothing about the size of his kingdom.

6. **The spiritual sense.** Solomon builds the house of 7:13, but his line falls in 587/586. Christian reading therefore takes the "for ever" of 7:13 and 7:16 as fulfilled in Christ. Gabriel promises "the throne of David his father ... of his kingdom there shall be no end" (Luke 1:32–33). Hebrews 1:5 applies "I will be to him a Father" to the Son. Augustine, *City of God* XVII.8 (NPNF), puts it this way: "He who thinks this grand promise was fulfilled in Solomon greatly errs; for he attends to the saying, 'He shall build me an house,' but he does not attend to the saying, 'His house shall be faithful, and his kingdom for evermore before me.'" This is [typology](../reference.md#typology): Solomon is a shadow of Christ, and the literal sense (a promise to David's line) stays in place. **Jewish reading**, stated as its own: the promise is still open. A king of David's line is awaited. The daily *Amidah* prays for the "sprout" of David, and Maimonides' law code (*Laws of Kings* 11) makes descent from David a mark of the messianic king.

**Mark the levels.** That Jesus is the Christ belongs to the faith, and the Catechism (CCC 436–439) teaches that he fulfils the messianic hope as David's heir. Reading 2 Samuel 7 as pointing to him is the New Testament's own use of the text, and the tradition's reading of it. That the oracle promises David's line lasting favour is its **literal sense**, which a Catholic reading must respect. **Everything in steps 3–5 is scholarly reconstruction** with no magisterial standing ([Church teaching vs hypothesis](../reference.md#church-teaching-vs-hypothesis)). The Pontifical Biblical Commission's 1905 response on apparently historical narratives, in substance, said such books should be taken as history unless solid arguments show the writer did not intend it. It was binding when issued. Its practical force was changed by *Divino Afflante Spiritu*'s literary forms and the 1955 statements, and since 1971 the Commission is consultative ([its weight](../reference.md#the-biblical-commission-and-its-weight)). Neither the extent of David's kingdom nor Noth's model is Church teaching.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean): who killed Goliath?** Run the method. *Literal sense first:* 1 Samuel 17 tells a story of faith against strength. Its point is that the Lord "saveth not with sword and spear: for it is his battle" (17:47); it is not a military report. *Textual evidence:* the Septuagint's shorter chapter, the double introduction of David and 2 Samuel 21:19 show that more than one tradition fed the account. *Three readings:* (i) a heroic deed of Elhanan was transferred to David, the common critical view; (ii) 1 Chronicles 20:5 harmonizes by making Elhanan's victim Goliath's brother; (iii) Rashi takes a Jewish traditional line: "Elhanan" is David himself, and "son of Jaare-oregim" refers to his family's weaving of the sanctuary curtain. None of the three touches the chapter's theology, and none is Church teaching.

**Example 2 (hard): Solomon's gates.** 1 Kings 9:15 (Douay 3 Kings) says Solomon built Hazor, Megiddo and Gezer. Yigael Yadin found similar six-chambered gates at all three and called them Solomonic. That looked like archaeology confirming the Bible. The problem is that the strata were dated partly by that very verse. Finkelstein's low chronology redates them to the ninth century, and radiocarbon work since has not ended the dispute. Evidence dated by the text cannot independently confirm the text. Absence of monuments is also weak evidence where later building destroyed the layers, as in Jerusalem. Both sides must say which data are independent.

## Watch out

- **"Catholics must hold David's empire historical because the Commission said the narratives are history."** That turns a 1905 disciplinary response into standing doctrine. Its force was later loosened, and the Commission is now consultative. The extent of David's kingdom is **freely disputed**.
- **"The minimalists won, so the promise is void."** A reconstruction of tenth-century politics cannot settle what 2 Samuel 7 teaches as Scripture. The inverse error is to treat Tel Dan as having "proved the Bible".
- **"Solomon fulfilled it."** Solomon built the house, but the "for ever" outruns him. Augustine's point is that the text itself pushes past its first referent.

## One-liner

> David wanted to build God a house; God promised to build David one. The promise outlived the dynasty, the text outlived the kingdom, and the history of both is still argued.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢)** Nathan's parable, 2 Samuel 12:1–7a (Douay 2 Kings 12):

> "There were two men in one city, the one rich, and the other poor. ... But the poor man had nothing at all but one little ewe lamb, ... which had grown up in his house together with his children, eating of his bread, and drinking of his cup, and sleeping in his bosom: and it was unto him as a daughter. And when a certain stranger was come to the rich man, he spared to take of his own sheep and oxen ... but took the poor man's ewe, and dressed it for the man that was come to him. And David's anger being exceedingly kindled against that man, he said to Nathan: As the Lord liveth, the man that hath done this is a child of death. He shall restore the ewe fourfold, ... and had no pity. And Nathan said to David: Thou art the man."

(a) *(Exegetical)* Map the parable onto 2 Samuel 11: who is the rich man, the poor man, the ewe? Name two details that make the lamb more than property. (b) *(Exegetical)* How does the parable make David pass sentence on himself, and which law is "fourfold" drawn from? **Three sentences per part.**

**P2 (🟡)** The Succession Narrative (2 Samuel 9–20; 1 Kings 1–2) names God's action only in three short notes: David's deed "was displeasing to the Lord" (2 Sam 11:27); Solomon is born "and the Lord loved him" (12:24); "by the will of the Lord the profitable counsel of Achitophel was defeated, that the Lord might bring evil upon Absalom" (17:14).

(a) *(Exegetical)* What do the three notes have in common, and how does this way of showing God at work differ from the Exodus narrative? **Two sentences.** (b) *(Evaluative)* Rost read the narrative as written to legitimate Solomon; other scholars read it as a critique of David's house. Using the notes and one episode, say which reading fits better. **150 words or fewer.** Any verdict passes.

**P3 (🔴)** *(Exegetical)* Assign each claim a status: **Church teaching** (name the level or document), **literal sense of the text**, **scholarly hypothesis**, **old Biblical Commission response** (with its present weight), or **disputed date**. One line each.

1. "God promises that David's house and throne will stand for ever."
2. "Jesus is the Christ, heir of David, in whom Israel's messianic hope is fulfilled."
3. "Joshua through Kings were compiled by a single author in exile."
4. "Catholics must read the narratives of Samuel as strict history unless the author clearly did not intend it."
5. "Solomon began the Temple 480 years after the Exodus, so the Exodus can be fixed at about 1446 BC."

<details>
<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical (a) · Exegetical (b))*

**Must hit, strict (a):** the rich man is **David** (many wives, 12:8); the poor man is **Uriah**; the ewe is **Bathsheba**, his only wife. Details that make the lamb more than property: it grew up **with his children**, ate **his bread**, drank from **his cup**, slept **in his bosom**, and was **"as a daughter"**. Any two.
**Must hit, strict (b):** the parable is presented as a real case for the king's judgment (a **juridical parable**, Uriel Simon's term), so David rules as judge before he knows he is the defendant. **"Thou art the man"** turns his verdict ("a child of death", no pity) onto himself. "Fourfold" is the **theft law of Exodus 22:1** (Hebrew 21:37): four sheep for one sheep.
**Wrong turns:** making the "stranger" Bathsheba or Nathan: he is the occasion of the rich man's greed, not a figure for a character in chapter 11. Saying David's sentence of death is the law's penalty for sheep theft: the law requires restitution, and "child of death" is David's anger outrunning it, which is the point.
**Model answer:** (a) David is the rich man with many wives, Uriah the poor man, Bathsheba the ewe. The lamb shares the poor man's bread and cup, sleeps in his bosom, and is "as a daughter": she is family, not stock. (b) Nathan brings the story as a legal case, so David judges it as king, and "Thou art the man" turns the verdict on him. The fourfold restitution is the sheep-theft law of Exodus 22:1, and his "child of death" goes beyond it.

**P2** *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b))*

**Must hit, strict (a):** in each note, **God acts behind and through human choices**, and the narrator names it rarely, in a single clause. There are **no miracles and no theophany**. In the Exodus, by contrast, God acts openly: plagues, the Sea, speech from the mountain. Credit: all three notes fall at hinges of the succession plot (the sin, Solomon's birth, Absalom's fall).
**Must hit, any verdict (b):** (1) State both readings fairly. (2) Use the notes: 12:24 ("the Lord loved him") favours legitimation, while 11:27 frames the whole line in David's sin. (3) Weigh one episode, e.g. 1 Kings 1 (Nathan and Bathsheba steer the dying David toward Solomon) or 1 Kings 2 (Solomon's killings of Adonijah and Joab). (4) Say what the other reading must explain away.
**Wrong turns:** treating either reading as Church teaching; the purpose of the Succession Narrative is a scholarly question. Reading the rarity of divine action as secularism: the three notes say God is sovereign over the whole plot.
**Model answer (b), one of several:** Critique fits better. The story begins its succession plot with adultery and murder that "displeased the Lord". Its heir comes through Bathsheba, whose son owes the throne to a palace manoeuvre in 1 Kings 1, when Nathan and Bathsheba work on a dying king's memory. Solomon then secures his throne by executions in 1 Kings 2. A court propagandist would hardly narrate all this. Still, 12:24 is a clear divine seal on Solomon, and an apology can admit ugly facts in order to answer them. The legitimation reading must explain why the narrator dwells on the ugliness. The critique reading must explain 12:24. I weigh the sustained narrative over the single note.

**P3** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict:** (1) **Literal sense** of 2 Samuel 7:13–16: the promise is what the text says; how and in whom it is fulfilled is a further question. (2) **Church teaching**: that Jesus is the Christ belongs to the faith (the creed), and the Catechism teaches that he fulfils Israel's messianic hope as David's heir (CCC 436–439). (3) **Scholarly hypothesis**: Noth's Deuteronomistic History, which covers Deuteronomy–Kings, rivalled by Cross's two editions and the Göttingen layers. It has **no magisterial standing**. (4) **Old Biblical Commission response** (1905, on apparently historical narratives): binding when issued. Its practical force was changed by *Divino Afflante Spiritu* and the 1955 statements, and the Commission is consultative since 1971. It is not current doctrine, and not simply void either. (5) **Disputed date**: the 480 years of 1 Kings 6:1 is widely read as schematic (12 × 40), and a fifteenth-century Exodus is a minority reconstruction against most critical and archaeological readings.
**Wrong turns:** (1) as Church teaching "about Solomon": the literal sense is a promise to David's line, and the Christological reading is its fuller or typological sense. (3) as settled because Noth is famous. (4) as either still binding or "abolished in 1943". (5) as secure because it is arithmetic on the text.
**Model answer:** (1) Literal sense of 2 Sam 7. (2) Church teaching: the faith confesses Jesus as Christ, and CCC 436–439 names him David's heir. (3) Hypothesis (Noth, 1943), freely disputed. (4) Old PBC response of 1905: binding then, practically loosened since, the Commission now advisory. (5) Disputed date: the 480 years looks schematic, and a 1446 Exodus is a minority view.

</details>

## Flashback

**From Lesson [2.6](02-06-covenant-and-law.md) (Covenant and law):** *(Exegetical.)* An **invented** study-Bible note on Leviticus 19:

> "Aquinas sorted the Old Law by subject: laws about worship are ceremonial, laws about money and property are judicial, and everything else is moral. So verse 13, 'The wages of him that hath been hired by thee shall not abide with thee until the morning', is judicial, and Christians are no more bound by it than by the price of a gored slave. Leviticus itself flags the three kinds for the reader, and the Church later defined the division as dogma."

Name three errors. For each, give the correction, and give the level where one is involved. **One or two sentences per error.**

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict:** any three of the following.
1. **The criterion is wrong.** Aquinas classifies a precept by **where its binding force comes from**, not by its topic. Moral precepts bind because reason dictates them. Ceremonial and judicial precepts are *determinations* that bind by divine institution (q.99 a.4; q.104 a.1).
2. **Verse 13 is misclassified.** Paying a hired worker what he is owed is dictated by reason, so the duty is **moral** and binds for ever. At most the fixed deadline ("until the morning") is a judicial determination of it. So Christians are still bound to pay just wages promptly.
3. **The text does not mark the kinds.** Leviticus 19 puts worship, justice and personal conduct side by side under one refrain, "I am the Lord". The three-way division is a **later theological reading** the reader brings to the text, not its literal sense.
4. **The level is overstated.** The division is **common theological teaching**, not defined dogma. Trent (Session VI, canon 19) defines only that the Ten Commandments bind Christians. Florence taught that the ceremonies ended. Neither defines the scheme.

**Wrong turns:** calling verse 13 judicial without qualification because it concerns money, which is the note's own error. Answering error 4 by making the division Trent's. Swinging to the opposite error: that because the division is not dogma it is only one theologian's private opinion.
**Model answer:** First, Aquinas asks where a precept gets its force, not what it is about: reason for moral precepts, divine institution for ceremonial and judicial ones. Second, paying a hired man his due is dictated by reason, so verse 13 is moral in substance and still binds, with only the overnight deadline a judicial detail. Third, Leviticus 19 mixes all three kinds under the one refrain "I am the Lord", so the division is a later reading. (Fourth: the scheme is common theological teaching, not a definition. Trent defined only that the Commandments bind.)

</details>
