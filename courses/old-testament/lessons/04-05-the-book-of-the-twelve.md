# The Old Testament · Lesson 4.5: The Book of the Twelve

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 4: The prophets · Builds on: [4.1 What a prophet is](04-01-what-a-prophet-is-amos-and-hosea.md), [4.4 Jeremiah and Ezekiel](04-04-jeremiah-and-ezekiel.md) · Unlocks: [5.1 The Psalms](05-01-the-psalms.md), [6.4 The Old Testament in light of the New](06-04-the-old-testament-in-light-of-the-new.md)

## Why this matters

Five texts the New Testament leans on come from twelve short books: Bethlehem, the king on a donkey, the spirit on all flesh, the sign of Jonah, Elijah before the day of the Lord. They close the prophets, and after them Jewish writers say prophecy stopped. This lesson shows how the collection closed, and how the New Testament claims to reopen it.

## The idea

"Minor" means short, not lesser. Augustine says so outright: the twelve are called minor "from the brevity of their writings" (*City of God* XVIII.29, NPNF). Ancient Jews copied them on **one scroll** and counted them as [**one book**](../reference.md#the-book-of-the-twelve).

- **The evidence is early.** Ben Sira, writing around 190–180 BC, praises "the twelve prophets" as a group (Sir 49:12 in the Douay; 49:10 in modern Bibles). The Babylonian Talmud (*Bava Batra* 14b) explains the single scroll: Hosea copied alone would be lost, being small. Jerome counts "the book of the Twelve Prophets" as the eighth of the Prophets.
- **The order varies.** The Hebrew order, which the Vulgate and Douay keep, runs Hosea, Joel, Amos, Obadiah, Jonah, Micah, Nahum, Habakkuk, Zephaniah, Haggai, Zechariah, Malachi. The Septuagint moves Amos and Micah up: Hosea, Amos, Micah, Joel, Obadiah, Jonah, and the last six as before. So the order is an editor's decision, and editors disagreed.

## Source

Micah 5:2 (Douay; Hebrew 5:1) beside Matthew 2:5–6, the scribes' answer to Herod:

> **Micah 5:2.** And thou Bethlehem Ephrata, art a little one among the thousands of Juda, out of thee shall he come forth unto me that is to be the ruler in Israel: and his going forth is from the beginning, from the days of eternity.
>
> **Matthew 2:5–6.** In Bethlehem of Juda. For so it is written by the prophet: And thou Bethlehem the land of Juda art not the least among the princes of Juda: for out of thee shall come forth the captain that shall rule my people Israel.

Three words decide the reading. Micah calls Bethlehem ***tsa'ir***, "little"; Matthew says "not the least". Micah's ***alfei*** means "thousands", that is, clans; Matthew has "princes", which the same consonants yield if read *allufei*. And "rule my people Israel" is not in Micah at all. It echoes God's charge to David, "Thou shalt feed my people Israel" (2 Kings 5:2 Douay; 2 Samuel 5:2).

## The argument

1. **Micah in his own century.** Micah worked under Jotham, Ahaz and Hezekiah (Mic 1:1). That puts him in the late eighth century, beside Isaiah. In the [literal sense](../reference.md#literal-sense) the oracle promises a new David. The ruler will come not from Jerusalem, which Micah condemns, but from David's home clan town, starting the dynasty over from its small beginning. "His going forth ... from the days of eternity" translates *miqqedem, mime 'olam*, "from of old, from ancient days". That most naturally means an ancient lineage. *In words:* Micah promises a king of David's line from David's village.

2. **The same verse, read on.** [The Targum](../reference.md#jewish-readings) on Micah renders the verse: "from you shall come forth before me the Messiah, ... whose name was spoken from of old" (the lesson's translation of the Aramaic). Rashi likewise reads "the Messiah, son of David", and glosses "from of old" with Psalm 72:17 [Douay 71:17]: his name existed before the sun. The Douay's own note goes further and reads "from the days of eternity" as the Son's eternal generation. That is a **spiritual** reading, distinct from what an eighth-century hearer understood. *In words:* Jewish and Christian readers agree the verse is messianic. They part on who the Messiah is and what "from of old" means. That dispute is argued in [`apologetics-foundations`](../../apologetics-foundations/syllabus.md), not here.

3. **[Jonah and genre](../reference.md#the-genre-of-jonah).** Jonah is a story about a prophet, not a collection of oracles. Its hero is a real eighth-century prophet, "Jonas, the son of Amathi", who predicted Jeroboam II's victories (4 Kings 14:25 Douay; 2 Kings 14:25). Three readings compete.
   - **History.** The book records events. The *Catholic Encyclopedia* article "Jonah" (1910) argued that Jesus's sayings decide the matter (Worked example 2).
   - **Parable.** It is a didactic story about a real figure. Its genre signals: a city "of three days' journey" (3:3); an unnamed "king of Ninive" and a whole empire converted in a day, cattle in sackcloth; a plant that grows up in one night; and a book that ends on God's unanswered question.
   - **Satire.** On this view the story laughs at a prophet. Every pagan in it, sailors and Ninevites alike, is quicker to repent than Jonah, and he is furious because God is merciful (4:2).
   
   Parable and satire overlap. The scholarly majority dates the book after the exile (**disputed**), and Nineveh itself fell in 612 (**secure**). In 1905 the Biblical Commission ruled that a book taken as historical may be read otherwise only when solid arguments prove that the author meant "a parable, an allegory", or some sense other than history (response of 23 June 1905, paraphrased).

4. **Joel's outpouring.** Joel 2:28–32 (Hebrew 3:1–5, since the Hebrew Joel has four chapters): "I will pour out my spirit (*ruchi*) upon all flesh". In context "all flesh" is "your sons and your daughters", Israel, and every member of it, down to the "servants and handmaids". It answers Moses's wish: "O that all the people might prophesy" (Num 11:29). Joel's date is **disputed**, from the ninth century to the fourth, and most scholars now put him after the exile. Acts 2:16–21 reads Pentecost as this outpouring. It changes "after this" to "in the last days", adds "and they shall prophesy", and stops before Joel's "in Mount Sion". Then 2:39 widens the promise "to all that are far off".

5. **Malachi and Elijah.** The collection ends with Malachi 4:5–6 (Hebrew 3:23–24): "Behold, I will send you Elias the prophet, before the coming of the great and dreadful day of the Lord", to turn fathers' hearts to children, "lest I come, and strike the earth with anathema". That last word is [*herem*](../reference.md#herem), the ban of [3.1](03-01-joshua-judges-and-the-land.md). Ben Sira already expects Elijah "to reconcile the heart of the father to the son" (Sir 48:10). Luke 1:17 applies the verse to John the Baptist, "in the spirit and power of Elias". In Matthew 17:12 Jesus says "Elias is already come". In the Mishnah (*Eduyot* 8:7) the Sages hold, citing this verse, that Elijah comes to make peace in the world.

6. **Prophecy falls silent.** Second Temple writers say [prophecy has stopped](../reference.md#the-cessation-of-prophecy). 1 Maccabees marks events "since the day, that there was no prophet seen in Israel" (9:27). The defiled altar's stones wait "till there should come a prophet" (4:46). They make Simon high priest "till there should arise a faithful prophet" (14:41). Josephus ranks later history below the twenty-two books "because there hath not been an exact succession of prophets since that time" (*Against Apion* I.8, Whiston), that is, since Artaxerxes. The Tosefta (*Sotah* 13) says that when Haggai, Zechariah and Malachi died, the holy spirit departed from Israel and only the heavenly voice, the *bat kol*, remained. Yet Josephus himself reports later prophet-like figures, and Rebecca Gray (1993) argues that what "ceased" was the classical, scripture-writing kind. *In words:* the silence is a claim about **canonical** prophecy, not a report that nobody claimed to prophesy.

7. **Reading the Twelve as one book.** Since the 1990s some redaction critics, James Nogalski among them, have read the Twelve as a designed whole with catchwords across the seams. Others, Ehud Ben Zvi among them, read an anthology of separate books. The best test case is the creed of Exodus 34:6. Joel 2:13 and Jonah 4:2 both quote it, down to God's being "ready to repent of the evil". Micah 7:18 praises a God who "delighteth in mercy". Nahum 1:3 then turns: "patient ... and will not ... acquit the guilty". Read together, Jonah's spared Nineveh meets Nahum's doomed Nineveh: a [canonical reading](../reference.md#canonical-reading) no single book produces.

**Mark the levels.** That these twelve books are inspired and canonical is **defined** (rung 1: Trent, via [`fundamental-theology` 3.3](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/03-03-the-canon-as-defined.md)). Their order, the unity of the scroll, Joel's date, the split of Zechariah and Jonah's genre are **scholarly questions with no magisterial standing**. The 1905 response bound Catholic exegetes when issued. The 1955 clarifications by the Commission's officials freed research on such literary questions ([weight](../reference.md#the-biblical-commission-and-its-weight); [`fundamental-theology` 4.5](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-05-reading-a-documents-weight.md)). So Jonah's genre is **freely disputed** (rung 5). It is not settled for "history" by the old decree, and not settled for "parable" by the modern majority. Dates: 612 is **secure**; Micah is **reconstructed**; Joel and Jonah are **disputed**.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean): Zechariah 9:9.** "BEHOLD THY KING will come to thee, the just and saviour: he is poor, and riding upon an ass, and upon a colt, the foal of an ass." **Literal sense first.** Most scholars assign undated chapters 9–14 to a later hand ("Second Zechariah", **disputed**). The king is "poor" (*'ani*, also "humble"). The next verse says what kind of king he is: he destroys the chariot and "the bow for war", and "shall speak peace to the Gentiles". The two animals are Hebrew [parallelism](../reference.md#parallelism), one donkey named twice. **Then the tradition.** The Talmud (*Sanhedrin* 98a) sets this verse beside Daniel 7:13. If Israel merits redemption, the Messiah comes with the clouds; if not, lowly on a donkey. Matthew 21:5 and John 12:15 read Palm Sunday as its fulfilment, and Matthew's two animals are treated in [`new-testament` 2.4](../../new-testament/lessons/02-04-john-and-the-synoptics.md). The literal sense is already a coming king, so the Christian reading extends the oracle instead of reversing it.

**Example 2 (hard): does the sign of Jonah settle the genre?** In Matthew 12:39–41 Jesus gives "the sign of Jonas": three days in the fish, and "the men of Ninive shall rise in judgment ... because they did penance". Luke 11:30 keeps only the preaching. The *Catholic Encyclopedia* (1910) argued that the comparison needs real events: "Christ contrasts fact with fact, not fancy with fancy, nor fancy with fact." The reply: a speaker can cite a story's characters against his hearers without ruling on its genre, as preachers do with the prodigal son. The sources strain too. Josephus, whom the 1910 article cites for historicity, tells the fish with "It is also reported", adds that he gives the account "as I found it written", and omits Nineveh's repentance (*Antiquities* IX.10.2, Whiston). The saying fits either genre; the decision needs an argument about the book itself.

## Watch out

- **Hypothesis stated as teaching:** "The Church teaches Jonah is a parable." No act teaches that. The majority's genre verdict has no magisterial standing.
- **Teaching stated as current law:** "The 1905 decree still requires Catholics to read Jonah as history." It was binding in 1905. Its present force on a literary question is what the 1955 clarifications changed ([1.3](01-03-the-historical-critical-method-and-its-limits.md)).
- **"Micah names Jesus's birthplace word for word."** Matthew's wording differs, and Micah's literal sense is a Davidic king from David's clan. Fulfilment is not dictation.
- **"Prophecy ended in 400 BC."** The cessation is a Second Temple claim about canonical prophecy, not a recorded date.

## One-liner

> Twelve short books on one scroll close the prophets on Elijah's promised return, and after them Judaism says prophecy fell silent; the New Testament answers with John as Elijah and Joel's spirit poured out at Pentecost.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢)** Jonah 4:2–3, 10–11 (Douay–Rheims):

> And he prayed to the Lord, and said: I beseech thee, O Lord, is not this what I said, when I was yet in my own country? therefore I went before to flee into Tharsis: for I know that thou art a gracious and merciful God, patient, and of much compassion, and easy to forgive evil. And now, O Lord, I beseech thee take my life from me: for it is better for me to die than to live. ... And the Lord said: Thou art grieved for the ivy, for which thou hast not laboured, nor made it to grow, which in one night came up, and in one night perished. And shall I not spare Ninive, that great city, in which there are more than a hundred and twenty thousand persons, that know not how to distinguish between their right hand and their left, and many beasts?

(a) *(Exegetical)* Why did Jonah flee in chapter 1, according to this passage? Which earlier text does his prayer quote, and what is ironic about his quoting it here? Name two features of the passage that readers of the parable or satire view cite as genre signals. **Three sentences.**
(b) *(Evaluative)* In **100 words or fewer**, say which genre reading (history, parable, satire) best explains chapter 4, and state the strongest point against your choice. Any verdict passes.

**P2 (🟡)** *(Exegetical)* (a) Give the Hebrew reference for each Douay reference: Joel 2:28; Joel 2:32; Joel 3:2; Malachi 4:5; Micah 5:2. **One line.** (b) Compare Joel 2:28–29, 32 (Douay) with Acts 2:17–18, 21, 39:

> **Joel.** And it shall come to pass after this, that I will pour out my spirit upon all flesh ... Moreover, upon my servants and handmaids in those days I will pour forth my spirit. ... every one that shall call upon the name of the Lord, shall be saved: for in Mount Sion, and in Jerusalem shall be salvation, as the Lord hath said, and in the residue whom the Lord shall call.
>
> **Acts.** And it shall come to pass, in the last days ... I will pour out of my Spirit upon all flesh ... And upon my servants indeed and upon my handmaids will I pour out in those days of my spirit: and they shall prophesy. ... whosoever shall call upon the name of the Lord shall be saved. ... to all that are far off, whomsoever the Lord our God shall call.

Name three changes Acts makes and say what each does to the scope or time of the promise. Then say who "all flesh" is in Joel's literal sense, and which words show it. **Four sentences.**

**P3 (🔴)** *(Exegetical)* An **invented** parish handout:

> "The minor prophets are the less important prophets, which is why they come last. Their words were fulfilled to the letter: Micah names Bethlehem, and Matthew quotes him word for word. The Church settled that Jonah is history when the Biblical Commission ruled in 1905, so Catholics may not read it as a parable. Prophecy then ended in 400 BC with Malachi, as Jews and Christians have always agreed, and that is why the Church teaches the Old Testament canon closed then."

Name **four** errors, and for each give the correction from this lesson. At least one must be a confusion of levels. **150 words or fewer.**

<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b))*

**Must hit, strict (a):** Jonah fled **because he knew God would forgive Nineveh**. He did not fear failure; he feared mercy ("is not this what I said ... therefore I went before to flee"). His prayer quotes the **divine self-description of Exodus 34:6** ("gracious and merciful ... patient ... of much compassion"), the creed Joel 2:13 also quotes. The irony: Israel's confession of God's mercy becomes Jonah's **complaint**, and the prophet asks to die because God is what the creed says he is. Any two genre signals: the plant that "in one night came up, and in one night perished"; the book **ends on God's unanswered question**; the prophet asking to die over the success of his own preaching; the huge round number (120,000) and "many beasts" as objects of God's care; the exaggerated contrast between pagans and the prophet.
**Must hit, any verdict (b):** a clear verdict; at least one feature of chapter 4 used as evidence; the strongest opposing point stated fairly. Against history: the one-night plant and the open ending read as teaching devices. Against parable/satire: the book names a real prophet (4 Kings 14:25) and tells its story in narrative form with no parable marker. Credit noting that parable and satire can be combined.
**Wrong turns:** saying Jonah fled from fear of the Ninevites or of failure; the passage says otherwise. Treating the genre as settled by the Church in either direction.
**Model answer (a):** Jonah fled because he expected God to forgive Nineveh, and says so. He quotes the creed of Exodus 34:6, so Israel's praise of God's mercy becomes a grievance in his mouth. The plant grown and withered in a night, and the book ending on God's unanswered question, are what parable readers point to.
**Model answer (b), one of several:** Satire fits chapter 4 best. The prophet's anger at the success of his own preaching is played for irony, and the plant is a staged lesson. The book ends on a question aimed at the reader, not a report of what Jonah did next. The strongest point against: the book names a historical prophet and uses the plain narrative style of Kings, with no marker like "a certain man" that signals a parable. A historical story can also carry irony.

**P2** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** Joel 2:28 = **3:1**; Joel 2:32 = **3:5**; Joel 3:2 = **4:2**; Malachi 4:5 = **3:23**; Micah 5:2 = **5:1**.
**Must hit, strict (b):** any three changes. (1) **"After this" becomes "in the last days"**: an indefinite future becomes the end-time, and Peter says it is now. (2) **"And they shall prophesy" added** after the servants: the gift is named as prophecy, which bears on the claim that prophecy has returned. (3) **Joel's "for in Mount Sion, and in Jerusalem" is cut**, so salvation is not tied to a place. (4) **2:39's "all that are far off, whomsoever the Lord our God shall call"** picks up Joel's last words, "whom the Lord shall call", and widens them past Israel. Also acceptable: "of my Spirit", a share of it. **"All flesh" in Joel** = all Israel, of every age, sex and rank. The words "*your* sons and *your* daughters", "your old men", "your young men" and "servants and handmaids" show it.
**Wrong turns:** reading Joel's "all flesh" as all nations in its literal sense; that is the extension Acts makes, not Joel's own horizon. Mixing up the directions (Hebrew 3:1 = Douay 2:28, not the other way round).
**Model answer:** Acts turns "after this" into "in the last days", making the promise an end-time event happening now. It adds "and they shall prophesy", naming the gift as renewed prophecy. It drops "in Mount Sion, and in Jerusalem" and then echoes Joel's closing "whom the Lord shall call" to reach "all that are far off". In Joel, "all flesh" means every Israelite, as "your sons and your daughters" and the servants show, without distinction of age, sex or rank.

**P3** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict:** any four.
- **"Minor" means short**, not less important (Augustine, *City of God* XVIII.29). They come last because they were one scroll, the Twelve, not by rank.
- **Matthew does not quote Micah word for word.** "Little" becomes "not the least", "thousands" becomes "princes", and "rule my people Israel" comes from 2 Samuel 5:2. The literal sense promises a Davidic king from David's clan.
- **Level error on 1905.** The response bound exegetes when issued, and the 1955 clarifications freed research on literary questions like genre. Jonah's genre is **freely disputed**. The response itself also allowed a non-historical reading where solid arguments proved it.
- **"Prophecy ended in 400 BC" is not a recorded date.** The cessation is a Second Temple *claim* (1 Macc, Josephus, the Tosefta), about canonical prophecy. Christians hold that prophecy returned with John and Pentecost, so they have not "always agreed".
- **Level error on the canon.** The Church defines **which books** are canonical (Trent). It teaches no date when prophecy ended or the canon closed; that is history.
**Wrong turns:** "correcting" the first error by saying the Twelve are later than the major prophets; Amos, Hosea and Micah are eighth-century, as early as Isaiah. Treating the 1905 response as never having bound anyone.
**Model answer:** "Minor" means short, as Augustine says, and the Twelve stand together because they were one scroll. Matthew does not quote Micah word for word: he changes "little" to "not the least" and adds a line from 2 Samuel 5:2. The 1905 response once bound exegetes, but after the 1955 clarifications Jonah's genre is freely disputed, and the response itself allowed for parable given solid proof. No one recorded prophecy ending in 400 BC; that is a Second Temple claim about canonical prophecy, and Christians hold it resumed. The Church defines which books are canonical, not a date when the canon closed.

## Flashback

**From Lesson [4.3](04-03-the-servant-of-the-lord.md) (The Servant of the Lord):** *(Exegetical.)* In the first Servant Song (Isaiah 42:1–4), God's chosen servant receives God's spirit and brings justice to the nations quietly, without breaking "the bruised reed". A few verses later the same chapter goes on (Douay–Rheims):

> **42:18–20.** Hear, ye deaf, and, ye blind, behold that you may see. Who is blind, but my servant? or deaf, but he to whom I have sent my messengers? Who is blind, but he that is sold? or who is blind, but the servant of the Lord? Thou that seest many things, wilt thou not observe them? thou that hast ears open, wilt thou not hear?
>
> **42:22, 24.** But this is a people that is robbed and wasted ... they are made a prey, and there is none to deliver them ... Who hath given Jacob for a spoil, and Israel to robbers? hath not the Lord himself, against whom we have sinned? And they would not walk in his ways, and they have not hearkened to his law.

"Servant" in 42:19 is *'ebed*, "servant, slave", both times it occurs.

(a) Who is the servant of 42:19 in the literal sense, and which words show it? (b) Name two ways this servant differs from the servant of 42:1–4. (c) Say how Bernhard Duhm (1892) used contrasts like this one, and how a reader who keeps the Songs in their context answers him. Then give the level of the question of who the Songs' servant is in the literal sense. **Two sentences per part.**

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):** the servant is **Jacob-Israel, the people**. The text slides from "my servant" (42:19) to "a people that is robbed and wasted" (42:22) and names it: "Who hath given **Jacob** for a spoil, and **Israel** to robbers?" (42:24). This matches the context's regular usage, "thou Israel, art my servant" (41:8).
**Must hit, strict (b):** any two of these. The first Song's servant **carries out a mission**; this servant is **blind and deaf** to what it sees and hears (42:19–20). That servant **brings justice** to the nations; this one is **prey with "none to deliver"** it, the object of rescue rather than its agent. This servant suffers **for its own sin**: "against whom we have sinned ... they would not walk in his ways" (42:24). The Song's servant is faithful.
**Must hit, strict (c):** **Duhm** took the gap between the faithful servant of the Songs and the failing servant Israel of the surrounding chapters as grounds to **separate the four Songs** as poems about a different figure. A **contextual reader** answers that the Songs portray what Israel is called to be: **ideal or faithful Israel**, or a remnant, set beside the blind nation God is still forming. Some add that the book sets the two side by side on purpose. Duhm's separation is itself **debated**. The servant's literal identity is a **scholarly question with no magisterial answer**. What the Church teaches is fulfilment in Christ (CCC 601), which does not settle the literal referent.
**Wrong turns:** reading the blind servant of 42:19 as the Messiah or as Christ; 42:24 names Jacob-Israel. Treating the contrast as proof that the Songs' servant is an **individual**: it shows only that he differs from the failing nation, and the ideal-Israel reading accounts for that. Stating that the Church teaches the Songs are separate compositions, or teaches that they are not.
**Model answer:** (a) The servant is the people Israel, blind and deaf to God's instruction. Verse 24 names it, "Jacob ... Israel", plundered because "we have sinned", and 41:8 already calls Israel God's servant. (b) The Song's servant brings justice to the nations, and this servant is itself plundered prey with "none to deliver". That servant is faithful, and this one "would not walk in his ways". (c) Duhm took such contrasts as a sign that the Songs describe a different figure, and so he set the four poems apart. A contextual reader answers that the Songs show the faithful Israel God is making out of the blind one. Who the servant is in the literal sense is a scholarly question, with no magisterial answer; the Church's teaching concerns fulfilment in Christ.
