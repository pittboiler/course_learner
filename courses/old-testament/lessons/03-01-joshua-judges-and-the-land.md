# The Old Testament · Lesson 3.1: Joshua, Judges, and the land

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 3: From conquest to return · Builds on: [1.2 The senses of Scripture](01-02-the-senses-of-scripture.md), [1.3 The historical-critical method and its limits](01-03-the-historical-critical-method-and-its-limits.md), [2.5 Exodus and Sinai](02-05-exodus-and-sinai.md) · Unlocks: [3.2 David and the house of David](03-02-david-and-the-house-of-david.md), [3.3 Two kingdoms and two falls](03-03-two-kingdoms-and-two-falls.md)

## Why this matters

Joshua narrates the taking of Canaan, and several times says that Israel, at God's command, left no one alive in a captured city. Three questions tangle here. The first is literary: how does the text portray the conquest? The second is historical: what happened in Canaan around 1200 BC? The third is theological: what is a Christian to do with the ban? Run together, they produce two mirror-image mistakes, "it's all true, so it's a model" and "it's all false, so skip it".

## The idea

The Bible tells the taking of the land twice, in two different voices. **Joshua** gives the sweeping version. In three campaigns (the centre, the south, the north) Israel crosses the Jordan, takes the country and divides it, and the land rests. **Judges** opens with a tribe-by-tribe ledger of what was *not* taken: Canaanites still in the plains, in Jerusalem and in the northern towns, living alongside Israel or paying it tribute. Then comes a downward spiral of apostasy and rescue, framed by the refrain that "there was no king in Israel: but every one did that which seemed right to himself" (Judg 21:25 [Douay 21:24]).

Three surprises follow. Joshua itself contains both voices. Archaeology's answer sits much closer to Judges than to Joshua 1–12. And the tradition never read the ban as a policy to imitate.

## Source

Joshua against itself (Douay–Rheims):

> "So Josue took all the land, as the Lord spoke to Moses, and delivered it in possession to the children of Israel, according to their divisions and tribes. And the land rested from wars." (Josh 11:23)
>
> "Josue was old, and far advanced in years, and the Lord said to him: Thou art grown old, and advanced in age, and there is a very large country left, which is not yet divided by lot" (13:1)
>
> "But the children of Juda could not destroy the Jebusite that dwelt in Jerusalem: and the Jebusite dwelt with the children of Juda in Jerusalem until this present day." (15:63)

Two chapters after "all the land", God tells Joshua that much is left. The Douay follows the Vulgate's "not yet divided by lot". The Hebrew is blunter: "very much of the land remains to be possessed" (the lesson's translation). And 15:63 is nearly the same sentence as Judges 1:21, with Benjamin swapped for Judah. The editor who kept 11:23 kept these too. So the tension is inside Joshua, not only between two books.

## The argument

1. **The summaries are a genre, not a census.** Joshua 10:40 and 11:16–23 total up the campaigns in absolute terms: every region, every king, no survivors. K. Lawson Younger (*Ancient Conquest Accounts*, 1990) showed that Assyrian, Hittite and Egyptian campaign reports use the same totalizing hyperbole of wars that plainly left the enemy standing. Most critical scholars also read Joshua as part of a history edited in the Deuteronomic style, probably between the seventh century and the exile. That is Noth's Deuteronomistic History, taught in [3.2](03-02-david-and-the-house-of-david.md). *In words:* "all the land" is the victory report's way of speaking, and the same book corrects it in its own later chapters.

2. **Four historical models.** Scholars explain Israel's appearance in the hill country in four ways (the table below). W. F. Albright defended a **conquest** in the thirteenth century, pointing to destruction layers at several Late Bronze cities. Albrecht Alt ("Die Landnahme der Israeliten in Palästina", 1925) proposed a gradual and mostly peaceful **infiltration** by pastoralists settling in the thinly populated highlands. George Mendenhall ("The Hebrew Conquest of Palestine", 1962) and Norman Gottwald (*The Tribes of Yahweh*, 1979) proposed a **revolt** of Canaanite peasants against the city-states, organized around the worship of YHWH. Israel Finkelstein (*The Archaeology of the Israelite Settlement*, 1988) and William Dever (*Who Were the Early Israelites and Where Did They Come From?*, 2003) argue for **emergence** from within Canaan.

3. **The test cases.** At **Jericho**, Kathleen Kenyon's excavations (1952–58) dated the destruction of the walled city to the sixteenth century BC and found little from the late thirteenth. Bryant Wood (1990) reargued a fifteenth-century destruction, a minority view. **Ai**, if it is et-Tell (the name means "the ruin"), was a large Early Bronze city destroyed in the third millennium and empty until a small village appeared around 1200. **Hazor**, the one city Joshua says was burned (11:13), does have a thirteenth-century destruction by fire, and who burned it is disputed. In the central highlands, surveys found hundreds of small new villages in Iron I (c. 1200–1000 BC). Their pottery continues the Canaanite tradition and their bone refuse lacks pig. The Merneptah stele (c. 1208 BC, [2.5](02-05-exodus-and-sinai.md)) already names "Israel" as a people in Canaan. *In words:* the evidence fits a gradual emergence, with some destructions, much better than one sweeping campaign. Most archaeologists now hold some version of emergence; the details remain contested.

4. **The ban.** *Herem* (Hebrew) is what is **devoted**, withdrawn from human use and given over to God, which in war means destroyed. The Vulgate renders it *anathema*, hence Douay "let this city be an anathema" (Josh 6:17). Deuteronomy distinguishes cases. Distant cities are first offered peace (Deut 20:10–15). The seven nations of the land are to be devoted (7:1–2; 20:16–18), and the stated reason is religious contamination: marriage and "strange gods" (7:3–4). On the Mesha stele (mid-ninth century BC, found 1868), Moab's king says of the Israelite town of Nebo, in King's 1878 translation, "for I devoted them to Ashtar-Chemosh". So *herem* was a Near Eastern war practice, not a biblical invention. A common critical view also holds that Deuteronomy's ban was written when the Canaanites were long gone, as a charter for Israel's exclusive loyalty to YHWH rather than a record of policy. The text also carries counter-voices: Rahab and her household spared (Josh 2; 6:25), the Gibeonites' treaty (Josh 9), the man of Bethel let go (Judg 1:24–25). Rahab ends up in Matthew's genealogy (Matt 1:5) and Hebrews' roll of faith (Heb 11:31).

5. **How the tradition read it.** Within the canon, Wisdom 12:8–10 already softens the account. God destroyed the inhabitants "by little and little" and gave them "place of repentance". Augustine, answering the Manichaean Faustus, defended the wars as commanded: in them Moses showed "not ferocity but obedience" (*Reply to Faustus* 22.74, NPNF). **Origen** went the other way. His *Homilies on Joshua* survive in Rufinus's Latin. There he reads Joshua, whose name in Greek is *Iēsous*, as a figure of Jesus. The Canaanite kings stand for the demons and vices that must be driven out of the soul, and none may be left standing. The war is Paul's "wrestling" against principalities (Eph 6:12). This is his rule at work: an unworthy letter signals a spiritual sense ([`patristics` 3.5](../../patristics/lessons/03-05-allegory-and-history-alexandria-and-antioch.md)). He used it against Marcionites, who took these wars as proof of a lesser God. Challoner's preface to Josue gives the same typology. **The rabbinic tradition** reads the ban through Deuteronomy 20:10. In *Deuteronomy Rabbah* (5:14), Joshua proclaimed that whoever wished to leave might leave, whoever wished to make peace might make peace, and whoever wished to fight might fight. The Girgashites left, and the Gibeonites made peace. Maimonides codifies this (*Mishneh Torah*, Kings and Wars 6:1–5): peace is offered first even to the seven nations, and only those who refuse are subject to the ban.

6. **The Church's reading rule.** *Verbum Domini* 42 (Benedict XVI, 2010, paraphrased) takes up these "dark" passages. Revelation is rooted in history and progressive. It met ancient peoples at their cultural and moral level, so it sometimes narrates violence and massacre without explicitly condemning them. The prophets then challenged every kind of violence. Such passages should not be skipped. They need a reading in their historical-literary context, with the paschal mystery as the final key. The philosophical question of whether God could command such a thing belongs to [`philosophy-of-religion`](../../philosophy-of-religion/syllabus.md) Module 4.

**Mark the levels.** Whether a conquest happened, and on what scale, is a **scholarly question with no magisterial answer** (see [Church teaching vs hypothesis](../reference.md#church-teaching-vs-hypothesis)). Each of the four models is a hypothesis. The emergence model is the majority's, and a majority is not a result. The Biblical Commission's response of 23 June 1905 allowed, in substance, that a narrative may be read as only apparently historical when solid arguments prove it, subject to the Church's judgment. Weigh it as [1.3](01-03-the-historical-critical-method-and-its-limits.md) weighs the early responses: binding discipline then, not a standing verdict now. What the Church teaches is conciliar: Scripture teaches without error the truth God willed for our salvation (Dei Verbum 11; [`fundamental-theology` 3.2](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/03-02-inerrancy.md)), and genre governs what a text asserts (DV 12). *Verbum Domini* is a post-synodal apostolic exhortation, **authentic ordinary magisterium** ([the ladder](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-04-the-ladder-of-doctrinal-authority.md)). It gives a reading principle and no verdict on historicity. Origen's and Augustine's readings are **patristic opinions**, not definitions. The rabbinic readings are Jewish tradition's own. **Dates:** Merneptah c. 1208 and the Mesha stele c. 840 are reconstructed to within a few years or decades. The date of any conquest is disputed: 1 Kings 6:1's 480 years point to the fifteenth century, while the archaeology favours the thirteenth to twelfth. The composition of Joshua–Judges is disputed.

## Map of positions

| Model | Main names | Where Israel came from | Evidence it leans on | Where it strains |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Conquest | Albright | outside, by war | destruction layers (Hazor, others) | Jericho and Ai empty at the time; destructions spread over decades |
| Infiltration | Alt (1925) | outside, peacefully | Judges 1; new highland villages | pottery continuity with Canaan; pastoralists need not come from outside |
| Revolt | Mendenhall (1962), Gottwald (1979) | inside, by social upheaval | continuity; a YHWH covenant against the city-kings | little direct evidence of a revolt |
| Emergence | Finkelstein, Dever | inside, gradually | village surveys, continuous pottery, no pig bones | explains Israel's origin, but not why Israel remembered coming from Egypt |

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean): Judges 1:19.** "He possessed the hill country: but was not able to destroy the inhabitants of the valley, because they had many chariots armed with scythes." Run the models. The verse puts Israel in the **highlands and not the plains**, which is where the Iron I villages cluster. Every model can live with that. Infiltration and emergence *predict* it, and conquest has to explain it away. Challoner's note blames "cowardly fear": the book's own theological frame, a reading of the literal sense and not a historical datum.

**Example 2 (hard): Jericho and inerrancy.** A reader says: "If Kenyon is right, Joshua 6 is false, and inerrancy fails." First ask what the text asserts in its genre (DV 12), and argue the genre *from the text*. Joshua itself corrects its own "all the land" (13:1; 15:63). The walls fall to a liturgy of priests and trumpets, not to a siege. And the book shares the idiom of ancient conquest accounts. Where it strains: if the genre appeal appears *only* where a spade bites, it is ad hoc, the same fault [`philosophical-method`](../../philosophical-method/lessons/02-01-inference-to-the-best-explanation.md) flags in any rescue hypothesis. Some Catholic exegetes hold that a real, smaller event lies behind Joshua 6. Neither answer is Church teaching.

## Watch out

- **"The Church teaches that the conquest happened as Joshua describes."** No magisterial act determines the conquest's scale or date. The 1905 response concerned historical narratives generally and weighs as [1.3](01-03-the-historical-critical-method-and-its-limits.md) says. Conversely, **"archaeology has disproved Joshua"** turns a majority reconstruction into a result, and a result about genre into one about falsehood.
- **Reading the ban as a model.** Neither the Fathers nor the rabbis did. Origen spiritualized it, Augustine confined it to a unique divine command, and the rabbis required peace to be offered first. *Verbum Domini* 42 reads it through the paschal mystery.
- **Treating Origen's reading as the literal sense.** His typology is a spiritual sense ([1.2](01-02-the-senses-of-scripture.md)). It does not answer the historical question and was never meant to.

## One-liner

> Joshua's "all the land" is a victory report its own book corrects; the land came slowly, historians still argue how, and the tradition read the ban as a war inside the soul or a peace first offered, never as a policy.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢)** *(Exegetical)* Close reading (Douay–Rheims):

> "So Josue took all the land, as the Lord spoke to Moses, and delivered it in possession to the children of Israel, according to their divisions and tribes. And the land rested from wars." (Josh 11:23)
>
> "Manasses also did not destroy Bethsan, and Thanac, with their villages; nor the inhabitants of Dor, and Jeblaam, and Mageddo, with their villages. And the Chanaanite began to dwell with them. But after Israel was grown strong, he made them tributaries, and would not destroy them." (Judg 1:27–28)

(a) State what each passage claims about the extent of the conquest, and name the words that decide it. (b) Judges 1:28 goes further than saying Israel *could not* destroy the Canaanites. Say what it adds. (c) Name one verse in Joshua that already agrees with Judges, and say what that does to the claim that "Joshua and Judges contradict each other". **Two sentences per part.**

**P2 (🟡)** Five data:

1. Kenyon: Jericho's walled city destroyed in the sixteenth century, little occupation in the late thirteenth.
2. A thirteenth-century destruction by fire at Hazor.
3. Hundreds of new small villages in the central highlands in Iron I.
4. Their pottery continues lowland Canaanite forms.
5. Pig bones are nearly absent from those villages.

(a) *(Exegetical)* For each datum, name the model (conquest, infiltration, revolt, emergence) it most helps and one it troubles. One line each. (b) *(Evaluative)* In 120 words or fewer, say which model best explains the whole set. Name the strongest datum against your choice and say what, if anything, your verdict requires of a Catholic reader's view of inspiration.

**P3 (🔴)** *(Exegetical)* Two **invented** homily excerpts:

> **Preacher A:** "Joshua shows us how God deals with those who corrupt a people: total separation, no compromise. The ban was God's model then, and the faithful should take it as the model for how we treat the enemies of the faith today."
>
> **Preacher B:** "Scholars have proven that none of this happened, and the Church now says these violent books were mistakes of a primitive people. We can simply leave Joshua out."

(a) For each preacher, name the misreading of the text or of the tradition, and cite the passage or document that corrects it. (b) For each, name the level error: which claim is given a weight it does not have? **Two sentences per preacher per part.**

<details>
<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** Joshua 11:23 claims a **complete** conquest: "took all the land" and "the land rested from wars". Judges 1:27–28 claims a **partial** one: "did not destroy", "the Chanaanite began to dwell with them".
**Must hit, strict (b):** 1:28 makes the failure partly a **choice**. Once Israel "was grown strong" it "made them tributaries, and would not destroy them", which prefers tribute to the ban. That is what Judges 2:2–3 then indicts.
**Must hit, strict (c):** Joshua 15:63 (the Jebusites in Jerusalem "until this present day", nearly Judges 1:21) or 13:1 (much land left). So the tension sits **inside Joshua**, and its editor kept both. That points to 11:23 as a summary in the idiom of conquest reports, not a contradiction between two books that the editor failed to notice.
**Wrong turns:** reading 1:28 as inability only. Arguing from the Douay's "not yet divided by lot" as if it were the Hebrew. Treating (c) as proof that no conquest happened, when it is a literary point.
**Model answer:** (a) Joshua 11:23 claims the whole land taken and at rest ("all the land", "rested from wars"). Judges 1:27–28 lists cities left standing whose Canaanites "dwell with them". (b) Verse 28 says that once strong, Israel chose tribute over destruction ("would not destroy them"). The failure is disobedience as well as weakness. (c) Joshua 15:63 already says Judah could not remove the Jebusites of Jerusalem. The contradiction is within Joshua, so 11:23 reads as a stylized summary its own editor qualified.

**P2** *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b))*

**Must hit, strict (a):** 1: troubles **conquest**, fits the others. 2: helps **conquest**, and is compatible with others because who burned Hazor is disputed. 3: helps **infiltration** and **emergence**, and is neutral to mildly troubling for conquest, which predicts destroyed cities rather than new villages. 4: helps **emergence/revolt** (continuity from inside Canaan) and troubles **conquest/infiltration** from outside. 5: marks a **distinct identity** usable by any model; it does not settle where the people came from.
**Must hit, any verdict (b):** choose a model; name the strongest datum against it (for emergence, usually the tradition of an origin in Egypt and Hazor; for conquest, Jericho and Ai); state that inspiration **does not require** any one model, because what Joshua asserts depends on its genre (DV 11–12) and no magisterial act fixes the conquest's scale.
**Wrong turns:** making datum 5 decisive for "Israelites came from outside". Saying a Catholic must hold the conquest model. Saying the emergence model is proven.
**Model answer (b), one of several:** Emergence explains the most: new highland villages, pottery continuous with Canaan, and a distinct diet all fit a population forming inside the land. Jericho's silence costs it nothing. Its weak point is Hazor's burning and, more deeply, Israel's insistent memory of coming from Egypt, which a small immigrant group within an emerging Israel could explain. Nothing here strains inspiration. The Church teaches that Scripture asserts without error what God wills for our salvation, read by genre. It does not teach any settlement model.

**P3** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** A turns a narrated ban into a **standing policy**. Correctives: the tradition never read it so (Origen's spiritual war via Eph 6:12; the rabbinic peace-first reading; Wis 12:10 "place of repentance"), and *Verbum Domini* 42 reads such passages through the paschal mystery. B misstates both the scholarship and the Church. The scale of the conquest is **disputed**, not "proven" false. *Verbum Domini* 42 says the dark passages must **not be neglected**, which is the opposite of leaving Joshua out.
**Must hit, strict (b):** A gives an **ancient narrative** (or Deuteronomy's program) the force of a present command; no Church teaching does so. B gives a **scholarly majority** the weight of a result, and invents a Church teaching ("mistakes") that contradicts Dei Verbum 11.
**Wrong turns:** answering A with a philosophical theodicy (ceded to `philosophy-of-religion`). Correcting B by asserting that the conquest happened as narrated, which swaps one level error for another.
**Model answer:** (a) A reads the ban as a model, which no stream of the tradition did: Origen spiritualized it, the rabbis required peace first, and *Verbum Domini* 42 reads it in light of the paschal mystery. B says the dark passages may be dropped, when *Verbum Domini* 42 says they must not be neglected but read in context. (b) A gives an ancient narrative, or Deuteronomy's program, the force of present Church teaching. B gives a contested majority reconstruction the force of proof, and attributes to the Church a doctrine of biblical error it rejects (DV 11).

## Flashback

**From Lesson [2.5](02-05-exodus-and-sinai.md) (Exodus and Sinai):** *(Exegetical.)* Two positions on the Sinai covenant and the ancient treaties, paraphrased:

- **In Mendenhall's line (1954):** the covenant has the shape of the Hittite vassal treaties of the fourteenth and thirteenth centuries BC: a preamble and a historical prologue recalling the overlord's favours ("I am the Lord thy God, who brought thee out of the land of Egypt", Ex 20:2), then stipulations, deposit and reading, witnesses, blessings and curses. Since that shape belongs to those centuries, Israel's covenant tradition reaches back to them.
- **In McCarthy's line (1963):** the treaty form stayed much the same for two millennia, and Deuteronomy's curses are closest to Esarhaddon's succession treaty of 672 BC. The full form is also clearer in Deuteronomy than in Exodus.

(a) Name the single premise the first position needs and the second denies. (b) Name one kind of evidence that would move it, and say which way. (c) Give the question's level in one sentence. **100 words or fewer in total.**

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):** the crux is **whether the treaty form, or the features Exodus shares with it, is distinctive of the late second millennium**, so that it can **date** a text. Both sides grant that Israel pictured its covenant as a vassal treaty.
**Must hit, strict (b):** a comparison of treaties across periods. If features Exodus shares, such as the **historical prologue**, occur in second-millennium treaties but **not** in first-millennium ones like Esarhaddon's, the first position gains. If first-millennium treaties show them too, the second gains. Either direction counts if stated.
**Must hit, strict (c):** a **scholarly hypothesis** with **no magisterial standing**. The date of the covenant form is **disputed**, while what the Church teaches, that God gave Israel his law at Sinai (CCC 62), does not depend on which side wins.
**Wrong turns:** making the crux "whether the Sinai covenant resembles a treaty", which both grant. Treating Esarhaddon's 672 BC date as disputed: it is secure. Making (c) Church teaching because CCC 62 affirms the Sinai covenant.
**Model answer:** (a) Whether the treaty shape is specific to the fourteenth and thirteenth centuries, and so can date Exodus; McCarthy denies it. (b) If Exodus's historical prologue has parallels only in Hittite treaties and none in first-millennium ones, the early date gains; if Assyrian treaties have it too, the form dates nothing. (c) A disputed scholarly hypothesis; the Church's teaching on Sinai does not ride on it.

</details>
