# The Old Testament · Lesson 2.3: The primeval history: fall, flood, Babel

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 2: The Pentateuch · Builds on: [2.1 Two accounts of creation](02-01-two-accounts-of-creation.md), [2.2 Who wrote the Pentateuch?](02-02-who-wrote-the-pentateuch.md) · Unlocks: [2.4 The patriarchs](02-04-the-patriarchs.md)

## Why this matters

Genesis 3–11 is where Christian doctrine finds the fall, where the West found its picture of the flood, and where the nineteenth century found its first great shock: a Babylonian flood story older than any copy of Genesis. Read episode by episode, the chapters look like a scrapbook. Read as a unit, they make a single argument about what sin does to the world and what God does about it. This lesson reads them that way, beside their Near Eastern parallels.

## The idea

Five stories, one plot. Each time humans cross a line, the damage spreads further. Eden: a couple and a tree. Cain: a brother. Lamech: a boast of revenge. The "sons of God": the boundary of heaven and earth. The flood: "all flesh". Babel: the whole race acting as one. Each time God judges, and each time, except the last, he also softens the blow. The garments of skins, Cain's mark and Noah's ark are all mercy inside the judgment.

The frame holds it together. Genesis marks its sections with the formula *elleh toledot*, "these are the generations of": 2:4, 5:1, 6:9, 10:1, 11:10, 11:27. The genealogies carry the blessing "increase and multiply" forward through the disasters, and they narrow from all humanity to Shem to Terah, whose son Abram ends the unit. Babel has no mercy scene; God's answer is the call of Abram.

## Source

Genesis 6:5–7 and 8:21 (Douay–Rheims), the flood's reason and the flood's end:

> "And God seeing that the wickedness of men was great on the earth, and that all the thought of their heart was bent upon evil at all times, It repented him that he had made man on the earth. And being touched inwardly with sorrow of heart, He said: I will destroy man, whom I have created, from the face of the earth ... "
>
> "And the Lord smelled a sweet savour, and said: I will no more curse the earth for the sake of man: for the imagination and thought of man's heart are prone to evil from his youth: therefore I will no more destroy every living soul as I have done."

The same Hebrew word stands behind "thought" in 6:5 and "imagination" in 8:21: *yetser*, "inclination" or "what is formed". The diagnosis is unchanged. Before the flood, the evil inclination is the reason to destroy. After it, the same inclination is the reason never to destroy again. The flood did not cure humanity; God resolves to bear with it. Rabbinic tradition built its teaching on the *yetser ha-ra*, the evil inclination, which Torah is given to master, partly on these verses. The Christian doctrine of concupiscence is a separate development, owned by [`creation-grace-last-things`](../../creation-grace-last-things/syllabus.md).

## The argument

1. **Eden.** The humans are "naked" (*'arummim*, 2:25) and the serpent is "subtle" (*'arum*, 3:1), a pun that ties tempter to tempted. Genesis 3 never calls the serpent the devil. That identification comes later, in Wisdom 2:24 ("by the envy of the devil, death came into the world") and Revelation 12:9. In 3:15 the Hebrew says the woman's offspring, *hu'* ("he" or "it"), will strike the serpent's head. The Septuagint has the masculine *autos*, and the Vulgate *ipsa*, "she", which the Douay follows. The Church reads the verse as the [Protoevangelium](../reference.md#protoevangelium), the first announcement of a Redeemer (CCC 410). The doctrine of original sin is taught in [`creation-grace-last-things`](../../creation-grace-last-things/syllabus.md), and Paul's Adam in [`new-testament` 5.3](../../new-testament/lessons/05-03-romans-5-11-adam-spirit-israel.md). *In words:* the literal sense tells how trust was lost; the doctrine is built on it but is not identical with it.
2. **Cain and Lamech.** Sin crouches "at the door" (4:7), and the first murder follows. Cain's line then builds a city and invents herding, music and metalwork (4:17–22). Culture and violence grow together. Lamech boasts: "Sevenfold vengeance shall be taken for Cain: but for Lamech seventy times sevenfold" (4:24). The Hebrew means seventy-seven. Many commentators hear Jesus reversing Lamech's number in Matthew 18:22.
3. **The flood and its parallels.** On 3 December 1872 George Smith read to the Society of Biblical Archaeology a flood story from a Nineveh tablet: Tablet XI of the epic of Gilgamesh, in which Utnapishtim, warned by the god Ea, builds a boat, rides out a storm of six days and seven nights, grounds on a mountain (Nimush, in older readings Nisir), sends out birds and offers a sacrifice. The gods smell it and crowd around like flies. *Atrahasis*, whose best copy is Old Babylonian (seventeenth century BC), gives the flood a cause. The gods made humans to do their labour, and human noise kept Enlil awake. The shared plot is unmistakable, and so is the difference ([Near Eastern flood parallels](../reference.md#near-eastern-flood-parallels)). In Genesis one God floods the earth because it is "filled with iniquity" (6:11; Hebrew *hamas*, violence), grieves over it, and ends by binding himself in a covenant. *In words:* Genesis retells a Mesopotamian story, and the retelling is its theology.
4. **Two strands, interwoven.** Inside Genesis 6–9 the story says things twice, and differently ([two flood strands](../reference.md#two-flood-strands)):

| | Strand A (non-Priestly, "J") | Strand B (Priestly, "P") |
|---|---|---|
| Animals | seven pairs of clean, one of unclean (7:2) | two of every kind (6:19–20; 7:15) |
| Timing | rain for forty days (7:4, 12) | waters prevail 150 days (7:24; 8:3); dated by Noah's age |
| Source of water | rain (7:4, 12) | deep and heavens burst open (7:11; 8:2) |
| Divine name | *YHWH*, "the Lord" (7:1, 5) | *Elohim*, "God" (6:22; 9:1) |
| Ending | sacrifice of clean animals (8:20–22) | covenant and bow (9:1–17) |

The documentary reading ([2.2](02-02-who-wrote-the-pentateuch.md)) takes these as two complete stories that a redactor wove together verse by verse. Supplementary models make one strand an expansion of the other. Unity readers such as Umberto Cassuto, and Gordon Wenham ("The Coherence of the Flood Narrative", *Vetus Testamentum*, 1978), read the whole as a single designed palistrophe. It rises to "God remembered Noe" (8:1) and descends in mirror order. Most critics hold two sources. How they relate is disputed.

5. **The Noahide covenant.** Genesis 9 is the first covenant made explicitly (promised at 6:18). It is made with "every living soul", not with Israel. It is unconditional, and its sign is the bow. It adds a law: no eating flesh with its blood, and no shedding of human blood, "for man was made to the image of God" (9:6). Rabbinic tradition names seven Noahide commandments binding on all humanity (Talmud, Sanhedrin 56a), among them the bans on bloodshed and on eating a limb torn from a living animal, which echo this chapter.
6. **Babel.** Brick and bitumen (11:3) are Mesopotamian building materials, and the tower evokes a ziggurat such as Babylon's Etemenanki. The builders want to "make our name famous" (11:4). The name is a pun: *Babel* sounds like *balal*, "confuse" (11:9). God scatters them and there is no mercy scene. In 12:2 God promises to "magnify" Abram's name, giving as a gift what Babel tried to seize. Christian tradition reads Pentecost (Acts 2) as Babel undone. That reading is typological, not the literal sense.

**Mark the levels.** That Genesis 3–11 is inspired and canonical is **defined dogma** ([`fundamental-theology` 3.1](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/03-01-inspiration.md), [3.3](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/03-03-the-canon-as-defined.md)). That a real primeval fault by our first parents marks all human history is **defined dogma** too (Trent, Session V, 1546). It belongs to `creation-grace-last-things`. CCC 390 says the account uses "figurative language" but affirms a primeval event. The Biblical Commission's response of 30 June 1909 insisted on the literal historical sense of Genesis 1–3 for facts touching the faith's foundations. In its day it bound under Pius X's *Praestantia Scripturae* (1907). The Commission's letter to Cardinal Suhard (16 January 1948) said the old replies were no hindrance to further scientific study. It also said the literary forms of Genesis 1–11 fit no classical category. *Humani Generis* 38 (1950) then warned that those chapters still belong to history "in a true sense", which exegetes must work out. Since 1971 the Commission has been consultative. Weigh these with [the Biblical Commission and its weight](../reference.md#the-biblical-commission-and-its-weight) and [`fundamental-theology` 4.5](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-05-reading-a-documents-weight.md). The two strands, Genesis's relation to *Atrahasis*, and which reading of 6:1–4 is right are **scholarly positions**, freely disputed. **Dates:** Smith's 1872 reading and the seventh-century Nineveh copies are *secure*. The seventeenth-century date for *Atrahasis* is *reconstructed* from a scribe's colophon. The dates of the strands are *disputed*. There is no date for the flood at all. The geological record shows no flood that covered the whole earth.

## The spread-of-sin pattern

David Clines (*The Theme of the Pentateuch*, 1978), building on Gerhard von Rad, traced a repeating sequence of sin, speech, mitigation and punishment ([the spread of sin pattern](../reference.md#the-spread-of-sin-pattern)):

| Episode | Sin | God's speech | Mitigation | Punishment |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Eden (3) | eating the fruit | 3:14–19 | garments of skins (3:21) | expulsion (3:23–24) |
| Cain (4) | fratricide | 4:11–12 | the mark (4:15) | exile (4:16) |
| Sons of God (6:1–4) | union of heaven and earth | 6:3 | none clear | life limited to 120 years (6:3) |
| Flood (6–9) | corruption, violence | 6:7, 13 | Noah, ark, covenant | the flood |
| Babel (11) | the tower and "a name" | 11:6–7 | **missing** | scattering (11:8) |

The empty cell is the point: the mitigation for Babel is Genesis 12.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean): Cain, 4:1–16.** Sin: Cain kills Abel (4:8), after being warned that sin lies at the door (4:7). Speech: "What hast thou done?" and the curse from the ground that "received the blood of thy brother" (4:10–12). Mitigation: Cain protests that anyone who finds him will kill him, and God sets a mark on him (4:15). Punishment: he goes "from the face of the Lord" to the land east of Eden (4:16). Every slot fills, and the escalation shows: Eden's sin was against God, Cain's against God *and* a brother, and the ground that yielded thorns (3:18) now yields nothing (4:12).

**Example 2 (hard): the sons of God, 6:1–4.** Here the pattern strains, because the passage's meaning is itself contested. There are three readings ([sons of God in Genesis 6](../reference.md#sons-of-god-in-genesis-6)).

- **Angels.** Elsewhere *bene ha-elohim* are heavenly beings (Job 1:6). 1 Enoch 6–11 tells of Watchers who came down, and Justin Martyr says angels "were captivated by love of women" (*Second Apology* 5). Most modern critics take the phrase this way. On 1 Enoch in Jude, see [`new-testament` 6.4](../../new-testament/lessons/06-04-peter-jude-and-the-letters-of-john.md).
- **Sethites.** Augustine (*City of God* XV.23) doubts that holy angels could fall so, and reads the sons of God as Seth's godly line marrying Cain's daughters. The Douay's note follows him.
- **Rulers.** Targum Onqelos renders the phrase "sons of the great ones". Rashi explains it as sons of princes and judges, taking *elohim* as "authority", and records the princely angels as a second opinion ([Jewish readings](../reference.md#jewish-readings)).

Is 6:3 mitigation or punishment? Whether the 120 years are a lifespan or a grace period is open (the Douay's note gives both). The pattern fits only roughly, which is the lesson: a structure scholars find guides reading; it does not settle a passage's meaning.

## Watch out

- **"The Church teaches that Genesis 3 is a myth" / "the Church teaches it is history in every detail."** Both overstate. What is defined is the reality of the original fault. CCC 390 calls the account's language figurative. How much of the narrative is figurative is for exegetes, within that doctrine.
- **"Genesis copied Gilgamesh."** The parallel shows a shared Mesopotamian tradition that Genesis reworked. It does not show copying from that tablet, and settles nothing about inspiration, which works through human sources ([`fundamental-theology` 3.1](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/03-01-inspiration.md)).
- **Treating the two-strand analysis as settled, or as dead.** Two sources is the majority view. Wenham's unity reading is a live minority, and neither view is Church teaching.
- **Reading the Douay's divine names as the Hebrew's.** At 7:9 the Douay has "the Lord" where the Hebrew has *Elohim*. Check the Hebrew before sorting verses by name.

## One-liner

> Genesis 3–11 is one story of sin spreading and mercy narrowing toward Abram, told in Mesopotamia's words with its own God.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢)** *(Exegetical)* A paraphrase of Gilgamesh XI: on the seventh day after the boat grounds, Utnapishtim releases a dove, which finds no resting place and comes back; then a swallow, which comes back; then a raven, which sees the waters receding, eats and does not come back. He lets everything out and offers a sacrifice; the gods smell the sweet savour and gather like flies around the sacrificer.

Genesis 8:6–12 (Douay–Rheims):

> "And after that forty days were passed, Noe opening the window of the ark, which he had made, sent forth a raven: Which went forth and did not return, till the waters were dried up upon the earth. He sent forth also a dove after him, to see if the waters had now ceased upon the face of the earth. But she not finding where her foot might rest, returned to him into the ark ... And having waited yet seven other days, he again sent forth the dove out of the ark. And she came to him in the evening carrying a bough of an olive tree, with green leaves, in her mouth ... And he stayed yet other seven days: and he sent forth the dove, which returned not any more unto him."

(a) Give two agreements and three differences between the two bird scenes. (b) Genesis 8:20–21 follows with Noah's holocaust and "the Lord smelled a sweet savour". Say in **two sentences** what the parallel is evidence for, and what it is not evidence for.

**P2 (🟡)** Genesis 7 (Douay–Rheims), selected verses. In the Hebrew, 7:16a has *Elohim* ("God") and 7:16b has *YHWH* ("the Lord"), as the Douay does.

> 7:2 "Of all clean beasts take seven and seven, the male and the female. 3 But of the beasts that are unclean two and two ... 4 For yet a while, and after seven days, I will rain upon the earth forty days and forty nights ... "
> 7:11 "In the six hundredth year of the life of Noe, in the second month, in the seventeenth day of the month, all the fountains of the great deep were broken up, and the floodgates of heaven were opened: 12 And the rain fell upon the earth forty days and forty nights."
> 7:15 "Went in to Noe into the ark, two and two of all flesh ... 16 And they that went in, went in male and female of all flesh, as God had commanded him: and the Lord shut him in on the outside."
> 7:24 "And the waters prevailed upon the earth a hundred and fifty days."

(a) *(Exegetical)* Sort these verses (splitting 7:16) into the two strands of the common documentary analysis, and name the criterion that places each. (b) *(Evaluative)* In **150 words or fewer**: do these data better support two interwoven sources or one designed narrative? Any verdict passes.

**P3 (🔴)** *(Exegetical)* An **invented** handout from a parish adult-education class:

> "Genesis 1–11 is myth, and the Church now admits it. Archaeologists found the flood in a Babylonian tablet, so the Bible simply copied Gilgamesh. The Catechism itself calls Genesis 3 'figurative language', which means nothing in it happened. Even Pius XII agreed that these chapters are not history."

(a) Name what the handout does to CCC 390 and to *Humani Generis* 38. (b) Name what the Gilgamesh parallel does and does not show, and state the level of the one claim about Genesis 3 that a Catholic is bound to. **Two sentences per part.**

<details>
<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** agreements (any two): birds are sent to test whether the waters have gone down; a **dove returns** because it finds no resting place; a **raven does not return**; a **sacrifice follows** whose smell the deity notices. Differences (any three): **order** (Genesis sends the raven first; Gilgamesh sends it last); **species** (Gilgamesh has a swallow; Genesis has no third species but sends the dove three times); **the olive leaf** exists only in Genesis; **seven-day intervals** between sendings in Genesis, none in Gilgamesh; the raven's non-return is **decisive** in Gilgamesh but **inconclusive** in Genesis, where the dove settles it.

**Must hit, strict (b):** evidence for a **shared Mesopotamian flood tradition** that Genesis knows and reworks, down to the motif of the deity smelling the offering; *not* evidence of direct copying from this tablet, of the direction of every detail, or of whether a flood happened. Credit also: the reworking is theological (one Lord who pledges never again, not gods swarming like flies).

**Wrong turns:** counting "a raven is sent" as a difference in species rather than noting the reversed order. Concluding that the parallel disproves inspiration: inspiration works through human sources.

**Model answer:** (a) Both send birds to test the waters, and in both a dove returns for lack of a resting place. Genesis sends the raven first and Gilgamesh last; only Gilgamesh has a swallow; only Genesis has the olive leaf and the seven-day waits. (b) The parallel shows that Genesis tells a flood story Mesopotamia already told, including the deity smelling the sacrifice. It does not show that the writer copied this tablet or tell us anything about whether a flood happened.

**P2** *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b))*

**Must hit, strict (a):** **Non-Priestly (J):** 7:2–4 (**seven pairs** of clean animals, and **forty days** of rain), 7:12 (**forty days**, rain), 7:16b (**"the Lord"**, *YHWH*, shutting Noah in). **Priestly (P):** 7:11 (**dated by Noah's age**, deep and heavens burst), 7:15–16a (**two and two**, **"God"**, *Elohim*), 7:24 (**150 days**). Each placement must name its criterion.

**Must hit, any verdict (b):** (1) state the two-source case: the pairs clash on numbers, duration and names, and 7:16 switches name mid-verse; (2) state the unity case: the clean-animal command anticipates the sacrifice, and Wenham's palistrophe reads the whole as designed, so 40 and 150 can be read as phases of one flood; (3) weigh which explanation handles the **7:2 vs 7:15 animal numbers** or the **mid-verse name switch** better.

**Wrong turns:** assigning 7:12 to P because it mentions the flood's start; its forty days and rain are the non-Priestly markers. Treating either verdict as Church teaching.

**Model answer (b), one of several:** The two-source reading explains more with less. Seven pairs and two of every kind are not two phases of one command, and a narrator who switches from "God" to "the Lord" inside 7:16 is doing exactly what a redactor joining two texts would do. Wenham's reply is strong on structure: the whole narrative does rise to 8:1 and fall away in mirror order, and forty days of rain inside 150 days of high water is arithmetically possible. But a redactor can produce a symmetrical whole out of two sources, so symmetry does not decide authorship. The animal numbers remain the hardest datum for single authorship. I lean two sources, with the unity of the final form as the text the Church reads.

**P3** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** it **truncates CCC 390**, which says the account uses figurative language **but affirms a primeval event**. It **inverts *Humani Generis* 38**, which says the chapters, though not history by the method of Greek, Latin or modern historians, **pertain to history in a true sense**.

**Must hit, strict (b):** the parallel shows a **shared tradition reworked**, not simple copying, and it has no bearing on inspiration or on the doctrine. The reality of **a primeval fault by our first parents** is **defined dogma** (Trent, Session V), while the degree of figuration in the narrative is freely disputed within that.

**Wrong turns:** answering by asserting that every detail of Genesis 3 is literal history, which swaps one overstatement for another. Treating the 1909 response as still binding in its original force. Assigning the doctrine to CCC 390 as its source rather than as its report.

**Model answer:** (a) It cuts CCC 390 in half: the Catechism says the language is figurative and that it affirms a real event at the start of human history. It reverses Pius XII, who said these chapters belong to history in a true sense even though they are not history as classical or modern historians write it. (b) Gilgamesh shows that Genesis reworks a Mesopotamian story, not that the Bible is "simply" a copy or that it is uninspired. What binds a Catholic is the reality of the original fault, defined at Trent, and how figurative the telling is remains open.

## Flashback

**From Lesson [2.1](02-01-two-accounts-of-creation.md) (Two accounts of creation):** *(Exegetical.)* Genesis 2:1–3 (Douay–Rheims), the close of the first account:

> "So the heavens and the earth were finished, and all the furniture of them. And on the seventh day God ended his work which he had made: and he rested on the seventh day from all his work which he had done. And he blessed the seventh day, and sanctified it: because in it he had rested from all his work which God created and made."

"Rested" renders *shavat*, "ceased", the root of *shabbat*. Recall that each of days 1–6 opens with "God said" and closes with "And the evening and morning were the ... day."

(a) Name three ways the seventh day breaks the pattern of the six, citing the words (or the missing words) that show each. (b) Say what these breaks tell you about the genre of Genesis 1:1–2:3, and name one question they do *not* settle. **Two sentences per part.**

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):** any three of: (1) **no "God said"** and no new work: the day is defined by **ceasing** ("ended", "rested", *shavat*); (2) **no "evening and morning" refrain** closes it; (3) God **blesses and sanctifies a time**, where earlier blessings fell on living creatures (fish and birds, 1:22; humans, 1:28); (4) "the **seventh day**" is **repeated three times** in three verses, the week's climax stated with emphasis.

**Must hit, strict (b):** the account is built **toward the Sabbath**: its seven-part, refrain-marked structure is a **liturgical week**, the rhythm of worship, not a chronicle's or a physicist's sequence (2.1, argument step 1). It does **not** settle the **length of a "day"** (*yom*), which the text uses loosely (2:4, "in the day that the Lord God made the heaven and the earth") and which the 1909 Commission's Response VIII left to free discussion. Credit also: it does not settle the sources question (P as author of the account is a hypothesis, [2.2](02-02-who-wrote-the-pentateuch.md)).

**Wrong turns:** reading "rested" as tiredness. The verb means ceasing, and the text gives no fatigue. Treating the missing refrain as proof that the seventh day is still going on as the *literal* sense: that is a later theological reading, not something the author asserts. Concluding that the liturgical shape makes the account "mere myth". Genre decides *how* it asserts, not *whether* it asserts anything.

**Model answer:** (a) The seventh day has no "God said" and no work, only God "ended" and "rested" (*shavat*, ceased). It lacks the closing "evening and morning" formula, and it is the one day God "blessed" and "sanctified", a blessing given to a time rather than to creatures. (b) The whole account is shaped to end in Sabbath, so it reads as a liturgical week that orders creation toward worship, not as a chronicle of stages. It leaves open how long a "day" is, which Genesis itself uses loosely (2:4) and the Church has left to exegetes.

</details>
