# The Old Testament · Lesson 5.3: Job

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 5: Psalms and wisdom · Builds on: [5.1 The Psalms](05-01-the-psalms.md), [5.2 Proverbs, Ecclesiastes, and the Song of Songs](05-02-proverbs-ecclesiastes-and-the-song-of-songs.md) · Unlocks: [5.4 Sirach and the Wisdom of Solomon](05-04-sirach-and-the-wisdom-of-solomon.md)

## Why this matters

Proverbs says the just prosper and the wicked fall. Job is the Bible's own stress test of that claim. An innocent man loses everything, three friends defend the claim at length, and God then says the friends were wrong. It is also the sharpest case of the Hebrew and the Vulgate saying different things about hope after death.

## The idea

The whole book hangs on one Hebrew word, *hinnam*, "for nothing, gratuitously". In the prologue's heavenly court the accuser asks whether Job fears God *hinnam* (1:9). His charge: piety is a trade, and without payment the worship stops. So the question is not "why do the innocent suffer?" in general, but whether anyone serves God for God's sake, and what he may say to God when the payment stops.

Then the book changes form. A prose folk tale (chapters 1–2 and 42:7–17) frames about forty chapters of dense Hebrew poetry. Job curses his birthday (3). Three cycles of debate follow with Eliphaz, Baldad and Sophar (Hebrew Bildad and Zophar), chapters 4–27. A poem on wisdom comes next (28), then Job's closing oath of innocence (29–31). A fourth speaker, Elihu (Douay Eliu), arrives unannounced (32–37). God answers from the whirlwind (38–41), Job twice replies, and the frame closes with the verdict and the restoration.

## Source

Job 1:8–11 (Douay–Rheims), with the last clause of 2:3:

> "And the Lord said to him: Hast thou considered my servant, Job, that there is none like him in the earth, a simple and upright man, and fearing God, and avoiding evil? And Satan answering, said: Doth Job fear God in vain? Hast thou not made a fence for him, and his house, and all his substance round about, blessed the works of his hands, and his possession hath increased on the earth? But stretch forth thy hand a little, and touch all that he hath, and see if he bless thee not to thy face." ... "thou hast moved me against him, that I should afflict him without cause."

"In vain" (1:9) and "without cause" (2:3) are the same word, *hinnam*. The accuser asks whether Job's piety is *hinnam*. God admits that Job's suffering is. The reader may not say Job deserved it. And "Satan" is *ha-satan*, "the accuser", with the article. It is a title held by a member of the heavenly court who reports to God, as in Zechariah 3:1. Challoner's note reads him as the devil, as the later tradition does; the literal sense shows an office, not yet a name.

## The argument

1. **Date and setting: disputed.** The book names no author and no king. Ezekiel 14:14 and 14:20 already know Job, with Noah and Daniel, as proverbial just men, so a story of Job is older than the sixth century. For the book as we have it, many scholars propose somewhere between the sixth and fourth centuries BC, citing its Aramaic-coloured Hebrew and the heavenly accuser. That date is **disputed**, with some earlier proposals still live ([dating discipline](../reference.md#dating-discipline)). Mesopotamia had its own righteous-sufferer poems: *Ludlul bel nemeqi* ("I will praise the lord of wisdom") and the so-called *Babylonian Theodicy*, a dialogue between a sufferer and a friend. They show the genre is old, not that Job borrows from them. The Talmud (*Bava Batra* 14b–15a) records a tradition that Moses wrote the book, rabbis who place Job anywhere from the patriarchs to the return from exile, and one sage who says Job never existed and the story is a *mashal*, a parable. Maimonides later reads the book as a parable (*Guide* III.22–23) ([Jewish readings](../reference.md#jewish-readings)).
2. **The friends' theology: [retribution](../reference.md#retribution-theology).** Eliphaz states it first: "who ever perished being innocent? or when were the just destroyed?" (4:7). Suffering proves sin, so Job should repent and be restored. Baldad and Sophar harden it cycle by cycle. By chapter 22 Eliphaz invents Job's sins to fit the rule. The third cycle breaks down: Baldad's last speech is six verses long (25), and Sophar never gives a third. Whether that is design or a damaged text is **disputed**.
3. **Job 14: no return.** A felled tree sprouts again at "the scent of water" (14:7–9), but "man when he is fallen asleep shall not rise again; till the heavens be broken, he shall not awake" (14:12). Job wishes God would hide him in Sheol until the anger passes (14:13), then asks, "Shall man that is dead ... live again?" (14:14). The literal sense is a wish Job cannot believe. Challoner's chapter heading nevertheless says Job "professes his belief of a resurrection": the heading reads the chapter in light of what came later.
4. **Job 19:25–27: the *go'el*.** A [***go'el***](../reference.md#goel) is the kinsman who buys back family land (Leviticus 25:25), avenges a killing (Numbers 35:19), or redeems a widow's line, as Boaz does (Ruth 4). My translation of the Hebrew: "But I know that my *go'el* lives, and at the last he will stand upon the dust. And after my skin has been struck off thus, from my flesh I shall see God." Who is the *go'el*? Most readers say God. Some say a heavenly advocate distinct from God, like the "witness ... in heaven" of 16:19 [DR 16:20]. "From my flesh" can mean *in* the flesh or *apart from* it. The Vulgate turns Job's hope into a first-person resurrection, *de terra surrecturus sim*, "I shall rise out of the earth", and the Douay follows: "in the last day I shall rise out of the earth". The Greek Job, too, ends with an addition (42:17a) saying that Job will rise again with those the Lord raises. Gregory the Great files his resurrection reading of 19:25 under the *literal* interpretation (*Moralia* XIV.67–68). *In words:* the Hebrew is a cry for a vindicator; the Latin is a creed.
5. **The wisdom poem and the oath.** Chapter 28 is calm where Job has been furious. Men mine silver and sapphire, "but where is wisdom to be found?" (28:12). Only God knows, and he tells man that "the fear of the Lord, that is wisdom" (28:28). Then Job's oath (29–31) swears innocence clause by clause and demands a hearing: "that he himself that judgeth would write a book" (31:35).
6. **Elihu.** A young man, angry with Job for justifying himself and with the friends for condemning him without an answer (32:2–3), argues that God speaks through suffering to warn and correct: "He shall ... open his ear in affliction" (36:15). That is suffering as *discipline* rather than punishment. Eliphaz touched the idea once (5:17); Elihu makes it his thesis. But Elihu appears nowhere in the prologue or epilogue, God never answers him, and his Hebrew differs in style. Most critics therefore take chapters 32–37 as a **later addition**. Others read them as a deliberate foil. The question is **disputed**.
7. **The whirlwind.** "Where wast thou when I laid the foundations of the earth?" (38:4). God answers Job's lawsuit with a tour of creation, from the sea and the dawn to Behemoth and Leviathan. God never charges Job with the sins the friends alleged, and never mentions the wager or retribution. Job's first reply is silence, "I will lay my hand upon my mouth" (40:4 [DR 39:34]). His second ends in 42:6, whose Hebrew verb has no object (P2).
8. **The verdict.** God tells Eliphaz, "you have not spoken the thing that is right before me, as my servant Job hath" (42:7). Job, who accused God, spoke rightly; the friends, who defended him, did not.
9. **Gregory's *Moralia*.** Gregory the Great's thirty-five books on Job ([`patristics` 5.3](../../patristics/lessons/05-03-gregory-the-great.md)) read it in the historical, allegorical and moral senses ([senses of Scripture](../reference.md#the-senses-of-scripture)). He sets authorship aside: who wrote the book, he says, "it is very superfluous to enquire", since the Holy Spirit is its Author (Preface 2, Oxford translation, 1844–50). Job is "a type of the Redeemer" with his Body the Church, and the friends figure heretics (Preface 14–15) ([typology](../reference.md#typology)). Yet he refuses to allegorize Job's works of mercy in chapter 31, since that would empty them of their historical truth (Epistle to Leander 4).

**Mark the levels.** That Job is inspired and canonical, with the books "entire with all their parts", Elihu included, is **defined** (Trent, Session IV; [`fundamental-theology` 3.3](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/03-03-the-canon-as-defined.md)). Bodily resurrection is **defined dogma**, but it is not defined *from* Job 19, and reading 19:25 in the Hebrew as a cry for a vindicator denies no doctrine. The date, the author, Elihu's status, the third cycle and the identity of the *go'el* are **scholarly questions** with no magisterial answer. The Biblical Commission's early decrees never ruled on Job ([the Biblical Commission and its weight](../reference.md#the-biblical-commission-and-its-weight)). Inerrancy covers what the sacred writer *asserts* ([`fundamental-theology` 3.2](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/03-02-inerrancy.md)), and the book asserts that the friends were wrong. Their speeches are reported, not taught. Dei Verbum 15 says the Old Testament books contain things "incomplete and temporary" yet show a true divine pedagogy, which is the frame for Job 14.

## The shape of the book

| Section | Chapters | Form | Retribution is... |
|---|---|---|---|
| Prologue | 1–2 | prose tale; heavenly court | suspended: the suffering is *hinnam* (2:3) |
| Job's lament | 3 | poetry, curse of the birthday | not yet discussed |
| Three cycles | 4–27 | poetic debate; the third cycle broken | asserted by the friends, denied by Job |
| Wisdom poem | 28 | meditative poem | set aside: wisdom is God's alone |
| Job's oath | 29–31 | legal oath of innocence | turned into a lawsuit against God |
| Elihu | 32–37 | four speeches, unanswered | revised: suffering as discipline |
| Whirlwind | 38:1–42:6 | two divine speeches, two replies | never mentioned |
| Epilogue | 42:7–17 | prose tale again | the friends rebuked; Job restored double |

The restoration can look like retribution returning by the back door. Note that it comes *after* God has declared Job in the right, not as the proof of it.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean): who speaks Job 28?** The text names no new speaker: 27:1 and 29:1 both read "Job also added, taking up his parable", so chapter 28 sits inside Job's discourse. But its serenity clashes with the Job of 27 and 29–31. Its last line, "the fear of the Lord, that is wisdom: and to depart from evil, is understanding" (28:28), repeats the narrator's description of Job in 1:1, "fearing God, and avoiding evil". Many scholars read 28 as the author's interlude. The words settle the echo, not the speaker, who stays **disputed**.

**Example 2 (hard): Job 13:15.** The Douay reads, "Although he should kill me, I will trust in him". The consonants of the Hebrew write *lo'*, "not": "he will slay me; I have no hope". The Jewish reading tradition pronounces *lo*, "to him": "I will hope in him". The Mishnah (*Sotah* 5:5) records Rabbi Joshua ben Hyrcanus arguing from this verse that Job served God out of love, not fear. It admits the word could go either way and settles it from 27:5 ("till I die I will not depart from my innocence"). **Where the method strains:** textual criticism can report both readings but not choose from the letters, since both are old. The rabbis chose from the whole book, and the question they answered is the accuser's in 1:9.

## Watch out

- **"The Bible teaches that suffering is punishment for sin (Job 4:7)."** That is Eliphaz, whom 42:7 rebukes. Quoting a speaker is not Scripture asserting what he says.
- **"Job 19:25 proves Old Testament Israel believed in bodily resurrection."** The Hebrew is uncertain, and the resurrection wording is the Vulgate's. Job 14 says the opposite. Resurrection faith is clear in Daniel 12:2 and 2 Maccabees 7 ([6.3](06-03-daniel-and-apocalyptic.md), [6.2](06-02-tobit-judith-baruch-and-the-maccabees.md)).
- **"Scholars have shown Elihu is not really part of Job, so Catholics may set him aside."** Elihu's later origin is a majority hypothesis. The canon is the book with all its parts.
- **Treating the whirlwind speeches as the answer to the problem of evil.** They are a text. Whether they answer it philosophically is [`philosophy-of-religion`](../../philosophy-of-religion/syllabus.md)'s question.

## One-liner

> Job asks whether anyone serves God for nothing, lets the friends defend retribution and God reject them, and hopes for a vindicator whom Jerome's Latin, not the Hebrew, made a resurrection.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢)** *(Exegetical)* Job 4:7–9 (Douay–Rheims), Eliphaz speaking:

> "Remember, I pray thee, who ever perished being innocent? or when were the just destroyed? On the contrary, I have seen those who work iniquity, and sow sorrows, and reap them, Perishing by the blast of God, and consumed by the spirit of his wrath."

(a) Reconstruct Eliphaz's argument as two premises and a conclusion about Job, and name the words that carry each premise. (b) A reader says: "This is in inspired Scripture, so a Catholic must hold that the innocent never perish." Using 42:7 and [`fundamental-theology` 3.2](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/03-02-inerrancy.md), say what is wrong in two sentences.

**P2 (🟡)** *(Exegetical)* Job 42:6 in the Douay–Rheims reads: "Therefore I reprehend myself, and do penance in dust and ashes." The Hebrew, glossed word by word, reads *'al-ken* "therefore", *em'as* "I reject / I despise" (no object given), *ve-nichamti* "and I repent / change my mind / am comforted", *'al* "concerning / upon", *'afar va-'efer* "dust and ashes". The same pair appears in Genesis 18:27 ("I am dust and ashes") and Job 30:19, and Job has sat among the *'efer*, ashes, since 2:8. (a) Name two things the Douay supplies that the Hebrew does not state. (b) Give two readings of the verse the Hebrew allows, and say what "dust and ashes" means in each. **100 words in total.**

**P3 (🔴)** *(Exegetical)* **Diagnose.** An invented parish handout says:

> "Scholars now agree that the Elihu speeches (Job 32–37) were added by a later writer, and the Church accepts this. Since they were not in the original Job, they are not inspired. In any case Elihu only repeats the friends: suffering is punishment for sin."

Name three errors, each in one or two sentences: one about scholarship, one about Church teaching, and one about what Elihu actually says (cite a verse).

<details>
<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** P1: the innocent never perish ("who ever perished being innocent? ... when were the just destroyed?"). P2: the wicked reap what they sow and perish by God's wrath ("sow sorrows, and reap them"; "perishing by the blast of God"). ∴ C, implied: Job's ruin is evidence of his sin, so he should turn to God. The conclusion is left for Job to draw; chapter 5 presses it ("I will pray to the Lord", 5:8).
**Must hit, strict (b):** inerrancy covers what the sacred writer **asserts**, and a reported speech is not asserted by the writer. Here the book positively **rejects** Eliphaz: God says the friends "have not spoken the thing that is right" (42:7).
**Wrong turns:** stating P2 as the conclusion. Answering (b) with "the Old Testament was simply mistaken": the text is inerrant precisely in reporting a view it judges wrong.
**Model answer:** (a) P1 the innocent never perish ("who ever perished being innocent?"); P2 the wicked reap what they sow ("sow sorrows, and reap them"); ∴ Job's ruin shows guilt. (b) The writer reports Eliphaz's claim without asserting it, and inerrancy covers only what the writer asserts. The book's own verdict in 42:7 says the friends did not speak rightly.

**P2** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** any two of: the **object "myself"** (the Hebrew verb has none); a verb of **self-reproach** where the Hebrew has "reject / despise"; **"do penance"**, which narrows *nichamti* to penitence and gives it a sacramental colour; **"in"** dust and ashes, where the Hebrew says "concerning / upon".
**Must hit, strict (b):** two readings, each with a sense for "dust and ashes". (1) **Penitential:** I reject (my words, cf. 40:4 [DR 39:34]) and repent, sitting **in** dust and ashes, the mourner's ash heap of 2:8. (2) **Retraction of mourning or acceptance of the human lot:** I reject (my lawsuit) and am comforted / change my mind **concerning** dust and ashes, meaning either Job leaves the ash heap, or he accepts being mortal "dust and ashes" as in Genesis 18:27.
**Wrong turns:** treating the Douay's "myself" as in the Hebrew. Offering two readings that differ only in wording but both give "dust and ashes" the same meaning.
**Model answer:** (a) The Douay supplies "myself" as the object and turns *nichamti* into "do penance". (b) Penitential: "I recant and repent in dust and ashes", sitting on the ash heap of 2:8. Or: "I retract, and am consoled concerning dust and ashes", accepting creaturely mortality, as in Genesis 18:27. In the second, Job's mourning ends rather than deepens.

**P3** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict:** (1) **Scholarship:** a later origin for Elihu is a **majority** view, not unanimous, since some read 32–37 as a deliberate foil. It rests on evidence (absent from prologue and epilogue, unanswered, different style). (2) **Church teaching:** the Church has made **no ruling** on Elihu's origin. Trent defines the books canonical "entire with all their parts", and inspiration attaches to the canonical book, not to a reconstructed first draft. So a later addition would still be inspired. (3) **Elihu:** he does **not** simply repeat the friends. He is angry with them for condemning Job without an answer (32:3) and argues that God uses suffering to warn and **correct**, opening the ear "in affliction" (36:15; also 33:16–19). That is discipline, not only punishment. Credit a note that Eliphaz glimpsed this once (5:17), but Elihu builds his case on it.
**Wrong turns:** saying the Church *rejects* the addition hypothesis (it takes no position). Saying Elihu is the book's answer (God never endorses him).
**Model answer:** The scholars do not "agree": Elihu's later origin is a majority view with dissenters. The Church has taught nothing about it, and Trent's canon covers the book with all its parts, so origin does not decide inspiration. Elihu criticises the friends (32:3) and treats suffering as correction, God opening the ear "in affliction" (36:15), which is not the friends' punishment theory.

</details>

## Flashback

**From Lesson [5.1](05-01-the-psalms.md) (The Psalms):** *(Exegetical.)* Douay Psalm 29 (Hebrew 30), with the Douay's verse numbers. Verse 1 is the title, "A psalm of a canticle, at the dedication of David's house".

> (2) I will extol thee, O Lord, for thou hast upheld me: and hast not made my enemies to rejoice over me. (3) O Lord my God, I have cried to thee, and thou hast healed me. (4) Thou hast brought forth, O Lord, my soul from hell: thou hast saved me from them that go down into the pit. (5) Sing to the Lord, O ye his saints ... (6) ... In the evening weeping shall have place, and in the morning gladness. (7) And in my abundance I said: I shall never be moved. ... (9) To thee, O Lord, will I cry ... (10) What profit is there in my blood, whilst I go down to corruption? Shall dust confess to thee, or declare thy truth? (11) The Lord hath heard, and hath had mercy on me ... (12) Thou hast turned for me my mourning into joy: thou hast cut my sackcloth, and hast compassed me with gladness. (13) ... O Lord my God, I will give praise to thee for ever.

"Hell" in verse 4 is Hebrew *she'ol*, the realm of the dead; "the pit" is *bor*. Many modern versions render verse 9's Hebrew verb as past: "To you, O LORD, I cried".

(a) Name the genre by Gunkel's kinds, and explain why a reader who sees the plea of verses 9–10 should not call it a lament. (b) Classify verse 4 and the second half of verse 6 by Lowth's kinds. (c) Give the modern English reference (psalm and verse) for Douay 29:6 and 29:12. **Two sentences for (a); one line each for (b) and (c).**

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):** an **individual thanksgiving**, a lament answered and retold. It opens with praise for a rescue already done ("thou hast upheld me", "thou hast healed me"), calls others to join (v. 5), and ends with a vow of praise (v. 13). The plea of 9–10 is **quoted from the past**: verse 7 ("I said") begins the retelling of the crisis, and the rescue that follows ("Thou hast turned for me my mourning into joy", v. 12) closes it. The complaint is inside the story of the rescue, not the psalm's present situation.
**Must hit, strict (b):** verse 4 **synonymous** ("brought forth my soul from hell" / "saved me from them that go down into the pit": *she'ol* and *bor* name the same realm of the dead). Verse 6b **antithetic** (evening / morning, weeping / gladness). Credit a Kugel-style note that the second line of 4 sharpens rather than repeats.
**Must hit, strict (c):** Douay 29:6 = **Psalm 30:5**; Douay 29:12 = **Psalm 30:11**. The psalm number is one higher and the verse one lower, since the Douay counts the title as verse 1.
**Wrong turns:** calling it an individual lament because verses 9–10 plead. Reading "hell" as the place of the damned, when *she'ol* is simply where the dead go, which is why "Shall dust confess to thee?" is an argument to God. Converting the psalm number but not the verse (30:6, 30:12).
**Model answer:** (a) An individual thanksgiving: it praises God for a rescue already done and vows lasting praise. Verses 9–10 quote the lament he prayed then, framed by "I said" (v. 7) and "The Lord hath heard" (v. 11). (b) Verse 4 synonymous; verse 6b antithetic. (c) Psalm 30:5 and 30:11.

</details>
