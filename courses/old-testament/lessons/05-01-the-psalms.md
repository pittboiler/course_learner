# The Old Testament · Lesson 5.1: The Psalms

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 5: Psalms and wisdom · Builds on: [1.2 The senses of Scripture](01-02-the-senses-of-scripture.md), [3.2 David and the house of David](03-02-david-and-the-house-of-david.md), [4.4 Jeremiah and Ezekiel](04-04-jeremiah-and-ezekiel.md) · Unlocks: [5.2 Proverbs, Ecclesiastes, and the Song of Songs](05-02-proverbs-ecclesiastes-and-the-song-of-songs.md)

## Why this matters

The Psalms are the Old Testament book the Church prays daily and the New Testament quotes most. Reading them takes three skills: hearing how Hebrew poetry pairs lines, knowing what kind of prayer a psalm is, and finding it at all, since a Douay and a modern Bible number most psalms differently. Then come the hard cases: a king's coronation song that the New Testament reads as Christ, and a psalm that blesses whoever smashes Babylon's babies.

## The idea

**Hebrew poetry rhymes thoughts, not sounds.** A psalm line usually comes in two halves, and the second half answers the first:

> When Israel went out of Egypt, / the house of Jacob from a barbarous people. (Ps 114:1 [Douay 113:1])

Say something, then say it again differently.

**Each psalm is a kind of prayer with a job to do.** A lament complains and asks. A hymn praises. A thanksgiving tells the story of a rescue. A royal psalm was sung for the king. Knowing the kind tells you what the parts are for.

**Two numbering systems.** The Hebrew and the Greek Septuagint divide the same 150 psalms at different points; the Vulgate, and so the Douay, follows the Greek, usually one lower.

## Source

Psalm 2:1–9 (Douay–Rheims; same number in both systems):

> Why have the Gentiles raged, and the people devised vain things? The kings of the earth stood up, and the princes met together, against the Lord, and against his Christ. Let us break their bonds asunder: and let us cast away their yoke from us. He that dwelleth in heaven shall laugh at them: and the Lord shall deride them. Then shall he speak to them in his anger, and trouble them in his rage. But I am appointed king by him over Sion, his holy mountain, preaching his commandment. The Lord hath said to me: Thou art my son, this day have I begotten thee. Ask of me, and I will give thee the Gentiles for thy inheritance, and the utmost parts of the earth for thy possession. Thou shalt rule them with a rod of iron, and shalt break them in pieces like a potter's vessel.

"His Christ" translates the Hebrew *meshicho*, "his anointed one", the title of Israel's king. Greek *christos* means the same. Every couplet pairs: "raged" / "devised vain things", "laugh" / "deride", "anger" / "rage".

## The argument

1. **[Parallelism](../reference.md#parallelism).** Robert Lowth's Oxford lectures on Hebrew poetry (*De sacra poesi Hebraeorum*, 1753) named the pairing *parallelismus membrorum*, "parallelism of the members", in three kinds. In **synonymous** parallelism the second line restates the first (Ps 114:1 above). In **antithetic** parallelism it states the opposite: "For the Lord knoweth the way of the just: and the way of the wicked shall perish" (Ps 1:6). In **synthetic** parallelism it completes or extends the first, with no echo, as in "But I am appointed king by him over Sion, his holy mountain" (2:6). James Kugel (*The Idea of Biblical Poetry*, 1981) argued that "synonymous" misleads, since the second line almost never just repeats: his shorthand is *A, and what's more, B*. Robert Alter (*The Art of Biblical Poetry*, 1985) described the usual movement as intensification: laughing becomes deriding, anger becomes rage. *In words:* read the second line for what it adds.

2. **[Gunkel's psalm genres](../reference.md#gunkels-psalm-genres).** Hermann Gunkel's form criticism (his commentary of 1926, and the *Einleitung in die Psalmen*, completed by Joachim Begrich in 1933) sorted the psalms by recurring structure and asked where in worship each kind was used. The kinds: the **hymn** (a call to praise, then the reason: "for he is good"); the **communal lament** (the people after a disaster); the **individual lament**, which is the largest group (invocation, complaint, petition, a statement of trust, a vow to praise, often with a sudden turn from complaint to confidence); the **individual thanksgiving** (a lament answered, retold); and the **royal psalm** (2, 45, 72, 110 and others, sung for the king). Smaller groups include **wisdom** psalms such as Psalm 1. Psalm 22 [Douay 21], which Jesus quotes on the cross ([`new-testament` 3.4](../../new-testament/lessons/03-04-the-passion-and-resurrection-narratives.md)), is an individual lament whose second half turns to praise. *In words:* the genre tells you what each part is doing.

3. **The five books.** The Psalter is divided into five books, each closing with a doxology ("Blessed be the Lord"): at 41:13 [Douay 40:14], 72:18–19 [71:18–19], 89:52 [88:53] and 106:48 [105:48]. After Book II comes a colophon: "The praises of David, the son of Jesse, are ended" (72:20 [71:20]). Yet psalms headed "of David" keep appearing afterwards: evidence that smaller collections were joined over time. **Dating:** individual psalms range from the monarchy to after the exile (reconstructed). Psalm 137 [Douay 136] was written after the fall of Jerusalem, and that fall itself is secure (587/586). When the Psalter received its final shape is **disputed**. The large Psalms Scroll from Qumran Cave 11 orders the later psalms differently from the Masoretic Text, and scholars dispute whether it is a variant Psalter or a liturgical collection.

4. **[Royal psalms](../reference.md#royal-psalms) read messianically.** The literal sense of Psalm 2 is a coronation. The nations plot, God laughs, and the new king recites the decree of his installation: "Thou art my son, this day have I begotten thee." That is adoption language, as in Nathan's promise to David's heir, "I will be to him a father, and he shall be to me a son" (2 Sam 7:14 [Douay 2 Kings 7:14]; see [3.2](03-02-david-and-the-house-of-david.md)). "This day" is the coronation day. After 587/586 there was no king, but the psalms were still sung, and a promise of worldwide rule to a throneless line became hope for a future anointed one. The New Testament reads that hope as fulfilled. Acts 13:33 applies "the second psalm" to the resurrection, and Hebrews 1:5 applies it to the Son. Psalm 110:1 [Douay 109:1] ("The Lord said to my Lord: Sit thou at my right hand") is among the Old Testament verses the New Testament cites most (Mark 12:35–37; Acts 2:34–35). Hebrews builds its priesthood argument on 110:4 ([`new-testament` 6.2](../../new-testament/lessons/06-02-hebrews.md)). **[Jewish readings](../reference.md#jewish-readings):** Rashi records that the Sages expounded Psalm 2 of the King Messiah (Talmud, Berakhot 7b). He himself gives its plain sense as David and the Philistines, citing 2 Samuel 5:17. He reads Psalm 110, following the rabbis and the Midrash on Psalms, as God's word to Abraham, whom the Hittites address as lord (Gen 23:6).

5. **[Imprecatory psalms](../reference.md#imprecatory-psalms).** Some psalms curse. Psalm 137 [Douay 136] begins "Upon the rivers of Babylon, there we sat and wept" and ends "Blessed be he that shall take and dash thy little ones against the rock" (v. 9). The literal sense is not tame: deportees who watched Jerusalem fall ask God to remember Edom (v. 7) and bless whoever repays Babylon like for like (v. 8). The tradition has prayed it in three ways.
   - **Spiritually.** Origen (*Against Celsus* VII.22) and Augustine read Babylon's little ones as sins in their infancy. Augustine, on this psalm (*Expositions on the Psalms*, §12; NPNF 1st ser. vol. 8): "What are the little ones of Babylon? Evil desires at their birth." Dash them while they are small, he says, against the rock that is Christ.
   - **As morally parsed.** Aquinas (ST II-II q.25 a.6 ad 3) takes scriptural imprecations "by way of prediction, not by way of wish", or as desire for God's justice, or as desire for the sin to be destroyed, "but that the man may live".
   - **By omission.** The *General Instruction of the Liturgy of the Hours* (1971), §131, removes Psalms 58, 83 and 109 [Douay 57, 82, 108] from the four-week cycle and drops verses from others, Psalm 137:7–9 among them. It gives a psychological difficulty as the reason, while noting that the New Testament prays in this way itself (Rev 6:10). The liturgy as such belongs to [`sacraments-and-liturgy`](../../sacraments-and-liturgy/syllabus.md) 6.2.

**Mark the levels.** That the 150 psalms are inspired and canonical is **defined dogma** (Trent, Session IV, lists the *Psalterium davidicum* of 150 psalms; [`fundamental-theology` 3.3](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/03-03-the-canon-as-defined.md)). The Biblical Commission's response of 1 May 1910 denied that David wrote the whole Psalter. It held that he was the principal author, that the titles preserve ancient Jewish tradition, that Psalms 2 and 110 among others are David's, that the view placing many psalms after Ezra and Nehemiah, some in the Maccabean age, is improbable, and that some psalms must be admitted as prophetic and messianic. It bound consciences when it was issued. After the 1955 clarifications its authorship and dating clauses are **freely disputed** among Catholics ([the Biblical Commission and its weight](../reference.md#the-biblical-commission-and-its-weight); [`fundamental-theology` 4.5](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-05-reading-a-documents-weight.md)). That the royal psalms speak of Christ is taught by the New Testament itself. *How* they do so is freely disputed: as the psalmist's own prediction, as a fuller sense, or by typology through the Davidic king. The 1971 omissions are **liturgical law**, a rule about what is prayed publicly, and they say nothing against inspiration. Lowth, Kugel and Gunkel are **scholarly analysis**, with no magisterial level ([church teaching vs hypothesis](../reference.md#church-teaching-vs-hypothesis)).

## The concordance

[Psalm numbering](../reference.md#psalm-numbering). The Hebrew divides where the Greek joins, and joins where the Greek divides:

| Hebrew (modern Bibles) | Douay / Vulgate / Septuagint | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| 1–8 | 1–8 | same |
| 9 and 10 | 9 (Heb 10 = Douay 9:22–39) | Greek joins two |
| 11–113 | 10–112 | Douay one lower |
| 114 and 115 | 113 (Heb 115 begins "Not to us, O Lord") | Greek joins two |
| 116 | 114 (vv. 1–9) and 115 (vv. 10–19) | Greek splits one |
| 117–146 | 116–145 | Douay one lower |
| 147 | 146 (vv. 1–11) and 147 (vv. 12–20) | Greek splits one |
| 148–150 | 148–150 | same |

The Douay's own note at 9:21 records both the Hebrew split and the join of 146–147. Verse numbers can differ too: where a psalm has a title, the Douay counts it as verse 1 (or verses 1–2) and most modern English Bibles do not. So Psalm 22:1 is Douay 21:2, and Psalm 51:1 is Douay 50:3.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean): Psalm 13 [Douay 12] by genre.** It opens with "How long, O Lord, wilt thou forget me unto the end?" and repeats "How long" four times. That is the complaint. Then comes the petition: "Consider, and hear me ... Enlighten my eyes, that I never sleep in death" (Douay v. 4). Next, the motive: "lest at any time my enemy say: I have prevailed". Then the turn: "But I have trusted in thy mercy ... I will sing to the Lord". Every part of Gunkel's individual lament is here in six verses, and the genre explains the sudden change of mood: it is a convention of the form, not a psychological puzzle.

**Example 2 (hard): Psalm 110:3 [Douay 109:3].** The Douay reads "from the womb before the day star I begot thee". That follows the Greek and Latin, which read the Hebrew consonants as "I begot thee". The Masoretic Text vocalizes the same consonants as "your youth", and the whole verse is one of the most obscure in the Psalter. The Fathers read the Vulgate line of the Son's eternal generation. A literal-sense reader must first say which text is being translated: the method strains because the literal sense itself is textually uncertain, and a Catholic reader need not pretend otherwise.

## Watch out

- **Hypothesis as teaching:** "The Church teaches David wrote the Psalms." The 1910 response said *principal author* and denied sole authorship, and today its authorship clauses are freely disputed. Nothing defined names the authors.
- **Teaching as hypothesis:** "Since scholars date the Psalms late, a Catholic can drop the messianic reading." The New Testament's christological reading of Psalms 2 and 110 is Scripture's own. What is open is its mode.
- **Omitted is not rejected:** the 1971 omissions govern public prayer, not the canon.
- **Numbering slips.** "Psalm 50" in a patristic or breviary text is the *Miserere*, Hebrew 51. Convert before you argue.

## One-liner

> Hear the second line, name the genre, convert the number (usually Douay = Hebrew minus one, from 10 to 147), then read the literal sense before the spiritual one, even when the psalm curses.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢)** *(Exegetical)* (a) Classify each Douay couplet under Lowth's three kinds, and for one of them say what Kugel's *A, and what's more, B* adds.

1. "The heavens shew forth the glory of God, and the firmament declareth the work of his hands." (Douay 18:2)
2. "For the Lord knoweth the way of the just: and the way of the wicked shall perish." (Douay 1:6)
3. "Let my tongue cleave to my jaws, if I do not remember thee: If I make not Jerusalem the beginning of my joy." (Douay 136:6)

(b) Give the Hebrew (modern) psalm number for each: Douay 18, Douay 1, Douay 136, and Douay 147. **One line each.**

**P2 (🟡)** *(Exegetical)* Psalm 13 is in Example 1, so here is another psalm. Douay 41:2–4, 6 (Hebrew 42:1–3, 5):

> As the hart panteth after the fountains of water; so my soul panteth after thee, O God. My soul hath thirsted after the strong living God; when shall I come and appear before the face of God? My tears have been my bread day and night, whilst it is said to me daily: Where is thy God? ... Why art thou sad, O my soul? and why dost thou trouble me? Hope in God, for I will still give praise to him ...

(a) Name the genre and point to the words that show each of two of its parts. (b) This psalm opens Book II. Name the doxology that closes Book I, in both numberings. **Two sentences for (a); one line for (b).**

**P3 (🔴)** *(Exegetical)* An **invented** parish study-guide:

> "The Church has always taught that David wrote all 150 psalms, and the Biblical Commission confirmed this in 1910. Psalm 110 is purely a prediction of Jesus; Jewish interpreters simply ignore it. And since the Church removed the cursing psalms from the breviary in 1971, it no longer regards verses like Psalm 137:9 as inspired."

Name **four** errors, correct each, and give the right level for each correction. **150 words or fewer.**

<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** 1 **synonymous** (heavens / firmament, shew forth / declareth, glory of God / work of his hands). 2 **antithetic** (the way of the just is known / the way of the wicked perishes). 3 **synonymous**, with each half an oath about remembering Jerusalem. In 1, "the work of his hands" makes the vague "glory" concrete. In 3, the oath moves from not forgetting to putting Jerusalem above every joy.

**Wrong turns:** answering "148" for Douay 147 by adding one out of habit. Calling 2 "synthetic" because its halves differ, when opposition is exactly what antithetic means.
**Model answer:** (a) 1 synonymous, 2 antithetic, 3 synonymous. By Kugel, 1's second line makes "glory" concrete as handiwork. (b) 19; 1; 137; 147:12–20.

**P2** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** an **individual lament**. Credit also "lament with a refrain" (the "Why art thou sad" refrain recurs in Hebrew 42:11 and 43:5, and many read 42–43 as one psalm). Two parts named with their words: **complaint** ("My tears have been my bread day and night", the mockers' "Where is thy God?"); **longing or petition** ("when shall I come and appear before the face of God?"); **trust and vow of praise** ("Hope in God, for I will still give praise to him").
**Must hit, strict (b):** Hebrew **41:13** = Douay **40:14**, "Blessed be the Lord the God of Israel from eternity to eternity. So be it. So be it."
**Wrong turns:** calling it a hymn because it is addressed to God. Hymns praise from the start and give reasons, and this psalm complains first. Giving the Book I doxology as 41:14 in the Hebrew/English system, which mixes the systems.
**Model answer:** (a) An individual lament: the complaint is "my tears have been my bread ... Where is thy God?", and the turn to trust is "Hope in God, for I will still give praise to him." (b) Psalm 41:13 [Douay 40:14].

**P3** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict:** four of these.
- **"Always taught David wrote all 150."** The 1910 response **denied** sole authorship. It held David to be the *principal* author. Its authorship clauses bound when issued, and after the 1955 clarifications they are **freely disputed**. No defined teaching names the psalmists.
- **"Confirmed" in 1910.** As above, it said the reverse on sole authorship.
- **"Purely a prediction."** The literal sense is a royal oracle to a Davidic king. The New Testament's christological reading is Scripture's own teaching, and **how** it applies (the psalmist's own prediction, a fuller sense, or typology) is **freely disputed**.

**Wrong turns:** "correcting" the first error by saying the Church teaches David wrote none. Treating the 1910 response as irrelevant, or as still binding in every clause.
**Model answer:** The 1910 Commission denied that David wrote every psalm and called him the principal author. Its authorship clauses once bound, and are now freely disputed among Catholics. No dogma names the psalmists. Psalm 110 is literally a royal oracle. That the New Testament reads it of Christ is Scripture's teaching; how it applies is freely disputed. Rashi does not ignore it: with the rabbis he reads it of Abraham. The 1971 omission is liturgical law about the public office. Psalm 137 remains part of the defined canon, and its inspiration is untouched.

## Flashback

**From Lesson [4.4](04-04-jeremiah-and-ezekiel.md) (Jeremiah and Ezekiel):** *(Exegetical.)* Jeremiah promises the Branch twice (Douay–Rheims):

> Behold the days come, saith the Lord, and I will raise up to David a just branch: and a king shall reign, and shall be wise: and shall execute judgment and justice in the earth. In those days shall Juda be saved, and Israel shall dwell confidently: and this is the name that they shall call him: The Lord our just one. (23:5–6)
>
> Behold the days come, saith the Lord, that I will perform the good word that I have spoken to the house of Israel, and to the house of Juda. In those days, and at that time, I will make the bud of justice to spring forth unto David, and he shall do judgment and justice in the earth. In those days shall Juda be saved, and Jerusalem shall dwell securely: and this is the name that they shall call him, The Lord our just one. (33:14–16)

The name is Hebrew *YHWH tsidqenu*, "the LORD is our righteousness". In 23:6 the Hebrew verb carries "him" (*yiqre'o*, "they will call him"). In 33:16 it has "to her" instead (*yiqra-lah*, "she will be called"), and the nearest feminine noun is Jerusalem. The Douay follows the Latin "him" in both. The Greek Jeremiah has 23:5–6 but lacks the whole of 33:14–26.

(a) List three differences between the passages besides the pronoun, citing the words. (b) In the Hebrew, who bears the name in each passage, and what does the shift do to the promise? (c) What do the words "the good word that I have spoken" (33:14) suggest about how the second passage was composed, on the majority text-critical view? **A list for (a); two sentences each for (b) and (c).**

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):** three of these. 33:14 adds an introduction: God will "perform the good word that I have spoken to the house of Israel, and to the house of Juda". 23:5's "a just branch" becomes "the bud of justice" (33:15). 23:5's "a king shall reign, and shall be wise" is **absent** from 33. 23:6's "Israel shall dwell confidently" becomes "**Jerusalem** shall dwell securely" (33:16). 33:15 adds "and at that time".
**Must hit, strict (b):** in 23:6 the name belongs to **the king**, the Davidic Branch. In 33:16 it belongs to **Jerusalem**, the city. The promise moves from a righteous king to a restored city that carries the LORD's righteousness, so the second passage is less exclusively royal. The verses that follow, on David's throne and the Levites (33:17–18), keep the king in view beside the city.
**Must hit, strict (c):** 33:14 presents itself as **fulfilling a promise already given**. Its near-verbatim reuse of 23:5–6 then reads as a later reworking of Jeremiah's own oracle. That fits the majority view (Janzen, Tov) that 33:14–26 is an expansion in the longer Hebrew edition, absent from the shorter edition behind the Greek and from 4QJer<sup>b</sup> and <sup>d</sup>. Credit: "perform my good word" is also the formula of 29:10 (the return after seventy years), so the phrase may point to the promise of restoration generally.
**Wrong turns:** reading the Douay's "him" in 33:16 as the Hebrew. Calling the doublet a copying slip, when the changes are deliberate. Concluding that 33:14–26 is not Scripture because it may be later: canonicity is defined of the book "with all their parts", and the dating is text criticism with no magisterial level.
**Model answer:** (a) 33:14 adds "I will perform the good word that I have spoken". "A just branch" becomes "the bud of justice". "A king shall reign, and shall be wise" drops out, and "Israel shall dwell confidently" becomes "Jerusalem shall dwell securely". (b) In 23:6 the Davidic king is called "the LORD our righteousness", and in 33:16 Jerusalem is. The promise widens from a righteous king to a righteous city. (c) The passage announces itself as keeping a word already spoken, and it reuses 23:5–6 almost word for word. On the majority view, that marks it as a later expansion of the longer Hebrew edition, reworking Jeremiah's own oracle.

</details>
