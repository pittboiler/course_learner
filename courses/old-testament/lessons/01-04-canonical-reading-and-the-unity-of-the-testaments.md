# The Old Testament · Lesson 1.4: Canonical reading and the unity of the Testaments

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 1: How the Church reads the Old Testament · Builds on: [1.2 The senses of Scripture](01-02-the-senses-of-scripture.md), [1.3 The historical-critical method and its limits](01-03-the-historical-critical-method-and-its-limits.md) · Unlocks: [2.1 Two accounts of creation](02-01-two-accounts-of-creation.md), [6.4 The Old Testament in light of the New](06-04-the-old-testament-in-light-of-the-new.md)

## Why this matters

Proverbs 26:4 tells you not to answer a fool according to his folly. Verse 5 tells you to answer him. Ecclesiastes wonders whether a man's spirit goes anywhere a beast's does not (3:21), and Daniel 12:2 says the dead will wake. [1.3](01-03-the-historical-critical-method-and-its-limits.md) gave you a tool that reads each text in its own century. It will tell you, correctly, that these texts come from different hands and different times. But the Church reads them as one book, bound to a second Testament. This lesson asks what reading a passage "within the whole" adds. It also asks how that differs from two ways of getting it wrong: smoothing the tension away, and letting the later text overwrite the earlier one.

## The idea

Think of a letter in a published collection of someone's correspondence. The collection does not change what the letter meant when it was sent. It does change what the letter *does*. It now sits beside the reply, the apology and the letter written ten years later, and a reader learns things from the arrangement that no single letter says. [Canonical reading](../reference.md#canonical-reading) treats the Bible like that. The [literal sense](../reference.md#literal-sense) of each text stays what it was ([1.2](01-02-the-senses-of-scripture.md)). The canon adds a context.

Three operations are easy to confuse:

- **Harmonizing** asks *how can both be true?* It removes the tension, usually with a distinction ("verse 4 is about X, verse 5 about Y") or with a supposed fact behind the text.
- **Canonical reading** asks *what does the whole say by holding both?* It leaves each voice standing and reads the arrangement.
- **Flattening** lets the later text replace the earlier one's meaning, so that the Old Testament says only what the New says.

Harmonizing is not illegitimate. It is often right, and the tradition does it constantly. It is just a different operation. Flattening is the risk canonical reading runs most often.

## Source

Augustine, *Questions on the Heptateuch* (*Quaestiones in Heptateuchum*) 2.73, comments on Israel at Sinai. The verse is Exodus 20:18–19 (Douay–Rheims):

> "And all the people saw the voices and the flames, and the sound of the trumpet, and the mount smoking; and being terrified and struck with fear, they stood afar off, Saying to Moses: Speak thou to us, and we will hear: let not the Lord speak to us, lest we die."

Augustine's comment (the lesson's translation of the Latin):

> "This signifies, strongly and solidly, that fear belongs rather to the Old Testament, as love to the New: although the New lies hidden in the Old, and the Old lies open in the New."

Read the grammar. The main claim is a *contrast*: fear marks the Old covenant, love the New. The famous line comes inside a concessive clause, *quamquam et in Vetere Novum lateat, et in Novo Vetus pateat* ("although ..."), with a play on *lateat* (lies hidden) and *pateat* (lies open). Augustine does not say the Testaments are the same. He says they differ, and that the difference is not a gulf. Dei Verbum 16 cites this passage and recasts it as God's design. The author of both Testaments arranged that the New should lie hidden in the Old and the Old be disclosed in the New (paraphrased; the Latin keeps Augustine's verbs, *lateret* and *pateret*). The Old Testament books, it goes on, gain and show their full meaning in the New, and in turn shed light on it.

## The argument

1. **Dei Verbum 12's three criteria.** After the historical criteria of [1.3](01-03-the-historical-critical-method-and-its-limits.md) (what the author meant, in his genre and setting), Scripture must be read in the Spirit in which it was written. So the interpreter gives no less care to the **content and unity of the whole of Scripture**, taking account of the **living Tradition of the whole Church** and the **analogy of faith** ([`fundamental-theology` 3.2](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/03-02-inerrancy.md) lists them). In the Latin the first is the main clause, and the other two are what it takes into account (*ratione habita*). *In words:* read the passage within the whole Bible, keeping the Church's reading of it and the coherence of the faith in view.
2. **The Catechism's gloss** (CCC 112–114, paraphrased). Scripture is one because God's plan is one, with Christ at its centre. Tradition carries the spiritual interpretation the Spirit gives the Church. The analogy of faith is the coherence of the truths of faith with one another and within the whole plan of revelation. *In words:* the [three criteria](../reference.md#three-criteria-of-dei-verbum-12) are three contexts: textual, ecclesial and doctrinal.
3. **Childs's canonical approach.** Brevard Childs sketched it in *Biblical Theology in Crisis* (1970) and applied it in *Introduction to the Old Testament as Scripture* (1979). The object of theological exegesis is each book's **final form**, as the community received it. The editors who framed, ordered and joined the material were already interpreting, and their shaping belongs to the meaning. He rejected the label "canonical criticism". Canon, for him, was not one more critical technique beside source and form criticism. It was the stance from which the Bible is read as Scripture.
4. **Sanders's canonical criticism.** James A. Sanders coined the phrase (*Torah and Canon*, 1972) and studied the **process**: how older traditions were reused in new situations until a stable but adaptable collection emerged.
5. **The Biblical Commission's assessment.** *The Interpretation of the Bible in the Church* (1993, I.C.1) distinguishes the two and welcomes the approach as a complement to historical criticism, not a substitute. It names two problems. First, there is no single canon: the Church received the wider collection of Greek-speaking Judaism, so canonical readings will differ. Second, the Church reads the Old Testament in light of the paschal mystery, but must respect each stage of salvation history. Emptying the Old Testament of its own meaning, it says, would cut the New off from its roots in history.
6. **So:** canonical reading is **literal sense first, then context**. It adds and does not replace. Where it replaces, it has become flattening.

**Mark the levels.** [Church teaching vs hypothesis](../reference.md#church-teaching-vs-hypothesis): that **one God is the author of both Testaments** is defined dogma (Trent, Session IV, 1546; rung 1 of [the ladder](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-04-the-ladder-of-doctrinal-authority.md); see [`fundamental-theology` 3.1](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/03-01-inspiration.md)). The **three criteria** (Dei Verbum 12) and the claim that **the New lies hidden in the Old and the Old is disclosed in the New** (Dei Verbum 16) are conciliar teaching in a dogmatic constitution, not a new definition: at least authentic magisterium at its heaviest (rung 3). The Catechism restates them. **Augustine's formula** is a Father's theology that the Council took up. **Childs's and Sanders's programmes** are scholarly methods with no magisterial standing. The **1993 document** comes from the post-1971 Commission and is not an act of the Magisterium ([the Commission's weight](../reference.md#the-biblical-commission-and-its-weight)). **Every particular canonical reading or harmonization**, including those below, is an exegetical judgment and freely disputed. Dates: 1970, 1972 and 1979 are publication facts. The date of Ecclesiastes is **disputed** (most scholars place it in the Persian or early Hellenistic period).

## Three ways to meet a tension

| | Harmonizing | Canonical reading | Flattening |
|---|---|---|---|
| Asks | How can both be true? | What does the whole say by holding both? | What does the later text say this means? |
| The tension | Removed | Kept, and read | Erased by the later text |
| Each text's literal sense | Often bent toward the other | Established first, then kept | Replaced |
| Typical tool | A distinction; a hidden fact | Placement, framing, juxtaposition | "It really means ..." |
| Right when | The texts really address different cases | Always available after the literal sense | Never |

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean): the two answers to a fool** (Proverbs 26:4–5, Douay–Rheims):

> "Answer not a fool according to his folly, lest thou be made like him. Answer a fool according to his folly, lest he imagine himself to be wise."

*Literal sense first.* The two sayings stand side by side, each with its own reason, and most commentators take the placement as deliberate: the collector knew they pulled against each other. Two verses later the chapter compares a proverb in a fool's mouth to a thorn in a drunkard's hand (26:9). Proverbs need judgment to apply.

*Two harmonizations.* Challoner's note on verse 4 narrows it: do not answer the fool by imitating him, only by reproving his folly. The Babylonian Talmud (*Shabbat* 30b) reports that the Sages wanted to withdraw Proverbs because its sayings contradict each other, and resolves this pair by assigning the verses different matters. You answer the fool on matters of Torah and not on worldly ones. That is how the rabbinic tradition kept the book in use. Both moves remove the tension with a distinction.

*Canonical reading.* It keeps both commands as commands. The pair teaches what no single proverb can: wisdom is knowing which saying the moment calls for. The wider canon says the same in Ecclesiastes 3:7: "A time to keep silence, and a time to speak." Neither verse has been bent.

**Example 2 (hard): Ecclesiastes inside the canon.** Qoheleth ("the Preacher") calls everything vanity (1:2) and says man has nothing more than a beast (3:19). He asks, "Who knoweth if the spirit of the children of Adam ascend upward?" (3:21). The book ends: "Fear God, and keep his commandments: for this is all man" (12:13).

- *Literal sense.* Most scholars assign the epilogue (12:9–14) to one or more editors rather than to Qoheleth; a minority read it as his own frame. The sceptical voice is real, and nothing inside the book answers it.
- *Canonical reading.* Childs reads the epilogue as the editors' instruction for using the book. Qoheleth's words stand as a sharp corrective within wisdom, set under the fear of God and the commandments. The Talmud (*Shabbat* 30b) shows the same instinct inside [Jewish tradition](../reference.md#jewish-readings): the Sages kept Ecclesiastes because it begins and ends with words of Torah. Across the whole canon, Qoheleth's open question meets Daniel 12:2's waking of the dead, and for Christians the resurrection.
- *Where it strains.* The failures come from opposite sides. A historical critic may dismiss the epilogue as a pious muzzle, which throws away the form the community actually received. A canonical reader may let Daniel or the Gospel answer 3:21 *inside* Ecclesiastes, as if Qoheleth had hoped for resurrection. That is flattening. The honest canonical claim is narrower. Scripture as a whole contains both the question and a later answer, and the question is kept because it is part of the witness.

## Watch out

- **"Canonical reading is how the Church resolves contradictions."** That describes harmonizing. Canonical reading can leave a tension standing and read it.
- **"Dei Verbum endorses Childs."** The Council closed in 1965, before Childs published his programme, and its criteria name no method. The 1993 document welcomes the approach, and that document is not magisterial. Claiming either endorsement is a level error.
- **"The unity of the Testaments means the Old says what the New says."** Augustine's own sentence contrasts them. Dei Verbum 16 has the Old gain its full meaning in the New, and the Commission warns against emptying it of its own. Unity of plan is not identity of content.
- **"Canonical reading replaces historical criticism."** Dei Verbum 12 puts the historical criteria first and the canonical ones after them. Childs himself used critical results.

## One-liner

> Read the text in its own century first, then in the whole book the Church received: canonical reading adds context and keeps the tensions, harmonizing removes them, and flattening lets the New talk over the Old.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢)** *(Exegetical)* Isaiah 2:4 (Douay–Rheims): "they shall turn their swords into ploughshares, and their spears into sickles: nation shall not lift up sword against nation." Joel 3:10 (Hebrew 4:10): "Cut your ploughshares into swords, and your spades into spears." For each reader's move, name the Dei Verbum 12 criterion it uses (unity of Scripture, living Tradition, analogy of faith), or label it **literal-sense work** or **flattening**. One line each.

1. Joel's summons is addressed to the nations gathered for the Lord's judgment (3:9–12), and he is probably turning a known peace saying (Isaiah 2:4, Micah 4:3) inside out on purpose.
2. Read in one canon, the two oracles give an order: judgment on the nations' war-making comes before the peace Isaiah promises, and neither cancels the other.
3. Justin Martyr (*First Apology* 39) reads Isaiah 2:3–4 as fulfilled when the apostles went out from Jerusalem and Christians who once killed one another stopped making war on their enemies.
4. A reading of Joel 3:10 as God commanding the Church to spread the faith by the sword is ruled out because it clashes with the Church's teaching that the act of faith must be free.
5. "Since Christ is 'our peace' (Ephesians 2:14), Isaiah 2:4 means only the peace in Christians' hearts, not peace among nations."

**P2 (🟡)** *(Exegetical)* An **invented** study-Bible note:

> "Some claim that Exodus 20:5 ('visiting the iniquity of the fathers upon the children, unto the third and fourth generation of them that hate me') contradicts Ezekiel 18:20 ('the son shall not bear the iniquity of the father'). Read canonically, there is no tension. Moses already meant only children who imitate their fathers' sins, as 'of them that hate me' shows, and Ezekiel simply spells this out. This is the Church's teaching, and it proves the unity of the two Testaments."

(a) Which operation is the note actually performing? Say what it gets right, and name one tradition that reads Exodus 20:5 this way. (b) What does it do to Ezekiel's literal sense? Use Ezekiel 18:2–3: "you use among you this parable as a proverb ... The fathers have eaten sour grapes, and the teeth of the children are set on edge. As I live, saith the Lord God, this parable shall be no more to you a proverb in Israel." (c) Name the level error and the second error in the note's last sentence. **Two sentences per part.**

**P3 (🔴)** *(Exegetical)* Two positions, paraphrased:

- **Childs:** each book's final form, shaped by the community that received it as Scripture, is the proper object of theological exegesis. The editors' shaping is itself interpretation and belongs to the text's meaning.
- **A historical critic** (in outline, the objection James Barr pressed in *Holy Scripture: Canon, Authority, Criticism*, 1983): the books were read as authoritative long before any list was closed, so canon is a later decision *about* the texts, not part of what they mean. And Jewish, Catholic and Protestant canons differ, so "the canonical context" has no single referent.

(a) Name the single premise one side must deny. Say what kind of evidence would move it. (b) Say which of the critic's points the 1993 Commission document itself grants, and what Dei Verbum 12 and Trent do and do not settle between the two. **Three sentences per part.**

<details>
<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict:** (1) **literal-sense work**: Joel's addressees, setting and deliberate allusion are about what the human author meant (Dei Verbum 12's historical criteria), even though the move cites another book. (2) **Content and unity of the whole of Scripture**: it reads the two in one canon and keeps both. (3) **Living Tradition**: a Father's reading, handed on in the Church. (4) **Analogy of faith**: the coherence of a reading with the truths of faith. (5) **Flattening**: the New Testament text replaces Isaiah's own meaning, peace among nations, instead of adding to it.

**Wrong turns:** labelling (1) "unity of Scripture" because it cites Isaiah and Micah. An author's own allusion is part of his literal sense; the canonical criterion begins when the arrangement, not the author, does the work. Labelling (5) a legitimate spiritual sense. A spiritual sense is *founded on* the literal ([1.2](01-02-the-senses-of-scripture.md)), and "means only" removes the foundation. Treating (4) as imposing doctrine on the text: the analogy of faith rules a reading out; it does not supply Joel's meaning.

**Model answer:** (1) Literal-sense work. (2) Unity of the whole of Scripture. (3) Living Tradition. (4) Analogy of faith. (5) Flattening.

**P2** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** it is **harmonizing** presented as canonical reading: it removes the tension by a distinction (only imitating children). It rightly notes that "of them that hate me" is in the text, and the reading has a long pedigree. **Targum Onqelos** adds that the children are punished when they continue to sin after their fathers, and **Aquinas** (ST I-II q.87 a.8 ad 1) appeals to the same phrase and to imitation. Either tradition earns the mark.

**Must hit, strict (b):** Ezekiel presents his word as **overturning a proverb** the exiles were using about their own situation ("shall be no more ... a proverb"). The note turns an announced change into a mere restatement, and so erases Ezekiel's own claim. A canonical reading keeps both texts, the Decalogue's solidarity across generations and Ezekiel's word to exiles who felt punished for their fathers, and reads the pair as development within the canon (compare Jeremiah 31:29–30).

**Must hit, strict (c):** **Level error:** the harmonization is an exegetical opinion (Onqelos's, Aquinas's) and freely disputed, not Church teaching. Dei Verbum 12 gives criteria and does not fix this reading. **Second error:** both texts are in the **Old Testament**, so their agreement says nothing about the unity *of the two Testaments*. The relevant criterion is the unity of the whole of Scripture, and a harmonization does not "prove" that unity in any case.

**Wrong turns:** calling the harmonization false. It may be right, and the task is to name the operation. Claiming the Hebrew settles that "them that hate me" refers to the children and not the fathers; how far the phrase restricts the threat is debated. Answering (b) with Deuteronomy 24:16 alone, which is a rule for human courts and leaves Ezekiel's proverb untouched.

**Model answer:** (a) It harmonizes: it dissolves the tension by restricting Exodus 20:5 to children who imitate their fathers, a reading with real support in the text's "of them that hate me" and in Targum Onqelos and Aquinas. That is a legitimate operation, but it is not canonical reading. (b) Ezekiel says the sour-grapes proverb will no longer be spoken in Israel, so his word presents itself as new, and the note reduces it to a gloss on Moses. A canonical reading lets both stand and reads Ezekiel as a development answering the exiles. (c) The harmonization is an exegetes' opinion, not Church teaching, and Dei Verbum 12 does not decide it. Both texts are Old Testament, so the note cannot be about the unity of the Testaments at all.

**P3** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** the crux is **whether the community's final shaping and reception belong to a text's meaning** or are later events *about* the text. Childs affirms it; the critic denies it. Evidence that would move it: cases where the final editors plainly act as **authors**, framing the material in ways the text itself signals (the Ecclesiastes epilogue, the ordering of Proverbs 26:4–5), favour Childs. Cases where the final frame demonstrably **distorts** an earlier text's sense favour the critic.

**Must hit, strict (b):** the 1993 document **grants the "which canon" point**: the corpus differs, so canonical interpretation cannot be identical in each case. Dei Verbum 12 makes the **unity of the whole of Scripture a criterion of meaning** for Catholic interpreters, so the critic's view in its strong form (canon irrelevant to meaning) is not open to a Catholic doing theological exegesis. It does **not adopt final-form priority** or Childs's method, and it puts the author's intention first. Trent fixes which books a Catholic reads as canonical ([`fundamental-theology` 3.3](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/03-03-the-canon-as-defined.md)), which answers "which canon?" for Catholics while leaving the Jewish–Christian difference in place.

**Wrong turns:** making the crux "whether the books are inspired" or "whether historical criticism is valid", which both sides can grant. Saying Dei Verbum 12 settles the dispute for Childs: it names no method, and it puts the historical criteria first. Saying the 1993 document "defines" anything.

**Model answer:** (a) The crux is whether the final shaping and reception of a book are part of its meaning, as Childs holds, or a later decision about it, as the critic holds. Texts where editors plainly frame the material, like the Ecclesiastes epilogue, would favour Childs. Frames that clearly distort an earlier sense would favour the critic. (b) The Commission concedes that canons differ, so canonical readings cannot be the same everywhere. Dei Verbum 12 makes the unity of Scripture a criterion of meaning, which excludes the critic's strong claim for Catholic theological exegesis, but it neither adopts final-form priority nor names any method. Trent fixes the Catholic canon, so "which canon?" has an answer for Catholics, though not for Jewish and Christian readers together.

## Flashback

**From Lesson [1.2](01-02-the-senses-of-scripture.md) (The senses of Scripture):** *(Exegetical.)* Josue sends "two men" to spy out Jericho (Joshua 2:1), and Rahab the harlot hides them. Before they escape they swear to spare her (Joshua 2:18–19, 21, Douay–Rheims):

> "If, when we come into the land, this scarlet cord be a sign, and thou tie it in the window, by which thou hast let us down: and gather together thy father and mother, and brethren, and all thy kindred into thy house. Whosoever shall go out of the door of thy house, his blood shall be upon his own head, and we shall be quit. But the blood of all that shall be with thee in the house, shall light upon our head, if any man touch them. ... And she answered: As you have spoken, so be it done: and sending them on their way, she hung the scarlet cord in the window."

Two Fathers read the scene (ANF translation):

- **Clement of Rome** (*1 Clement* 12): by the sign of the scarlet thread the spies "made it manifest that redemption should flow through the blood of the Lord to all them that believe and hope in God."
- **Irenaeus** (*Against Heresies* IV.20.12): Rahab received "the three spies", who figure the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit.

(a) Give the literal sense of the cord in one sentence. (b) For each Father, say whether the reading is typology or allegory in the narrow sense, which spiritual sense it aims at, and whether the literal sense supplies the *thing* it rests on. **Two sentences per Father.**

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):** the cord is an **agreed recognition sign under oath**: it marks Rahab's house, so that everyone gathered inside is spared when the city is taken, while anyone who goes out bears his own blood (2:19). Nothing in Joshua's literal sense is about Christ.
**Must hit, strict (b):** **Clement** reads a real saving sign in a real deliverance as prefiguring a real redemption: **typology**, **allegorical** sense (Christ's blood saving believers). The literal sense **does supply the thing**: a sign that saves all who stay under the marked roof, which parallels the Passover blood on the doorposts (Exodus 12). Credit a note that the link from the cord's colour to blood has an allegorical edge. **Irenaeus** reads a *number* as a code for a truth of faith: **allegory** in the narrow sense, aiming at the **allegorical** sense (the Trinity). But the literal sense **does not supply the thing**, because Joshua 2:1 says **two** men. A reading with no literal foundation is not a spiritual sense of the text; it is at best accommodation, and it carries one Father's weight.
**Wrong turns:** putting Christ's blood into the literal sense of Joshua 2. Treating Irenaeus's reading as authoritative because a Father made it, or as doctrinally suspect because it is unfounded: the doctrine is sound, and the *reading* is what fails. (The ANF translators put the "three" down to a slip of memory.) Calling Clement's reading allegory just because it involves a detail.
**Model answer:** (a) The scarlet cord is the sign the spies swear by: the house it marks, and everyone gathered inside it, will be spared when Jericho falls. (b) Clement's reading is typology in the allegorical sense: a real sign that saved those under one roof prefigures Christ's blood saving believers, and the literal sense supplies that sign, as the Passover blood did. Irenaeus's reading is allegory aiming at the Trinity, but Joshua sends two spies, not three, so the literal sense supplies nothing for it to rest on, and it is at best accommodation.
