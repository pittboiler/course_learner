# The Old Testament · Lesson 2.6: Covenant and law

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 2: The Pentateuch · Builds on: [2.2 Who wrote the Pentateuch?](02-02-who-wrote-the-pentateuch.md), [2.5 Exodus and Sinai](02-05-exodus-and-sinai.md) · Unlocks: [3.3 Two kingdoms and two falls](03-03-two-kingdoms-and-two-falls.md), [4.1 What a prophet is](04-01-what-a-prophet-is-amos-and-hosea.md)

## Why this matters

Much of Exodus, all of Leviticus and most of Deuteronomy is law: goring oxen, skin disease, slaves, the altar. A Christian reader runs into two questions at once. The first is historical: why does this law look so much like Babylon's, and why do Israel's own codes disagree with each other? The second is theological: which of it, if any, binds after Christ? Slogans ("Jesus abolished the law", "Moses copied Hammurabi") answer neither. This lesson reads the codes in their own terms first. Then it gives the tradition's sharpest tool for the second question, Aquinas's division of the Old Law into moral, ceremonial and judicial precepts.

## The idea

In the Pentateuch, law is never free-standing. It sits inside a story of rescue and a covenant (*berit*): *I brought you out of Egypt; therefore live like this.* The Hebrew *torah* means "instruction" before it means "statute". The treaty form behind that pattern was [2.5](02-05-exodus-and-sinai.md)'s subject. Here we look at the stipulations themselves.

There are three main blocks, and they do not come from one pen:

- **The Covenant Code** (Exodus 20:22–23:33), named from "the book of the covenant" Moses reads aloud in Exodus 24:7. It holds case law in the Near Eastern style: *if* a man does X, *then* Y. Its heading is *mishpatim* (21:1), "judgments" in the Douay.
- **Leviticus**: sacrifice (chapters 1–7), purity (11–15), the Day of Atonement (16), and the **Holiness Code** (17–26). That last name is usually credited to August Klostermann in 1877. Its refrain comes from 19:2: "Be ye holy, because I the Lord your God am holy." Holiness here extends from the sanctuary to the whole people and the land.
- **The Deuteronomic Code** (Deuteronomy 12–26), framed as Moses' farewell speech on the plains of Moab. It is a second covenant, "beside that covenant which he made with them in Horeb" (Deut 29:1).

Lay the blocks side by side and the laws **differ**. The same topic (the altar, the Hebrew slave, the festivals) gets different rules. So the codes are one of the main pieces of evidence in the composition debate of [2.2](02-02-who-wrote-the-pentateuch.md). Lay the Covenant Code beside the Laws of Hammurabi and it looks **familiar**, which raises a further question about what Israel borrowed and what it changed.

## Source

Aquinas, *Summa Theologiae* I-II q.99 a.4 (English Dominican Province translation, public domain):

> We must therefore distinguish three kinds of precept in the Old Law; viz. "moral" precepts, which are dictated by the natural law; "ceremonial" precepts, which are determinations of the Divine worship; and "judicial" precepts, which are determinations of the justice to be maintained among men.

Read the criterion, not just the labels. A **moral** precept binds because reason itself dictates it (compare [natural law in Aquinas](../../ethics/lessons/04-04-natural-law-aquinas.md)). The other two are ***determinations***: particular ways, fixed by divine institution, of carrying out a general moral duty. Everyone owes God worship; *this* sacrifice on *this* day is a determination of that duty, and so ceremonial. Everyone owes a neighbour justice; *thirty sicles* for a slave is a determination of that duty, and so judicial. The question to ask of any precept is therefore **where its binding force comes from**, not what topic it is about. Aquinas says it plainly at q.104 a.1: judicial precepts bind "not from reason alone, but in virtue of their institution."

## The argument

**A. The codes in their setting.**

1. **Hammurabi is the parallel that matters.** The Babylonian king reigned c. 1792–1750 BC on the usual "middle" chronology (*reconstructed*). His laws survive on a diorite stele found at Susa in 1901–02 (*secure*). They share the Covenant Code's form ("If a man ...") and much of its subject matter: injury, slaves, oxen, deposits. *In words:* Israel's case law belongs to a legal culture that is older than Israel.
2. **Talion in both.** Hammurabi §196 (Johns, 1903): "If a man has caused the loss of a gentleman's eye, his eye one shall cause to be lost." Exodus 21:24 has "Eye for eye, tooth for tooth." The differences are what count. Hammurabi applies talion only between equals: injuring a "poor man" costs a mina of silver (§198), and injuring a servant costs half his price (§199). Exodus frees a slave who loses an eye (21:26). Leviticus 24:22 demands "equal judgment ... whether he be a stranger, or a native."
3. **How the similarity got there is disputed.** David P. Wright (*Inventing God's Law*, 2009) argues that the Covenant Code depends directly on a written text of Hammurabi, adapted in the Neo-Assyrian period, about 740–640 BC. The more common view explains the resemblance through a shared legal tradition, taught and transmitted over centuries, with no single text in between. Raymond Westbrook's work on a common Near Eastern legal tradition, studied and taught by scribes, is the usual reference for that view. *In words:* the family resemblance is agreed; a direct literary ancestor is not.
4. **Law develops between Israel's own codes.** Exodus 20:24 allows an altar "in every place where the memory of my name shall be". Deuteronomy 12:13–14 forbids sacrifice "in every place that thou shalt see" and requires it "in the place which the Lord shall choose." The slave law also changes between Exodus 21 and Deuteronomy 15. Deuteronomy adds women, provisions and a motive clause, and the ear-boring ceremony moves from "the gods" to "the door of thy house". That comparison is a boss problem, so it is only named here. Its legal setting is [`history-of-debt` 1.3](../../history-of-debt/lessons/01-03-release-laws-in-ancient-israel.md), and its theology belongs to [`theology-of-debt`](../../theology-of-debt/syllabus.md). Bernard Levinson (*Deuteronomy and the Hermeneutics of Legal Innovation*, 1997) reads Deuteronomy as deliberately revising the Covenant Code while presenting its revisions as Moses' own. *In words:* Deuteronomy is renewed covenant law, not a copy of the earlier code.

**B. The tradition's reading tool.**

5. **One law, three kinds of precept** (q.99 a.2–4). The Old Law holds moral precepts (the Decalogue sums them up, q.100 a.3), ceremonial precepts (sacrifices, holy things, sacraments, observances, q.101–103) and judicial precepts (rulers, dealings between people, foreigners, the household, q.104–105).
6. **They end differently** (q.103 a.3; q.104 a.3). The moral precepts last "for ever", simply and absolutely (q.103 a.3 ad 1). The ceremonial precepts foreshadowed Christ, so they ended when he came, and observing them as still binding would deny that he had come. The judicial precepts are, in Aquinas's phrase, "dead indeed, because they have no binding force: but they are not deadly." A Christian ruler could enact one without sin, provided he did not claim that it bound *as Mosaic law*. *In words:* figures end when the reality arrives, and a nation's civil code ends with the nation's special role. The moral law does not end.

**Mark the levels.** That the Ten Commandments bind Christians is **defined**: Trent Session VI, canon 19, anathematizes anyone who says they "nowise appertain to Christians." The Council of Florence's Decree for the Copts (1442) taught that the legal ceremonies came to an end when Christ came. That is conciliar teaching; its bearing on Jewish observance today belongs to [6.4](06-04-the-old-testament-in-light-of-the-new.md). That the Old Law is holy but imperfect, a "first stage" and a preparation for the Gospel, is catechetical teaching (CCC 1961–1964, paraphrased), at the level of **authentic ordinary magisterium**. Aquinas's **three-way division is common theological teaching**, a tool the tradition relies on, not a defined doctrine. Everything in part A is **scholarly hypothesis** with no magisterial standing: the codes' dates (the Covenant Code *disputed*; Deuteronomy's core often tied to the late seventh century and Josiah, [3.3](03-03-two-kingdoms-and-two-falls.md); the Holiness Code *disputed*, with Israel Knohl and Jacob Milgrom placing it after the Priestly core, which it edits) and Wright's dependence thesis (a live position, not the majority). See [Church teaching vs hypothesis](../reference.md#church-teaching-vs-hypothesis) and [dating discipline](../reference.md#dating-discipline).

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean): the parallel method on talion.** Put the texts in parallel before interpreting them. Hammurabi §§196–201 grade talion by class: an eye for a gentleman's eye, a mina of silver for a poor man's, half the price for a servant's. Exodus 21:23–25 states talion without class. In 21:26–27 the slave who loses an eye or a tooth goes free. The literal sense is therefore a principle of **proportion**: the penalty is capped at the injury and does not depend on rank. It is not a licence for revenge. The Jewish reading stated as its tradition states it: the Mishnah (Bava Kamma 8:1) takes "eye for eye" to mean monetary compensation, reckoned under five heads (damage, pain, healing, loss of time and shame). For the rabbis this is what the verse has always required, not a softening of it. See [lex talionis](../reference.md#lex-talionis). Then read the spiritual sense separately. Matthew 5:38–39 does not correct a barbaric rule. It asks the disciple to give up even the limited claim the law allows.

**Example 2 (hard): the Sabbath.** Run Aquinas's criterion. "Remember that thou keep holy the sabbath day" is in the Decalogue, so it looks moral. Aquinas splits it (q.100 a.3 ad 2). It is moral "in so far as it commands man to give some time to the things of God," and ceremonial "as to the fixing of the time." That split is how Christians can keep Sunday without keeping the seventh day. Three strains show:

- **The text does not divide itself.** Leviticus 19 puts "Thou shalt love thy friend as thyself" (19:18), the ban on sowing "different seeds" (19:19) and "Let the balance be just" (19:36) side by side, all under the same refrain "I am the Lord." The division is something a later reader brings to the text. It is a theological reading, not the literal sense.
- **Borderline cases.** "Do not any unjust thing ... in weight" is moral. The "bushel" and the "sextary" are determinations of it. One verse can hold two kinds of precept.
- **Jewish tradition does not use this division.** A saying attributed to Rabbi Simlai (Babylonian Talmud, *Makkot* 23b) counts 613 commandments. Every one binds Israel, and the Noahide laws are what bind the nations. From inside that tradition, a Sabbath that is only "ceremonial" is not the Sabbath at all.

The tool still works: it answers a Christian question, what binds after Christ, not what Leviticus 19 meant to its author. See [moral, ceremonial and judicial precepts](../reference.md#moral-ceremonial-and-judicial-precepts).

## Watch out

- **"Trent defined that only the Ten Commandments still bind."** Canon 19 defines that the commandments *do* bind Christians. It says nothing about the rest of the law. Love of neighbour (Lev 19:18) is a moral precept outside the Decalogue, and Aquinas reduces such precepts to the Decalogue's heads without discarding them (q.100 a.3; a.11).
- **"The moral/ceremonial/judicial division is Church dogma."** It is Aquinas's theological tool and common teaching. The Church's documents speak of the Old Law's moral prescriptions and its ending ceremonies, but none defines the three-way scheme.
- **"Hammurabi proves Moses copied."** Resemblance is agreed. Direct literary dependence is Wright's thesis, argued seriously and disputed. Inspiration does not require that the law had no human sources ([`fundamental-theology` 3.1](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/03-01-inspiration.md)), so neither answer threatens it.
- **"Eye for eye was primitive vengeance."** In both codes it limits punishment by law. The dispute is over who counts as equal.

## One-liner

> Israel's law is covenant instruction that shares its grammar with Babylon and revises itself between codes; Aquinas's question of where each precept gets its force explains what binds after Christ, but that division is not the text's own.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢)** *(Exegetical)* Classify each precept as **moral**, **ceremonial** or **judicial** by Aquinas's criterion (where its binding force comes from), in one line each. If a precept is mixed, say which part is which.

1. "Thou shalt love thy friend as thyself." (Lev 19:18)
2. "Thou shalt not sow thy field with different seeds." (Lev 19:19)
3. "He shall give thirty sicles of silver to their master." (Ex 21:32)
4. "Because the life of the flesh is in the blood ... that you may make atonement with it upon the altar." (Lev 17:11)
5. "Do not any unjust thing in judgment, in rule, in weight, or in measure. Let the balance be just and the weights equal, the bushel just, and the sextary equal." (Lev 19:35–36)

**P2 (🟡)** Exodus 21:28–32 (Douay–Rheims):

> If an ox gore a man or a woman, and they die, he shall be stoned: and his flesh shall not be eaten, but the owner of the ox shall be quit. But if the ox was wont to push with his horn yesterday, and the day before, and they warned his master, and he did not shut him up, and he shall kill a man or a woman: then the ox shall be stoned, and his owner also shall be put to death. And if they set a price upon him, he shall give for his life whatsoever is laid upon him. If he have gored a son, or a daughter, he shall fall under the like sentence. If he assault a bondman or bondwoman, he shall give thirty sicles of silver to their master, and the ox shall be stoned.

Laws of Hammurabi §§250–252 (Johns, 1903):

> If a wild bull in his charge has gored a man and caused him to die, that case has no remedy. If the ox has pushed a man, by pushing has made known his vice, and he has not blunted his horn, has not shut up his ox, and that ox has gored a man of gentle birth and caused him to die, he shall pay half a mina of silver. If a gentleman's servant, he shall pay one-third of a mina of silver.

(a) *(Exegetical)* List what the two laws share, and give three differences. For each difference, quote the words that decide it. (b) *(Evaluative)* Do (a)'s results favour Wright's direct-dependence thesis or a shared legal tradition? Answer in 120 words or fewer. Any verdict passes.

**P3 (🔴)** *(Exegetical)* An **invented** parish handout:

> "Christ abolished the Law of Moses, and the Council of Trent defined that Catholics keep only the Ten Commandments; everything else was ceremonial. That is why 'love thy neighbour' is New Testament teaching. The Old Testament laws were copied from Hammurabi anyway, as archaeology has proved, and 'eye for eye' shows how primitive they were."

Name four errors. For each, give the correction and, where it matters, the level. **One or two sentences per error.**

<details>
<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict:** (1) **Moral**: reason dictates it, and it binds for ever. (2) **Ceremonial**: a prohibition that binds only because God instituted it. Aquinas treats it among the ceremonial "observances" (q.102 a.6) and gives it a figurative reason. (3) **Judicial**: a fixed penalty between persons, binding by institution. (4) **Ceremonial**: it concerns sacrificial atonement and worship. (5) **Mixed**: "do not any unjust thing ... in weight" is **moral**, since reason dictates justice in exchange. The particular measures ("the bushel ... the sextary") are **judicial** determinations of it.

**Wrong turns:** calling (2) moral because it sits beside (1) in the same chapter. The criterion is where the precept gets its force, not where it stands in the text. Calling (3) moral because it concerns justice: the duty is moral, but a sum of thirty sicles is a determination of it. Calling (5) simply judicial, which makes honest weights optional after Christ.

**Model answer:** (1) Moral. (2) Ceremonial. (3) Judicial. (4) Ceremonial. (5) Moral as to just dealing, judicial as to the specific measures.

**P2** *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b))*

**Must hit, strict (a):** Shared: the **distinction between an ox with no known vice and one known to be dangerous**, where the owner is warned ("they warned his master" / "made known his vice"), and liability for the owner who "did not shut him up" / "has not shut up his ox". Both also set a **lower, monetary rate for a slave** ("thirty sicles" / "one-third of a mina"). Differences, any three: (i) **the ox is stoned and its flesh not eaten** in Exodus ("he shall be stoned: and his flesh shall not be eaten"); Hammurabi does nothing to the animal. (ii) **Death for the negligent owner** ("his owner also shall be put to death"), with ransom as an option ("if they set a price upon him"), against a **fine** ("he shall pay half a mina"). (iii) **The unwarned case**: Exodus clears the owner but still stones the ox; Hammurabi's "that case has no remedy". (iv) **Class**: Hammurabi's rate is for "a man of gentle birth"; Exodus says "a man or a woman", and "a son, or a daughter" fall "under the like sentence", with no rank among free persons.

**Must hit, any verdict (b):** State both theses accurately. Wright: direct literary use of Hammurabi, Neo-Assyrian period. The alternative: a shared legal tradition with no textual intermediary. Say which feature of (a) cuts which way. The same sequence and the same key condition (warning, not confining) favour dependence. Changes as systematic as stoning the ox, a capital sentence and dropping class could suit either a revising author or independent development. Say what further evidence would decide it.

**Wrong turns:** listing the slave rate as a difference in principle, when both keep a status distinction. Treating the similarity as settling dependence. Treating either verdict as a question of faith.

**Model answer (b), one of several:** Dependence fits well. The case runs in the same order: a first incident, then a known vice and a warning, then an owner who did not confine the ox, then a lower rate for a slave. The Covenant Code then changes exactly the points a reviser with a theology would change. The animal bears blood-guilt, a human life is not priced by rank, and the negligent owner answers with his life, though ransom is allowed. A shared tradition explains the order just as well, since the goring ox was a stock case of Near Eastern law, and Israel's changes could have grown up over centuries without a text in front of anyone. On this passage alone the order is suggestive but not decisive. Only a run of close verbal and sequential matches across many laws would decide it.

**P3** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict:** (1) **Trent is misreported.** Session VI canon 19 defines that the Ten Commandments *do* bind Christians. It does not define that *only* they bind, or that everything else was ceremonial. (2) **"Everything else was ceremonial" drops the judicial and non-Decalogue moral precepts.** In the tradition's scheme (Aquinas, common teaching, not defined) the moral precepts last for ever, the ceremonial ended with Christ (taught by Florence, 1442), and the judicial lost their binding force. (3) **"Love thy neighbour" is Old Testament law**, Leviticus 19:18, which Jesus quotes. (4) **Hammurabi**: the resemblance is agreed, but direct copying is one live scholarly thesis (Wright), not a proven result. It is a hypothesis with no magisterial standing either way. (Credit also as a fourth error: **"eye for eye" as primitive vengeance**. It limits penalties in law, Exodus drops Hammurabi's class grading, and the rabbis read it as compensation.)

**Wrong turns:** correcting (2) by saying the tripartite scheme is defined dogma, which only swaps one level error for another. Saying Christ "abolished" the moral law. Calling the dependence thesis false; it is disputed, not refuted.

**Model answer:** Trent defined that the Ten Commandments bind Christians, not that nothing else does. The tradition, through Aquinas's division (common teaching), holds that moral precepts last, ceremonial ones ended with Christ (Florence) and judicial ones lost their force. "Love thy neighbour" is Leviticus 19:18. The Hammurabi parallels are real, but copying is Wright's disputed thesis, not something archaeology has proved. "Eye for eye" set a limit on punishment and, in Exodus, one that ignored rank.

## Flashback

**From Lesson [2.4](02-04-the-patriarchs.md) (The patriarchs):** *(Exegetical.)* Two covenants with Abram (Douay–Rheims):

> "And he took all these, and divided them in the midst, and laid the two pieces of each one against the other ... And when the sun was set, there arose a dark mist, and there appeared a smoking furnace, and a lamp of fire passing between those divisions. That day God made a covenant with Abram, saying: To thy seed will I give this land" (Genesis 15:10, 17–18)
>
> "Again God said to Abraham: And thou therefore shalt keep my covenant, and thy seed after thee in their generations. This is my covenant which you shall observe between me and you ... All the male-kind of you shall be circumcised. ... The male whose flesh of his foreskin shall not be circumcised, that soul shall be destroyed out of his people: because he hath broken my covenant." (Genesis 17:9–10, 14)

Jeremiah 34:18 (Douay) threatens the men "that have transgressed my covenant ... when they cut the calf in two and passed between the parts thereof". The Hebrew for making a covenant is literally "to cut" one (*karat berit*). The "destroyed" of Genesis 17:14 renders the same verb: "cut off".

(a) In each chapter, who takes on an obligation, and which words decide it? (b) Use Jeremiah 34:18 to say what passing between the pieces means, and why it matters who passes in Genesis 15. (c) Most source critics assign the two chapters to different sources. Give that claim its status in one line. **Two sentences each for (a) and (b).**

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):** In **Genesis 15, God alone** binds himself. Only the "smoking furnace, and a lamp of fire", figures of God's presence, pass "between those divisions". Abram has fallen into a deep sleep (15:12) and does nothing. The content is a promise: "To thy seed **will I give**". In **Genesis 17, Abraham and his seed** take on an obligation: "thou therefore **shalt keep**", "which you **shall observe**". It has a sign (circumcision) and a sanction: the uncircumcised is "destroyed" (cut off), because "he hath broken my covenant".

**Must hit, strict (b):** passing between the halves is a **self-curse**: whoever passes calls the animals' fate down on himself if he breaks faith, as Jeremiah's covenant-breakers now suffer. In Genesis 15 **only God passes**, so God alone takes the curse on himself, and the promise of land does not rest on Abram's performance.

**Must hit, strict (c):** a **majority scholarly hypothesis** (Gen 17 Priestly, Gen 15 non-Priestly), with no magisterial standing. The *difference* between the chapters is in the text, whatever its source.

**Wrong turns:** reading Genesis 17 as the same covenant told twice. Reading the fire as scenery rather than as the party who passes. Treating (c) as settled, or as Church teaching.

**Model answer:** (a) In Genesis 15 only God acts: the furnace and lamp pass between the pieces while Abram sleeps, and the words are a promise, "To thy seed will I give this land". In Genesis 17 Abraham and his descendants must "keep" and "observe" the covenant through circumcision, on pain of being cut off for having "broken my covenant". (b) Jeremiah shows that whoever walked between the halves invoked the animals' fate on himself if he broke the covenant. Since only God passes in Genesis 15, God alone takes the oath and the curse, and the promise depends on him. (c) A majority source-critical hypothesis, not settled and not Church teaching.

</details>
