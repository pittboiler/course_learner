# The Old Testament · Lesson 4.2: Isaiah and Immanuel

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 4: The prophets · Builds on: [4.1 What a prophet is: Amos and Hosea](04-01-what-a-prophet-is-amos-and-hosea.md), [3.3 Two kingdoms and two falls](03-03-two-kingdoms-and-two-falls.md), [1.3 The historical-critical method and its limits](01-03-the-historical-critical-method-and-its-limits.md) · Unlocks: [4.3 The Servant of the Lord](04-03-the-servant-of-the-lord.md), [6.4 The Old Testament in light of the New](06-04-the-old-testament-in-light-of-the-new.md)

## Why this matters

Isaiah is the prophet the New Testament quotes most, and one verse of it, 7:14, is among the most argued-over sentences in the Bible. Two questions hang on the book. The first is who wrote it: one eighth-century prophet, or a school working across two centuries? The second is what the sign of Immanuel meant to a frightened king in 734 BC, and what Matthew means when he says it was fulfilled. Neither answer is Church teaching in the form usually claimed.

## The idea

Read Isaiah like a family firm's archive. The founder's letters come first (chapters 1–39). Then comes a section in his style and vocabulary, addressed to people living two centuries after his death (40–55), and a last one for a community already back home (56–66). One author, or a founder and heirs? Either way the archive is filed, and meant to be read, as one book.

Isaiah 7 is a miniature of the same problem. A besieged king refuses a sign; the sign is a child whose mother the Hebrew calls an *almah*. Everything turns on that word, the sign's time limit, and whether a sign given to Ahaz can also point beyond him.

## Source

Isaiah 7:10–16 (Douay–Rheims):

> And the Lord spoke again to Achaz, saying: Ask thee a sign of the Lord thy God, either unto the depth of hell, or unto the height above. And Achaz said: I will not ask, and I will not tempt the Lord. And he said: Hear ye therefore, O house of David: Is it a small thing for you to be grievous to men, that you are grievous to my God also? Therefore the Lord himself shall give you a sign. Behold a virgin shall conceive, and bear a son and his name shall be called Emmanuel. He shall eat butter and honey, that he may know to refuse the evil, and to choose the good. For before the child know to refuse the evil and to choose the good, the land which thou abhorrest shall be forsaken of the face of her two kings.

The "you" of verses 13–14 is plural: in Hebrew *lakhem*, "to you all", the house of David. The Douay's "virgin" renders the Hebrew *ha-almah*, with the definite article: *the* young woman. *Harah* is an adjective, "pregnant". It can mean "is pregnant" or "will be pregnant". Verse 16 sets a deadline: before this child can tell good from bad, the lands of the two kings Ahaz fears will be abandoned. *Immanu-el* means "God is with us".

## The argument

1. **The crisis.** Around 734 BC Rezin of Damascus ("Rasin") and Pekah of Israel ("Phacee, son of Romelia") marched on Jerusalem. They meant to put a puppet, "the son of Tabeel", on Judah's throne (7:6), and so force Judah into their coalition against Assyria: this is the [Syro-Ephraimite crisis](../reference.md#syro-ephraimite-crisis). Isaiah's counsel is to trust God and not buy protection, with a pun in 7:9: *ta'aminu ... te'amenu*, "If you will not believe, you shall not continue." Ahaz sent silver and gold to Tiglath-pileser III instead (2 Kings 16:7–9 [Douay 4 Kings]). Assyria took Damascus in 732 and killed Rezin.

2. **The word.** *Almah* means a young woman of marriageable age. It occurs seven times: Genesis 24:43 (Rebekah), Exodus 2:8 (Miriam as a girl), Psalm 68:25 [Douay 67:26], Proverbs 30:19, Song 1:3 and 6:8, and here. It neither specifies nor rules out virginity. The Hebrew for "virgin" is *betulah*, and Genesis 24:16 still has to add "not known to man". Jerome replied, in *Against Jovinianus* I.32, that an *almah* is "a hidden virgin", secluded, and challenged his opponents to find the word used of a married woman. The Greek translators of Isaiah, working in the second or first century BC (reconstructed), chose *parthenos*, and Matthew 1:23 quotes their Greek. *In words:* the Hebrew is open, the Greek is narrower, and Matthew reads the Greek. See [almah and parthenos](../reference.md#almah-and-parthenos).

3. **The readings of the sign in Ahaz's day.**
   - *A royal child.* Trypho, Justin Martyr's Jewish interlocutor (*Dialogue with Trypho* 67, mid-second century), says the text has "young woman" and that the whole prophecy refers to Hezekiah. The reading's strength is the dynasty: 8:8 addresses the land as "thy land, O Emmanuel", and 9:6–7 announce a child on David's throne.
   - *Isaiah's own son.* Rashi (1040–1105) takes the *almah* to be the prophet's wife, who would conceive that year. His support is 8:3–4: the prophet goes in to "the prophetess", a son is born, and the same deadline follows. Before the child can say "father" and "mother", Damascus and Samaria are plundered. Rashi rejects Hezekiah on arithmetic: Ahaz acceded at twenty and reigned sixteen years (2 Kings 16:2), and Hezekiah was twenty-five at his accession (18:2), so he was born nine years *before* Ahaz reigned.
   - *A present or imminent birth.* Many modern critical commentators, Jewish and Christian, read the *almah* as a woman already pregnant, perhaps of the royal house. On this reading the sign lies in the timing: disaster for Damascus and Samaria comes within the child's infancy.

   *In words:* every literal reading ties the sign to Ahaz's own years, because verse 16 does.

4. **The Christian reading.** Matthew 1:22–23 says the virginal conception happened "that it might be fulfilled". Justin, Irenaeus (*Against Heresies* III.21.1) and Jerome defended *parthenos* against Jewish readers and the later Greek versions of Aquila and Theodotion ("young woman"). Modern Catholic exegesis usually holds both together: a literal sense in Ahaz's crisis (David's house will endure) and a fuller or typological fulfilment in Christ (see [sensus plenior](../reference.md#sensus-plenior) and [1.2](01-02-the-senses-of-scripture.md)). The Biblical Commission's 2001 document reads 7:14 among the prophecies that the dynasty would endure (§62). It calls the Christian perception of fulfilment "retrospective" and treats the Jewish reading as a possible one (§§21–22). How Matthew builds his formula quotations is [`new-testament` 2.2](../../new-testament/lessons/02-02-matthew.md).

5. **The book in three parts.** Chapters 40–55 name Cyrus (44:28; 45:1), address exiles in Babylon as present hearers, and never mention Isaiah; his name last appears at 39:8. Chapters 56–66 assume a community back in Judah, arguing over the Temple. On this evidence most critical scholars, including the *New Jerome Biblical Commentary*, assign 40–55 to an anonymous prophet of the exile (c. 550–539, reconstructed). They assign 56–66 to prophets after the return, separated out as "Trito-Isaiah" by Bernhard Duhm's commentary of 1892. The critical case for [the three parts of Isaiah](../reference.md#the-three-parts-of-isaiah) is usually traced to J. C. Döderlein and J. G. Eichhorn in the late eighteenth century.

6. **The case for unity.** The title "the Holy One of Israel" runs through both halves, about two dozen times in all, and is rare outside Isaiah. The Great Isaiah Scroll from Qumran (second century BC, dated by the style of its script) copies all sixty-six chapters as one book. Sirach 48:24 [Douay Ecclesiasticus 48:27], about 180 BC, already credits Isaiah with having "comforted the mourners in Sion", the language of 40:1 and 61:2–3. Conservative scholars (O. T. Allis, *The Unity of Isaiah*, 1950; J. A. Motyer) defend one author and genuine long-range prediction. Others accept several hands but a deliberate literary unity: H. G. M. Williamson (*The Book Called Isaiah*, 1994) argues that the exilic prophet edited the older collection. Brevard Childs and Christopher Seitz read the final form canonically. *In words:* for most current scholars, "three authors" and "one book" are not opposites.

**Mark the levels.** The Biblical Commission's response of June 1908 (dated the 28th in some collections and the 29th in others) answered five questions in the negative. Isaiah's predictions may not be explained away as after-the-fact narration or shrewd guessing, and neither style nor all the arguments together demonstrate more than one author. Pius X's *Praestantia Scripturae* (1907) bound Catholics to such responses when they were issued. Their practical force was then changed by *Divino Afflante Spiritu* (1943), the 1948 letter to Cardinal Suhard and the 1955 statements, and since 1971 the Commission has been consultative ([1.3](01-03-the-historical-critical-method-and-its-limits.md); [the Commission and its weight](../reference.md#the-biblical-commission-and-its-weight)). Today the authorship of 40–66 is **freely disputed**: Second Isaiah is a **scholarly majority position**, not doctrine, and single authorship is a live minority view, not heresy. What still stands at a higher level is the faith point inside response I: that God can reveal the future, and that prophecy is a sign of revelation (Vatican I, *Dei Filius* ch. 3; [`fundamental-theology` 2.2](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/02-02-credibility-and-the-preambles-of-faith.md)). Holding Second Isaiah does not deny it. The **virginal conception** is dogma (rung 1 of [the ladder](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-04-the-ladder-of-doctrinal-authority.md)), resting on Matthew and Luke, and its basis belongs to `ecclesiology-and-mariology` ([syllabus](../../ecclesiology-and-mariology/syllabus.md)). That Isaiah 7:14 is fulfilled in it is taught by CCC 497. What the prophet himself foresaw, and how exactly the fulfilment works, is **freely disputed**. Arguing it against the Jewish reading belongs to [`apologetics-foundations`](../../apologetics-foundations/syllabus.md). See [church teaching vs hypothesis](../reference.md#church-teaching-vs-hypothesis).

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean): Isaiah 45:1.** "Thus saith the Lord to my anointed Cyrus." Literal sense first: the oracle calls Cyrus, whose capture of Babylon is secure at 539 ([3.4](03-04-exile-and-return.md)), God's anointed (*mashiach*), and speaks to him as already on the move. The single-author reading takes this as a prediction by name 150 years ahead; the multiple-author reading, as a prophet of the 540s announcing God's purpose in events under way. Either way the text claims one thing: the pagan king is God's instrument for Israel's return. Authorship changes *when* that was said, not *what* was said.

**Example 2 (hard): three versions of one verse.** Set 7:14 side by side:

| | Hebrew (Masoretic) | Greek Isaiah | Matthew 1:23 |
|---|---|---|---|
| The woman | *ha-almah*, the young woman | *hē parthenos*, the virgin | *hē parthenos* |
| Who names him | *qarat*, she will call | *kaleseis*, you (one person) will call | *kalesousin*, they will call |

The method strains here. Does *parthenos* show the Greek translators expected a miraculous birth? Not by itself. Greek Genesis 34:3 calls Dinah a *parthenos* after Shechem has lain with her. The word could simply mean "girl". Still, *parthenos* is the ordinary Greek for "virgin", and Matthew reads it in light of an event he believes happened. Even the naming shifts. Matthew's "they will call" fits his point that believers will confess this child as "God with us". The Hebrew's literal sense, the Greek's sense and Matthew's reading are three distinct questions; collapsing them is the commonest error on every side.

## Watch out

- **"Catholics must hold that Isaiah wrote all sixty-six chapters."** That turns a 1908 disciplinary response into standing dogma. Authorship is freely disputed; response I's point about real prophecy is what keeps a higher weight.
- **"Second Isaiah is established fact."** It is the majority hypothesis; single authorship has serious defenders.
- **"*Almah* means virgin" or "*almah* means not a virgin."** It means a young woman of marriageable age. The word leaves virginity open either way.
- **"The virginal conception depends on winning the *almah* argument."** The dogma rests on Matthew 1 and Luke 1. The argument concerns how Isaiah 7:14 relates to it.
- **"The Jewish reading was invented to answer Christians."** Trypho gives a Hezekiah reading in the second century. Rashi's reading of the prophet's wife rests on 8:3–4, and he rejects Hezekiah himself. State each as its holders state it ([Jewish readings](../reference.md#jewish-readings)).

## One-liner

> Isaiah is one book on the shelf and, for most scholars, several prophets behind it; Immanuel is a sign with a deadline in Ahaz's reign, which Matthew reads through the Greek *parthenos* as filled full in Christ.

## Flashback

**From Lesson [3.4](03-04-exile-and-return.md) (Exile and return):** *(Exegetical.)* Ezra gives Cyrus's decree twice (Douay–Rheims, 1 Esdras). The first is in Hebrew, proclaimed "throughout all his kingdom" (1:1). The second is in Aramaic, a record found in the archive at Ecbatana (6:2):

> **1:2–4.** Thus saith Cyrus king of the Persians: The Lord the God of heaven hath given to me all the kingdoms of the earth, and he hath charged me to build him a house in Jerusalem ... Who is there among you of all his people? His God be with him. Let him go up to Jerusalem ... and build the house of the Lord the God of Israel: he is the God that is in Jerusalem. And let all the rest ... help him every man from his place, with silver and gold, and goods, and cattle ...
>
> **6:3–5.** Cyrus the king decreed, that the house of God should be built, which is in Jerusalem, in the place where they may offer sacrifices ... the height of threescore cubits, and the breadth of threescore cubits ... and the charges shall be given out of the king's house. And also let the golden and silver vessels of the temple of God ... be restored ...

In 1:2–3, "the Lord" renders the divine name *YHWH*. In 6:3 the Aramaic has only *elaha*, "God".

(a) Name three differences in content between the two versions. (b) Elias Bickerman argued that both are genuine: a herald's proclamation and an archive docket. Say which features of each version fit his labels, and which features lead many scholars to judge 1:2–4 recast in Israel's idiom. (c) Give the status of three things: the question of which version is closer to Cyrus's words, the date 539, and the date 538 for the edict. **Two sentences per part.**

<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):** any three of these.
1. **Return:** 1:3 invites the exiles to "go up". 6:3–5 says nothing about anyone returning; it orders only the building.
2. **Funding:** in 1:4 the neighbours contribute silver, gold and cattle. In 6:4 the costs come "out of the king's house", the royal treasury.
3. **Specifications:** only 6:3–4 gives dimensions and building materials, and defines the site as a place of sacrifice.
4. **The vessels:** 6:5 makes their return part of the decree. Chapter 1 narrates it separately (1:7) and leaves it out of the edict.
5. **Theology:** only 1:2–3 confesses that God gave Cyrus "all the kingdoms of the earth" and "charged" him.

**Must hit, strict (b):** **Proclamation:** 1:2–4 speaks to people: a public call in the second person ("Who is there among you ... let him go up") that also appeals to the neighbours. **Docket:** 6:3–5 addresses no one. It is an administrative record in the third person, with measurements, a budget line and an inventory item, and it was found in an archive. **Recast:** in 1:2–3 a Persian king names Israel's God as *YHWH*, says *YHWH* gave him every kingdom, and confesses "he is the God that is in Jerusalem". That is Israel's own confession in Cyrus's mouth. The Aramaic memo has only a generic "God". Credit also: the memo is in Aramaic, the empire's administrative language, and the proclamation is in Hebrew.
**Must hit, strict (c):** **Which version is closer to Cyrus's words** is a historical question with **no magisterial level**. It is debated: Bickerman accepts both, and many scholars accept the memo in substance. **539** is **secure**. **538** ("the first year of Cyrus", 1:1) is **reconstructed**, and the edict's wording is debated.
**Wrong turns:** treating the two versions as a contradiction that one must lose, when on Bickerman's view they are two genres recording one policy. Citing the Cyrus Cylinder as the text of either version, although it never mentions Jerusalem. Saying that because Ezra is canonical (defined), the Church has settled the edict's exact wording.
**Model answer:** (a) The Hebrew edict lets the exiles go home and asks their neighbours to fund them. The Aramaic memo mentions no return, charges the costs to the royal treasury, and adds the Temple's dimensions and the return of the vessels. (b) The Hebrew addresses a public ("Who is there among you ... let him go up"), as a herald would, and the Aramaic is a filed record of specifications and costs. What marks the Hebrew as recast is a Persian king confessing that *YHWH* gave him every kingdom and "is the God that is in Jerusalem", where the memo says only "God". (c) Which version is nearer Cyrus's words is a debated historical question with no magisterial level. The year 539 is secure; 538 is reconstructed.
