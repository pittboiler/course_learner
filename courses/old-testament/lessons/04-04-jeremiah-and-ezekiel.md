# The Old Testament · Lesson 4.4: Jeremiah and Ezekiel

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 4: The prophets · Builds on: [4.1 What a prophet is](04-01-what-a-prophet-is-amos-and-hosea.md), [3.3 Two kingdoms and two falls](03-03-two-kingdoms-and-two-falls.md), [1.1 What the Old Testament is](01-01-what-the-old-testament-is.md) · Unlocks: [4.5 The Book of the Twelve](04-05-the-book-of-the-twelve.md), [6.4 The Old Testament in light of the New](06-04-the-old-testament-in-light-of-the-new.md)

## Why this matters

The phrase "New Testament" comes from one oracle of Jeremiah, the only place the Old Testament says "new covenant". Jeremiah preached inside Jerusalem as it fell; Ezekiel preached to the exiles in Babylon. Between them they give the Church its vocabulary for interior renewal: the law written on the heart, the new heart, the dry bones that live. And Jeremiah survives in two editions of different length, the Bible's clearest case of a text that grew.

## The idea

Both prophets make two moves.

- **Tear down the guarantee.** Judah believed God could not let his Temple fall. Jeremiah calls that a lie; in Ezekiel's vision God's glory leaves the Temple before the Babylonians arrive.
- **Promise what Sinai could not produce.** The old covenant failed because the people broke it, so both look past new rules to a changed heart that God himself gives.

Underneath runs a third thread. After the king burned Jeremiah's scroll, Baruch rewrote it, and "there were added besides many more words than had been before" (36:32, Douay-Rheims). The book reports its own growth; the manuscripts confirm it.

## Source

Jeremiah 7:2–4, 8–11, 14 (Douay-Rheims), the Temple sermon. Jeremiah 26 tells the same event as a story and dates it "in the beginning of the reign of Joakim" (Jehoiakim; 26:1), that is about 609/608 BC:

> Stand in the gate of the house of the Lord, and proclaim there this word ... Thus saith the Lord of hosts the God of Israel: Make your ways and your doings good: and I will dwell with you in this place. Trust not in lying words, saying: The temple of the Lord, the temple of the Lord, it is the temple of the Lord. ... Behold you put your trust in lying words, which shall not profit you: To steal, to murder, to commit adultery, to swear falsely, to offer to Baalim ... And you have come, and stood before me in this house ... and have said: We are delivered ... Is this house then, in which my name hath been called upon, in your eyes become a den of robbers? ... I will do to this house ... as I did to Silo.

Read what decides it. God's promise to "dwell with you in this place" is **conditional**: *if* the people amend their ways (7:5–7). The threefold "temple of the Lord" (Hebrew *hekal YHWH*) is a slogan repeated like a charm. Shiloh (Douay "Silo"), where the ark once stood, is a sanctuary God has already let fall.

## The argument

1. **Two prophets, one catastrophe.** Jeremiah's call is dated to Josiah's thirteenth year (Jer 1:2), about 627 BC: reconstructed from regnal years, tight to a year or so. William Holladay proposed reading 627 as the prophet's *birth* year (compare 1:5), a live minority view. Ezekiel, a priest, was deported with King Jehoiachin in 597 (secure; the Babylonian Chronicle records the capture of Jerusalem) and called "the fifth year of the captivity" (Ezek 1:2), about 593. Jerusalem fell in 587/586 (secure; [3.3](03-03-two-kingdoms-and-two-falls.md)).

2. **The Temple sermon.** In its setting, Jer 7 attacks a theology, not the Temple: the idea that the sanctuary guarantees safety whatever the people do. Jeremiah 26 records the sequel. Priests and prophets demand his death; elders save him by recalling that Micah had foretold Zion ploughed like a field (26:18; Mic 3:12). Jesus quotes 7:11 while clearing the Temple (Matt 21:13, "a den of thieves" in the Douay): Jeremiah's words at Jeremiah's site.

3. **The confessions.** Scholars group first-person laments as Jeremiah's "confessions", usually 11:18–12:6; 15:10–21; 17:14–18; 18:18–23; 20:7–18 (counts vary). The rawest opens: "Thou hast deceived me, O Lord, and I am deceived" (20:7). The verb, *pittitani* from *patah*, means "you enticed me"; it is what Delilah is told to do to Samson (Judg 16:5). Whether these are the prophet's own voice or liturgical laments spoken for the community is disputed; Henning Graf Reventlow (1963) argued the latter.

4. **The [new covenant](../reference.md#the-new-covenant) (31:31–34).** A covenant unlike Sinai's, "which they made void", with the law put in their inward parts (Hebrew *qereb*) and written on their heart (*lev*). Exactly what is new is P2's question. One word to watch: the law is still "my law" (*torati*). The Biblical Commission's 2001 document *The Jewish People and their Sacred Scriptures in the Christian Bible* (§39) reads it so: not a change of law but a new, interior relationship to it (its word is "interiorization"). It notes that Qumran's Damascus Document already speaks of a "new covenant", and that Ezekiel develops the promise.

   In the **Jewish reading**, the oracle promises the renewal of God's covenant with Israel and Judah, the Torah itself inscribed within them. The **Christian reading** begins in the New Testament: "the new covenant in my blood" (Luke 22:20; 1 Cor 11:25), and Hebrews 8, which quotes the oracle from the Greek (where it is chapter 38) and infers that "new" makes the first covenant old ([`new-testament` 6.2](../../new-testament/lessons/06-02-hebrews.md)). Augustine reads the writing on the heart as the Spirit's gift:

   > What then is God's law written by God Himself in the hearts of men, but the very presence of the Holy Spirit, who is "the finger of God," and by whose presence is shed abroad in our hearts the love which is the fulfilling of the law ... (*On the Spirit and the Letter* 36, NPNF I/5)

5. **Ezekiel: the glory moves.** The inaugural vision by the river Chebar (Douay "Chobar", 1:1) shows God's glory on a throne borne by living creatures and wheels: mobile, and appearing in Babylon. (Hebrew 1:28b, "This was the vision of the likeness of the glory of the Lord", is Douay 2:1.) In chapters 8–11 the glory leaves the defiled Temple by the east gate (10:18–19) and "stood over the mount that is on the east side" (11:23). It returns by that gate only in the vision of the restored Temple (43:2–5). *In words:* God left before Babylon destroyed; Jeremiah's point, seen from the other side.

6. **[Individual responsibility](../reference.md#individual-responsibility-in-ezekiel-18) (Ezek 18).** The exiles' proverb, "The fathers have eaten sour grapes, and the teeth of the children are set on edge" (18:2), means *we pay for our parents' sins*. Ezekiel abolishes it: "the soul that sinneth, the same shall die" (18:4); Jeremiah 31:29–30 does the same for the days to come. "Soul" is *nefesh*, the living person. The chapter is addressed to the "house of Israel" (18:30–31): a summons to repent now, not modern individualism. The Talmud puts the Jewish reading sharply: R. Yose bar Hanina counts it among the words of Moses a later prophet revoked, Ezekiel 18:4 replacing the visiting of the fathers' iniquity on the children (Exod 20:5; *Makkot* 24a, paraphrased).

7. **The dry bones (Ezek 37:1–14).** The vision interprets itself: "All these bones are the house of Israel", saying "our hope is lost" (37:11). The literal sense is **national restoration** from exile, told through *ruach*, which means breath, wind and spirit ("put my spirit in you, and you shall live", 37:14). Irenaeus reads it as a promise of **bodily resurrection** (*Against Heresies* V.15.1). The Talmud (*Sanhedrin* 92b) records rabbis who took the raising literally and one who called it truth and parable at once. Explicit individual resurrection comes later, in Daniel 12:2 ([6.3](06-03-daniel-and-apocalyptic.md)).

8. **[Two editions of Jeremiah](../reference.md#the-two-editions-of-jeremiah).** The Septuagint of Jeremiah is roughly one-eighth shorter than the Masoretic text (some 2,700 words; some counts say one-seventh). It places the oracles against the nations after 25:13, in a different order; the Hebrew has them at the end (46–51). It lacks 33:14–26 (the Branch promise, echoing 23:5–6) and 10:6–8, 10. Two Hebrew fragments from Qumran, 4QJer<sup>b</sup> and 4QJer<sup>d</sup>, share the short text and the Greek arrangement; 4QJer<sup>a</sup> and <sup>c</sup> match the Masoretic text. Most text critics, following J. Gerald Janzen and Emanuel Tov, conclude that the Greek faithfully translates an **earlier, shorter Hebrew edition**, expanded in the Masoretic text. A minority, Jack Lundbom among them, explains much of the shortfall as scribal omission. *In words:* two Hebrew Jeremiahs coexisted before Christ.

**Mark the levels.** That Jeremiah and Ezekiel are canonical is **defined dogma, rung 1** ([the ladder](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-04-the-ladder-of-doctrinal-authority.md)): Trent received the books "entire with all their parts" as in the Vulgate ([`fundamental-theology` 3.3](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/03-03-the-canon-as-defined.md)), which follows the longer Hebrew. **Which edition is earlier has no magisterial level.** *Divino Afflante Spiritu* (1943) calls the Vulgate's authenticity juridical, not critical (§21), and urges recourse to the original texts (§16). That the new covenant is fulfilled in Christ is the New Testament's own assertion (Luke 22:20; Heb 8), taught by the Church (CCC 64, 580; the Catechism carries the weight of what it summarizes). The Commission's 2001 reading is **consultative, not a magisterial act**. Holladay, Reventlow and Lundbom hold **scholarly positions** ([church teaching vs hypothesis](../reference.md#church-teaching-vs-hypothesis)). 597 and 587/586 are secure; 627 is reconstructed; 609/608 for the sermon rests on Jer 26:1 ([dating discipline](../reference.md#dating-discipline)).

## The synopsis: Jeremiah in two editions

| Feature | Masoretic text (Hebrew; Vulgate, Douay) | Septuagint (Greek) |
|---|---|---|
| Length | Longer | Roughly one-eighth to one-seventh shorter |
| Oracles against the nations | Chapters 46–51, ending with Babylon | After 25:13, in a different order |
| New covenant | 31:31–34 | Chapter 38 (Hebrews 8 quotes this) |
| 33:14–26 (David's Branch, Levites) | Present | Absent |
| 10:6–8, 10 (hymn to God's greatness) | Present | Absent |
| Hebrew support at Qumran | 4QJer<sup>a</sup>, 4QJer<sup>c</sup> | 4QJer<sup>b</sup>, 4QJer<sup>d</sup> |

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean): Ezekiel 10–11, literal sense then spiritual.** *[Literal sense](../reference.md#literal-sense):* a priest in exile explains the catastrophe. The Temple fell because God had already left it, driven out by the idolatry of chapter 8: God judging, not God defeated. *Spiritual reading:* the glory that leaves by the east returns by the east (43:2–5). John says "we saw his glory" of the Word who "dwelt among us" (John 1:14) and calls Jesus' body a temple (2:19–21), so the tradition reads the returning glory typologically, as God's presence dwelling in Christ. Keep them distinct: Ezekiel's claim concerns a building in 592 BC and is complete without John.

**Example 2 (hard): is Jeremiah 33:14–26 Scripture?** It is absent from the Greek and from the edition 4QJer<sup>b</sup> and <sup>d</sup> attest; on the majority view it was added to the Hebrew later. Yet it stands in the Masoretic text, the Vulgate and the Douay, and Trent received the books "with all their parts". A Catholic may hold, as a text critic, that it is post-Jeremian: inspiration does not require single authorship or a first draft ([`fundamental-theology` 3.1](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/03-01-inspiration.md)). The strain is elsewhere. Was the shorter Greek, read by the apostolic Church, then a *defective* Scripture? Augustine refused that conclusion:

> For the same Spirit who was in the prophets when they spoke these things was also in the seventy men when they translated them ... (*City of God* XVIII.43, NPNF I/2)

What the Hebrew alone has, he says, the Spirit chose to say through the prophets only. That is a theological proposal about two editions, not a defined answer.

## Watch out

- **Hypothesis stated as teaching:** "The Church teaches the Masoretic text is the original Jeremiah." It teaches that the book is canonical; which edition came first is text criticism, and the evidence currently favours the shorter.
- **Teaching stated as hypothesis:** "Since 33:14–26 may be late, it is not really Scripture." Canonicity is defined of the books with their parts; lateness concerns authorship.
- **"Ezekiel 18 invented individualism."** It is about who bears the exile's punishment, addressed to the nation.
- **"Jeremiah was against the Temple."** He attacked trust in it as a guarantee (7:5–7), not worship.
- **"The new covenant replaces the law."** Jeremiah says "my law", written within. Hebrews' inference about the old covenant's end is the New Testament's reading, not the oracle's literal sense.

## One-liner

> Jeremiah and Ezekiel knock out the false guarantee (the Temple, the fathers' sins) and promise what Sinai never produced: the same law written on a new heart by God's own spirit, in a book that itself survives in two editions.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢)** *(Exegetical)* Close reading, Ezekiel 18:1–4, 19–20 (Douay-Rheims):

> And the word of the Lord came to me, saying: What is the meaning? That you use among you this parable as a proverb in the land of Israel, saying: The fathers have eaten sour grapes, and the teeth of the children are set on edge. As I live, saith the Lord God, this parable shall be no more to you a proverb in Israel. Behold all souls are mine: as the soul of the father, so also the soul of the son is mine: the soul that sinneth, the same shall die. ... And you say: Why hath not the son borne the iniquity of his father? Verily, because the son hath wrought judgment and justice, hath kept all my commandments, and done them, living, he shall live. The soul that sinneth, the same shall die: the son shall not bear the iniquity of the father, and the father shall not bear the iniquity of the son ...

(a) What does the proverb claim, and which words of the oracle replace it with what principle? (b) Someone reads verse 4 as a proof text for the soul's survival after death. Say what "soul" (*nefesh*) means here and what "die" refers to, and give the words that decide it. (c) What complaint does verse 19 put in the hearers' mouths, and what does it show about how they had understood the proverb? **Two sentences per part.**

**P2 (🟡)** *(Exegetical)* Close reading, Jeremiah 31:31–34 (Douay-Rheims):

> Behold the days shall come, saith the Lord, and I will make a new covenant with the house of Israel, and with the house of Juda: Not according to the covenant which I made with their fathers, in the day that I took them by the hand to bring them out of the land of Egypt, the covenant which they made void, and I had dominion over them, saith the Lord. But this shall be the covenant that I will make with the house of Israel, after those days, saith the Lord: I will give my law in their bowels, and I will write it in their heart: and I will be their God, and they shall be my people. And they shall teach no more every man his neighbour, and every man his brother, saying: Know the Lord: for all shall know me from the least of them even to the greatest, saith the Lord: for I will forgive their iniquity, and I will remember their sin no more.

(a) List what the oracle says is **new**, and what it keeps **the same**, citing the words for each. (b) State the Jewish reading of the oracle and the reading of Hebrews 8, each as its own interpreters would state it, and name the words each leans on. **Two sentences per reading in (b); a list for (a).**

**P3 (🔴)** *(Exegetical)* An **invented** study-Bible note on Jeremiah:

> "The Greek Jeremiah is a corrupt text. Its careless translators dropped an eighth of the prophet's words, including the promise of the righteous Branch in 33:14–26, and scrambled the oracles against the nations. The Dead Sea Scrolls have now proved that our Hebrew text is the original, and this is why the Church has always followed the Hebrew alone."

Name **three** errors and correct each from this lesson. Then say what, in this area, *is* Church teaching and at what level. **150 words or fewer.**

<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** the proverb claims that **children suffer for their parents' sins**: the exile generation is paying for its fathers' sins. God abolishes it ("this parable shall be no more to you a proverb") and puts in its place **"the soul that sinneth, the same shall die"**, grounded in "all souls are mine". Each person answers for his own sin, "the son shall not bear the iniquity of the father", and the reverse.

**Must hit, strict (b):** *nefesh* is the **living person or life**, so "the soul that sinneth" means "the person who sins". "Die" is **death as judgment** (in context, perishing under the catastrophe), opposed to "living, he shall live". The pairing of "soul" with "father" and "son", and the contrast of dying with living, decide it. The verse is about who bears guilt, not about what survives death.

**Must hit, strict (c):** the hearers ask why the son should *not* bear his father's guilt. So they had taken inherited guilt as **just, or as God's settled way** (it echoes Exod 20:5), and the oracle must overturn an expectation, not just a complaint. (Credit for noting the proverb's fatalism: if the fathers caused it, repentance is pointless, which 18:30–32 answers.)

**Wrong turns:** reading "soul" as the immortal soul (the Greek-philosophical sense) or "die" as damnation. Calling the chapter an invention of modern individualism: it is addressed to the "house of Israel" as a call to repent.

**Model answer:** (a) The proverb says the children pay for their fathers' sins. God retires it and replaces it with "the soul that sinneth, the same shall die": each person answers for his own sin, since every life belongs to God. (b) *Nefesh* here means the living person, and "die" means perishing under judgment, set against "living, he shall live". The verse decides who bears guilt, not whether a soul survives death. (c) They ask why the son should not bear his father's iniquity. That shows they had taken inherited guilt as how God's justice works, which the oracle overturns.

**P2** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** **New:** a covenant "not according to" the Exodus covenant "which they made void"; the law placed "in their bowels" and written "in their heart" (interior, not on stone); no more need to teach "Know the Lord", "for all shall know me from the least ... to the greatest"; forgiveness, "I will ... remember their sin no more". **Same:** the partners ("the house of Israel, and ... the house of Juda"); the law itself ("**my** law", *torati*); the covenant formula ("I will be their God, and they shall be my people"); God's initiative.

**Must hit, strict (b):** **Jewish reading:** a promise to Israel and Judah that God will renew his covenant after its breach, with the **same Torah** written on the heart so that it is kept. It leans on "house of Israel ... house of Juda", "my law", and the unchanged covenant formula. **Hebrews 8:** the oracle shows the first covenant was faulty and is fulfilled in Christ, the mediator of a better covenant. It leans on "**new**", from which 8:13 infers that the first is "made old" and "near its end", and on the promise of forgiveness (taken up in 10:16–18). Each stated without refuting the other.

**Wrong turns:** listing "a new law" as new. The text says "my law". Stating the Jewish reading as "denial of Jesus" rather than on its own terms. Attributing Hebrews' inference (8:13) to Jeremiah.

**Model answer:** (a) New: a covenant unlike Sinai's broken one, the law inside and on the heart, knowledge of God without teaching, and sins forgotten. Same: the parties, "my law", and "I will be their God, and they shall be my people". (b) Jewish interpreters read a renewal of God's covenant with Israel and Judah, with the Torah itself inscribed within, leaning on the named houses and on "my law". Hebrews reads the oracle as announcing Christ's better covenant. It leans on the word "new" to infer that the first is growing old, and on the promise to forget sins.

**P3** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict:** any three of these errors, each with its correction.
- **"Careless translators dropped" the words.** 4QJer<sup>b</sup> and 4QJer<sup>d</sup> show a **shorter Hebrew edition** with the Greek's arrangement. The majority (Janzen, Tov) holds that the Greek faithfully translates it. Scribal omission is a minority view (Lundbom), and "careless abridgement" is not the evidence's verdict.
- **"The Scrolls proved the Hebrew original."** Qumran has **both** text types (4QJer<sup>a</sup>, <sup>c</sup> match the Masoretic text). The Scrolls show two editions coexisting, and if anything they favour the shorter as earlier.
- **"Scrambled."** The Greek order is attested in Hebrew (4QJer<sup>b</sup> in ch. 10), so it is a different edition's arrangement.
- **"The Church has always followed the Hebrew alone."** The Greek-speaking Church read the Septuagint, and Augustine held both inspired (*City of God* XVIII.43). Trent made the **Latin Vulgate** authentic, and *Divino Afflante Spiritu* §21 calls that authenticity juridical, not critical.

**Teaching and level:** Jeremiah is canonical, "with all their parts" as in the Vulgate: **defined dogma, rung 1** (Trent). Which edition is earlier has **no magisterial level**; it is text criticism.

**Wrong turns:** calling the Masoretic text "the Church's text" by definition. Swinging to "the Septuagint is the true Jeremiah", which overstates a majority text-critical view. Saying 33:14–26 is non-canonical because it may be late.

**Model answer:** Two Qumran fragments, 4QJer<sup>b</sup> and <sup>d</sup>, preserve a shorter Hebrew Jeremiah with the Greek's arrangement, so most scholars think the Greek translates an earlier edition rather than abridging carelessly. The Scrolls also contain the longer text, so they show two editions side by side and prove no single "original". The Church did not follow the Hebrew alone: the Greek churches read the Septuagint, Augustine held it inspired, and Trent authorized the Latin Vulgate, whose authority *Divino Afflante Spiritu* calls juridical, not critical. What is defined is that Jeremiah, with its parts, is canonical (rung 1). Which edition came first is a text-critical question with no magisterial level.

## Flashback

**From Lesson [4.2](04-02-isaiah-and-immanuel.md) (Isaiah and Immanuel):** *(Exegetical.)* The seam between the two halves of Isaiah (Douay–Rheims):

> And Isaias said to Ezechias: Hear the word of the Lord of hosts. Behold the days shall come that all that is in thy house, and that thy fathers have laid up in store until this day, shall be carried away into Babylon: there shall not any thing be left, saith the Lord. ... And Ezechias said to Isaias: The word of the Lord, which he hath spoken, is good. And he said: Only let peace and truth be in my days. (39:5–6, 8)
>
> Be comforted, be comforted, my people, saith your God. Speak ye to the heart of Jerusalem, and call to her: for her evil is come to an end, her iniquity is forgiven: she hath received of the hand of the Lord double for all her sins. (40:1–2)

In 40:2 "her evil" renders Hebrew *tseva'ah*, "her term of hard service". Its three verbs ("is come to an end", "is forgiven", "hath received") are Hebrew perfects, the form normally used for completed action.

Two positions, paraphrased. **The majority:** 40:1–2 speaks to people for whom the Babylonian captivity is present and nearly over, so the speaker is an anonymous prophet of the exile, c. 550–539 BC (reconstructed). **The single-author view** (O. T. Allis, J. A. Motyer): 39:6 shows Isaiah foreseeing the exile, and chapter 40 continues his prophecy, addressed to a generation still to come.

(a) Name the single premise on which the verdict actually turns. (b) Name one piece of evidence from elsewhere in the book that each side would bring to move the other. (c) A reader says: "The real question is whether God can reveal the future." Say why that is not the crux. **Two sentences each for (a) and (b); one sentence for (c).**

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):** the crux is **whether a prophet addresses a far-future generation as his present audience**, that is, whether the hearers of 40:1–2 locate the speaker. The majority holds that prophets speak to their own contemporaries, forthtelling before foretelling ([4.1](04-01-what-a-prophet-is-amos-and-hosea.md)), so an audience at the end of the exile means a speaker there. The single-author view holds that Isaiah, having foreseen the exile in 39:6, can be carried forward to speak to its survivors as if present. Credit: the perfects do not settle it alone, since Hebrew prophecy can put a future event seen as certain in the perfect (the grammarians' "prophetic perfect").

**Must hit, strict (b):** **Majority:** Cyrus named and addressed as already on the move (44:28; 45:1); Isaiah's name absent after 39:8; chapters 56–66 assuming a community back in Judah. **Single author:** "the Holy One of Israel" running through both halves; the Great Isaiah Scroll copying all sixty-six chapters as one book; Sirach 48:24 [Douay Ecclesiasticus 48:27] crediting Isaiah with comforting the mourners in Sion. One from each side.

**Must hit, strict (c):** both sides can grant that God reveals the future. Holding an exilic author for 40–55 does not deny the faith point of *Dei Filius* ch. 3 or of the 1908 response's first answer. The dispute is historical and literary (who is addressed, and when), and authorship stays **freely disputed**.

**Wrong turns:** treating the perfect tense as proof of an exilic date. Treating 39:6 as settling it: it foretells deportation, not the comfort and return of chapter 40. Offering the Qumran scroll or Sirach as proof of authorship: they show how the book was copied and read by the second century BC, which fits both views. Framing the majority as "denying prophecy".

**Model answer:** (a) The verdict turns on whether the audience of 40:1–2 is the speaker's own. The majority says a prophet speaks to his contemporaries, so the speaker stands at the end of the exile, and the single-author view says Isaiah, having foreseen the exile, speaks ahead to its survivors. (b) The majority points to Cyrus named in 44:28 and 45:1 as a figure already in motion. The single-author side points to "the Holy One of Israel" running through both halves. (c) Both sides can grant that God reveals the future, so the dispute is over who is addressed and when, which is freely disputed history, not the faith point of *Dei Filius*.

</details>
