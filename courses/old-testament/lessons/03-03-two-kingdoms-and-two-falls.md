# The Old Testament · Lesson 3.3: Two kingdoms and two falls

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 3: From conquest to return · Builds on: [3.2 David and the house of David](03-02-david-and-the-house-of-david.md), [2.2 Who wrote the Pentateuch?](02-02-who-wrote-the-pentateuch.md), [1.3 The historical-critical method and its limits](01-03-the-historical-critical-method-and-its-limits.md) · Unlocks: [3.4 Exile and return](03-04-exile-and-return.md), [4.1 What a prophet is](04-01-what-a-prophet-is-amos-and-hosea.md), [4.4 Jeremiah and Ezekiel](04-04-jeremiah-and-ezekiel.md)

## Why this matters

Between Solomon's death and the burning of the Temple lie about three and a half centuries, two kingdoms, some forty kings and two catastrophes. This is the first stretch of the Old Testament where outside records check the Bible's story at several points, including Assyrian stone monuments, a Moabite king's boast and Babylonian chronicles. The Bible also tells the story twice. Kings (DR 3–4 Kings) tells it to explain a disaster, and Chronicles (DR 1–2 Paralipomenon) retells it to rebuild a community. Side by side, they show what a biblical "history" is for.

## The idea

Kings is not a court archive. It is a **verdict**. Each reign gets a formula: accession, length, mother's name (for Judah), and a grade. The grade is "he did evil in the sight of the Lord" or, rarely, "he did that which was right". Every northern king fails, for keeping to "the sins of Jeroboam", who set up rival shrines at Bethel and Dan (1 Kings 12:28–30, DR 3 Kings). Omri founded a dynasty that Assyria still named a century later, and Kings gives him eight verses and a failing grade.

So the book answers one question, the question of the exiles: *why did God let both kingdoms fall?* Its answer is covenant infidelity, above all worship outside Jerusalem and of other gods. That is the standard of Deuteronomy, which is why scholars call Joshua–Kings the [Deuteronomistic History](../reference.md#deuteronomistic-history) (Noth's thesis, from [3.2](03-02-david-and-the-house-of-david.md)).

Chronicles, written later for the returned community, tells the same history from Judah's side. It keeps the Temple and the Levites at the centre and has a sharper rule of reward and punishment. It is not a correction slip for Kings. It is a [second history](../reference.md#chronicles-as-a-second-history) with its own purpose.

## Source

2 Kings 23:25–27 (DR 4 Kings), Douay–Rheims (Challoner). Josiah has just purged the Temple and kept the Passover:

> "There was no king before him like unto him, that returned to the Lord with all his heart, and with all his soul, and with all his strength, according to all the law of Moses: neither after him did there arise any like unto him. But yet the Lord turned not away from the wrath of his great indignation, wherewith his anger was kindled against Juda: because of the provocations, wherewith Manasses had provoked him. And the Lord said: I will remove Juda also from before my face, as I have removed Israel: and I will cast off this city Jerusalem, which I chose, and the house, of which I said: My name shall be there."

Read what it holds together. Josiah is praised in the words of the Shema (Deut 6:5, "heart ... soul ... strength"), the highest grade Kings gives anyone. Yet the verdict stands, and it rests on a sin two reigns back. Here Kings' own scheme strains. Many scholars read the strain as a seam: a first edition celebrating Josiah, extended after 586 to explain why his reform failed (F. M. Cross's "double redaction", in *Canaanite Myth and Hebrew Epic*, 1973). Others, following Noth, read one exilic author. That dispute stays open.

## The argument

The story in six steps, with the outside evidence where it exists.

1. **The division.** At Shechem the northern tribes ask Rehoboam to lighten Solomon's forced labour. He refuses, and they answer "What portion have we in David?" (1 Kings 12:16). Jeroboam rules the north (Israel), and Rehoboam keeps Judah with Benjamin. Jeroboam fears that pilgrimage to Jerusalem will return his people's hearts to David's house (12:26–27), so he sets up golden calves at Bethel and Dan: "Behold thy gods, O Israel" (12:28). *In words:* the political split becomes a cultic one, and Kings judges every northern king by it. The date is **reconstructed**, about 930 BC.

2. **Omri and Ahab in the records.** Kings dismisses Omri. Outsiders did not. The **Mesha stele** (found at Dhiban in 1868) has Moab's king say that Omri, king of Israel, oppressed Moab many days. Shalmaneser III's **Kurkh monolith** lists "Ahab the Israelite" with 2,000 chariots and 10,000 soldiers in the coalition that met Assyria at **Qarqar in 853 BC**. That date is **secure**, because the Assyrian year-lists are pinned by an eclipse in 763. Kings never mentions Qarqar. *In words:* the book selects by theology, and the omission shows it. Elijah and Elisha (1 Kings 17 to 2 Kings 13) begin under this dynasty. Elijah on Carmel (1 Kings 18) is the covenant's prosecutor, a role [4.1](04-01-what-a-prophet-is-amos-and-hosea.md) takes up.

3. **The fall of Samaria, 722 BC (secure, within a year).** 2 Kings 17:3–6 names Shalmaneser V as besieger for three years, then says only that "the king of the Assyrians took Samaria". Sargon II's inscriptions claim the capture and a mass deportation. Whether Shalmaneser took the city and Sargon claimed it, or Sargon finished it, is **disputed**. Kings then preaches on why (17:7–23). *In words:* the event is secure, and the credit for it is not.

4. **Sennacherib, 701 BC (secure).** Kings says Sennacherib took Judah's fortified cities and Hezekiah paid tribute (2 Kings 18:13–16). Then an angel struck the Assyrian camp and Sennacherib went home (19:35–36). Sennacherib's annals, on clay prisms, claim forty-six walled towns and Hezekiah shut up in Jerusalem like a caged bird. They record tribute and no capture of the city. *In words:* both sources agree that Jerusalem was not taken. They disagree about why, and each tells it for its own god.

5. **Josiah's book.** In Josiah's eighteenth year (**reconstructed**, 622 BC) the high priest Hilkiah finds "the book of the law" during Temple repairs (2 Kings 22:8). Josiah centralizes all worship in Jerusalem, destroys the high places and Bethel's altar, and keeps a Passover like no other (23:4–23). Those are Deuteronomy's distinctive demands (Deut 12; 16:1–8). Identifying the book as Deuteronomy is old: Jerome calls it Deuteronomy in passing (*Against Jovinianus* I), and Challoner's note on 22:8 reads "That is, Deuteronomy." What changed in **1805** was W. M. L. de Wette's Jena dissertation. He argued that Deuteronomy differs from the rest of the Pentateuch and was *composed* late, near the time it was found. Most critics still date a core of Deuteronomy to the seventh century, a **disputed** majority view; composition *for the occasion* is contested more sharply ([2.2](02-02-who-wrote-the-pentateuch.md) places both in the Pentateuch debate). Josiah dies at Megiddo in **609** (secure within a year) confronting Pharaoh Necho.

6. **The fall of Jerusalem, 587 or 586 BC.** The Babylonian Chronicle dates Nebuchadnezzar's first capture of Jerusalem to March **597**. It breaks off before the second siege. The Temple's burning, "the nineteenth year of the king of Babylon" (2 Kings 25:8), is computed from 597 using regnal years. Counting accession years and the new year differently gives **587 or 586**. The event is **secure**, and the one-year dispute is real. Lamentations mourns it: "How doth the city sit solitary that was full of people!" (Lam 1:1). Its first four chapters are acrostics on the Hebrew alphabet. The Hebrew text names no author. The Greek adds a preface naming Jeremiah, which Challoner's note says Jeremiah did not write.

**Mark the levels.** That Kings and Chronicles are inspired is **defined dogma** (Vatican I, restated in Dei Verbum 11). That they teach without error what God willed for our salvation is Church teaching at the highest weight, though its *scope* is freely disputed ([inerrancy](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/03-02-inerrancy.md)). Dei Verbum 12 tells the reader to find the author's meaning through the [literal sense](../reference.md#literal-sense) and its genre. The Deuteronomistic History, Cross's two editions, Deuteronomy's seventh-century date, and whether Chronicles had sources beyond Samuel–Kings are all **scholarly hypotheses** with no magisterial standing ([Church teaching vs hypothesis](../reference.md#church-teaching-vs-hypothesis)). The early Biblical Commission bears on these books directly. Its responses of 13 February 1905 (on "implicit quotations" of non-inspired documents) and 23 June 1905 (on narratives "historical only in appearance") allowed such readings only when proved by solid arguments. They were binding when issued. The 1948 Suhard letter named the June 1905 response among those that are "in no way a hindrance" to further scientific study. The Commission has been consultative since 1971. Weigh them with [`fundamental-theology` 4.5](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-05-reading-a-documents-weight.md) and [the Commission's weight](../reference.md#the-biblical-commission-and-its-weight), neither as live bans nor as void. Dates follow the [dating discipline](../reference.md#dating-discipline).

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean): Chronicles and David.** Put 2 Samuel 11 beside 1 Chronicles 20:1 (DR 2 Kings / 1 Paralipomenon). Samuel's chapter begins with Joab besieging Rabbah while "David stayed at Jerusalem", and goes on to Bathsheba and Uriah. The Chronicler copies the opening almost word for word, "But David stayed at Jerusalem", and then goes straight to Rabbah's fall. Bathsheba, Uriah and Nathan's rebuke are gone. Method: first ask what the Chronicler's purpose predicts. His David is the founder of the Temple's worship, the ideal king for a community rebuilding that worship. He assumes his readers know Samuel. He omits what Samuel already told them; he does not deny it. *Paralipomenon*, the Greek title, means "things left out", but the ancient translators thought the book *supplied* omissions. It also makes them.

**Example 2 (hard): Josiah's death.** Kings, 2 Kings 23:29: Necho went up to the Euphrates, "and king Josias went to meet him: and was slain at Mageddo." There is no reason given. The most faithful king in the book dies young, and the book's scheme of reward says nothing. The Chronicler supplies a cause (2 Chronicles 35:21–22, DR 2 Paralipomenon). Necho warns Josiah that God sent him, and Josiah "hearkened not to the words of Nechao from the mouth of God". Now the death follows a disobedience. The strain is this: did the Chronicler know a tradition Kings omitted, or did his theology of immediate retribution demand a cause? Both are argued. What the method rules out is saying that either book *errs*. Each says what its author intended, and that is judged by genre and purpose (Dei Verbum 12). Harmonizing the two by flattening them is also ruled out. The difference *is* the Chronicler's theology.

## Watch out

- **"Church teaching requires Kings to be a complete record."** Inerrancy concerns what the author asserts, not what he omits. Kings says openly that it selects: "are they not written in the book of the words of the days of the kings of Juda?" (e.g. 2 Kings 21:17).
- **"De Wette discovered the book was Deuteronomy."** The identification is patristic. De Wette's claim was about when Deuteronomy was *written*. That remains a **disputed** majority view, not a result and not something Catholic doctrine settles.
- **Dating slippage.** 722, 701, 597 and 587/586 are secure within a year. The division (c. 930) and Josiah's eighteenth year are reconstructed. The early Biblical Commission's responses are not "still binding" and not "abolished". Their force was loosened in steps, and the lesson has to say which step.
- **Lamentations is "by Jeremiah."** That is a Greek and later tradition (and the Talmud's, *Bava Batra* 15a, which also gives him Kings). The Hebrew text is anonymous. Report the attribution as tradition.

## One-liner

> Kings grades every reign to explain two falls (722 and 587/586); Chronicles retells the same kings to rebuild Judah, and the outside records confirm the frame while the theology stays the authors' own.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢)** Close-read 2 Kings 22:8–13 (DR 4 Kings), Douay–Rheims:

> "And Helcias, the high priest, said to Saphan, the scribe: I have found the book of the law in the house of the Lord: and Helcias gave the book to Saphan, and he read it. And Saphan, the scribe, came to the king ... And Saphan, the scribe, told the king, saying: Helcias, the priest, hath delivered to me a book. And when Saphan had read it before the king, And the king had heard the words of the law of the Lord, he rent his garments. And he commanded Helcias, the priest, ... saying: Go and consult the Lord for me, and for the people, and for all Juda, concerning the words of this book which is found: for the great wrath of the Lord is kindled against us, because our fathers have not hearkened to the words of this book, to do all that is written for us."

(a) *(Exegetical)* What does the text itself say about the book: what it is called, how old it is taken to be, and what it contains? Name the words that decide each. (b) *(Exegetical)* Give the status of each claim in one line: (i) "The book was a form of Deuteronomy." (ii) "The book had been composed shortly before it was found." (iii) "Kings is inspired and teaches without error what God willed to be recorded for our salvation."

**P2 (🟡)** Two accounts of Manasseh (Douay–Rheims):

> **2 Kings 21:16–18 (DR 4 Kings):** "Moreover, Manasses shed also very much innocent blood, till he filled Jerusalem up to the mouth ... Now the rest of the acts of Manasses, and all that he did, and his sin, which he sinned, are they not written in the book of the words of the days of the kings of Juda? And Manasses slept with his fathers, and was buried in the garden of his own house."
>
> **2 Chronicles 33:11–13, 15 (DR 2 Paralipomenon):** "Therefore he brought upon them the captains of the army of the king of the Assyrians: and they took Manasses, and carried him bound with chains and fetters to Babylon. And after that he was in distress he prayed to the Lord his God: and did penance exceedingly before the God of his fathers. And he entreated him, and besought him earnestly: and he heard his prayer, and brought him again to Jerusalem into his kingdom, and Manasses knew that the Lord was God. ... And he took away the strange gods, and the idol out of the house of the Lord."

Supplied facts: Kings gives Manasseh a fifty-five-year reign (21:1) and makes his sins the reason Judah falls (23:26). Assyrian inscriptions of Esarhaddon and Ashurbanipal list "Manasseh king of Judah" among tributary kings, and no known Assyrian text records his captivity. Mishnah *Sanhedrin* 10:2 lists Manasseh among three kings with no share in the world to come. Rabbi Judah dissents, citing 2 Chronicles 33:13. The Sages reply that God brought him back to his *kingdom*, not to the world to come.

(a) *(Exegetical)* List what Chronicles adds to Kings' account and what the addition does to Kings' explanation of the fall. Two sentences. (b) *(Exegetical)* State the Mishnah's dispute faithfully, including the Sages' reply and which words of Chronicles each side uses. Two sentences. (c) *(Evaluative)* In 150 words or fewer: is the captivity and repentance best read as a historical tradition the Chronicler had, or as his theology of retribution at work? Any verdict passes.

**P3 (🟡)** *(Exegetical)* Mark each date *secure*, *reconstructed* or *disputed*, and give the anchor or the reason in one line each: (1) Qarqar, 853. (2) The division of the kingdom, c. 930. (3) Samaria taken by Sargon II rather than Shalmaneser V. (4) The Temple burned in 586 rather than 587. (5) The first capture of Jerusalem, 597. (6) A core of Deuteronomy composed in the seventh century.

<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical (a) · Exegetical (b))*

**Must hit, strict (a):** it is called **"the book of the law"** and **"the words of the law of the Lord"**, and in 22:13 **"this book which is found"**. The text treats it as **old and neglected, not new**: "our fathers have not hearkened to the words of this book". It contains **threats**: hearing it kindles "the great wrath of the Lord", and Josiah **rends his garments**. The text **does not name Deuteronomy or Moses** as its author.
**Must hit, strict (b):** (i) An **identification**, old (Jerome; Challoner's note) and shared by most critics, with **no magisterial standing**. It is a scholarly judgement from the match with Deuteronomy's demands (centralization, Passover). (ii) **De Wette's thesis (1805)** in its strong form, a **disputed hypothesis**. The majority dates a core of Deuteronomy to the seventh century, but "composed just before" is narrower and contested. (iii) **Church teaching**: inspiration is defined dogma (Vatican I; Dei Verbum 11); inerrancy is taught at the highest weight, its scope freely disputed (`fundamental-theology` 3.2).
**Wrong turns:** reading "Deuteronomy" out of the Douay text, where it is only in Challoner's note. Saying the text *claims* the book is new. The fathers' neglect implies the opposite. Calling (ii) "the scholarly consensus" or "condemned".
**Model answer:** (a) "The book of the law"/"the law of the Lord", presented as old, since "our fathers have not hearkened" to it. It contains threats, since it provokes "great wrath" and rent garments. It is not named as Deuteronomy and has no author. (b) (i) Traditional and critical identification, not Church teaching. (ii) Disputed hypothesis (de Wette's strong form). (iii) Church teaching, Dei Verbum 11.

**P2** *(Exegetical (a) · Exegetical (b) · Evaluative (c))*

**Must hit, strict (a):** Chronicles adds **capture by Assyrian captains, chains, Babylon, prayer and penance, God hearing him, restoration, and a reform** removing the idols. Kings has none of this and lets Manasseh die unrepentant. The addition **explains the long reign** (repentance earns years) but **removes the unrepented sin that Kings makes the cause of the fall** (23:26). The Chronicler has to explain Jerusalem's fall differently, through later kings' own sins.
**Must hit, strict (b):** the Mishnah names Jeroboam, Ahab and Manasseh as having no share. **R. Judah** says Manasseh has a share, citing **"he heard his prayer"** (33:13). The **Sages** reply from the same verse: he was brought back **"to Jerusalem into his kingdom"**, which is a restoration to the throne and not to the world to come.
**Must hit, any verdict (c):** (1) The case for a tradition: the Chronicler cites sources ("the words of Hozai", 33:19). Assyria did hold Manasseh as a vassal. Taking a vassal to Babylon is not impossible, since Assyria ruled Babylon. (2) The case for theology: the story fits the Chronicler's rule that long reigns follow fidelity. It repeats his own patterns of humbling and restoration. No Assyrian text records it. (3) Say what inspiration does and does not require here: the genre and the author's intent (Dei Verbum 12), not a verdict on the captivity's historicity.
**Wrong turns:** treating the Mishnah's dispute as about whether the captivity happened; both sides accept the verse. Setting the Jewish reading up to be refuted. Saying the silence of Assyrian records disproves the story. Silence is weak evidence.
**Model answer (c), one of several:** Theology does most of the work, but it may be working on a tradition. The Chronicler's rule predicts that a fifty-five-year reign needs repentance, and the story follows his template: sin, distress, humbling, restoration. That makes it suspect as independent history. But he names a source, the Assyrians did control Manasseh, and their kings did deal with Babylon, so a summons of a vassal is plausible. The silence of the inscriptions weighs little. My verdict is a historical core of Assyrian pressure on a vassal king, which the Chronicler shaped into a conversion. Nothing in the doctrine of inspiration decides this. It asks what the Chronicler meant to teach, and he clearly meant to teach that repentance is heard.

**P3** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict:** (1) **Secure**: the Kurkh monolith, dated by the Assyrian year-lists pinned by the eclipse of 763. (2) **Reconstructed**: biblical regnal totals counted back from fixed Assyrian points, with a margin of years. (3) **Disputed**: the fall in 722/721 is secure, but Kings names Shalmaneser as besieger, Sargon claims the capture, and historians divide. (4) **Disputed** within a secure event: 2 Kings 25:8's "nineteenth year" counted from 597, where different regnal-year counting gives 587 or 586. (5) **Secure**: the Babylonian Chronicle, to the month. (6) **Disputed**: majority critical view since de Wette, and no composition theory is settled.
**Wrong turns:** marking (4) as simply secure. The event is secure within a year, but the question asks *which* year. Marking (6) secure because it is the majority. Marking (2) secure because the Bible gives regnal years; they must be synchronized, not read off.
**Model answer:** as the must-hit list, one line each.

## Flashback

**From Lesson [3.1](03-01-joshua-judges-and-the-land.md) (Joshua, Judges, and the land):** *(Exegetical.)* Deuteronomy 20:10–11, 15–17 (Douay–Rheims):

> "If at any time thou come to fight against a city, thou shalt first offer it peace. If they receive it, and open the gates to thee, all the people that are therein, shall be saved, and shall serve thee paying tribute. ... So shalt thou do to all cities that are at a great distance from thee, and are not of these cities which thou shalt receive in possession. But of those cities that shall be given thee, thou shalt suffer none at all to live: But shalt kill them with the edge of the sword, to wit, the Hethite, and the Amorrhite, and the Chanaanite, the Pherezite, and the Hevite, and the Jebusite"

The omitted verses 12–14 say that a city refusing peace is besieged, its men are killed, and its women and children are spared. Where the Douay has "kill them with the edge of the sword" (v. 17), the Hebrew has *haḥarem taḥarimem*, "you shall utterly devote them", the verb of *herem*.

Two readings. **Reading 1** (the sequential reading 3.1 gave): the offer of peace is for distant cities only, and the seven nations of the land fall under the ban with no offer. **Reading 2** (the rabbinic reading: *Deuteronomy Rabbah* 5:14; Maimonides, *Kings and Wars* 6): peace is offered first to every city, the seven nations included, and the ban falls only on those who refuse.

(a) Name the one phrase whose scope the readings dispute, and say what each takes it to cover. (b) Name one verse outside Deuteronomy 20 that each side can cite, and say what it shows. **Two sentences per part.**

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):** the crux is **"So shalt thou do"** (v. 15). On **Reading 1** it covers the whole procedure of vv. 10–14, *including the offer of peace*, so v. 10 applies only to distant cities. On **Reading 2** it covers only the treatment of a city *after it refuses* (vv. 13–14: men killed, women, children and spoil kept). The offer of v. 10 then stays general, and vv. 16–17 only make the outcome of refusal harsher for the cities of the land: none spared.
**Must hit, strict (b):** for **Reading 1**, Deuteronomy 7:2 ("thou shalt utterly destroy them. Thou shalt make no league with them") or Joshua 9:6–7. There the Gibeonites pretend to come "from a far country", because Israel answers that if they dwell in the land "we can make no league with you". For **Reading 2**, Joshua 11:19–20: "There was not a city that delivered itself to the children of Israel, except the Hevite, who dwelt in Gabaon". The cities fought because their hearts were hardened, which implies that surrender and peace were open to them.
**Wrong turns:** treating Reading 2 as an apologetic softening rather than a reading of the text's structure, stated as the tradition states it. Making the crux historical (did a conquest happen?) when it is a question about the scope of a law. Thinking the Douay's "kill" against the Hebrew *herem* decides between the readings. It does not touch the scope of v. 15.
**Model answer:** (a) The dispute is over what "So shalt thou do" in verse 15 refers back to. Reading 1 takes it to cover the whole procedure, offer of peace included, so the seven nations get no offer. Reading 2 takes it to cover only what is done to a city that has refused, so every city is offered peace first. (b) Reading 1 cites Deuteronomy 7:2's "make no league with them", borne out when Israel tells the Gibeonites it could make no league with people of the land (Josh 9:7). Reading 2 cites Joshua 11:19–20: no city "delivered itself" except Gibeon, because the rest were hardened to fight, which assumes that peace was on offer.
