# The Old Testament · Lesson 2.5: Exodus and Sinai

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 2: The Pentateuch · Builds on: [2.2 Who wrote the Pentateuch?](02-02-who-wrote-the-pentateuch.md), [2.4 The patriarchs](02-04-the-patriarchs.md) · Unlocks: [2.6 Covenant and law](02-06-covenant-and-law.md), [3.1 Joshua, Judges, and the land](03-01-joshua-judges-and-the-land.md)

## Why this matters

Israel's creed does not begin "In the beginning". It begins "I am the Lord thy God, who brought thee out of the land of Egypt" (Exodus 20:2). Israel's law, prayer and prophets keep returning to the exodus, and Christians read Easter and baptism through it. It is also where the historical question is sharpest: no Egyptian text mentions Hebrew slaves or their escape, and the text's numbers are enormous. This lesson reads the name, the Passover, the Sea and the covenant in their literal sense, lays out what historians hold, and marks what the Church teaches and what it leaves open.

## The idea

Keep three questions apart.

1. **What does the text say?** A God who hears slaves, gives his name, destroys an army at the sea, and binds the freed people to himself.
2. **What happened?** A historian's reconstruction from thin evidence, ranging from "much as told" to "a small group's memory" to "a later national story".
3. **What does the Church hold?** That God formed Israel by freeing it and gave it his law at Sinai: revelation, not a date or a head-count.

Most confusion about the exodus comes from answering one question as if it were another.

## Source

Exodus 3:13–15 (Douay–Rheims), with Bishop Challoner's footnote:

> "Moses said to God: Lo, I shall go to the children of Israel, and say to them: The God of your fathers hath sent me to you. If they shall say to me: What is his name? What shall I say to them? God said to Moses: I AM WHO AM. He said: Thus shalt thou say to the children of Israel: HE WHO IS, hath sent me to you. [...] The Lord God of your fathers [...] hath sent me to you; this is my name for ever, and this is my memorial unto all generations."
>
> Challoner's note: "That is, I am being itself, eternal, self-existent, independent, infinite; without beginning, end, or change; and the source of all other beings."

The Hebrew is *ehyeh asher ehyeh*. *Ehyeh* is the first person imperfect of *hayah*, "to be, to become, to happen", and the imperfect can point to the future. Translations range from "I am who I am" to "I will be what I will be". Three verses earlier, Moses asked "Who am I?" and God answered *ki-ehyeh immakh*, "I will be with thee" (3:12). The same verb answers both questions, and "the Lord" of verse 15 renders YHWH, which the text links to it.

## The argument

1. **The name: three literal readings.** [*Ehyeh asher ehyeh*](../reference.md#ehyeh-asher-ehyeh) has three readings with serious defenders:
   - **Being.** The Septuagint renders *egō eimi ho ōn*, "I am the one who is", and the Vulgate *ego sum qui sum*. Challoner's note and Aquinas read the name as God's very being. Aquinas calls "He Who Is" the most proper name of God (ST I q.13 a.11; see [`thomistic-synthesis` 4.3](../../thomistic-synthesis/lessons/04-03-the-names-of-god.md)).
   - **Presence.** The name repeats 3:12's promise: I will be there with you. Rashi, drawing on the Talmud (b. Berakhot 9b), paraphrases it as God saying he will be with Israel in this trouble and in later subjugations. Many modern exegetes favour it.
   - **Reserve.** In the ancient world a name gave power over a god; here God gives one that cannot be used that way: I am who I am.

   *In words:* the Greek pushed the verse toward metaphysics, and the Hebrew verb leans toward promise. The readings can coexist; they are not the same.

2. **Passover and the Sea.** Exodus 12 prescribes a lamb "without blemish, a male, of one year" (12:5), with its blood on the doorposts and not a bone broken (12:46). It is "the Phase (that is the Passage) of the Lord" (12:11). The Mishnah (Pesachim 10:5) requires every Jew in every generation to count himself as one who came out of Egypt. The sea is *yam suf*, "sea of reeds" (*suf* is the reeds of 2:3); the Septuagint made it the Red Sea. The Song of the Sea (15:1–18) and Miriam's couplet (15:21) are poetry in archaic Hebrew. Frank Moore Cross and David Noel Freedman (*Studies in Ancient Yahwistic Poetry*, dissertation 1950, published 1975) argued the Song is among the oldest poems in the Bible, perhaps twelfth century BC. Others date it later. If they are right, Israel sang the event before it narrated it.

3. **The historical question: the evidence.**
   - **The [Merneptah stele](../reference.md#merneptah-stele)**, found by Flinders Petrie at Thebes in 1896, records a campaign in Merneptah's fifth year, usually put c. 1208 BC. It names "Israel" among the defeated in Canaan. Its sign marks Israel as a *people*, not a city or land. This is the earliest mention of Israel outside the Bible.
   - **Two biblical dates.** 1 Kings 6:1 [Douay 3 Kings 6:1] puts the exodus 480 years before Solomon's fourth year (c. 966), which gives c. 1446 BC. Exodus 1:11 has the slaves build "Ramesses", which most connect with Ramesses II's city (he reigned 1279–1213), and that points to the thirteenth century. Many read the 480 as twelve generations of forty years.
   - **The numbers.** Exodus 12:37 counts "about six hundred thousand men on foot, beside children", which means two million or more people in all. No trace of such a population has been found in Sinai. Canaan's new hill-country villages c. 1200 BC look local in pottery and houses.

4. **[The positions](../reference.md#positions-on-the-exodus)** (mapped below): **maximal** (James Hoffmeier, *Israel in Egypt*, 1997), **a smaller exodus group** (Richard Elliott Friedman, *The Exodus*, 2017: the Levites), and the **critical majority**. The majority denies an exodus on the scale told; many allow a group's memory of escape at the root, others see later national myth. *In words:* the live question is not "exodus or no exodus" but how much history, and whose.

5. **The [covenant form](../reference.md#covenant-treaty-form).** George Mendenhall (*Biblical Archaeologist*, 1954) compared Sinai with Hittite vassal treaties of the fourteenth and thirteenth centuries BC. Their parts: preamble, historical prologue (the overlord's past favours), stipulations, deposit and public reading, witnesses, blessings and curses. Exodus 20:2 is preamble plus prologue, and the commandments follow as stipulations. Deuteronomy's curses (ch. 28) are also close to Esarhaddon's succession treaty (672 BC). Dennis McCarthy, S.J. (*Treaty and Covenant*, 1963) replied that the treaty form stays much the same across two millennia, so it cannot date a text. He also argued the full form is clearer in Deuteronomy than in Exodus. *In words:* Israel imagined its bond with God as a vassal's oath to a great king; the parallel shows that, not the text's date.

6. **[Two Decalogues](../reference.md#the-two-decalogues).** Exodus 20:2–17 and Deuteronomy 5:6–21 differ, chiefly in the sabbath and coveting commandments. Deuteronomy frames itself as Moses restating the words given at Horeb (5:2–5), not reproducing a transcript.

**Mark the levels.** [Church teaching vs hypothesis](../reference.md#church-teaching-vs-hypothesis). That God formed Israel by freeing it from Egypt, made the Sinai covenant and gave it the law through Moses is **Church teaching**, taught as the history of revelation (CCC 62, paraphrased). Inerrancy concerns what the author asserts, read by genre ([`fundamental-theology` 3.2](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/03-02-inerrancy.md)); no magisterial act fixes the exodus's date, route, numbers or mechanism. The hard case is a [Biblical Commission](../reference.md#the-biblical-commission-and-its-weight) decree of 27 February 1934. It censured F. Schmidtke's *Die Einwanderung Israels in Kanaan* (1933). The book held, among other things, that only part of Israel, chiefly Joseph's tribe, was in Egypt, and it explained many miracles as purely natural. Weigh it like the other early responses: owed submission when issued, not a definition. The decree applied the 1905 response on narratives only apparently historical, and the 1948 letter to Cardinal Suhard called that response, with those of 1906 and 1909, no hindrance to further scientific study; then came the 1955 clarification ([`new-testament` 1.1](../../new-testament/lessons/01-01-reading-the-new-testament-in-the-church.md)). Calling it binding today overstates it; calling it irrelevant understates it. The positions in step 4 are **scholarly hypotheses** with no magisterial standing. Aquinas's reading of 3:14 as subsistent being is a **theological reading of great weight**, not a ruling on the verse's literal sense. **Dates:** Merneptah c. 1208 BC, *reconstructed*. The exodus, 1446 or thirteenth century, is *disputed*, as are the Song of the Sea's date and the Pentateuch's composition. Esarhaddon's treaty, 672 BC, is *secure*. See [dating discipline](../reference.md#dating-discipline).

## Map of positions

| | Maximal | Smaller group | Critical majority |
|---|---|---|---|
| Representative | Hoffmeier (1997) | Friedman (2017) | most historians and archaeologists; views range widely |
| Who left Egypt | Israel, much as told (some read "thousand", *elef*, as a clan or military unit) | a few thousand, e.g. Levites, later merged with Israelites already in Canaan | no large exodus; perhaps a small group, perhaps none |
| Leans on | Egyptian colour: Semites in the Delta, Egyptian names, the Ramesses city | only Levites have Egyptian names (Moses, Phinehas, Hophni); he reads the oldest poems as tying the exodus to Levites | silence of Sinai; local continuity in the hill-country settlements |
| Date | thirteenth century (or 1446) | thirteenth century | question doesn't arise, or a small group's date unknown |
| Weakest point | explaining the silence of the record | reading tribal lists as evidence of a tribe's history | explaining why a nation would invent a slave origin |

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean): the coveting commandment.**

| Exodus 20:17 | Deuteronomy 5:21 |
|---|---|
| "Thou shalt not covet thy neighbour's house; neither shalt thou desire his wife, nor his servant ..." | "Thou shalt not covet thy neighbour's wife: nor his house, nor his field, nor his manservant ..." |
| Hebrew: *tachmod* ("covet") twice; house first | Hebrew: *tachmod* for the wife, then a different verb, *titavveh* ("desire"), for the house and goods; the field added |

**Literal sense:** Exodus places the wife in the household headed by "house"; Deuteronomy sets her apart with her own verb. **Composition:** most critics read Deuteronomy's version as a later reworking. **Tradition:** Deuteronomy's split matches Augustine's division, which CCC 2066 says the Catechism follows, as do the Lutheran confessions: images join the first commandment, and coveting a wife (ninth) is counted apart from goods (tenth). The Greek Fathers' division (Orthodox, Reformed) counts images separately; Jewish tradition counts "I am the Lord thy God" as the first word. Numbering is convention, not doctrine.

**Example 2 (hard): the Sea.** Exodus 14:21 says the Lord drove the sea back "by a strong and burning wind blowing all the night, and turned it into dry ground". Verse 22 says "the water was as a wall on their right hand and on their left". Source critics hear two strands here: a wind that dries the sea overnight, and Moses's staff splitting the water into walls ([2.2](02-02-who-wrote-the-pentateuch.md)). The method strains twice. Two strands say how the text was built, not whether anything happened, and the poem of chapter 15 may be older than both. And a wind does not make the event "merely natural" (the move the 1934 decree faulted): the text says the Lord sent it. The spiritual sense builds on the literal one. Paul: "all in Moses were baptized, in the cloud and in the sea" (1 Corinthians 10:2); "Christ our pasch is sacrificed" (5:7). John applies "you shall not break a bone of him" to the cross (John 19:36; Exodus 12:46). That is [typology](../reference.md#typology): a real event that prefigures a later one. It needs the event as Scripture presents it, not a historian's head-count.

## Watch out

- **"The Church teaches the exodus happened in 1446 BC with two million people."** No magisterial act fixes date or numbers. 1446 comes from one biblical verse read arithmetically, and that reading is disputed.
- **"The 1934 decree still settles that all Israel was in Egypt"**, or **"it was never binding"**. Both misweigh it. It was owed submission when issued. Its force today is read through the 1948 letter and the 1955 clarification.
- **Reading *ehyeh* only as metaphysics**, or only as presence. The Hebrew verb and the Greek tradition pull different ways. State which sense you mean.

## One-liner

> The exodus is the event Israel confesses God by; the Church teaches that God freed and bound his people, and leaves the date, the numbers and the route to historians.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢)** *(Exegetical)* Close-read the two sabbath commandments (Douay–Rheims):

> **Exodus 20:8, 10–11:** "Remember that thou keep holy the sabbath day. [...] on the seventh day is the sabbath of the Lord thy God: thou shalt do no work on it, thou [...] nor thy beast [...] For in six days the Lord made heaven and earth [...] and rested on the seventh day: therefore the Lord blessed the seventh day, and sanctified it."
>
> **Deuteronomy 5:12, 14–15:** "Observe the day of the sabbath, to sanctify it, as the Lord thy God hath commanded thee. [...] [...] nor thy ox, nor thy ass, nor any of thy beasts [...] that thy manservant and thy maidservant may rest, even as thyself. Remember that thou also didst serve in Egypt, and the Lord thy God brought thee out from thence [...] Therefore hath he commanded thee that thou shouldst observe the sabbath day."

(a) List four differences and the words that carry each. (b) In one sentence each, state how rabbinic tradition holds the two openings together, and how historical critics explain the difference in motive. Take no side.

**P2 (🟡)** *(Exegetical)* Assign each claim its status: **Church teaching** (name the source), **early Biblical Commission act** (say how it is weighed today), **historical datum** (with its dating label), or **scholarly hypothesis**. One line each.

1. "God formed Israel as his people by freeing it from slavery in Egypt, and gave it his law at Sinai."
2. "The exodus took place in 1446 BC."
3. "A people called Israel existed in Canaan by about 1208 BC."
4. "Only part of Israel, chiefly the house of Joseph, was ever in Egypt."
5. "The literal sense of *ehyeh asher ehyeh* is that God is subsistent being itself."

**P3 (🔴)** *(Exegetical)* An **invented** documentary voice-over:

> "For forty years archaeologists combed the Sinai for two million wandering Hebrews and found nothing. The Merneptah stele shows Israel already sitting in Canaan in 1208 BC, so there was no time for an exodus. Even the Vatican's Biblical Commission admitted in 1993 that the exodus is legend, and that is now official Catholic teaching."

Diagnose three errors: (a) what the silence of Sinai does and does not tell against; (b) what the stele actually constrains; (c) the level error in the last sentence. **Two sentences per part.**

<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** any four of: (1) the opening verb, **"Remember"** (*zakhor*) vs **"Observe"** (*shamor*); (2) Deuteronomy's **"as the Lord thy God hath commanded thee"**, which refers back to an earlier giving; (3) the **motive**: creation and God's rest ("in six days the Lord made heaven and earth") vs deliverance ("thou also didst serve in Egypt ... brought thee out"); (4) the **purpose clause** "that thy manservant and thy maidservant may rest, even as thyself", so rest for the dependent is the point; (5) **"ox" and "ass" named** where Exodus has only "beast".
**Must hit, strict (b):** rabbinic tradition holds that "Remember" and "Observe" were **spoken in a single utterance** (b. Shevuot 20b), so the two texts give one commandment heard both ways. Critics commonly read Exodus 20:11 as tied to the **Priestly creation account** (Gen 2:2–3), and Deuteronomy's motive as Deuteronomy's own theology of remembering the deliverance and caring for the servant. That sentence describes the method; it does not decide a date.
**Wrong turns:** counting the family members as a difference: both lists name the same ones. Declaring one version the "original" in (b). Treating the rabbinic reading as naive harmonization rather than stating it as its tradition states it.
**Model answer:** (a) "Remember" vs "Observe"; Deuteronomy adds "as the Lord thy God hath commanded thee"; Exodus grounds the day in creation ("in six days the Lord made"), Deuteronomy in the release from Egypt ("thou also didst serve in Egypt"); Deuteronomy adds "that thy manservant and thy maidservant may rest, even as thyself". (b) The rabbis teach that God said "Remember" and "Observe" in one utterance. Critics assign Exodus's motive to the Priestly creation theology and Deuteronomy's to its own exodus-centred reworking.

**P2** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict:** (1) **Church teaching**, CCC 62, taught as the history of revelation. It fixes no date, number or route. (2) **Scholarly hypothesis**: a *disputed* date from 1 Kings 6:1 (480 years before c. 966), with no magisterial standing; the thirteenth-century date is its rival. (3) **Historical datum**, the Merneptah stele, *reconstructed* date (Merneptah's fifth year, within a few years). (4) **Scholarly hypothesis**, which the **Biblical Commission's 1934 decree** on Schmidtke censured. That decree was owed submission when issued. It is not a definition, and its force today is read through the 1948 Suhard letter and the 1955 clarification. (5) **Scholarly hypothesis about the literal sense**, freely disputed (the presence and reserve readings are its rivals). The Thomist doctrine of God as subsistent being is a separate theological claim and does not depend on this exegesis.
**Wrong turns:** calling (1) "just a hypothesis" or (2) Church teaching. Calling (4) forbidden today, or saying the 1934 decree never bound. Calling (5) defined doctrine because Aquinas and Challoner read it so.
**Model answer:** (1) Church teaching, CCC 62. (2) Disputed scholarly date. (3) Historical datum, c. 1208 reconstructed. (4) Hypothesis; censured by the 1934 decree, a pre-1971 act weighed through 1948 and 1955, not a definition. (5) Disputed exegetical hypothesis; the metaphysics stands on other grounds.

**P3** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** the silence tells against an exodus **on the scale told** (two million people over forty years). It does not tell against a **small group** leaving Egypt, which would leave little trace (Friedman; many in the majority).
**Must hit, strict (b):** the stele shows an **Israel in Canaan c. 1208 BC** and so sets a latest date for Israel's presence there. A thirteenth-century exodus under Ramesses II falls before it, so the stele constrains the dating without excluding an exodus.
**Must hit, strict (c):** a Biblical Commission document of 1993 comes **after the 1971 reorganization**. The Commission is then consultative, and its documents are **not magisterial acts**. Whatever it says, it cannot make anything "official Catholic teaching". Credit also: the only teaching in view (CCC 62) affirms the deliverance.
**Wrong turns:** answering (a) by asserting the maximal view as proven. Saying the stele "proves the exodus". Answering (c) by claiming to know what the 1993 document says about the exodus, when the level error does not depend on its content.
**Model answer:** (a) Finding nothing in Sinai counts against two million people wandering there. It does not count against a small group escaping Egypt. (b) The stele puts a people Israel in Canaan by about 1208 BC. A thirteenth-century exodus fits before that, so the stele limits the dates rather than excluding the event. (c) After 1971 the Commission is an advisory body, and its 1993 document is not a magisterial act. The teaching that does exist (CCC 62) says the opposite of "legend".

## Flashback

**From Lesson [2.3](02-03-the-primeval-history.md) (The primeval history: fall, flood, Babel):** *(Exegetical.)* Two causes of one flood.

- ***Atrahasis*** (best copy Old Babylonian, seventeenth century BC, a date reconstructed from a scribe's colophon), paraphrased: the gods create humans to do the gods' labour. The humans multiply, and their noise keeps the god Enlil from sleeping, so the gods send the flood.
- **Genesis 6:11–13** (Douay–Rheims): "And the earth was corrupted before God, and was filled with iniquity. And when God had seen that the earth was corrupted (for all flesh had corrupted its way upon the earth), He said to Noe: The end of all flesh is come before me, the earth is filled with iniquity through them, and I will destroy them with the earth." "Iniquity" renders Hebrew *hamas*, "violence".

(a) Compare the two causes: what offends the deity in each, and what each implies about why humans exist. Use Genesis 1:28's "Increase and multiply" in your answer. (b) *Hamas* ties the flood back to two episodes in Genesis 4. Name them, and say what the link shows about the plot of Genesis 3–11. **Two sentences per part.**

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):** in *Atrahasis* the offence is **noise and numbers**, a disturbance to the gods' rest with **no moral fault** in it, and humans exist **to labour for the gods**. In Genesis the offence is **moral**, corruption and **violence**. Humans were made in blessing, and their multiplying is **commanded** (1:28, repeated to Noah in 9:1), not the problem. So Genesis reworks the shared story's cause into a moral judgment by one God.
**Must hit, strict (b):** **Cain's murder of Abel** (4:8) and **Lamech's boast** of seventy-sevenfold vengeance (4:23–24). Violence grows from one murder to a boast of revenge to an earth "filled" with it. That is the **spread of sin** that structures Genesis 3–11: each transgression reaches further, and God judges each one, while also mitigating the judgment until Babel.
**Wrong turns:** reading the Genesis flood as punishment for overpopulation: the text blesses multiplying. Making the parallel prove direct copying from *Atrahasis*, or settle whether a flood happened. Picking Eden as one of the two: its sin is disobedience, not violence.
**Model answer:** (a) The gods of *Atrahasis* are kept awake by the noise of a labour force grown too large, so the flood fixes an inconvenience and involves no guilt. Genesis blesses human increase (1:28) and sends the flood because the earth is full of violence, so humans are blessed creatures judged for what they do, not servants culled for their noise. (b) Cain's murder of his brother and Lamech's seventy-sevenfold vengeance are the violence that now fills the whole earth. The flood caps a story in which sin spreads from one household to all flesh.

</details>
