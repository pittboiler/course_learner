# The Old Testament · Lesson 6.1: How the Old Testament canon formed

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 6: Canon, deuterocanon, apocalyptic, and the Old in light of the New · Builds on: [1.1 What the Old Testament is](01-01-what-the-old-testament-is.md), [`fundamental-theology` 3.3 The canon as defined](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/03-03-the-canon-as-defined.md) · Unlocks: [6.2 Tobit, Judith, Baruch, and the Maccabees](06-02-tobit-judith-baruch-and-the-maccabees.md)

## Why this matters

[1.1](01-01-what-the-old-testament-is.md) counted 24, 39 and 46 books. This lesson asks how the counts came apart. Two stories are usually told. In one, a rabbinic council at Jamnia closed the Hebrew canon around AD 90 and the Church drifted away from it. In the other, the Church always had the 46 books and the Reformers cut seven out. Both are too neat. The evidence is a trail of witnesses, and each one says less than the story it gets drafted into.

## The idea

Keep three things apart.

- **A collection in use.** Scrolls read in synagogue or church, quoted and copied.
- **A count.** A number with a reason attached: Josephus's 22, the Talmud's 24.
- **A closed list backed by authority.** A council or a school that says "these and no others".

For Jews, the Law and the Prophets were settled early. The Writings, the third part with no fixed name in Sirach's prologue (1.1), are where the argument happened. Christians inherited their Scriptures mostly in Greek, and Greek codices carried more books, the [deuterocanonical books](../reference.md#deuterocanonical-books) among them. The Church read those books for centuries before any council listed them. So its later lists record a use that already existed, as in the New Testament story ([`new-testament` 6.6](../../new-testament/lessons/06-06-the-formation-of-the-canon.md)).

## Source

Two texts from the 390s, one from Bethlehem and one from North Africa. First, Jerome's preface to his translation of the books of Solomon (about 393; NPNF 2nd ser. vol. 6):

> As, then, the Church reads Judith, Tobit, and the books of Maccabees, but does not admit them among the canonical Scriptures, so let it read these two volumes for the edification of the people, not to give authority to doctrines of the Church.

"These two volumes" are Wisdom and Sirach. Second, canon 24 of the African code, which preserves the list of Hippo (393) as it stood in 419 (NPNF 2nd ser. vol. 14). It opens and closes:

> That nothing be read in church besides the Canonical Scripture. ... Let this be sent to our brother and fellow bishop, Boniface, and to the other bishops of those parts, that they may confirm this canon, for these are the things which we have received from our fathers to be read in church.

Both texts start from the same fact: the Church **reads** these books. They draw opposite conclusions. For Jerome, reading in church is one thing and canonical authority another, and a book can have the first without the second. For the African bishops, what is received "to be read in church" *is* the canon, and between Genesis and Maccabees their list names Tobit, Judith, "the five books of Solomon" (Wisdom and Sirach included) and 1–2 Maccabees. The disagreement is about what the Church's own reading practice means.

## The argument

1. **The [Septuagint](../reference.md#the-septuagint) and its legend.** The *Letter of Aristeas* claims to be by a courtier of Ptolemy II (reigned 285–246 BC). It tells how seventy-two elders, six from each tribe, translated **the Law** at Alexandria, and the Jewish community there approved the result. Josephus retells it (*Antiquities* XII.2) and adds that the work took seventy-two days. The legend then grew. Philo (*Life of Moses* II) treats the translators as inspired, all writing the same words. Irenaeus (*Against Heresies* III.21.2, ANF vol. 1) has the king separate them, "commanded them all to write the same translation", and extends the miracle to all the books. *In words:* a story about the Torah became a warrant for a whole Greek Bible. Scholars date the *Letter* itself to the second century BC (reconstructed) and read it as apologetic, not a record.

2. **Qumran.** Every book of the Hebrew Bible except Esther turns up among the scrolls (1.1). So do fragments of Tobit (in Aramaic and Hebrew) and Sirach, and many copies of books no canon kept, such as *1 Enoch* and *Jubilees*. One Psalms scroll (11QPs^a) orders the psalms differently and adds others, Psalm 151 among them. "Canon" is anachronistic here. The scrolls show which books a community copied and valued, not a list it had closed.

3. **Josephus's [twenty-two books](../reference.md#the-twenty-two-books).** *Against Apion* I.8, written in the 90s AD: five books of Moses, thirteen of prophets "from the death of Moses till the reign of Artaxerxes", four of hymns and precepts. History written after Artaxerxes "hath not been esteemed of the like authority" (Whiston), because the succession of prophets failed. That is the earliest explicit Jewish count. It is also an apologetic boast against the Greeks. Which books fill the 13 and the 4 is a reconstruction; Josephus names none after Moses. Rabbinic tradition counts the same Hebrew books as 24 (*Bava Batra* 14b). It excludes Sirach by name: "the books of Ben Sira and all books written from then on" do not "defile the hands" (Tosefta *Yadayim* 2:13, numbered 2:5 on Sefaria; the lesson's translation). That odd phrase is the rabbis' mark of a holy scroll.

4. **["The Council of Jamnia"](../reference.md#the-council-of-jamnia-myth).** Heinrich Graetz proposed in 1871 that the rabbis gathered at Yavneh (Jamnia) after 70 closed the canon, and textbooks turned the proposal into a "council of AD 90". Jack P. Lewis ("What Do We Mean by Jabneh?", *Journal of Bible and Religion*, 1964) showed how little the sources support it. David Aune (*JBL*, 1991) traced how the myth arose. What the Mishnah actually records (*Yadayim* 3:5) is a debate over whether the Song of Songs and Ecclesiastes "defile the hands", settled, Ben Azzai says, "on the day" Rabbi Eleazar ben Azariah became head of the academy. Rabbi Akiva protests that nobody ever doubted the Song, "the holy of holies". There was no council and no list, and nothing about Greek books. The question was about two books already in use.

5. **The Fathers' lists.** Melito of Sardis (about 170) went east "to the place where these things were preached and done" to learn the books "accurately" (Eusebius IV.26). His list has no Esther and no deuterocanonical book. Origen's twenty-two "as the Hebrews have handed them down" (Eusebius VI.25) sets Maccabees outside the count, yet puts "the epistle" (of Jeremiah, which no Hebrew list has) inside Jeremiah. Athanasius (367) counts twenty-two with Baruch and the Letter inside Jeremiah. He sets Wisdom, Sirach, Esther, Judith and Tobit apart as books "appointed by the Fathers to be read" by converts.

6. **[Jerome and Augustine](../reference.md#jerome-and-augustine-on-the-canon).** Jerome's *Hebraica veritas* led to his "helmeted preface" (about 391). It gives the twenty-two Hebrew books and sends everything else "amongst the Apocryphal writings" ([`patristics` 4.2](../../patristics/lessons/04-02-jerome.md) reads it). Augustine, *De Doctrina Christiana* 2.8, lets the "greater number of catholic churches" decide. He counts forty-four books with Tobit, Judith, the Maccabees, Wisdom and Sirach. Boss problem 6 reads both closely.

7. **[The African councils](../reference.md#the-african-councils).** Hippo (393) and Carthage (397), with Augustine present, adopted the Source's list. They were regional synods.

8. **Florence and Luther.** The Council of Florence's decree of union with the Copts (session 11, 4 February 1442) professes one God as author of both Testaments and lists the books: Tobit, Judith, Wisdom, Ecclesiasticus, Baruch and two of Maccabees among them. [Luther's complete Bible of 1534](../reference.md#luthers-apocrypha) gathered those books into a separate section between the Testaments, headed (the lesson's translation of the German) "Apocrypha: that is, books which are not held equal to Holy Scripture, and yet are useful and good to read". He added the Greek parts of Esther and Daniel and the Prayer of Manasseh, and left out 3–4 Esdras. That is Jerome's two-tier view, printed; he moved the books to a separate section, he did not delete them. Trent then defined the list (8 April 1546): [`fundamental-theology` 3.3](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/03-03-the-canon-as-defined.md). The Protestant case against the deuterocanon is argued in [`apologetics-catholic-claims`](../../apologetics-catholic-claims/syllabus.md).

**Mark the levels.** That the 46 books with all their parts are canonical is **defined dogma** (Trent, Session IV; rung 1 of [the ladder](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-04-the-ladder-of-doctrinal-authority.md)). Florence's list is the teaching of an ecumenical council. It carries no anathema, so how far it already defined the list is a question for historians of dogma, and Trent's decree removed any doubt. Hippo and Carthage are **regional witnesses**, not definitions. **Everything else here is history with no magisterial level** ([church teaching vs hypothesis](../reference.md#church-teaching-vs-hypothesis)). That includes Aristeas's value, what Josephus's 22 contain, how representative Melito is, and whether a Jewish canon was closed by AD 100 or later. Dates: Hippo 393, Carthage 397, the African code 419, Athanasius 367, Florence 1442, Luther 1534 and Trent 1546 are **secure**. The Septuagint Pentateuch in the third century BC, the *Letter* in the second, Melito about 170 and the Yavneh debates about 70–130 are **reconstructed**. When the Jewish canon closed is **disputed**.

## The lists side by side

| Witness | Count | Esther | Deuterocanonical books |
|---|---|---|---|
| Josephus, *Against Apion* I.8 (90s AD) | 22 | probably (reconstructed) | none counted |
| Melito (c. 170) | none stated | absent | none ("Wisdom" there is Proverbs) |
| Origen (Eusebius VI.25) | 22 | in | Letter of Jeremiah in; Maccabees "besides these" |
| Athanasius, Festal Letter 39 (367) | 22 | read, not canon | Baruch and Letter in; Wisdom, Sirach, Judith, Tobit read, not canon |
| Jerome, helmeted preface (c. 391) | 22 (or 24) | in | all apocryphal |
| Augustine, *DDC* 2.8; Hippo 393 / Carthage 397 | 44 / list | in | Tobit, Judith, Wisdom, Sirach, 1–2 Maccabees in |
| Florence (1442) | list | in | all seven by name |
| Luther (1534) | — | Hebrew parts in | printed apart as Apocrypha |

Read the columns separately. "Twenty-two" is the Hebrew alphabet's number, and the lists that use it do not all hold the same books.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean): tracking Esther.** Ask each witness whether it names the book, and on which tier. Qumran has no copy. Melito leaves it out, Athanasius puts it among the books read but not in the canon, and Origen, Jerome and Carthage include it. Trent includes it with its Greek parts ("entire with all their parts"). A book both Jews and Protestants accept had one of the most uneven records in the early Church. So no single tidy line separates "Hebrew" books from "Greek" ones.

**Example 2 (hard): Baruch.** No Hebrew text of it survives. Jerome left it untranslated, and the Vulgate's Baruch is Old Latin. Yet Athanasius, who excludes Tobit and Judith, counts Baruch and the Letter inside his twenty-two as part of Jeremiah. Carthage's list never names Baruch, and most read it as tucked under Jeremiah. Florence names it outright. The method strains here because a book can sit inside another book. To answer "who accepted Baruch?" you have to know how each list bundled Jeremiah. The count alone will not tell you.

## Watch out

- **Hypothesis stated as teaching.** "The Church teaches that the Jews closed their canon at Jamnia and so lost the right to define it." No Church document says this. The "council" is a nineteenth-century reconstruction that the evidence does not support.
- **Teaching stated as hypothesis.** "Jerome, a Doctor of the Church, rejected Judith, so Catholics may too." A Father's opinion is not the Church's definition. After Trent the canonicity of Judith is defined, and the historical fact of Jerome's doubt stands beside it untouched.
- **"Luther removed seven books."** He printed them in a separate section with a title that praised them. Dropping them from Protestant Bibles was a later printers' practice.
- **"The Septuagint was the Alexandrian Jewish canon."** It is a translation tradition preserved in Christian codices, not a Jewish list. The "Alexandrian canon" hypothesis has few defenders now.

## One-liner

> Use came before lists and lists before definitions: the Law and the Prophets settled early, the Writings and the Greek books were argued over for centuries, Jamnia never closed anything, and Trent defined what Florence and Africa had listed.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢)** *(Exegetical)* Josephus, *Against Apion* I.8 (Whiston, public domain; the brackets are Whiston's):

> For we have not an innumerable multitude of books among us, disagreeing from and contradicting one another, [as the Greeks have,] but only twenty-two books, which contain the records of all the past times; which are justly believed to be divine; and of them five belong to Moses ... but as to the time from the death of Moses till the reign of Artaxerxes king of Persia, who reigned after Xerxes, the prophets, who were after Moses, wrote down what was done in their times in thirteen books. The remaining four books contain hymns to God, and precepts for the conduct of human life. It is true, our history hath been written since Artaxerxes very particularly, but hath not been esteemed of the like authority with the former by our forefathers, because there hath not been an exact succession of prophets since that time.

(a) State the criterion that closes the collection, and the words that carry it. (b) Name two things a reader may **not** infer from this passage about Judaism's Bible in the 90s AD, and say why. **Two sentences per part.**

**P2 (🟡)** *(Exegetical)* Sort each claim as **attested** (the sources say it), **grown legend or myth** (the sources say less or something else), or **half right** (say which half). **One line each.**

(i) A council of rabbis at Jamnia around AD 90 closed the Hebrew canon and rejected the Greek books.
(ii) Seventy-two translators, working apart, produced identical Greek versions of the whole Old Testament.
(iii) The Qumran community copied Tobit and Sirach.
(iv) Jerome rejected the deuterocanonical books and would not translate any of them.
(v) Luther took the deuterocanonical books out of the Bible.
(vi) The Mishnah records a dispute over whether Ecclesiastes "defiles the hands".

**P3 (🔴)** *(Exegetical)* Melito of Sardis, in Eusebius, *Church History* IV.26 (NPNF 2nd ser. vol. 1, public domain):

> Accordingly when I went East and came to the place where these things were preached and done, I learned accurately the books of the Old Testament, and send them to thee as written below. Their names are as follows: Of Moses, five books: Genesis, Exodus, Numbers, Leviticus, Deuteronomy; Jesus Nave, Judges, Ruth; of Kings, four books; of Chronicles, two; the Psalms of David, the Proverbs of Solomon, Wisdom also, Ecclesiastes, Song of Songs, Job; of Prophets, Isaiah, Jeremiah; of the twelve prophets, one book; Daniel, Ezekiel, Esdras.

The translator notes that "Wisdom also" renders Greek *hē kai Sophia*, "which is also [called] Wisdom". (a) Which book of the Protestant 39 is missing, and which books are probably present though not named? (b) Does the list contain the Wisdom of Solomon? Say which words decide it. (c) Mark the level of each claim: "Melito's list omits Esther"; "the second-century Church held a 22-book Old Testament"; "Esther is canonical Scripture". **Two sentences for (a) and (b); one line per claim in (c).**

<details>
<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** the criterion is **prophetic authorship within an unbroken succession of prophets**, ending at **Artaxerxes**. Later histories are not "of the like authority ... because there hath not been an exact succession of prophets since that time". The Tosefta's exclusion of Ben Sira and "all books written from then on" works with a similar cut-off.

**Must hit, strict (b):** any two, each with a reason. (1) **Which books** make up the 13 and the 4: Josephus names none after Moses, so the contents are a reconstruction, and Esther and the Song are argued over. (2) That **all Jews agreed**: the passage is an apologetic contrast with "the Greeks", and the Mishnah still records debate over the Song and Ecclesiastes. (3) That **a council had closed the list**: he appeals to ancestral esteem ("by our forefathers"), not to any decision.

**Wrong turns:** reading "twenty-two" as proof that the list matched the modern 24 book for book. Taking "no one has been so bold as to add" (later in the section) as neutral reporting and not rhetoric. Making Artaxerxes a date of composition, not the end of the prophetic era.

**Model answer:** (a) Books count if prophets wrote them in the succession running from Moses to Artaxerxes. The words are "the prophets, who were after Moses" and "there hath not been an exact succession of prophets since that time". (b) We cannot infer which books fill the thirteen and the four, since Josephus names none after the Torah. Nor can we infer a uniform Jewish consensus or a formal closing, because this is an apologetic boast against the Greeks and rests on "our forefathers", not a ruling.

**P2** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict:**
(i) **Myth.** No council or list is attested (Graetz 1871; Lewis 1964). The Mishnah records a debate about the Song and Ecclesiastes, not the Greek books.
(ii) **Grown legend.** Aristeas has the Law only, done together and approved. Separate work with identical results, and "all the books", come from Irenaeus III.21.2 (Philo already has miraculous agreement).
(iii) **Attested.** There are fragments of both, but copying is not canonizing.
(iv) **Half right.** He ranked them outside the canon (helmeted preface; preface to Solomon), but he did translate Tobit and Judith. His Judith preface says Nicaea was reported to have counted it.
(v) **Half right / legend.** He separated them under the heading "Apocrypha", useful and good to read. He did not delete them.
(vi) **Attested** (*Yadayim* 3:5), with Akiva defending the Song.

**Wrong turns:** calling (iii) evidence that Qumran had a 46-book canon. Treating (i) as "attested but debated". Marking (iv) plain true.

**Model answer:** as above.

**P3** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** **Esther** is missing. **Lamentations** (probably counted with Jeremiah) and **Nehemiah** (inside "Esdras", as the Hebrew tradition counts Ezra-Nehemiah) are probably present, but neither is named.

**Must hit, strict (b):** **No, on the natural reading.** "Wisdom also" (*hē kai Sophia*) is a second title for **Proverbs**, which it follows directly, and not a separate book. That reading has a live minority. Either way the list has no Tobit, Judith, Sirach or Maccabees.

**Must hit, strict (c):** omits Esther: **historical fact, attested** in the text, with no magisterial level. The second-century Church held 22: **an unsupported generalization**. It is a scholarly claim at most, and one bishop's inquiry "in the East" is not the Church's usage. Esther canonical: **defined dogma** (Trent, Session IV), Greek parts included.

**Wrong turns:** counting "Wisdom" as the Wisdom of Solomon and so finding a deuterocanonical book. Treating Melito's omission of Esther as a problem only for Catholics, when Esther is in the Protestant 39 too. Giving the second claim a magisterial grade either way.

**Model answer:** (a) Esther is missing, while Lamentations and Nehemiah are probably counted under Jeremiah and "Esdras". (b) No: *hē kai Sophia* is an alternative name for Proverbs, the book it follows. (c) Attested fact; overgeneralized historical claim with no magisterial level; defined dogma.

## Flashback

**From Lesson [5.3](05-03-job.md) (Job):** *(Exegetical.)* Baldad (Hebrew Bildad) answers Job, Job 8:3–7 (Douay–Rheims):

> "Doth God pervert judgment, or doth the Almighty overthrow that which is just? Although thy children have sinned against him, and he hath left them in the hand of their iniquity: Yet if thou wilt arise early to God, and wilt beseech the Almighty: If thou wilt walk clean and upright, he will presently awake unto thee, and will make the dwelling of thy justice peaceable: In so much, that if thy former things were small thy latter things would be multiplied exceedingly."

Hebrew notes: 8:4 opens with *'im*, "if", not "although". In 8:7 "former things" is *reshit*, "beginning", and "latter things" is *acharit*, "end". The prologue told the reader that Job's children died when a violent wind struck the house where they were feasting (1:18–19). The epilogue reads: "the Lord blessed the latter end [*acharit*] of Job more than his beginning [*reshit*]" (42:12). The Douay's 42:10, "The Lord also was turned at the penance of Job, when he prayed for his friends", renders a Hebrew that says, in the lesson's translation, "the LORD turned back the fortune of Job when he prayed for his friends". It has no word for "penance".

(a) What does Baldad infer about Job's children, and why may the reader of the book not accept it? (b) An invented study-Bible note says: "42:12 repeats the very words of 8:7. Baldad was proved right in the end: Job repented and was restored, just as retribution predicts." Name two errors. **Two sentences per part.**

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):** Baldad argues from God's justice (8:3) to the children's guilt: they died, so they must have sinned and been handed over to their own transgression. That is the friends' retribution rule applied to the dead. The reader knows from the prologue that the disasters come from the wager in the heavenly court, and God himself calls Job's suffering *hinnam*, "without cause" (2:3). The narrator never gives the children's sin as a reason, and 42:7 rules that the friends "have not spoken the thing that is right".

**Must hit, strict (b):** any two of: (1) **Order.** God declares Job in the right and the friends in the wrong (42:7–8) *before* the restoration, so the restoration is not the proof of the verdict. (2) **"Job repented" leans on the Douay.** The Hebrew of 42:10 has no "penance". The turning comes "when he prayed for his friends", not when Job confessed the sins Baldad demanded. (3) **The echo works against the note, not for it.** The book gives Baldad's outcome (a greater *acharit*) while denying his premise (the children sinned, and Job must "walk clean" to be restored), and God says the friends spoke wrongly. (4) **Reported speech is not assertion.** Baldad's speech is recorded, not taught, so a later verbal echo does not turn it into the book's teaching.

**Wrong turns:** reading *'im* ("if") as softening Baldad into kindness. Baldad still makes the children's death a verdict on their sin. Treating 42:6's repentance as confession of guilt when God has just declared Job innocent. Saying the epilogue "contradicts" the dialogues and so must be a later addition. Whether the frame and the poetry had different origins is a separate question, and it does not change the reading of the book as it stands.

**Model answer:** (a) Baldad reasons that God cannot pervert justice, so Job's children must have sinned and been handed over to their own guilt. The reader knows from 1:18–19 and 2:3 that they died in a test God calls "without cause", and 42:7 condemns the friends' speech. (b) The restoration follows God's verdict for Job (42:7–8) and does not prove Baldad right. God rebukes the friends by name. And the Hebrew of 42:10 ties the turning to Job's prayer for his friends, not to a "penance" for sins he never committed.

</details>
