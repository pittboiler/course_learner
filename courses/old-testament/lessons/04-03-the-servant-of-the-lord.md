# The Old Testament · Lesson 4.3: The Servant of the Lord

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 4: The prophets · Builds on: [4.2 Isaiah and Immanuel](04-02-isaiah-and-immanuel.md), [1.2 The senses of Scripture](01-02-the-senses-of-scripture.md) · Unlocks: [4.4 Jeremiah and Ezekiel](04-04-jeremiah-and-ezekiel.md), [6.4 The Old Testament in light of the New](06-04-the-old-testament-in-light-of-the-new.md)

## Why this matters

In Isaiah 40–55 God keeps speaking of "my servant", usually plainly Israel. In four poems, though, the servant has a mission *to* Israel, suffers, and in the last dies "for our iniquities". Christians read these poems as the Old Testament's clearest portrait of Christ; Jewish tradition has mostly read them as a portrait of Israel. This lesson reads them first as words to the exiles, lays out the serious identifications, and asks how the Christian reading relates to the literal sense.

## The idea

The Hebrew *'ebed*, "servant, slave", carries the question. A king's *'ebed* is his trusted minister. In Isaiah 40–55 God's *'ebed* is first of all Jacob-Israel: "thou Israel, art my servant, Jacob whom I have chosen" (41:8; also 44:1, 44:21, 45:4). That servant is frightened, blind and deaf (42:19), and God comforts him.

In his commentary *Das Buch Jesaia* (1892) Bernhard Duhm pulled out four poems in which the servant is different: faithful, called from the womb, sent to Israel and the nations, abused without resisting. He called them the **[Servant Songs](../reference.md#servant-songs)**:

1. **42:1–4** (some extend it to 9): the chosen one who brings justice to the nations quietly. "The bruised reed he shall not break."
2. **49:1–6** (some to 13): the servant speaks, is named "Israel", and is sent to restore Israel and be "the light of the Gentiles".
3. **50:4–9** (some to 11): the disciple who does not turn his face from "them that ... spit upon me".
4. **52:13–53:12**: others speak of a servant despised, wounded "for our iniquities", "cut off out of the land of the living", and then exalted.

Duhm's separation of the Songs from their context is itself debated; many scholars now read them with the surrounding chapters. The question survives either way: **who is this servant?**

## Source

Acts 8:30–35 (Douay–Rheims). A court official of the Ethiopian queen is reading Isaiah 53:7–8 aloud in his chariot, in the Greek:

> "And Philip running thither, heard him reading the prophet Isaias. And he said: Thinkest thou that thou understandest what thou readest? Who said: And how can I, unless some man shew me? ... And the place of the scripture which he was reading was this: He was led as a sheep to the slaughter: and like a lamb without voice before his shearer, so openeth he not his mouth. In humility his judgment was taken away. His generation who shall declare, for his life shall be taken from the earth? And the eunuch answering Philip, said: I beseech thee, of whom doth the prophet speak this? Of himself, or of some other man? Then Philip, opening his mouth and beginning at this scripture, preached unto him Jesus."

Read it for the question. "Of himself, or of some other man?" is a literal-sense question: whom did the prophet mean? Philip does not argue about Isaiah's intention; he "begins at this scripture" and preaches Jesus. The quotation follows the Septuagint. "In humility his judgment was taken away" differs from the Hebrew of 53:8, which the Douay renders "taken away from distress, and from judgment".

## The argument

1. **The setting.** Isaiah 40–55 addresses exiles in Babylon and names Cyrus of Persia as God's "shepherd" and "anointed" (44:28; 45:1). Most scholars therefore date these chapters to the exile, around 550–539 BC. That date is **reconstructed**, a strong majority view tied to the Second Isaiah hypothesis weighed in [4.2](04-02-isaiah-and-immanuel.md). Cyrus's capture of Babylon in 539 is **secure** ([dating discipline](../reference.md#dating-discipline)).
2. **The collective reading: the Servant is Israel.** The context names Israel God's servant repeatedly, and the second Song names him outright, "Thou art my servant Israel, for in thee will I glory" (49:3). One medieval Hebrew manuscript alone lacks "Israel". So read, the fourth Song is the nations confessing that the people they despised suffered for them. It is the main line of medieval Jewish commentary. Rashi (1040–1105) glosses the servant of 42:1 as Jacob and the servant of 52:13 as Jacob, meaning the righteous within Israel. On 53:4 he has the nations admit that Israel's suffering brought *them* atonement. It is older than Rashi: Origen, writing around 248, recalls a debate with Jewish scholars ([Jewish readings](../reference.md#jewish-readings)):

   > "my Jewish opponent replied, that these predictions bore reference to the whole people, regarded as one individual, and as being in a state of dispersion and suffering, in order that many proselytes might be gained" (*Against Celsus* I.55, ANF vol. 4).

   Many modern scholars refine it to an *ideal* or faithful Israel.
3. **The strain: a servant sent to Israel.** In 49:5–6 the servant is formed "to bring back Jacob" and "to raise up the tribes of Jacob". Collective readers distinguish the faithful remnant from the nation at large. Individual readers take "Israel" in 49:3 as a title: one person who is what Israel was meant to be. In 49:5 the written Hebrew has *lo'*, "not", and the Douay follows it: "Israel will not be gathered together". The Jewish reading tradition pronounces the same letters *lo*, "to him": "that Israel be gathered to him". *In words:* the text calls the Servant Israel and then sends him to Israel, and both readings must explain the verse the other finds easy.
4. **The prophet himself.** The eunuch's first option. Songs two and three speak in the first person ("The Lord hath called me from the womb", 49:1). Rashi himself, collective in 42 and 53, reads 49:1–6 as the prophet speaking about his own call. Some modern scholars read the fourth Song as his disciples' reflection on his suffering or death, a live minority view.
5. **Cyrus?** A minority reads the first Song, at least, of Cyrus. God "called thee in justice, and taken thee by the hand" (42:6), much as he takes Cyrus's right hand (45:1). But Isaiah never calls Cyrus *'ebed*: he is called "for the sake of my servant Jacob" (45:4).
6. **The Targum: a Messiah who does not suffer.** The Aramaic paraphrase called Targum Jonathan reached its final form in the early centuries AD, from older material; its date is **disputed**. It opens the fourth Song "Behold, my servant the Messiah shall prosper" (52:13). The exaltation, the intercession for sins and the rebuilding of the Temple go to the Messiah. The contempt and the suffering are largely transferred to Israel. *In words:* the Targum is messianic and individual, but its Messiah does not suffer.
7. **The Christian reading.** The New Testament applies the Songs to Jesus: 42:1–4 in Matthew 12:18–21, 53:4 in Matthew 8:17, 53:12 in Luke 22:37, 53:7–8 in Acts 8, and 53:5–9 in 1 Peter 2:22–25. Challoner's note on 42:1 reads "Christ, who according to his humanity, is the servant of God." Chapter 53 as sacrifice is [`christology` 5.2](../../christology/lessons/05-02-ransom-christus-victor-and-sacrifice.md)'s work; the question here is how this reading relates to the literal sense ([1.2](01-02-the-senses-of-scripture.md)).
8. **The *sensus plenior*.** One Catholic answer is the **[fuller sense](../reference.md#sensus-plenior)**. The term goes back to Andrés Fernández in the 1920s, and Raymond Brown's doctoral dissertation (St Mary's, Baltimore, 1955) made it standard. Brown defined it as a deeper meaning intended by God but not clearly intended by the human author, seen when the text is read in light of later revelation. It let an exegete say both that Isaiah wrote of a figure in his own time and that God meant Christ. Critics asked how an intention the author did not have could be known, or whether the idea simply renamed typology, where the *realities* described point forward ([typology](../reference.md#typology)). The debate, lively in the 1950s–60s, is generally said to have faded after the Council. The Biblical Commission's *The Interpretation of the Bible in the Church* (1993, II.B.3) kept the term as a kind of spiritual sense, valid only when a later biblical text or an authentic doctrinal tradition controls it.

**Mark the levels.** That Christ fulfils the Servant prophecies is **Church teaching**. It is the New Testament's own reading, restated in CCC 601 (compare Dei Verbum 16). *How* the fulfilment relates to the prophet's meaning is **freely disputed among Catholics**: whether Isaiah foresaw Christ, whether God intended a fuller sense, or whether a type was fulfilled. The identity of the Servant in the literal sense (Israel, a remnant, the prophet, an unnamed individual) is a **scholarly question** with no magisterial answer. Challoner's notes are a translator's commentary, not teaching. The Biblical Commission's 1993 and 2001 documents are advisory scholarship, since the Commission became a consultative body in 1971 ([the Biblical Commission and its weight](../reference.md#the-biblical-commission-and-its-weight)). The 2001 document, *The Jewish People and their Sacred Scriptures in the Christian Bible*, calls the Jewish reading a "possible" one (no. 22).

## Map of positions

| Identification | Leans on | Holders | Where it strains |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Israel** (the nation, the faithful remnant, or ideal Israel) | 41:8, 44:1, 45:4; "my servant Israel" (49:3); the "we" of ch. 53 as the nations | Origen's Jewish interlocutor (c. 248); Rashi on 42 and 53; much modern scholarship | sent *to* Israel (49:5–6); the innocent sufferer (53:9) |
| **The prophet** | first person in 49:1–4 and 50:4–9; call "from the womb" | the eunuch's first guess; Rashi on 49; a modern minority | the scale of 49:6 and 52:13–15 |
| **Cyrus** | "taken thee by the hand" (42:6; cf. 45:1) | a minority, for the first Song only | Cyrus is never *'ebed*; the later Songs don't fit |
| **The Messiah, not suffering** | "my servant the Messiah" (Targum, 52:13) | Targum Jonathan | reassigns the suffering verses to Israel |
| **Jesus** | Acts 8:35; Matthew 8:17, 12:18–21; 1 Peter 2:22–25 | the New Testament and the Fathers | the literal sense: what the exilic author meant |

Rows 1–4 are literal-sense claims; row 5 works at a different level. A Catholic can hold row 1 or 2 as the literal sense *and* row 5 as fulfilment.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean): the third Song, 50:4–9.** *Literal sense first.* The speaker is a disciple: "The Lord hath given me a learned tongue" (50:4). He learns daily, does not rebel, and gives "my body to the strikers, and my cheeks to them that plucked them", and does not hide from "them that ... spit upon me" (50:6). He expects to be declared in the right in God's court: "He is near that justifieth me, who will contend with me?" (50:8). Genre: a first-person psalm of confidence. The first person favours the prophet or a figure like him; 50:10 then urges the audience to obey "the voice of his servant", which separates the servant from those addressed. *Spiritual sense second:* the evangelists' account of the mocking uses the same vocabulary (Matthew 26:67, spitting and striking). The fulfilment is clear, and it does not require that the prophet saw Jesus.

**Example 2 (hard): the first Song, 42:1–4.** The Hebrew names no one: "Behold my servant, I will uphold him: my elect". The Septuagint, a Jewish Greek translation (probably second century BC), adds names: "Jacob is my servant ... Israel is my chosen". It already reads the Song collectively. The following verses (42:6–7) echo the Cyrus oracle, and 42:19 calls the servant "blind". So within ten verses the text supports three candidates. **Where the method strains:** literal-sense exegesis can say which identification the *verses* favour, not whether the Songs had one referent at all. They may have been composed for a figure of the exiles' own time, then set in a book where "servant" also means Israel. That is a composition question, and it stays **disputed**.

## Watch out

- **"The Church teaches that Isaiah 53 was written about Jesus and foresaw him."** Fulfilment in Christ is Church teaching. That the prophet consciously foresaw Jesus is one Catholic position among several.
- **"The Israel reading is a medieval invention to avoid Christian claims."** Origen already met it around 248, and the Septuagint names Israel in 42:1 before Christ. Whether it is right is argued in [`apologetics-foundations`](../../apologetics-foundations/syllabus.md), not here.
- **Citing the 2001 document as papal teaching.** Ratzinger prefaced it as the Commission's president; it remains an advisory body's work.

## One-liner

> The Servant is Israel, the prophet, or a figure no one can name in the literal sense, and Christ in the Church's reading; the Catholic question is how those two levels meet, not which one cancels the other.

**P1 (🟢)** *(Exegetical)* Give each claim a status: **Church teaching** (name the text), **scholarly position** (majority or minority), **Jewish reading** (name the source) or **advisory document**. One line each.

1. "Christ's death fulfils Isaiah's prophecy of the suffering Servant."
2. "Isaiah 40–55 was composed during the Babylonian exile."
3. "The servant of Isaiah 42:1 is Jacob."
4. "The Jewish reading of the Bible is a possible reading."

**P2 (🟡)** *(Exegetical)* Isaiah 49:3–6 (Douay–Rheims):

> "And he said to me: Thou art my servant Israel, for in thee will I glory. And I said: I have laboured in vain, I have spent my strength without cause and in vain: therefore my judgment is with the Lord, and my work with my God. And now saith the Lord, that formed me from the womb to be his servant, that I may bring back Jacob unto him, and Israel will not be gathered together: and I am glorified in the eyes of the Lord, and my God is made my strength. And he said: It is a small thing that thou shouldst be my servant to raise up the tribes of Jacob, and to convert the dregs of Israel. Behold, I have given thee to be the light of the Gentiles, that thou mayst be my salvation even to the farthest part of the earth."

(a) Name the words that best support a collective reading and those that best support an individual one, two each. (b) The Hebrew of "will not be gathered" can be read "*lo'*, not" (as written) or "*lo*, to him" (as traditionally pronounced). Say what each reading does to the verse. (c) State Rashi's reading of 49:1–6 in one sentence. **100 words in total.**

**P3 (🔴)** *(Exegetical)* (a) **Find the crux.** Position A: the Servant of 49:3–6 is the faithful remnant, sent to restore the rest of the nation. Position B: he is one individual given the title "Israel". Name the single premise one side must deny, and say what evidence would move it. (b) Matthew 12:18–21 (Douay–Rheims) quotes Isaiah 42:1–4: "Behold my servant whom I have chosen, my beloved ... I will put my spirit upon him ... And in his name the Gentiles shall hope." Compare Isaiah 42:4 (Douay–Rheims, from the Hebrew): "the islands shall wait for his law". The Septuagint's 42:1 reads "Jacob is my servant ... Israel is my chosen" and its 42:4 reads "in his name shall the Gentiles trust". Say what Matthew takes from the Greek, what he does not take, and what the omission does to the identification. **Two sentences per part.**

<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict:** (1) **Church teaching**: CCC 601, restating the New Testament (Acts 8:35; Matthew 8:17). (2) **Scholarly position**: the majority view, and a **reconstructed** date, not Church teaching. (3) **Jewish reading**: Rashi on 42:1. Credit also the Septuagint's "Jacob is my servant", a pre-Christian Jewish rendering. (4) **Advisory document**: the Biblical Commission's 2001 document, no. 22. It is not a magisterial act. (5) The *sensus plenior* theory, **freely disputed among Catholics**: Brown's 1955 formulation. The 1993 document keeps the term under controls, and that document is also advisory.

**Wrong turns:** marking (4) as Church teaching because Ratzinger wrote the preface. Marking (5) as Church teaching because the 1993 document uses the term. Marking (2) as "settled" or as contrary to Church teaching.

**Model answer:** (1) Church teaching, CCC 601. (2) Majority scholarly position, reconstructed date. (3) Jewish reading, Rashi (and already the Septuagint). (4) Advisory document, PBC 2001 no. 22. (5) Freely disputed Catholic theory (Brown), kept in advisory form by PBC 1993.

**P2** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** collective: **"my servant Israel"** (3), and **"in thee will I glory"**, God glorified in his people, as in 44:23. Individual: **"formed me from the womb"** (5) and a mission **to "bring back Jacob" / "raise up the tribes of Jacob"** (5–6), so the servant stands over against Israel. Credit also "I have laboured in vain" (4), a personal career.

**Must hit, strict (b):** "not" (Douay, Vulgate) makes the servant's mission to Israel **fail**, which leads into the turn to the Gentiles in verse 6. "To him" makes it a mission to gather Israel **to God**, which verse 6 then enlarges. Either way the servant acts on Israel.

**Must hit, strict (c):** Rashi takes the speaker of 49:1–6 to be **the prophet himself**, called from the womb to prophesy consolation. God's "light of the nations" charge means prophesying Babylon's fall.

**Wrong turns:** claiming Rashi reads this Song of the nation, as he does 42:1 and 52:13. Citing 49:3's "Israel" as decisive: the individual reading reads it as a title.

**Model answer:** (a) Collective: "my servant Israel", "in thee will I glory". Individual: "formed me from the womb", sent "to raise up the tribes of Jacob". (b) "Not" makes Israel refuse the gathering and motivates verse 6's turn to the nations. "To him" states the gathering as the goal. (c) Rashi reads it of Isaiah himself.

**P3** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** the crux is **whether "Israel" in 49:3 names a group within the nation or is a title given to one person**. Equivalently: whether the one sent to Israel can be *part* of Israel. A reads the singular "I" and "from the womb" as a personified collective, which Isaiah 40–55 does for Jacob-Israel elsewhere (44:2, 24). B must deny that the personification extends here. Evidence that would move it: whether the rest of chapters 40–55 uses "from the womb" of the *nation* (it does: in the Hebrew of 44:2 and 44:24 God formed Jacob "from the womb", which helps A). Or whether the first-person career details (labour "in vain", 49:4) fit a group (which helps B if they do not).

**Must hit, strict (b):** Matthew takes the Greek's **"in his name the Gentiles shall hope"** (12:21) over the Hebrew's "the islands shall wait for his law". He does **not** take the Septuagint's added **"Jacob" and "Israel"**, so his text names no one and leaves the servant free to be Jesus.

**Wrong turns:** making the crux "whether Israel means the nation", which both sides can grant elsewhere in 40–55. Saying Matthew simply quotes the Septuagint: his wording also departs from it ("my beloved", "whom I have chosen").

**Model answer:** (a) The crux is whether "Israel" in 49:3 is a collective personified or a title for one person, since both agree the servant is sent to Israel. That the same book says God formed the nation "from the womb" (44:2, 24) supports the collective side. First-person details that no group could have would support the individual side. (b) Matthew follows the Greek in "in his name the Gentiles shall hope", but drops its "Jacob" and "Israel". His quotation therefore carries no collective label, and it can point to one person.

</details>

## Flashback

**From Lesson [4.1](04-01-what-a-prophet-is-amos-and-hosea.md) (What a prophet is: Amos and Hosea):** *(Exegetical.)* An **invented** study-bible note on Hosea:

> "Hosea's marriage to Gomer was a vision, not an event: the Church has settled this, since God could not command a prophet to marry a harlot. When Hosea 4:1 says the Lord 'shall enter into judgment' (Hebrew *rib*), he uses the fixed legal form that all Israel's prophets used for every oracle, as *Divino Afflante Spiritu* confirms. The 'mercy' of 4:1 and 6:6 means God's pity on sinners. Like Isaiah, Hosea preached to Jerusalem. The Holy Spirit spoke through him, as the Creed says."

(a) Name four errors and, for each, the text, reading or teaching that corrects it. (b) Name the sentence that is right and give its level. **150 words or fewer.**

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):** (1) **"The Church has settled" event versus vision.** Nothing magisterial settles it. Hosea 1 narrates it as fact ("went and took Gomer"), the Talmud (*Pesachim* 87a–b) takes it as real, Maimonides (*Guide* II.46) as a prophetic vision, and many moderns as a real marriage read as a sign-act. (2) **The *rib* as a fixed form used for every oracle.** The lawsuit genre is a form-critical hypothesis (Huffmon, Wright), doubted by De Roche and others. Prophets also use the judgment speech, the woe oracle and more. *Divino Afflante Spiritu* encouraged the study of forms but endorsed no classification. (3) **"Mercy" as pity.** It is *hesed*, loyal covenant love owed both ways. In 4:1 it is what *Israel* lacks, a charge, not a comfort, and in 6:6 it is what God asks of Israel. (4) **Hosea preached to Jerusalem.** He is the northern kingdom's own prophet, addressing Israel and Samaria before 722.

**Must hit, strict (b):** "The Holy Spirit spoke through him": the Creed's "spake by the Prophets" (Constantinople I, 381), **creedal**, rung 1.

**Wrong turns:** overcorrecting to "the marriage was certainly real" or "certainly a sign-act"; the point is that it is open. Treating De Roche's doubt as the Church's position. Glossing *rib* as anything other than a legal case or dispute.

**Model answer:** (1) Event versus vision is open: the Talmud reads a real marriage, Maimonides a vision, moderns often a sign-act, and no Church teaching decides. (2) The covenant lawsuit is a contested form-critical hypothesis, one form among several, and *Divino Afflante Spiritu* endorsed no classification. (3) "Mercy" is *hesed*, covenant loyalty, and 4:1 charges Israel with lacking it. (4) Hosea prophesied to the northern kingdom, not Jerusalem. Right: the Spirit "spake by the Prophets" is creedal, rung 1.

</details>
