# The Old Testament · Lesson 6.2: Tobit, Judith, Baruch, and the Maccabees

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 6: Canon, deuterocanon, apocalyptic, and the Old in light of the New · Builds on: [6.1 How the Old Testament canon formed](06-01-how-the-old-testament-canon-formed.md), [5.4 Sirach and the Wisdom of Solomon](05-04-sirach-and-the-wisdom-of-solomon.md) · Unlocks: [6.3 Daniel and apocalyptic](06-03-daniel-and-apocalyptic.md)

## Why this matters

[6.1](06-01-how-the-old-testament-canon-formed.md) told how these books were argued over. This lesson reads them. They are late and very different from one another. Later doctrine leans on three verses of 2 Maccabees, so you need to know exactly what those verses say before you hear what is built on them.

## The idea

Ask two questions of each book, in order: **what kind of writing is it**, and **what does it contribute**. Genre comes first: it fixes what the book asserts. Dei Verbum 12 says truth is told differently in texts that are "variously historical" ([literary forms](../reference.md#literary-forms)).

- **Tobit** is an edifying family story set among the exiles in Nineveh.
- **Judith** is a novella that signals in its first verses that it is not a chronicle.
- **Baruch** is an anthology of prayer, wisdom poem and consolation. Its chapter 6 is a separate work, the Letter of Jeremiah.
- **Greek Esther** adds six passages to the Hebrew book. They put God's name into a book that never uses it.
- **1 Maccabees** is sober dynastic history. **2 Maccabees** retells part of the same story as theology, with martyrs, miracles and prayers.

All are **[deuterocanonical](../reference.md#deuterocanonical-books)**: in the Greek Old Testament the Church received, absent from the Hebrew collection. The label records a history of dispute, not a lower rank ([`fundamental-theology` 3.3](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/03-03-the-canon-as-defined.md)).

## Source

2 Maccabees 7:27–29 (Douay–Rheims). Antiochus has killed six brothers for refusing pork. He urges their mother to save the youngest.

> So bending herself towards him, mocking the cruel tyrant, she said in her own language: My son have pity upon me, that bore thee nine months in my womb, and gave thee suck three years, and nourished thee, and brought thee up unto this age. I beseech thee, my son, look upon heaven and earth, and all that is in them, and consider that God made them out of nothing, and mankind also: So thou shalt not fear this tormentor, but being made a worthy partner with thy brethren, receive death, that in that mercy I may receive thee again with thy brethren.

The argument runs from **origin to hope**. The God who made the world and the child without any material to start from can give the child back. "Out of nothing" renders the Greek *ouk ex ontōn*, "not out of things that existed". Some editions order the words *ex ouk ontōn*, with the same sense. Her sons say the same: the King of the world "will raise us up ... in the resurrection of eternal life" (7:9).

## The argument

1. **Tobit.** Tobit, an exile of the northern tribes in Nineveh, buries the dead against the king's order and is blinded. The angel Raphael, in disguise, heals him and frees Sara from a demon for Tobit's son Tobias, then reveals himself: "I am the angel Raphael, one of the seven, who stand before the Lord" (12:15; compare the seven angels of Rev 8:2). **Texts:** fragments of four Aramaic copies and one Hebrew copy were found at Qumran (4Q196–199, 4Q200), published by Joseph Fitzmyer in 1995. They generally support the longer Greek form (Codex Sinaiticus), which most modern translations follow. The Douay follows the Vulgate, which Jerome made in "one day's hasty" work from an Aramaic text read out to him (preface to Tobit, as summarised in NPNF 2nd ser. vol. 6). **Date:** reconstructed, usually the third or early second century BC. The eighth-century setting is the story's frame, not its date. *In words:* providence working through ordinary piety.

2. **Judith.** "Nabuchodonosor king of the Assyrians, who reigned in Ninive" (Jdt 1:1 in the Greek; DR 1:5) is a double impossibility. Nineveh fell in 612 and Nebuchadnezzar ruled Babylon from 605 to 562 (both **secure**). Yet the Jews in the story have recently come back from captivity and hold Jerusalem again (DR 5:22–23; Greek 4:3, 5:19). Holofernes and Bagoas are Persian names; Bethulia is unknown. Most modern exegetes, Catholic ones included, read these as **signals of genre**. Piling up history's great enemies, the author writes a parable: Israel's God defeats every empire through a widow's hand. **Date:** reconstructed, Hasmonean, around the late second century BC. The original was probably Hebrew but is lost; the book survives in Greek. Older commentators, Challoner among them, defended its history by identifying the king with another ruler.

3. **Baruch and the Letter of Jeremiah.** Baruch claims to be read aloud to the exiles in Babylon (1:1–4). It has three parts. The first is a penitential prayer close to Daniel 9. The second is a wisdom poem that ends "This is the book of the commandments of God, and the law, that is for ever" (4:1), identifying wisdom with Torah as Sirach 24 does ([5.4](05-04-sirach-and-the-wisdom-of-solomon.md)). The third is a consolation of Jerusalem. The Fathers read 3:38, "Afterwards he was seen upon earth, and conversed with men", of the Incarnation. So does Augustine, who gives it to Jeremiah (*City of God* XVIII.33). In context the subject is wisdom. **Date:** disputed, most often second century BC. DR Baruch 6 is the **Letter of Jeremiah**, a satire on idols. The Greek Bibles print it as a separate book. A small Greek fragment from Qumran Cave 7 (7Q2) is usually identified as part of it.

4. **Greek Esther.** The Hebrew Esther never names God, and [3.4](03-04-exile-and-return.md) read it as a counter-voice. The Greek adds six passages: Mordecai's dream, two royal edicts, the prayers of Mordecai and Esther, Esther's audience with the king, and the dream's interpretation with a closing note. Jerome moved them to the end of his Latin text, so in the Douay they run from **10:4 to 16:24**. The Douay's first added verse is the dream's interpretation: "Then Mardochai said: God hath done these things" (10:4). The closing note (DR 11:1) dates the Greek translation's arrival to "the fourth year of the reign of Ptolemy and Cleopatra", taken as 114 or 78/77 BC (**disputed**). Trent's "entire with all their parts" covers them.

5. **1 Maccabees.** It runs from Antiochus IV Epiphanes' accession to Simon's murder, 175 to about 134 BC: the Hasmonean house's own story. Jerome found it "to be Hebrew" (the helmeted preface, NPNF 2nd ser. vol. 6), but only the Greek survives. It is usually dated about 100 BC (reconstructed). It gives dates by the **Seleucid era**, counted from 312/311 BC, so year 145 is 167 BC: $312 - 145 = 167$. Antiochus set "the abominable idol of desolation upon the altar" on 15 Casleu, year 145 (1:57). Judas rededicated the altar on 25 Casleu, year 148, that is 164 BC (4:52), and decreed an eight-day feast (4:59). These **[Maccabean crisis](../reference.md#the-maccabean-crisis)** dates are **secure**.

6. **2 Maccabees.** The author says he condenses into one book what "Jason, of Cyrene" wrote "in five books" (2:24), and he ends by asking pardon if he has done it "not so perfectly" (15:39). It covers about 180 to 161 BC, in Greek; a letter prefixed to it is dated 124 BC. It gives the martyrs of chapters 6–7, and in 12:38–46 Judas has a sin-offering made for dead soldiers, found wearing idol charms, "thinking well and religiously concerning the resurrection". Where the Douay says twelve thousand drachms, the Greek says two thousand.

**Mark the levels.** That all seven books and the Greek parts of Esther are sacred and canonical is **defined dogma** (Trent, Session IV; [`fundamental-theology` 3.3](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/03-03-the-canon-as-defined.md)). Three doctrines cite these chapters, and each is **defined on its own grounds and owned elsewhere**: creation out of nothing (Lateran IV, Vatican I; CCC 297 cites 2 Macc 7:28), the resurrection of the body (CCC 992 cites 2 Macc 7:9, 7:14), and purgatory (Florence, Trent; CCC 1032 cites 2 Macc 12:46 for the *practice* of prayer for the dead). All three belong to [`creation-grace-last-things`](../../creation-grace-last-things/syllabus.md). Whether Judith or Tobit is history, and every date of writing above, are **scholarly judgments with no magisterial level** ([church teaching vs hypothesis](../reference.md#church-teaching-vs-hypothesis); [dating discipline](../reference.md#dating-discipline)). The Biblical Commission's 1905 response on narratives only apparently historical demanded solid proof before reading a book as non-history: binding when issued, its force since changed ([1.3](01-03-the-historical-critical-method-and-its-limits.md); [the Biblical Commission and its weight](../reference.md#the-biblical-commission-and-its-weight)).

## The synopsis

| Book | Genre | Original language (what survives) | Date of writing | Later use |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Tobit | edifying family tale | Aramaic or Hebrew (Qumran fragments; Greek, two forms) | 3rd–early 2nd c. BC, reconstructed | angels (Raphael, 12:15); marriage prayer (ch. 8) |
| Judith | novella | probably Hebrew (Greek) | late 2nd c. BC, reconstructed | Marian typology: the woman who defeats the enemy |
| Baruch 1–5 | prayer, wisdom poem, consolation | Hebrew for part, disputed (Greek) | disputed | 3:38 read of the Incarnation |
| Letter of Jeremiah (DR Bar 6) | satire on idols | disputed (Greek; 7Q2) | disputed | against idolatry |
| Greek Esther | additions to a court tale | mostly Greek, some possibly Semitic | before 114 or 78/77 BC | God named; prayers |
| 1 Maccabees | dynastic history | Hebrew (Greek) | c. 100 BC, reconstructed | Hanukkah (4:59) |
| 2 Maccabees | theological history, an epitome | Greek | late 2nd c. BC, reconstructed | creation out of nothing, resurrection, prayer for the dead |

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean): Hanukkah from three sides.** The text: Judas decrees the feast of the dedication for eight days from 25 Casleu (1 Macc 4:59). Year 148 of the Seleucid era is 164 BC, a **secure** date. Josephus says they "call it Lights" (*Antiquities* XII.7.7, Whiston). Jesus walks in the Temple at "the feast of the dedication" in winter (John 10:22; [`new-testament` 2.4](../../new-testament/lessons/02-04-john-and-the-synoptics.md)). The [Jewish reading](../reference.md#jewish-readings) of the feast is the Talmud's. Babylonian Talmud *Shabbat* 21b asks "What is Hanukkah?" and answers with a cruse of oil, sealed by the high priest, that burned for eight days. The rabbis tell the miracle of the oil, not the victory: their own tradition, not a gloss on 1 Maccabees, which is outside the Jewish canon.

**Example 2 (hard): which Tobit is canonical?** The Douay (from the Vulgate), the short Greek and the long Greek differ in whole verses. Trent defined the *book*, "entire with all their parts, as they have been used to be read in the Catholic Church, and as they are contained in the old Latin vulgate edition" (Schaff). Does that make the Vulgate's wording the inspired text? No. The Vulgate's authority is juridical, a guarantee that the Latin is free of error in faith and morals, not a ruling on which manuscript preserves the original. Qumran's Aramaic favouring the long Greek is **textual criticism**, and Dei Verbum 22 asks for translations from the original texts. The canonical Tobit is the book the Church reads; which wording its author wrote is a question for the evidence.

## Watch out

- **Teaching stated as hypothesis.** "Scholars say Judith is fiction, so it is not really Scripture." Its canonicity is defined. Genre decides what it asserts, not whether it is inspired.
- **Hypothesis stated as teaching.** "The Church teaches that Judith is history." No act defines this. The 1905 response set a burden of proof in its day. It did not rule on Judith.
- **"2 Maccabees proves purgatory."** The text shows a Jewish **practice** of prayer for the dead, grounded in hope of resurrection. The doctrine was defined by councils and does not stand on one verse. Challoner's note calling it "evident and undeniable proof" is a translator's comment, not magisterium.
- **Reading a later dogma back into 7:28.** Scholars dispute whether it already means *absolute* nothing or only "not from anything that was there before" (so Jonathan Goldstein's commentary). Gerhard May's study of the doctrine's origins (1978) argues that the technical formula came in second-century Christian writers. The Church cites the verse as a witness (CCC 297), not as the definition.

## One-liner

> Read genre first: Tobit and Judith are stories, Baruch an anthology, Greek Esther a retelling, 1 Maccabees history and 2 Maccabees theology; all are defined as canonical, and none of the doctrines that cite them rests on them alone.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢)** *(Exegetical (a) · Exegetical (b))* 2 Maccabees 12:42–46 (Douay–Rheims), after Judas's men find idol charms under the tunics of soldiers killed in battle:

> And so betaking themselves to prayers, they besought him, that the sin which had been committed might be forgotten. ... And making a gathering, he sent twelve thousand drachms of silver to Jerusalem for sacrifice to be offered for the sins of the dead, thinking well and religiously concerning the resurrection. (For if he had not hoped that they that were slain should rise again, it would have seemed superfluous and vain to pray for the dead,) And because he considered that they who had fallen asleep with godliness, had great grace laid up for them. It is therefore a holy and wholesome thought to pray for the dead, that they may be loosed from sins.

(a) State what Judas does, for whom, and the reason the text gives, naming the words that decide it. Name one thing the text does **not** say. (b) Give the level of each claim: (i) 2 Maccabees is canonical Scripture; (ii) purgatory exists; (iii) this passage by itself proves purgatory. **Two sentences for (a); one line per claim in (b).**

**P2 (🟡)** *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b))* Judith (Douay–Rheims): "Now in the twelfth year of his reign, Nabuchodonosor king of the Assyrians, who reigned in Ninive the great city, fought against Arphaxad" (1:5). Achior later tells Holofernes that the Israelites were "led away captive into a strange land", but "of late returning to the Lord their God ... possess Jerusalem again" (5:22–23). Challoner's note on 1:5 says this is "not the king of Babylon, who took and destroyed Jerusalem, but another of the same name, who reigned in Ninive", contemporary with Manasseh. (a) List three genre signals in these verses and the fact that makes each one a signal. Say why Challoner's identification cannot account for 5:23. (b) Is Judith best read as a deliberately unhistorical novella? Give either verdict, name the evidence each side leans on, and say what inspiration does and does not require of the answer. **150 words or fewer for (b).**

**P3 (🔴)** *(Exegetical)* An **invented** note from a study Bible:

> "The Hebrew Esther is famous for never mentioning God. Later Greek scribes found this embarrassing and inserted prayers to fix it. Since these additions are late and not in the original Hebrew, Catholic readers may safely skip them: the Church's real Esther is the Hebrew one."

(a) What does the note get right as history? (b) Name the level error in the last sentence and correct it. (c) State how the Talmud accounts for Esther's silence about God, as its own tradition states it. **150 words or fewer in all.**

<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical (a) · Exegetical (b))*

**Must hit, strict (a):** Judas collects money and sends it to Jerusalem for a **sin-offering for the dead soldiers**, men who died carrying idol charms. The reason is **hope of resurrection**: "thinking well and religiously concerning the resurrection", "if he had not hoped that they ... should rise again", and "great grace laid up" for those who "had fallen asleep with godliness". The text does **not** name a place or state of purification after death, does not describe what happens to the dead before the resurrection, and does not say how the offering helps. (Any one.)
**Must hit, strict (b):** (i) **Defined dogma** (Trent, Session IV). (ii) **Defined dogma** (Florence; Trent, Session XXV), a doctrine owned by `creation-grace-last-things`. (iii) **Not Church teaching.** The Church cites the passage as a scriptural witness to the *practice* of prayer for the dead (CCC 1032), alongside other grounds. "Proves by itself" is a polemical overstatement, and so is Challoner's "undeniable proof", which is only a translator's note.
**Wrong turns:** reading the passage's motive as the avoidance of purgatorial fire, when the text gives hope of resurrection. Marking (iii) as defined because (i) and (ii) are. Swinging the other way and calling the passage irrelevant to the doctrine, when it is the Church's own cited witness to the practice.
**Model answer:** (a) Judas sends silver to Jerusalem for a sacrifice "for the sins of the dead", soldiers who fell wearing idol charms, because he hoped they would "rise again" and that those who died "with godliness" had grace laid up for them. The text says nothing of a place of purification or of how the offering helps. (b) (i) Defined, Trent IV. (ii) Defined, Florence and Trent XXV. (iii) Not teaching: the Church cites the passage as a witness to the practice of prayer for the dead, not as a proof on its own.

**P2** *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b))*

**Must hit, strict (a):** three of these: (1) **"Nabuchodonosor king of the Assyrians"**: Nebuchadnezzar was king of *Babylon* (605–562). (2) **"Reigned in Ninive"**: Nineveh fell in 612, before his reign. (3) **"Arphaxad"**, a king of the Medes whom no other source names (Challoner's note on 1:1 guesses Herodotus's Deioces). (4) **5:22–23, a people back from exile, holding Jerusalem again**: a post-exilic setting, after 539, which no Assyrian king ever saw. Challoner's king reigned in Manasseh's time, in the seventh century, **before** the exile, so the returned community of 5:23 cannot fit his date.
**Must hit, any verdict (b):** state both positions. **Novella:** the anachronisms are too many and too famous to be slips, and the names and plot are typological (*Yehudit*, "Jewess"; a widow beheading the empire). **Historical kernel or history:** the older Catholic and patristic reading, identifications like Challoner's, and the 1905 response's demand for solid proof. Then say what inspiration requires: that the book teach without error what its **genre** asserts (Dei Verbum 11–12). It does not require that every narrative be chronicle. A verdict either way leaves canonicity untouched.
**Wrong turns:** calling the anachronisms errors that inspiration forbids, which assumes the genre in question. Stating the novella reading as Church teaching, or the historical reading as binding because of the 1905 response.
**Model answer (b), one of several:** Yes. The first verse puts Babylon's king on Assyria's throne in a city that had already fallen, and chapter 5 sets him after the exile. An author who knew that Nebuchadnezzar destroyed Jerusalem is not confused about it. He is gathering every empire into one enemy. Challoner's harmonisation fixes 1:5 and breaks on 5:23. The best case for history is the book's wealth of circumstantial detail and the older reading of the tradition, and the 1905 response asked for solid proof, which I think the anachronisms supply. Inspiration requires that Judith teach truly what a novella asserts: God saves the lowly who trust him. It does not require that the siege of Bethulia happened.

**P3** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict:** (a) Right: the Hebrew never names God, and the additions (DR 10:4–16:24) are **later and Greek**, with some perhaps drawn from a Semitic source. Their motive ("embarrassing") is a guess, but a common scholarly one. (b) The error is making the Hebrew alone "the Church's real Esther". Trent defined the books "entire with all their parts" (**defined dogma**; [`fundamental-theology` 3.3](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/03-03-the-canon-as-defined.md)). The additions are canonical Scripture for Catholics, whatever their date. Late origin is a historical judgment and does not settle canonicity. (c) *Chullin* 139b finds Esther in the Torah in Deut 31:18, "I will surely hide (*haster astir*) my face". God's hiddenness is the point of the book, and his name's absence is how the book says it.
**Wrong turns:** denying the additions are late in order to defend them. Treating the Talmud's reading as a failure to see God, when it is a theology of divine hiddenness.
**Model answer:** The note is right that the Hebrew never names God and that the Greek additions are later. The reason for them is a plausible guess. It is wrong that Catholics may treat them as optional. Trent defined Esther "with all their parts", so the additions are canonical whatever their date of writing. The Talmud (*Chullin* 139b) hears Esther's name in God's "I will surely hide (*haster*) my face" (Deut 31:18). On that reading, the hiddenness is the book's theology.

## Flashback

**From Lesson [5.4](05-04-sirach-and-the-wisdom-of-solomon.md) (Sirach and the Wisdom of Solomon):** *(Exegetical.)* Two prayers for wisdom (Douay–Rheims). First, Solomon's prayer in a dream at Gibeon, 1 Kings 3:7, 9 [DR 3 Kings 3:7, 9]. "An understanding heart" renders Hebrew *lev shomea*, "a listening heart":

> And now, O Lord God, thou hast made thy servant king instead of David, my father: and I am but a child, and know not how to go out and come in; ... Give therefore to thy servant an understanding heart, to judge thy people, and discern between good and evil.

Second, Wisdom of Solomon 9:4, 7–10, 15. "That sitteth by thy throne" renders Greek *paredros*, "the one seated beside":

> Give me wisdom, that sitteth by thy throne, and cast me not off from among thy children ... Thou hast chosen me to be king of thy people, and a judge of thy sons and daughters: And hast commanded me to build a temple on thy holy mount ... And thy wisdom with thee, which knoweth thy works, which then also was present when thou madest the world ... Send her out of thy holy heaven, and from the throne of thy majesty, that she may be with me, and may labour with me ... For the corruptible body is a load upon the soul, and the earthly habitation presseth down the mind that museth upon many things.

(a) Name two things Wisdom 9 keeps from the Gibeon prayer and two things it adds. What has happened to wisdom between the two texts? (b) An **invented** study-Bible note on 9:7–8 says: "Here Solomon speaks in his own person, so the Church holds him to be the author; to deny it is to doubt the book's inspiration." Name the error. Give the correct level for the book's authorship and for its canonicity, and name one feature of the passage that points away from Solomon. **Two sentences per part.**

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):** *Kept* (any two): the speaker is **the king God chose** over his people; he asks for wisdom **in order to judge and govern God's people**; he asks **in humility**, as a servant who cannot rule without it. *Added* (any two): the **command to build the temple** (9:8); wisdom **enthroned beside God** (*paredros*) and **present at creation** (9:9), which is Proverbs 8's Lady Wisdom; wisdom **sent from heaven to labour with** the king (9:10); a Greek **body-and-soul** line (9:15). What has happened: in 3 Kings wisdom is a **faculty God puts into the king**, a listening heart. In Wisdom 9 she is **personified**, a figure at God's throne whom God sends. That is 5.4's "translation" of Lady Wisdom into the language of the Greek schools. Her literal sense is still God's own wisdom personified, not a second person.
**Must hit, strict (b):** the error is to read the **first-person royal voice as an authorship claim** and then as Church teaching. Speaking as Solomon is a **literary convention**: the book never names him, and chapter 9 retells the Gibeon prayer in his voice. **Authorship** is a scholarly question with **no magisterial level**, and non-Solomonic authorship is the consensus. **Canonicity** is **defined dogma** (Trent, Session IV), and it rests on inspiration and reception, not on the name ([canonicity vs authorship](../reference.md#canonicity-vs-authorship)). Pointing away from Solomon: **9:15's body-as-burden-on-the-soul**, a Platonist commonplace, from a book composed in Greek with no known Hebrew original.
**Wrong turns:** correcting the note by saying "the Church teaches Solomon did not write it", when no act rules either way. Concluding "not Solomon, so not canonical". Reading 9:9 as the author's teaching of a second divine person. Calling Wisdom 9 a contradiction of 3 Kings 3, when it is an expansion of it.
**Model answer:** (a) Both kings confess that God made them king and ask for wisdom to judge his people. Wisdom 9 adds the temple commission and a wisdom who sits by God's throne, was present at creation and is sent down to work beside the king. So Kings' listening heart, a gift inside Solomon, has become Proverbs' Lady Wisdom in Greek dress: God's wisdom personified. (b) The note takes a literary voice, speaking as Solomon, for an authorship claim, and then makes that claim teaching. Authorship has no magisterial level (the consensus is "not Solomon"), while canonicity is defined at Trent and does not depend on who wrote the book. And 9:15's burdened soul is Greek philosophy, not tenth-century Jerusalem.
