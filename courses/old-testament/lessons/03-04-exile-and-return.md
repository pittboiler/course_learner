# The Old Testament · Lesson 3.4: Exile and return

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 3: From conquest to return · Builds on: [3.3 Two kingdoms and two falls](03-03-two-kingdoms-and-two-falls.md), [3.2 David and the house of David](03-02-david-and-the-house-of-david.md) · Unlocks: [4.2 Isaiah and Immanuel](04-02-isaiah-and-immanuel.md), [4.4 Jeremiah and Ezekiel](04-04-jeremiah-and-ezekiel.md), [6.1 How the Old Testament canon formed](06-01-how-the-old-testament-canon-formed.md)

## Why this matters

In 587/586 Judah lost the three things its faith seemed to stand on: the land, the king and the Temple. Its religion came back as something new: a people gathered round a written Law, read aloud and explained. Much of the Old Testament was collected, edited or written in the century and a half after the fall, so the exile is the vantage point from which much of the story was told.

## The idea

Think of exile as a stress test, with three answers written into the canon.

- **The prophetic answer.** The disaster was judgment, not Babylon's gods defeating Israel's, and the same God can use a pagan emperor to end it. Isaiah calls Cyrus of Persia God's *anointed* (45:1).
- **The reformers' answer.** The community survives by holding to the Torah and keeping its boundaries sharp: the public reading of the Law (Nehemiah 8), and the dissolution of marriages with foreign women (Ezra 9–10).
- **The counter-voices.** Ruth, a Moabite woman, becomes David's great-grandmother. Esther saves her people in a Persian court in a book whose Hebrew text never names God.

The canon keeps all three without harmonising them.

## Source

Nehemiah 8:1–3, 8–9 (Douay–Rheims, 2 Esdras 8), the day the returned community heard the Law:

> And the seventh month came: and the children of Israel were in their cities. And all the people were gathered together as one man to the street which is before the water gate, and they spoke to Esdras the scribe, to bring the book of the law of Moses ... And he read it plainly in the street that was before the water gate, from the morning until midday, before the men, and the women, and all those that could understand ... And they read in the book of the law of God distinctly and plainly to be understood: and they understood when it was read. And Nehemias ... and Esdras the priest and scribe, and the Levites who interpreted to all the people, said: This is a holy day to the Lord our God: do not mourn, nor weep: for all the people wept, when they heard the words of the law.

Read it for what is missing: no king, no sacrifice, no prophet. The people ask for a **book**, a scribe reads it, and Levites **interpret** it to men and women alike. The Babylonian Talmud (*Nedarim* 37b) reads verse 8 phrase by phrase, taking "distinctly" as the Aramaic translation, the Targum. On that reading the synagogue's practice of reading and translating Scripture begins here. History or not, the scene describes a religion centred on a text.

## The argument

1. **Three deportations.** Nebuchadnezzar took Jerusalem in 597, and the Babylonian Chronicle dates the capture to his seventh year. He deported King Jehoiachin and the elite. The city fell in 587/586, and a third deportation followed in 582/581. Jeremiah 52:28–30 counts 3,023, then 832, then 745 people, "four thousand six hundred" in all. Probably adult males or a partial tally, but most of Judah stayed in the land. *In words:* "the exile" was the removal of the leading class, not the emptying of a country.

2. **Faith reshaped in Babylon.** Jeremiah's letter tells the exiles to build houses, marry, and pray for Babylon's peace (Jer 29:5–7). Practices that need no Temple rose to prominence: circumcision, the Sabbath, the food laws. Many scholars date the Priestly material of the Pentateuch and the final shaping of the Deuteronomistic History ([3.2](03-02-david-and-the-house-of-david.md)) to this period. Isaiah 40–55 is dated to the exile's last years by the scholarly majority ([4.2](04-02-isaiah-and-immanuel.md) weighs the unity arguments). Psalm 137 [Douay 136] preserves the grief. *In words:* Israel learned faith without land, king or Temple.

3. **Cyrus.** Cyrus of Persia took Babylon in 539. Isaiah 44:28 calls him God's shepherd, and 45:1 calls him God's *mashiach*, "anointed". This is the only foreign king the Hebrew Bible gives that title. Ezra 1:2–4 gives his edict in Hebrew; Ezra 6:3–5 gives a different version in Aramaic, a memorandum from the archive at Ecbatana. Elias Bickerman (1946) argued that both are genuine, a herald's proclamation and an archive docket. Many scholars accept the Aramaic memo in substance and judge the Hebrew version recast in Israel's idiom. *In words:* a Persian policy of return is well supported; the edict's wording is debated.

4. **The [Cyrus Cylinder](../reference.md#the-cyrus-cylinder).** Found at Babylon in 1879 and now in the British Museum, this Akkadian cylinder presents Cyrus as chosen by Marduk, Babylon's god, and says he returned gods' images to their sanctuaries and resettled their peoples (paraphrase). The places it names are Mesopotamian; it never mentions Judah, Jews or Jerusalem. It shows a *policy* of restoring local cults that fits Ezra 1. It is not the Jewish decree, and "the first charter of human rights" is a modern slogan.

5. **The Second Temple.** The work stalled, and under the prodding of Haggai and Zechariah it was finished "in the sixth year of the reign of king Darius" (Ezra 6:15). That is 515 on the usual reckoning (some compute 516). The Second Temple period ([`new-testament` 1.2](../../new-testament/lessons/01-02-second-temple-judaism.md)) runs from here to AD 70.

6. **Ezra and Nehemiah, and a [dating puzzle](../reference.md#ezra-and-nehemiah-chronology).** Nehemiah came "in the twentieth year of Artaxerxes" (Neh 2:1). Most scholars take that king to be Artaxerxes I, which gives 445. The Elephantine papyri support this: a petition of 407 names Sanballat of Samaria, Nehemiah's opponent, as a father with grown sons. Ezra came in "the seventh year of Artaxerxes" (Ezra 7:7). Under Artaxerxes I that is 458, and Ezra came first, as the books say. Under Artaxerxes II it is 398, and Nehemiah came first, the order proposed by the Louvain Catholic scholar Albin van Hoonacker in the 1890s. Others emend to the thirty-seventh year. **Disputed**, with no consensus.

7. **The Law read, and the boundary drawn.** Ezra carries the Law with a Persian king's warrant: whoever will not keep "the law of thy God, and the law of the king" is to be punished (Ezra 7:26). Then come the mixed marriages. The leaders report that the people have "mingled the holy seed" with the peoples of the land, Moabites among them (9:1–2). The assembly swears "to put away all the wives, and such as are born of them" (10:3). Nehemiah 13:1–3 reads Deuteronomy 23:3 aloud: "The Ammonite and the Moabite ... shall not enter into the church of the Lord for ever". Nehemiah 5, where Judeans mortgage fields and sell their children to pay the king's tax and Nehemiah makes the creditors forgive, is the debt thread's text, handed to [`theology-of-debt`](../../theology-of-debt/syllabus.md). The pledge to forgo debts in the seventh year (Neh 10:31) sits among the release laws in [`history-of-debt` 1.3](../../history-of-debt/lessons/01-03-release-laws-in-ancient-israel.md).

8. **[Persian imperial authorization](../reference.md#persian-imperial-authorization).** Ezra 7:26 is the anchor of a hypothesis by Peter Frei (1984). He argued that the Persian crown ratified local law codes as imperial law, the Pentateuch among them. James W. Watts's volume *Persia and Torah* (2001) tested the idea, and many contributors doubted that any formal ratification took place. The broader claim, that the Torah reached substantially its final form in the Persian period, is a majority view, not a settled fact ([2.2](02-02-who-wrote-the-pentateuch.md)).

9. **Counter-voices.** The Moabite Ruth says "thy people shall be my people, and thy God my God" (1:16) and becomes David's great-grandmother (4:17; Matt 1:5). Ruth's date is **disputed**: many read it as a Persian-period reply to Ezra–Nehemiah, others date it earlier on linguistic grounds, as a story of David's line with no polemic. [Jewish readings](../reference.md#jewish-readings) resolve the tension their own way: Mishnah *Yevamot* 8:3 rules that the exclusion binds Ammonite and Moabite men, while their women may enter at once. Ruth is read at Shavuot. Esther, a court story set in Susa, never names God in Hebrew. The Talmud (*Chullin* 139b) finds Esther hinted at in Deuteronomy 31:18, where God will surely hide (*haster astir*) his face: the book is read, at Purim, as providence hidden. The Greek additions, which name God and supply prayers, belong to [6.2](06-02-tobit-judith-baruch-and-the-maccabees.md).

**Mark the levels.** That Ezra, Nehemiah, Ruth and Esther are sacred and canonical is **defined dogma** (rung 1; Trent, via [`fundamental-theology` 3.3](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/03-03-the-canon-as-defined.md)). Their inspiration and inerrancy ([`fundamental-theology` 3.2](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/03-02-inerrancy.md)) do **not** make every policy a book narrates into one it commends: Ezra 10 narrates a community's decision, and the canon answers it with Ruth. **Everything else is history or hypothesis, with no magisterial level** ([Church teaching vs hypothesis](../reference.md#church-teaching-vs-hypothesis)): the authenticity of the edict, the Ezra–Nehemiah order, Ruth's date, and Frei's thesis. For [dating discipline](../reference.md#dating-discipline): 597, 587/586 (a one-year dispute) and 539 are **secure**. 582 and 515 are closely reconstructed; 445 is well supported. Ezra's 458 or 398 and the dates of Ruth and Esther are **disputed**.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean): 2 Chronicles 36:22–23 and Ezra 1:1–3.** Chronicles ends, almost word for word, with the opening of Ezra: Cyrus's proclamation, ending "let him go up". In the order of most printed Hebrew Bibles, Chronicles is the last book, so the Jewish Bible closes on an invitation to go up to Jerusalem. The Christian order puts Ezra after Chronicles and ends with the prophets and Malachi's promised Elijah ([6.4](06-04-the-old-testament-in-light-of-the-new.md)). The edict's literal sense is the same in both; the note the collection ends on changes. That is [canonical reading](../reference.md#canonical-reading) at its simplest: placement changes a text's weight.

**Example 2 (hard): who is the "anointed" in Isaiah 45:1?** The literal sense is plain. *Mashiach* is a title of installation, used for anointed kings and priests. God commissions a pagan king for a task, "for the sake of my servant Jacob", while Cyrus "hast not known me" (45:4). It does not make him *the* Messiah; the word was not yet a fixed title for a future king. Jewish reading felt the strain. The Talmud (*Megillah* 12a) asks whether Cyrus was the messiah. Its answer is that God is speaking *to* his anointed *about* Cyrus, and complaining that Cyrus only let the exiles go and did not build (paraphrase). Early Christians felt a different pull. The Septuagint reads *tō christō mou Kyrō*, "to my anointed, Cyrus", and a copyist's slip from *Kyrō* to *Kyriō*, "Lord", gave "to Christ my Lord". That text appears in Barnabas 12, Cyprian's *Testimonies* I.21 and Novatian's *On the Trinity* 26 (the ANF notes flag the slip). It is a real patristic reading of a corrupt text, and no warrant for the literal sense. A sounder typology keeps Cyrus as Cyrus: God saves through an agent outside the covenant. The famous Jewish and Christian readings both answer one embarrassment: a pagan called God's anointed.

## Watch out

- **Hypothesis as teaching:** "The Church teaches the Pentateuch was finalised under Persian authority." Frei's thesis is a debated hypothesis with no magisterial standing, and the wider Persian-period dating is a majority view only.
- **Teaching confused with commendation:** "Ezra 10 is inspired, so the Church teaches that mixed marriages must be dissolved." Inspiration guarantees what the sacred author *asserts*, not that every act he narrates is a model to follow ([`fundamental-theology` 3.2](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/03-02-inerrancy.md)). The literal sense reports a community's decision, and the canon also contains Ruth.
- **The Cylinder over-read:** it never mentions Jews; it shows a policy, not the text of Ezra 1.
- **"The exile emptied the land":** Jeremiah's own figures tell against it; the deportations removed the elite.

## One-liner

> Exile cost Judah its land, king and Temple, and the returned community rebuilt itself round a Law read aloud; the canon keeps Ezra's hard boundary and Ruth's Moabite great-grandmother of David side by side.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢)** *(Exegetical)* Isaiah 45:1–4 (Douay–Rheims):

> Thus saith the Lord to my anointed Cyrus, whose right hand I have taken hold of, to subdue nations before his face, and to turn the backs of kings, and to open the doors before him, and the gates shall not be shut. I will go before thee, and will humble the great ones of the earth: I will break in pieces the gates of brass, and will burst the bars of iron. And I will give thee hidden treasures, and the concealed riches of secret places: that thou mayest know that I am the Lord who call thee by thy name, the God of Israel. For the sake of my servant Jacob, and Israel my elect, I have even called thee by thy name: I have made a likeness of thee, and thou hast not known me.

(a) Translate the Hebrew *mashiach* ("anointed") in one or two words of your own, and say what it means *in this passage*. Name the two phrases that show whom Cyrus's commission is for and how much he knows of the God who commissions him. (b) Barnabas 12 quotes this verse as "The Lord said to Christ, my Lord". Explain how that reading arose, and whether it can be the literal sense. **Two sentences per part.**

**P2 (🟡)** *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b))* Read Ezra 9:1–2 and 10:3 (quoted in The argument, step 7) against Ruth 1:16 and 4:17 (step 9) and Deuteronomy 23:3. (a) State what each text holds about a Moabite joining Israel, and the words that decide it. Then state how Mishnah *Yevamot* 8:3 reconciles Ruth with Deuteronomy. (b) Is Ruth best read as a deliberate counter-voice to Ezra–Nehemiah? Give either verdict, name the evidence each side leans on, and say what a canonical reading gains whichever way the date falls. **150 words or fewer for (b).**

**P3 (🔴)** *(Exegetical)* An **invented** museum-tour script:

> "This is the Cyrus Cylinder, the world's first charter of human rights. On it Cyrus decrees that the Jews may go home and rebuild their Temple, the very decree quoted in Ezra 1, which is why the Church teaches that Ezra 1 is a verbatim Persian document. Ezra then brought the Law in 458 BC and Nehemiah followed in 445 BC, as every historian agrees."

Name **three** errors of fact or of level, and correct each from this lesson. **150 words or fewer.**

<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** a gloss such as "anointed one" or "installed agent". It means someone God has **commissioned for a task**, in the royal idiom of anointing, **not** "the Messiah" as a fixed future title. The two phrases: "**For the sake of my servant Jacob, and Israel my elect**" (the commission serves Israel), and "**thou hast not known me**" (Cyrus is used without knowing Israel's God). Also acceptable: "that thou mayest know that I am the Lord", which marks the knowledge as a purpose not yet achieved.
**Must hit, strict (b):** the Greek *Kyrō* (to Cyrus) was miscopied as *Kyriō* (to the Lord), which turned "my anointed, Cyrus" into "Christ, my Lord". It **cannot be the literal sense**, because it rests on a corrupt text. The Hebrew names Cyrus (*le-Koresh*). A typology that keeps Cyrus as Cyrus remains open.
**Wrong turns:** translating *mashiach* as "Messiah" and concluding that the prophet calls Cyrus the Messiah. Treating Barnabas's reading as a legitimate spiritual sense of the correct text, when it is a reading of a different, corrupted text.
**Model answer:** (a) *Mashiach* is "anointed one": a king God has installed and commissioned, here to overthrow nations "for the sake of my servant Jacob". Cyrus serves a God he does not recognise: "thou hast not known me". (b) A scribe wrote *Kyriō*, "Lord", for *Kyrō*, "Cyrus", and the Christian reading built on the slip. The Hebrew names Cyrus, so this cannot be the literal sense.

**P2** *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b))*

**Must hit, strict (a):** **Deuteronomy 23:3** excludes the Moabite from "the church of the Lord for ever". **Ezra 9:1–2 and 10:3** list Moabites among the peoples of the land, call the marriages a mingling of "the holy seed", and vow to "put away all the wives, and such as are born of them". **Ruth 1:16–17** has a Moabite woman take Israel's people and God as her own ("thy God my God"), and **4:17** makes her David's great-grandmother. **Mishnah *Yevamot* 8:3**: the exclusion binds Ammonite and Moabite **men**, while their women may enter at once. So Ruth breaks no law.
**Must hit, any verdict (b):** both positions. **For** the counter-voice reading: a Persian-period date, the book's insistence on "Ruth the Moabitess", and the Davidic payoff. **Against**: arguments from language for an earlier date, and a story whose stated purpose is David's ancestry, with no polemic signalled. Then the canonical point: whatever the author intended, the canon now sets Ruth beside Ezra, and a reader must hold both (1.4).
**Wrong turns:** stating the counter-voice reading as settled. Saying the Mishnah "refutes" Ezra, when it harmonises Ruth with Deuteronomy on its own terms. Claiming Ezra 10 is Church teaching on marriage.
**Model answer (b), one of several:** Ruth is at least *usable* as a counter-voice, and probably meant as one. The book keeps calling her "the Moabitess", in a way that seems to press on exactly the question Nehemiah 13 raises, and its ending makes a Moabite convert the root of David's house. Against this, its Hebrew has been argued to be early, and it never alludes to Ezra. A story about David's ancestors needs no opponent. The date cannot be fixed, so the intent stays uncertain. But canonically the question matters less. The Church received both books, so a reader cannot take Ezra 10 as the whole word on outsiders, and Matthew 1:5 shows the New Testament already reading Ruth that way.

**P3** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict:** any three of these.
1. The **Cylinder never mentions Jews**, Judah or Jerusalem. It records Cyrus returning gods and peoples to Mesopotamian sanctuaries, a policy that *fits* Ezra 1 but is not its text.
2. "**First charter of human rights**" is a modern slogan. The text is Babylonian royal propaganda that credits Marduk.
3. The **Church teaches nothing** about the edict's authenticity. That is a historical question with no magisterial level. Scholars debate whether the Hebrew edict or the Aramaic memorandum (Ezra 6:3–5) is closer to a Persian original.
4. **458 is disputed.** If "Artaxerxes" in Ezra 7:7 is Artaxerxes II, Ezra came in 398, after Nehemiah (van Hoonacker). "Every historian agrees" is false. 445 for Nehemiah is well supported.
**Wrong turns:** "correcting" the script by saying the Cylinder is a forgery or irrelevant. It is genuine and relevant as evidence of the policy. Swapping one overstated date for another, for example asserting 398 as fact.
**Model answer:** The Cylinder never names Jews or Jerusalem. It records Cyrus restoring gods and peoples to Babylonian sanctuaries, which fits the policy behind Ezra 1 but is not that decree, and "human rights charter" is a modern label. The Church has no teaching on whether Ezra 1 is verbatim; that is historians' work, and many prefer the Aramaic memo of Ezra 6. Nehemiah's 445 is well supported, but Ezra's 458 is disputed: if the king was Artaxerxes II, Ezra came in 398, after Nehemiah.

## Flashback

**From Lesson [3.2](03-02-david-and-the-house-of-david.md) (David and the house of David):** *(Exegetical.)* Two passages on the first king, 1 Samuel [DR 1 Kings] (Douay–Rheims):

> "And they said to him: Behold thou art old, and thy sons walk not in thy ways: make us a king, to judge us, as all nations have. And the word was displeasing in the eyes of Samuel ... And the Lord said to Samuel: Hearken to the voice of the people in all that they say to thee. For they have not rejected thee, but me, that I should not reign over them." (8:5–7)
>
> "To morrow about this same hour I will send thee a man of the land of Benjamin, and thou shalt anoint him to be ruler over my people Israel: and he shall save my people out of the hand of the Philistines: for I have looked down upon my people, because their cry is come to me." (9:16)

(a) For each passage, say who initiates the kingship and how God regards it, and name the deciding words. (b) Give the status of two explanations of the tension: (i) 1 Samuel 8–12 combines older anti-monarchic and pro-monarchic strands; (ii) the book as it stands presents kingship as a concession that God then adopts. **Two sentences per part.**

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):** In **8:5–7 the people initiate**: the elders ask for a king "as all nations have". God reads the request as **rejecting him** ("not rejected thee, but me, that I should not reign over them"), yet he tells Samuel to "hearken" and grant it. In **9:16 God initiates**: "I will send thee", "thou shalt anoint him". The king is **God's gift**, a deliverer ("he shall save my people") sent out of compassion ("their cry is come to me", the language of Exodus 3:9).
**Must hit, strict (b):** (i) is a **scholarly hypothesis** about the book's composition, widely held and with no magisterial standing. (ii) is a reading of the **literal sense of the final text**, which holds both attitudes in one story. The two do not compete: one explains how the tension arose, the other what the book as it stands says.
**Wrong turns:** reading 8:7 as God refusing a king, when he orders Samuel to grant one. Merging the "cry" of 9:16 (the Philistine oppression) with the elders' request of 8:5 (to be like the nations). Treating (i) as proven, or as a threat to inspiration.
**Model answer:** (a) In chapter 8 the elders ask for a king to be like the nations, and God calls it a rejection of his own rule, though he tells Samuel to grant it. In 9:16 God himself sends and anoints the king to rescue his people, because their "cry is come" to him, as in the Exodus. (b) The two-strands theory is a scholarly hypothesis about how 1 Samuel was put together, with no magisterial weight. The concession-adopted reading describes what the final text says. It is a literal-sense reading and does not depend on any source theory.
