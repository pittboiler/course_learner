# The Old Testament · Lesson 6.3: Daniel and apocalyptic

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 6: Canon, deuterocanon, apocalyptic, and the Old in light of the New · Builds on: [1.3 The historical-critical method and its limits](01-03-the-historical-critical-method-and-its-limits.md), [6.2 Tobit, Judith, Baruch, and the Maccabees](06-02-tobit-judith-baruch-and-the-maccabees.md) · Unlocks: [6.4 The Old Testament in light of the New](06-04-the-old-testament-in-light-of-the-new.md)

## Why this matters

Daniel gave Jesus his favourite title for himself, gave the Old Testament its clearest statement of resurrection, and gave the New Testament a whole genre. Its date is argued harder than any other book's: most critics place its visions in the persecution of 167–164 BC, four centuries after the Babylon it describes. This lesson reads both halves, lays out the dating case, and reads Daniel 7 and 12 closely.

## The idea

Daniel is two books sewn together, and the seams run in two directions.

- **By genre.** Chapters 1–6 are **court tales**, told in the third person: Jewish exiles at a pagan court keep the Law, survive the furnace and the lions' den, and outdo the king's wise men. Chapters 7–12 are **visions**, told in the first person: beasts, horns, angels and numbered days.
- **By language.** The book opens in Hebrew, switches to Aramaic at 2:4b, when the Chaldeans answer the king "in Syriac" (the Douay's word for Aramaic), and returns to Hebrew at 8:1. So the language break does not match the genre break: chapter 7 is an Aramaic vision.
- **The Greek additions.** The Septuagint and Theodotion's Greek add the Prayer of Azariah and the Song of the Three Young Men (Douay 3:24–90), Susanna (Douay chapter 13) and Bel and the Dragon (chapter 14). Jerome noted that Daniel "in Hebrew contains neither the history of Susanna, nor the hymn of the three youths, nor the fables of Bel and the Dragon", translated them anyway because they were read everywhere, and marked them with an obelus, his sign for a passage missing from the Hebrew (Preface to Daniel, NPNF 2/6). They are part of the Catholic canon ([deuterocanonical books](../reference.md#deuterocanonical-books)).

The genre that the visions start is **[apocalyptic](../reference.md#apocalyptic)**. John J. Collins's definition (*Semeia* 14, 1979), in paraphrase: a revelation, set in a narrative frame, given to a human seer by an otherworldly being such as an angel. It discloses a reality beyond ordinary sight in two directions. In time, it looks to a final salvation. In space, it shows a supernatural world behind this one.

## Source

Daniel 7:13–14 and 7:18 (Douay–Rheims):

> "I beheld, therefore, in the vision of the night, and lo, one like the Son of man came with the clouds of heaven, and he came even to the ancient of days: and they presented him before him. And he gave him power, and glory, and a kingdom: and all peoples, tribes, and tongues shall serve him: his power is an everlasting power that shall not be taken away: and his kingdom that shall not be destroyed. ... But the saints of the most high God shall take the kingdom: and they shall possess the kingdom for ever and ever."

Read it for three things. The figure is described by *likeness*: the Aramaic *kebar enash* means "like a son of man", that is, like a human being. He is set against four beasts from the sea (7:3), so the contrast is human against bestial. He *receives* a kingdom and does not seize it. And the angel's interpretation gives the kingdom to "the saints of the most high", a group (7:18, 22, 27).

## The argument

1. **The setting the book claims.** Daniel is deported in "the third year of the reign of Joakim" (1:1), about 606/605 BC, and serves "even to the first year of king Cyrus" (1:21). His last vision is dated "the third year of Cyrus" (10:1). Taken at face value, that is the traditional sixth-century date.
2. **Porphyry's thesis.** The Neoplatonist Porphyry (third century AD) gave the twelfth book of his work *Against the Christians* to Daniel. Jerome's *Commentary on Daniel* (407) preserves his argument, in paraphrase: the book was written by someone in Judea under Antiochus IV Epiphanes, it narrated the past rather than foretelling the future, and what it says up to Antiochus is accurate history while what it guesses beyond him is false.
3. **The modern case for the Maccabean date** ([the date of Daniel](../reference.md#the-date-of-daniel)). (a) **Daniel 11 sharpens as it nears 167.** Its kings of the north and south track the Seleucids and Ptolemies. The "one despised" of 11:21 is Antiochus IV, and even Challoner's note says so. The prophecy grows more exact through his Egyptian campaigns, Rome's intervention ("the galleys and the Romans", 11:30, in 168) and the "abomination unto desolation" (11:31), which 1 Maccabees dates to Kislev 167. (b) **Then it misses.** 11:40–45 predicts a new conquest of Egypt and the king's end "between the seas, upon a glorious and holy mountain", that is, in Palestine. 1 Maccabees 6 has him fail to plunder a temple in Persia and die "in a strange land" in the year 149 of the Seleucid era, which is late 164. (c) **Supporting points, each weaker alone.** The Babylonian and Persian details are uneven: Belshazzar was Nabonidus's son and never king in name, and "Darius, the Mede" (5:31) is unknown outside the book. Greek names of instruments appear in 3:5 ("psaltery", "symphony"). Ben Sira, around 180, lists the prophets (Sir 49 [Douay 49:9–12]) with no Daniel. And the book sits among the Writings, not the Prophets, in the Hebrew canon ([1.1](01-01-what-the-old-testament-is.md)).
   *In words:* the text is precise up to the point where a writer of 165 would stop knowing and vague or wrong after it, which is exactly what *[vaticinium ex eventu](../reference.md#vaticinium-ex-eventu)* ("prophecy from the outcome") predicts.
4. **The traditional reply.** Jerome and others read the passage from 11:21 on as looking past Antiochus to the Antichrist, "of whom this Antiochus was a figure" (Challoner on 11:21). On that reading 11:40–45 is not a failed forecast. Defenders of an early date add that the tales were known to the Maccabees (1 Macc 2:59–60), that Josephus has Alexander the Great shown the book (*Antiquities* XI.8.5), and that Jesus cites "Daniel the prophet" (Matt 24:15). Critics answer that these show reception, not composition, and many hold the court tales older than the visions.
5. **Form A or Form B?** [1.3](01-03-the-historical-critical-method-and-its-limits.md) separated two arguments for a late date. **Form A:** prediction is impossible, so the text is late. That premise is ruled out for a Catholic, because Leo XIII condemned assuming prophecy impossible in advance (*Providentissimus Deus* §17). **Form B:** the text's own evidence dates it. Daniel's strongest argument is Form B. The claim is not that prophecy cannot happen. It is that 11:40–45 shows where the author's knowledge ran out.
6. **Daniel 7 read literally.** In their own setting the four beasts are most naturally Babylon, Media, Persia and Greece, and the "little horn" (7:8) that makes war on the saints for "a time, and times, and half a time" (7:25) is Antiochus. Challoner's note on 7:3 gives the traditional series ending in Rome, and adds that "some rather choose" the successors of Alexander. The "one like the Son of man" then has three live literal readings: the faithful people personified, whom the interpretation names (7:18, 27); an angelic leader, Michael (Collins's view, from 10:21 and 12:1); or an individual, messianic king. Jewish tradition includes that last reading. Rashi on 7:13 says "he is the King Messiah" (the lesson's translation). The Talmud (*Sanhedrin* 98a) sets 7:13 beside Zechariah 9:9: if Israel is worthy the Messiah comes "with the clouds of heaven", and if not, lowly on a donkey ([Jewish readings](../reference.md#jewish-readings)). At his trial Jesus answers with this verse (Mark 14:62).
7. **Daniel 12:2, the resurrection.** "Many of those that sleep in the dust of the earth, shall awake: some unto life everlasting, and others unto reproach." It is the first unambiguous statement of bodily resurrection to judgment in the Hebrew Bible, with Isaiah 26:19 the nearest earlier text, and it is tied to "that time" of distress (12:1). Its first readers were the martyrs of 11:33–35, and 2 Maccabees 7:9 gives the same hope to the martyred brothers.

**Mark the levels.** That Daniel, "entire with all their parts", including 3:24–90 and chapters 13–14, is sacred and canonical is **defined dogma** (Trent, Session IV; [`fundamental-theology` 3.3](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/03-03-the-canon-as-defined.md)), and so is its inspiration. The **date and author are scholarly questions with no magisterial answer.** None of the Biblical Commission's 1905–1915 responses addressed Daniel. The 1908 response on Isaiah is the nearest analogue, and its force was reinterpreted in 1948 and 1955 ([the Biblical Commission and its weight](../reference.md#the-biblical-commission-and-its-weight)). A Maccabean date for the visions is the **critical majority**. The sixth-century date is a **live minority**, held mainly by conservative scholars. Dates: 175 (Antiochus's accession), Kislev 167 (the profanation), Kislev 164 (the rededication) and late 164 (his death) are **secure**. The composition of the visions around 167–164 is **reconstructed**, and the court tales' date is **disputed**. The resurrection of the dead is **defined dogma** (the creeds), a doctrine owned by [`creation-grace-last-things`](../../creation-grace-last-things/syllabus.md). Dan 12:2 is a text it leans on, not its proof. Challoner's notes are commentary, not teaching ([church teaching vs hypothesis](../reference.md#church-teaching-vs-hypothesis)).

## The case: Daniel 11 against the record

| Daniel 11 | Event it matches | Date |
|---|---|---|
| 11:21 "one despised ... shall obtain the kingdom by fraud" | Antiochus IV takes the throne ahead of his nephew | 175, secure |
| 11:25–28 war with "the king of the south", returns "with much riches", heart "against the holy covenant" | first Egyptian campaign; Temple treasury plundered (1 Macc 1:17–24 [Douay 1:18–25]) | 170/169, secure within a year |
| 11:29–30 "the galleys and the Romans shall come upon him" | Rome's envoy Popilius Laenas orders him out of Egypt | 168, secure |
| 11:31 "take away the continual sacrifice ... the abomination unto desolation" | altar profaned, Kislev of year 145 (1 Macc 1:54 [Douay 1:57]) | 167, secure |
| 11:40–45 conquers Egypt, Libya, Ethiopia; ends "between the seas, upon a glorious and holy mountain" | **no match:** he fails at Elymais and dies in Persia (1 Macc 6:1–16) | late 164, secure |

The fit is close through row four, and row five breaks. The dating argument lives in that gap.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean): Daniel 3 as a court tale.** *Genre first.* A king demands worship of an image. Three Jews refuse, are thrown into a furnace, and walk out unharmed, and the king praises their God (3:95–96 Douay). Chapter 6 repeats the plot shape. *Literal sense:* a story of fidelity under a foreign power, told to Jews living under one. That is why 1 Maccabees 2:59–60 puts it in Mattathias's mouth as a charge to his sons. *Spiritual sense second:* the Fathers read the fourth figure "like the son of God" (3:92) as a figure of Christ. The canticle became the Church's *Benedicite*. Neither reading needs a verdict on the date.

**Example 2 (hard): "a time, and times, and half a time."** 7:25 and 12:7 give three and a half "times". 12:11 counts 1,290 days from the abomination, and 12:12 at once blesses whoever reaches 1,335. Critics read the second figure as a recalculation added when the first passed, so as evidence of composition during the crisis. Challoner, on 7:25, applies the span to the Antichrist. **Where the method strains:** the numbers fit Antiochus's persecution only roughly, whatever dates you choose. They show either reworking or symbols that are not calendars, and cannot say which. The datum is real; its weight is **disputed**.

## Watch out

- **"The Church teaches Daniel wrote in the sixth century."** No definition, no Commission response and no conciliar text fixes Daniel's date. Canonicity and inspiration are defined; the date is not.
- **"Critics date Daniel late because they deny prophecy."** That is Porphyry's premise and some moderns', but the Form B argument from 11:40–45 does not need it. Report the argument that is actually made.
- **"The Greek additions are less Scripture."** Their language is a historical question; their canonicity is defined.
- **Flattening Daniel 7.** Individual, collective and angelic readings all have textual anchors, and Rashi's messianic reading stands beside the Christian one.

## One-liner

> Daniel is court tales and visions, Hebrew and Aramaic and Greek, canonical in all its parts; its visions most likely speak from the persecution of 167–164 BC, and that is a historian's verdict, not the Church's.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢)** *(Exegetical)* Give each claim a status: **defined dogma** (name the source), **secure / reconstructed / disputed date**, **scholarly position** (majority or minority), or **not Church teaching**. One line each.

1. "Susanna and Bel and the Dragon are Scripture."
2. "The visions of Daniel 7–12 were written between 167 and 164 BC."
3. "Antiochus IV profaned the altar in Jerusalem in 167 BC."
4. "A Catholic must hold that the prophet Daniel wrote the whole book in Babylon."
5. "The dead will rise, some to everlasting life."

**P2 (🟡)** *(Exegetical)* Read Daniel 11:40, 44–45 and 1 Maccabees 6:1–4, 16 (Douay–Rheims):

> "And at the time prefixed the king of the south shall fight against him, and the king of the north shall come against him like a tempest, with chariots, and with horsemen, and with a great navy ... And tidings out of the east, and out of the north, shall trouble him ... And he shall fix his tabernacle, Apadno, between the seas, upon a glorious and holy mountain: and he shall come even to the top thereof, and none shall help him." (Dan 11:40, 44–45)

> "Now king Antiochus was going through the higher countries, and he heard that the city of Elymais in Persia, was greatly renowned ... So he came, and sought to take the city and to pillage it; but he was not able ... and he fled away from thence, and departed with great sadness, and returned towards Babylonia. ... So king Antiochus died there in the year one hundred and forty-nine." (1 Macc 6:1, 3–4, 16)

(a) Name two concrete points where the forecast and the record diverge. (b) **Find the crux.** Position A (the critical majority): the "king of the north" of 11:40–45 is still Antiochus, so these verses are a forecast written before his death that did not come true, which dates the chapter to about 165. Position B (Jerome and the tradition Challoner follows): from 11:21 the prophecy looks past Antiochus to the Antichrist, of whom Antiochus is a figure, so 11:40–45 does not describe Antiochus's death. Name the single premise one side must deny, and one textual feature that would move it. **Two sentences per part.**

**P3 (🔴)** *(Exegetical)* (a) Close-read Daniel 12:1–3 (quoted in The argument, step 7, and continuing): "But they that are learned, shall shine as the brightness of the firmament: and they that instruct many to justice, as stars for all eternity." Say who rises, to what, when, and who "they that are learned" are, naming the words that decide each. (b) An invented catechist's handout reads: *"Daniel 12:2 proves that all human beings will rise at the end of the world, as Israel had always believed."* Diagnose it: what it gets right, and the two claims the text does not support. **120 words in total.**

<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict:** (1) **Defined dogma**: Trent, Session IV, the books "entire with all their parts", with the Vulgate's Daniel including chapters 13–14 ([`fundamental-theology` 3.3](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/03-03-the-canon-as-defined.md)). (2) **Scholarly position, the critical majority**. The date is **reconstructed**, and it is not Church teaching. (3) **Secure date** (1 Macc 1:54 [Douay 1:57]: Kislev, year 145 of the Seleucid era). (4) **Not Church teaching.** No definition or Commission response fixes Daniel's author or date. The sixth-century date is a live minority scholarly position. (5) **Defined dogma** (the creeds' resurrection of the dead), for which Dan 12:2 is a supporting text, not the definition.

**Wrong turns:** grading (1) as disputed because Jerome obelized the additions. Trent settled canonicity, whatever the additions' original language. Grading (4) as Church teaching because Jesus calls Daniel "the prophet" (Matt 24:15): a title of reception is not a definition of authorship. Calling (2) "proven".

**Model answer:** (1) Defined dogma, Trent IV. (2) Majority scholarly view, reconstructed date. (3) Secure date, 1 Macc 1:54. (4) Not Church teaching; a minority scholarly view. (5) Defined dogma; Dan 12:2 a supporting text.

**P2** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** any two of: **place of death**, Palestine "between the seas, upon a ... holy mountain" versus Persia, near Elymais, "in a strange land"; **the campaign**, a victorious southern war against Egypt versus a failed raid in the east; **circumstances**, a king at the height of conquest versus a king who "fled away" in sadness. Credit also: the "tidings out of the east" (11:44) are an eastern threat in the forecast but the setting of his defeat in the record.

**Must hit, strict (b):** the crux is **whether the subject of 11:40–45 is the same king as 11:21–39**, that is, whether the text marks a change of referent. A denies any change; B affirms one. Textual evidence that would move it: whether anything in the Hebrew marks a new subject at 11:36 or 11:40. "The king" continues without a new name, and "at the time of the end" (*be'et qets*, DR "at the time prefixed", 11:40) already appears for the crisis at 11:35. That continuity helps A. A clear shift of scale or vocabulary would help B.

**Wrong turns:** making the crux "whether prophecy is possible". Both positions allow it, and B's move is a change of referent, not a denial of forecast. Treating Challoner's note as a Church ruling on the referent.

**Model answer:** (a) The forecast ends the king in Palestine after conquering Egypt; the record has him fail in Persia and die there. (b) The crux is whether 11:40–45 still speaks of Antiochus. A says yes and B says the subject has passed to the Antichrist. The absence of any new subject or name, with "time of the end" already used for 11:35's crisis, favours A; a marked shift in scope would favour B.

**P3** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** **"many"** of those who sleep in the dust, not stated as all; **two destinies**, "life everlasting" and "reproach"; **"at that time"** (12:1), the unparalleled distress of the crisis in chapter 11; "they that are learned" are **the same group as the "learned among the people" who "teach many" and fall (11:33, 35)**, the faithful teachers of the persecution. They receive a special glory.

**Must hit, strict (b):** right: 12:2 does teach bodily resurrection to judgment, and the Church's doctrine leans on it. Unsupported: **"all"**, because the text says "many" (a universal reading comes with later texts such as John 5:28–29, "all that are in the graves"); and **"always believed"**, because this is the first clear statement, with Isaiah 26:19 the nearest earlier text, so the belief developed.

**Wrong turns:** calling the doctrine "not Church teaching" because Daniel says "many". The dogma stands on the creeds and the New Testament. Reading "learned" as scholars in general, ignoring 11:33–35.

**Model answer:** (a) Many sleepers rise, some to everlasting life and some to reproach, at the time of distress in 12:1. The "learned" are 11:33's teachers, the martyrs, who shine like stars. (b) It is right that 12:2 affirms resurrection to judgment. But "many" is not "all", and this is the belief's first clear statement, not its constant form. Universality comes from John 5:28–29 and the creeds.

## Flashback

**From Lesson [6.1](06-01-how-the-old-testament-canon-formed.md) (How the Old Testament canon formed):** *(Exegetical.)* Athanasius, Festal Letter 39 (AD 367; NPNF 2nd ser. vol. 4). From his list of the twenty-two Old Testament books, and then from the paragraph after the New Testament list:

> "Then Isaiah, one book, then Jeremiah with Baruch, Lamentations, and the epistle, one book; afterwards, Ezekiel and Daniel, each one book. ... there are other books besides these not indeed included in the Canon, but appointed by the Fathers to be read by those who newly join us, and who wish for instruction in the word of godliness. The Wisdom of Solomon, and the Wisdom of Sirach, and Esther, and Judith, and Tobit, and that which is called the Teaching of the Apostles, and the Shepherd. But the former, my brethren, are included in the Canon, the latter being [merely] read; nor is there in any place a mention of apocryphal writings."

(a) Athanasius sorts books into three classes. Name them, with the words that mark each. (b) An invented study-Bible note reads: *"Athanasius, the champion of Nicaea, confirms the 39-book Protestant Old Testament, so that canon is the true ancient one."* Give two facts in the passage that the note gets wrong, and say what authority a Father's list like this carries. **Two sentences per part.**

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):** (1) **The canon**: "included in the Canon", the twenty-two books. (2) **Books to be read**: "not indeed included in the Canon, but appointed by the Fathers to be read" by converts ("those who newly join us"). This is a middle tier, not a rejection. (3) **Apocrypha**: the books of which there is "not ... in any place a mention", and the letter goes on to call them heretics' inventions.

**Must hit, strict (b):** any two of these facts. (i) **Baruch and the epistle** (the Letter of Jeremiah) are *inside* his canon, counted with Jeremiah, and the Protestant 39 have neither. (ii) **Esther** is *outside* his canon, among the books read, and the 39 include it. (iii) Wisdom, Sirach, Judith and Tobit are **recommended reading**, not thrown out with the apocrypha, so his scheme has two tiers the 39-book model does not have. On authority: a Father's list is a **historical witness with no magisterial level**. The Old Testament canon of 46 books is **defined dogma** (Trent, Session IV).

**Wrong turns:** filing Wisdom and Sirach with the apocrypha. Adding Maccabees to the read tier, when the passage names them nowhere. Claiming the passage shows whether his "Daniel" contained the Greek additions, when it says nothing either way. Calling a Doctor's list binding, or saying Trent "overruled" a definition that was never made. Reading the middle tier as an Old Testament category only, when the *Teaching of the Apostles* and the *Shepherd* are in it too.

**Model answer:** (a) There is the canon ("included in the Canon"), and below it the books "appointed by the Fathers to be read" by new converts, such as Wisdom, Sirach, Esther, Judith and Tobit. Outside both are the apocrypha, which get "not ... in any place a mention". (b) His canon counts Baruch and the Letter of Jeremiah inside Jeremiah and leaves Esther out, so it is not the Protestant 39, and it keeps the other books as recommended reading instead of discarding them. A Father's list is a historical witness with no magisterial level, while the 46-book canon is defined dogma at Trent.
