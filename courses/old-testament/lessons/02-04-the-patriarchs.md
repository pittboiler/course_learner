# The Old Testament · Lesson 2.4: The patriarchs

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 2: The Pentateuch · Builds on: [2.2 Who wrote the Pentateuch?](02-02-who-wrote-the-pentateuch.md), [2.3 The primeval history](02-03-the-primeval-history.md) · Unlocks: [2.5 Exodus and Sinai](02-05-exodus-and-sinai.md), [3.2 David and the house of David](03-02-david-and-the-house-of-david.md)

## Why this matters

Genesis 1–11 is about humanity; at 12:1 the lens narrows to one family, and the rest of the Bible is the story of what God promised it. Paul calls Abraham "the father of all them that believe" (Rom 4:11), Hebrews 11 makes him faith's model, and the Roman Canon names him "our patriarch". Yet historians disagree sharply about whether Abraham, Isaac and Jacob can be reached as history at all. This lesson reads the promise closely, then reports that debate honestly and asks what, if anything, faith needs from its outcome.

## The idea

Genesis 12–50 is built like a suspense story. God makes a promise in three parts: **land**, **seed** (descendants) and **blessing** for the nations. Then everything conspires against it. The promised wife is barren (Sarah, then Rebekah, then Rachel). The land is held by others and struck by famine. The heir is nearly lost, twice. The brothers sell the favourite into Egypt. Each crisis asks: will God keep his word? The book ends with seventy souls in Egypt, the land still unpossessed. The promise survives, and it is unfinished.

Keep two questions apart the whole way. **What does the text say?** That is the literal sense, read in its genre. **What happened in, say, 1800 BC?** That is a historian's question, answered with other evidence. The first can be answered well even while the second stays open.

## Source

Genesis 15:1, 5–6 (Douay–Rheims):

> "Now when these things were done, the word of the Lord came to Abram by a vision, saying: Fear not, Abram, I am thy protector, and thy reward exceeding great. ... And he brought him forth abroad, and said to him: Look up to heaven and number the stars if thou canst. And he said to him: So shall thy seed be. Abram believed God, and it was reputed to him unto justice."

The Hebrew of 15:6 is *we-he'emin ba-YHWH wa-yachshevehah lo tsedaqah*. My gloss: "And he trusted in the LORD, and he reckoned it to him as righteousness." Three words carry it:

- ***he'emin***, from the root *'mn* (as in "amen"): to hold firm, to rely on. It is trust in a person's word, not assent to a proposition.
- ***yachshevehah***, "he reckoned it", the accountant's verb, with no named subject. Grammar lets either party be the one who reckons.
- ***tsedaqah***, righteousness: right standing within a relationship.

So the verse has a real ambiguity, and Jewish commentators divided over it. **Rashi** (11th century) reads it as God counting Abram's trust to him as merit. **Nachmanides** (Ramban, 13th century) objects that a prophet trusting God's plain word is hardly meritorious. He reads it as Abram counting the promise as *God's* righteousness: the gift did not depend on Abram's deserving it. Both read the Hebrew faithfully. Neither is set up to be refuted ([Jewish readings](../reference.md#jewish-readings)). Note also that the Douay's "believed God" follows the Greek and Latin (*credidit Deo*); the Hebrew has the divine name. How Paul reads the verse is [`new-testament` 5.1](../../new-testament/lessons/05-01-galatians-faith-law-and-the-gentiles.md) and [5.2](../../new-testament/lessons/05-02-romans-1-4-the-righteousness-of-god.md). This lesson stays in Genesis.

## The argument

1. **The call (12:1–3).** One command ("Go forth") carries a string of promises: a land to be shown, a great nation, blessing, a great name, and the nations blessed "in thee" ([the patriarchal promise](../reference.md#the-patriarchal-promise)). Abram obeys at once and says nothing. *In words:* Genesis 11 ends with humanity making a name for itself at Babel; Genesis 12 has God promising to make a name for one man.
2. **The covenant between the pieces (15:7–21).** Abram asks for a sign, and God commands a ritual. Animals are cut in two, and at nightfall "a smoking furnace, and a lamp of fire" pass between the halves (15:17). Jeremiah 34:18 shows the logic: those who "cut the calf in two and passed between the parts" invoked its fate on themselves if they broke faith. Here only God passes. *In words:* God takes the self-curse alone; Abram sleeps.
3. **Circumcision (Gen 17).** Abram becomes Abraham, "father of many nations" (17:5), and the covenant is called "perpetual" with a sign in the flesh (17:10–11). Genesis 15 is a promise God binds himself to. Genesis 17 adds a sign Abraham's household must keep. Most source critics assign Gen 17 to the Priestly material and Gen 15 to non-Priestly layers, a majority reading on the models of [2.2](02-02-who-wrote-the-pentateuch.md), not a settled one.
4. **Jacob becomes Israel (32:22–32).** Jacob wrestles a "man" till dawn at the Jabbok and demands a blessing. The name comes with a Hebrew reason, *ki sarita im-elohim ve-im-anashim vatukhal*: "for you have striven with God and with men, and have prevailed" (32:28; Hebrew verse 29). The nation's name remembers a fight with God that ended in a limp and a blessing.
5. **The Joseph novella (Gen 37–50).** This is a single, carefully plotted story, not a string of episodes. God works through hidden providence, not visions. Its key is Joseph's words to his brothers: "You thought evil against me: but God turned it into good" (50:20). Gerhard von Rad ("The Joseph Narrative and Ancient Wisdom", 1953) argued that it is shaped by wisdom teaching: Joseph is the wise, self-controlled courtier of Proverbs. That thesis is influential and contested.
6. **The historicity debate** ([historicity of the patriarchs](../reference.md#historicity-of-the-patriarchs)). In the mid-twentieth century, W. F. Albright and his school (John Bright's *History of Israel*) placed a "patriarchal age" in the early second millennium BC. Their evidence was customs in Genesis that seemed to match tablets from Nuzi, a Hurrian town of the fifteenth–fourteenth centuries BC. Examples were a childless man adopting a servant as heir (Eliezer, 15:2–3) and a barren wife giving her maid to her husband (Hagar). Two books of 1974–75 broke that consensus. T. L. Thompson's *The Historicity of the Patriarchal Narratives* argued that the Nuzi parallels were misread or not distinctive. John Van Seters's *Abraham in History and Tradition* argued that the customs fit the first millennium equally well and dated the stories late, near the exile. There are also apparent anachronisms. "Ur of the Chaldees" (11:31) names a people attested only in the first millennium. Philistines (21:34; 26:1) settled the coast around the twelfth century. Domestic camels (12:16; 24:10) are discussed in Example 2.
7. **Where the field stands now.** Most critical historians doubt that the patriarchs can be recovered as history. They read the stories as Israel's later account of its origins, possibly built from older clan traditions. A live minority, notably K. A. Kitchen (*On the Reliability of the Old Testament*, 2003), argues for plausibility. One of his examples is Joseph's price of "twenty pieces of silver" (37:28), which he argues matches slave prices of the early second millennium rather than later ones. Catholic scholarship spans the range: Roland de Vaux, O.P., defended a moderate historical core.

**Mark the levels.** That God called Abraham, bound himself by promise, and counted Abraham's faith as righteousness is **the content of Scripture as the Church reads it**. The Catechism teaches it (CCC 59, 145–146), and the Roman Canon names "our patriarch Abraham" (*patriarchae nostri Abrahae*). That Scripture teaches without error what God wanted written for our salvation is **conciliar teaching** (Dei Verbum 11), owned by [`fundamental-theology` 3.2](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/03-02-inerrancy.md). Dei Verbum 12 adds that truth is expressed differently in texts that are "variously historical", so genre decides what a narrative asserts. **No magisterial act has defined the historicity of the patriarchs, or dated them.** The Biblical Commission's response of 23 June 1905 held that narratives in historical books must be taken as history properly so called, unless solid arguments prove the writer meant otherwise. That was binding then. It is read today with the 1948 letter to Cardinal Suhard, *Divino Afflante Spiritu*, and the Commission's post-1971 consultative status ([the Commission's weight](../reference.md#the-biblical-commission-and-its-weight), [1.3](01-03-the-historical-critical-method-and-its-limits.md)). The historicity question is now **freely disputed** among Catholic exegetes. The patriarchs' dates are **disputed**, and any "patriarchal age" is a **reconstruction** ([dating discipline](../reference.md#dating-discipline)). That the Albright synthesis collapsed is a fair report of the field; "Abraham never existed" is not a finding anyone can prove.

## Map of positions

| Position | Representatives | Date of the patriarchs | Chief evidence | Main difficulty |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Patriarchal age** | Albright, Bright, Speiser (1940s–60s) | early 2nd millennium BC (reconstructed) | Nuzi customs; names; migration patterns | parallels not distinctive; anachronisms |
| **Late composition, no recoverable history** | Thompson (1974), Van Seters (1975); now the majority | stories written in the 1st millennium; persons unreachable | first-millennium features; the Nuzi critique | arguing from silence about the persons; old traditions may survive in late texts |
| **Plausible core** | Kitchen (2003); de Vaux, more cautiously | 2nd millennium (disputed) | slave prices, treaty forms, personal-name types | anachronisms need to be explained as later updating |

All three are **scholarly positions**. None has magisterial standing.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean): literal first, then spiritual, at the Jabbok.**
*Literal sense.* Genesis 32 is an **etiology**, a story that explains a name. It explains "Israel" (*sarita*, "you strove"), "Phanuel" ("face of God", 32:30) and a food custom: Israelites do not eat the thigh sinew (32:32). In the plot, Jacob has cheated his brother and is about to meet him, and the one he must really face is God. The "man" is left mysterious on purpose: he refuses to give his name (32:29). Hosea 12:3–4 (Hebrew 12:4–5) later calls him an angel.
*Spiritual sense.* Augustine, *City of God* XVI.39 (NPNF), calls the angel "most evidently a type of Christ". He reads Jacob's victory as a figure of the Passion, and he takes "Israel" to mean "seeing God", the reward of the saints. The **typology** ([typology](../reference.md#typology)) is a separate layer from the etiology and does not replace it. Augustine's etymology is not the text's own. Genesis explains the name from *sarah*, "strive", not from "see". A good reading reports both: what the author meant, and what the tradition saw through it.

**Example 2 (hard): the camels.** Abram receives camels in Egypt (12:16), and Abraham's servant takes ten on his journey (24:10). Zooarchaeological work at the Aravah copper mines (Sapir-Hen and Ben-Yosef, 2013) found domesticated camels in the southern Levant appearing only around the late tenth century BC. What follows?
- **It does show** that these details belong to the storyteller's world, centuries after any early-second-millennium Abraham. The narrator describes a wealthy man the way wealth looked in his own day.
- **It does not show** that Abraham did not exist. A late detail in a telling dates the telling, not necessarily its core. Defenders of plausibility, Kitchen among them, point to scattered earlier evidence for camels. Van Seters would add the detail to a larger pattern pointing to late composition.
- **The method strains here.** One anachronism can be explained as updating. The question is whether the anachronisms together form a pattern. That is a judgment about cumulative evidence, an [inference to the best explanation](../../philosophical-method/lessons/02-01-inference-to-the-best-explanation.md), and serious scholars weigh it differently.

## Watch out

- **"The Church teaches that Abraham is a historical person living around 1850 BC."** No act of the Magisterium defines or dates him. The 1905 response set a burden of proof. It did not date anyone, and its force has since changed. Promoting a reconstruction to doctrine is a level error.
- **"Archaeology has disproved the patriarchs."** Archaeology can show anachronisms in a telling. It cannot show the absence of a nomadic family chief who left no inscription. "Unrecoverable" is the majority view; "refuted" overstates it.
- **Reading Paul back into Genesis 15:6.** *He'emin* is trust in a promise-maker. Whether the reckoning is imputed or infused is a later question from Romans, and Rashi and Ramban even disagree about who does the reckoning.
- **Treating Gen 15 and Gen 17 as the same covenant told twice.** One is unilateral (God alone passes between the pieces). The other carries an obligation with a sign. Whether they come from different sources is a hypothesis; the difference in the text is not.

## One-liner

> Genesis 12–50 is a promise under siege, land, seed and blessing, kept by God through every threat; whether its heroes can be dated is an open historians' question that the Church has never closed.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢)** *(Exegetical)* Genesis 12:1–3 (Douay–Rheims):

> "And the Lord said to Abram: Go forth out of thy country, and from thy kindred, and out of thy father's house, and come into the land which I shall shew thee. And I will make of thee a great nation, and I will bless thee, and magnify thy name, and thou shalt be blessed. I will bless them that bless thee, and curse them that curse thee, and IN THEE shall all the kindreds of the earth be blessed."

(a) Sort the promises into land, seed and blessing, quoting the words for each. (b) The Hebrew of "shall ... be blessed" is *wenivrekhu*, a form that can be passive ("shall be blessed") or reflexive ("shall bless themselves by you", i.e. use Abram's name in blessing). Say in two sentences what each rendering claims about the nations. **Two sentences per part.**

**P2 (🟡)** *(Exegetical)* Give each claim a status: **Church teaching** (name the source and level), **scholarly position** (majority, minority, or past consensus), or **not a claim anyone can establish**. One line each.

1. "Abram's trust was reckoned to him as righteousness."
2. "The patriarchs lived in the early second millennium BC, as the Nuzi tablets show."
3. "The patriarchal stories took their present form in the first millennium BC."
4. "Scripture teaches without error what God wished recorded for our salvation."
5. "Archaeology proves Abraham never existed."

**P3 (🔴)** *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b))* An **invented** parish bulletin column:

> "Scholars now say Abraham may never have existed. If that is true, our faith collapses: Paul built justification on Abraham's faith, and the Church has always taught the patriarchs as history. Thankfully the Biblical Commission ruled in 1905 that biblical history must be taken as history, and that settles it for Catholics."

(a) Name two errors about the levels or the state of scholarship. (b) Does a Catholic reading of Genesis 15:6 need a historical Abraham? Any verdict passes if you use the distinction between the literal sense and historical reportage and state the strongest reason for the other side. **150 words or fewer for (b).**

<details>
<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** **Land**: "come into the land which I shall shew thee". **Seed**: "I will make of thee a great nation". **Blessing**: "I will bless thee", "magnify thy name", "thou shalt be blessed", blessing and cursing others according to how they treat Abram, and "in thee shall all the kindreds of the earth be blessed". Credit noting that the land is promised only as a destination here; it is not granted until 12:7 and 15:18.
**Must hit, strict (b):** **Passive**: the nations *receive* blessing through Abram; he is its channel, the reading Paul uses (Gal 3:8). **Reflexive**: the nations *invoke* Abram as the standard of blessedness ("may you be like Abram"); he is its model, and the verse does not say they share it.
**Wrong turns:** putting "great nation" under blessing and leaving seed empty. Calling one rendering correct: the form allows both, and the Hebrew elsewhere (e.g. 22:18) uses a form usually taken as reflexive, which is why translators divide.
**Model answer:** (a) Land: "the land which I shall shew thee". Seed: "a great nation". Blessing: "bless thee", "magnify thy name", blessing those who bless him, and "in thee shall all the kindreds of the earth be blessed". (b) Read as passive, the nations will be blessed through Abram, so he is the means of a blessing meant for everyone. Read as reflexive, they will use his name when they bless, so he is the model of blessedness without the verse promising that they will share it.

**P2** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict:** (1) **Church teaching**: Scripture's own statement (Gen 15:6), taught in the Catechism (CCC 146). *How* the reckoning works is a further, disputed question. (2) **Past scholarly consensus** (Albright), now a minority, and the Nuzi support is contested. It is not doctrine. (3) **Majority scholarly position**, with no magisterial standing either way. (4) **Church teaching**: Dei Verbum 11, conciliar teaching (owned by `fundamental-theology` 3.2). (5) **Not establishable**: archaeology can show anachronism in the telling, not the absence of an individual.
**Wrong turns:** marking (2) as Church teaching because of the 1905 response. Marking (3) as settled fact. Marking (5) as the majority view: the majority says "unrecoverable", not "disproved".
**Model answer:** (1) Church teaching, Gen 15:6 via CCC 146. (2) Former consensus, now a minority. (3) Majority hypothesis. (4) Church teaching, Dei Verbum 11. (5) Unprovable overstatement.

**P3** *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b))*

**Must hit, strict (a):** two of: (i) "the Church has always taught the patriarchs as history" turns a traditional assumption into defined teaching. **No magisterial act defines or dates the patriarchs.** (ii) The 1905 response is treated as settling the matter today. It set a burden of proof and was binding then, but its force changed with the 1948 Suhard letter, *Divino Afflante Spiritu* and the Commission's consultative status after 1971. The question is now freely disputed. (Equally wrong would be calling it meaningless.) (iii) "Scholars now say Abraham may never have existed" is roughly fair as a report, since most doubt he is recoverable. "Collapses" skips Dei Verbum 12: genre decides what a narrative asserts.
**Must hit, any verdict (b):** state what the literal sense of 15:6 asserts: trust in God's promise is counted as right standing. Distinguish that assertion from the claim that the episode happened as reported in a given century. Take a verdict. State the strongest opposing reason. For "yes", that is Dei Verbum 12's recognition of genres, and the fact that the verse's theological point survives in a story of origins. For "no", that is the worry that a faith grounded in God acting in history needs some real history, and that Paul's argument presupposes a real Abraham.
**Wrong turns:** answering (b) by asserting a historicity verdict as fact. Swapping one level error for its mirror ("the 1905 decree no longer matters at all").
**Model answer (b), one of several:** Not in full. What Genesis 15:6 asserts is a truth about God and trust: faith in a promise is counted as righteousness. That truth holds if the chapter tells Israel's origins in a genre closer to a family saga than to a chronicle, and Dei Verbum 12 lets genre decide what is asserted. Still, the strongest reason for the other side is real. Biblical faith claims that God acted in history, and Paul argues from the order of events in Abraham's life (Rom 4:10). A wholly invented founder would weaken that. So I would say the faith needs a real history of promise behind Israel, not a verified biography of Abraham.

</details>

## Flashback

**From Lesson [2.2](02-02-who-wrote-the-pentateuch.md) (Who wrote the Pentateuch?):** *(Exegetical.)* Two verses (Douay–Rheims):

> "Which when Abram had heard, to wit, that his brother Lot was taken, he numbered of the servants born in his house, three hundred and eighteen, well appointed: and pursued them to Dan." (Gen 14:14)
>
> "Calling the name of the city Dan, after the name of their father, who was the son of Israel, which before was called Lais." (Judg 18:29, in the period of the judges)

An **invented** study-bible note on Genesis 14:14:

> "The town was not called Dan until the tribe of Dan renamed it in the days of the Judges. This one verse proves that Genesis was written centuries after Moses, by the Yahwist, whom scholars have securely dated to about 950 BC. A Catholic who still thinks Moses had any hand in the Torah is denying the inerrancy of Scripture."

Diagnose three errors: (a) what one late place name does and does not show; (b) the note's picture of the source theory and its dates; (c) the level error in the last sentence. **Two sentences per part.**

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):** "Dan" is a **post-Mosaic notice**: it shows that **this wording** comes from after the Judges-era renaming. It does **not** show that the whole book is late, since a later hand can **update** an old place name, and even the **tradition allowed later additions**: the baraita in Bava Batra 14b–15a gives Deuteronomy's last verses to Joshua, and Challoner assigns Deut 34 to Joshua or later prophets. One verse dates the verse, not the book.
**Must hit, strict (b):** J's date is **reconstructed and disputed**, not secure. Since the 1970s the classic Yahwist has been dated to the exile (Van Seters) or dissolved into blocks (Rendtorff); much of European scholarship now works with a Priestly/non-Priestly model **without a J**, while the neo-documentarians revive J redefined. **There is no consensus**, so "securely dated" misreports the field.
**Must hit, strict (c):** **authorship is not defined**: the Church defines the books as canonical and inspired, and "the five books of Moses" is a traditional title, not a ruling on who wrote them. Inspiration attaches to the books and their true human authors, whoever they were, and inerrancy concerns what the text asserts, so holding a **substantial Mosaic origin**, or denying it, is a **freely disputed** position that denies nothing.
**Wrong turns:** answering (a) by insisting Moses wrote every word, 14:14's "Dan" included: the tradition itself did not require that. Answering (c) with the mirror error, "the Church requires Mosaic authorship". Treating the divine name in the surrounding chapter as the decisive evidence; the note's argument does not use it.
**Model answer:** (a) The name "Dan" shows that the verse, as worded, was written or updated after the Danites renamed Lais. It cannot date the whole Torah, since tradition itself allowed later additions, such as Deuteronomy's account of Moses's death. (b) The Yahwist's date has been disputed since the 1970s, and many scholars no longer posit a continuous J at all. The note presents one model's reconstruction as a settled result. (c) Who wrote the Pentateuch has never been defined; the Church defines the books' canonicity and inspiration. A Catholic may hold a substantial Mosaic origin or a later composition without touching inerrancy, which concerns what the text asserts.

</details>
