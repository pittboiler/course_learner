# The Old Testament · Lesson 5.4: Sirach and the Wisdom of Solomon

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 5: Psalms and wisdom · Builds on: [5.2 Proverbs, Ecclesiastes, and the Song of Songs](05-02-proverbs-ecclesiastes-and-the-song-of-songs.md), [5.3 Job](05-03-job.md) · Unlocks: [6.1 How the Old Testament canon formed](06-01-how-the-old-testament-canon-formed.md), [6.2 Tobit, Judith, Baruch, and the Maccabees](06-02-tobit-judith-baruch-and-the-maccabees.md)

## Why this matters

Proverbs sent Lady Wisdom out into the whole world. The last two wisdom books ask where she lives. Sirach answers from Jerusalem, in Hebrew: in Israel, as the Law of Moses. Wisdom of Solomon answers from Alexandria, in Greek: she is the radiance of God's own light, words the New Testament borrows for the Son. Both books are [deuterocanonical](../reference.md#deuterocanonical-books): in the Catholic Old Testament, not in the Jewish canon.

## The idea

Two books, two cities, two languages, about a century apart.

**Sirach** (the Vulgate's *Ecclesiasticus*) is signed "Jesus the son of Sirach, of Jerusalem" (DR 50:29 [Greek 50:27]); scholars call him Ben Sira. He wrote Proverbs-style instruction in Hebrew, on speech, money, friendship and banquets, and ended with a "praise of famous men" (chapters 44–50). His grandson put the book into Greek in Egypt and added a prologue ([1.1](01-01-what-the-old-testament-is.md) read it for the canon's three parts).

**Wisdom of Solomon** was written in Greek, most likely by an Alexandrian Jew. It speaks as a king whom God "commanded ... to build a temple" (9:8), yet never names Solomon. Its vocabulary is philosophical: the four cardinal virtues (8:7), and "the corruptible body is a load upon the soul" (9:15), a line a Platonist would sign.

So late wisdom does two things with Lady Wisdom. Sirach **brings her home**: she settles in Zion and turns out to be the Torah. Wisdom of Solomon **translates her** into Greek philosophy, as an emanation of God's glory. The first move shaped Judaism; the second gave the New Testament words for Christ.

## Source

Ecclesiasticus 24, Wisdom speaking (Douay–Rheims, selected verses; Greek numbers in brackets):

> "I came out of the mouth of the most High, the firstborn before all creatures ... [DR 24:5; Greek 24:3] Then the creator of all things commanded, and said to me: and he that made me, rested in my tabernacle, And he said to me: Let thy dwelling be in Jacob, and thy inheritance in Israel, and take root in my elect. [DR 24:12–13; Greek 24:8] ... All these things are the book of life, and the covenant of the most High, and the knowledge of truth. Moses commanded a law in the precepts of justices, and an inheritance to the house of Jacob, and the promises to Israel. [DR 24:32–33; Greek 24:23]"

Three moves. Wisdom comes from God's *mouth*: she is his word. She roams the cosmos seeking rest and is told where to pitch her tent (Greek *kataskēnoun*, "to tent, encamp"). Then the speaker names her: *all this* is the covenant book, the Law Moses gave. My translation of the shorter Greek of 24:23: "All these things are the book of the covenant of God Most High, the law which Moses commanded us, an inheritance for the assemblies of Jacob."

The Douay's numbers run ahead because it translates the Latin, a longer text. "The firstborn before all creatures" (*primogenita ante omnem creaturam*) is a Latin addition; the Greek of 24:3 lacks it.

## The argument

1. **Dating Ben Sira.** The grandson came to Egypt "in the eight and thirtieth year" of "Ptolemy Evergetes". Ptolemy III Euergetes reigned about 25 years (246–222 BC), so the king must be Ptolemy VIII Euergetes II, who counted his years from 170 BC. Year 38 is then 133/132, usually given as **132 BC**: **reconstructed** from a stated regnal year, but firm. Two generations back puts the grandfather around 190–175 BC. Chapter 50 praises the high priest "Simon ... the son of Onias", usually taken as Simon II (about 200 BC), and the book knows nothing of Antiochus IV's persecution (167 BC, **secure**). Ben Sira's date is **reconstructed**, not disputed ([dating discipline](../reference.md#dating-discipline)).
2. **Wisdom becomes Torah.** In Proverbs 8 Wisdom is universal ([5.2](05-02-proverbs-ecclesiastes-and-the-song-of-songs.md)). Sirach keeps that: "in every nation" she had rule (DR 24:10). Then he gives her one address: Jacob, Zion, the Temple liturgy (DR 24:14–15), the book of Moses. Baruch 4:1 makes the same move ([6.2](06-02-tobit-judith-baruch-and-the-maccabees.md)). *In words:* the wisdom that orders the world is no secret of the sages; Israel has it in writing. This is **[wisdom identified with Torah](../reference.md#wisdom-identified-with-torah)**, and the rabbis carried it on ([Jewish readings](../reference.md#jewish-readings)). *Genesis Rabbah* opens (1:1) by reading Proverbs 8:30 as the Torah speaking: God looked into the Torah, as an architect consults his plan, and created the world. The rabbis did not canonize Ben Sira, yet the Talmud quotes him by name (*Hagigah* 13a), and *Sanhedrin* 100b records an unresolved debate over whether reading him is forbidden.
3. **Hebrew Sirach, lost and found.** For centuries Sirach was known only in Greek, Latin and Syriac. In 1896 Solomon Schechter identified a leaf from the Cairo Genizah, a medieval synagogue's storeroom, as the Hebrew Ben Sira. The Genizah yielded parts of several medieval copies, about two-thirds of the book. Ancient copies followed: fragments at Qumran (2Q18; Sir 51 in the Psalms scroll 11QPs^a), and a scroll of 39:27–44:17 found at Masada in 1964 by Yigael Yadin's excavation, older than the fortress's fall in 73/74 AD. So there are **three layers**: the Hebrew, the grandson's Greek, and longer Greek and Latin forms. Jerome did not retranslate Sirach or Wisdom, so the Vulgate and the Douay carry the Old Latin with its additions.
4. **Wisdom of Solomon: Greek, Alexandrian, anonymous.** No Hebrew original is known; the wordplay works only in Greek. Chapters 11–19 retell the Exodus for Jews living among Egyptian idolaters. Most scholars date it between the late first century BC and the early first century AD. David Winston (1979) argued for a date after Rome took Egypt in 30 BC, from its vocabulary and an apparent allusion to the ruler cult (14:16–20). The date is **reconstructed**. The ancient Church knew the Solomonic voice was a convention. The Muratorian fragment (probably late second century) calls it "written by the friends of Solomon in his honour", and Augustine reports the learned view that it is not Solomon's (*City of God* XVII.20).
5. **Immortality.** "The souls of the just are in the hand of God", and "their hope is full of immortality" (3:1, 4; Greek *athanasia*). Set this between Job's "man ... shall not rise again" ([5.3](05-03-job.md)) and 2 Maccabees 7's bodily resurrection ([6.2](06-02-tobit-judith-baruch-and-the-maccabees.md)): the canon holds a development, which Boss problem 5 examines.
6. **The just man persecuted (2:12–20).** *Literal sense:* the ungodly, sure that death is final (2:1–9), plot against a righteous man whose life rebukes them. He "calleth himself the son of God" (2:13), so "if he be the true son of God, he will defend him ... Let us condemn him to a most shameful death" (2:18, 20). The Greek of 2:13 is *pais kyriou*, "child" or "servant of the Lord", the *pais* of the Greek Isaiah's Servant ([4.3](04-03-the-servant-of-the-lord.md)). The just man is a type, the faithful Jew under mockery, not a named individual. *Spiritual sense:* at the cross the chief priests say "He trusted in God: let him now deliver him if he will have him. For he said: I am the Son of God" (Matt 27:43). The first clause quotes Psalm 22:8 [Douay 21:9]; many commentators hear Wisdom 2:18 in the second. Augustine says that in Wisdom "the passion of Christ is most openly prophesied" and quotes 2:12–21 (*City of God* XVII.20; [typology](../reference.md#typology)).
7. **Wisdom 7:26 and the Son.** Wisdom is "the brightness of eternal light, and the unspotted mirror of God's majesty, and the image of his goodness". "Brightness" is *apaugasma*, the radiance streaming from a light (some render it "reflection"). Its one New Testament use is Hebrews 1:3: the Son is "the brightness of his glory and the figure of his substance". Colossians 1:15 calls him "the image [*eikōn*] of the invisible God". In its literal sense, most exegetes read Wisdom 7 as a bold personification of God's own wisdom within strict monotheism, with no second divine person in view. The New Testament applies that vocabulary to a person ([`new-testament` 6.2](../../new-testament/lessons/06-02-hebrews.md); [`trinity-and-god` 2.2](../../trinity-and-god/lessons/02-02-the-father-and-the-son-in-the-new-testament.md)). Origen reads the three texts together: the Son's generation is eternal "as the brilliancy which is produced from the sun" (*De Principiis* I.2.4–5, ANF vol. 4). *In words:* a light is never without its shining, and that became a standard image of eternal generation.

**Mark the levels.** That Sirach and Wisdom are **inspired and canonical** is **defined dogma**: Trent lists "Wisdom, Ecclesiasticus" with Genesis, with no second rank ([`fundamental-theology` 3.3](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/03-03-the-canon-as-defined.md); [ladder](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-04-the-ladder-of-doctrinal-authority.md)). **No Church teaching makes Solomon the author of Wisdom**; non-Solomonic authorship is the scholarly consensus. Canonicity rests on inspiration and the Church's reception, not the name attached ([canonicity vs authorship](../reference.md#canonicity-vs-authorship)). The dates are **reconstructed**. Which form of Sirach's text is original is **textual criticism**: *Divino Afflante Spiritu* (1943, §21) explained that the Vulgate's Tridentine "authenticity" is juridical, not critical ([church teaching vs hypothesis](../reference.md#church-teaching-vs-hypothesis)). That the Son is the Father's radiance is **Scripture and Church teaching** (Heb 1:3; the Creed's "Light from Light"). How Wisdom 2 and 7 relate to him, as literal prophecy, fuller sense or type, is **freely disputed** among Catholics ([4.3](04-03-the-servant-of-the-lord.md)).

## The two books side by side

| | Sirach | Wisdom of Solomon |
|---|---|---|
| Author | Ben Sira, Jerusalem scribe, named (DR 50:29) | anonymous; speaks as a king, never names Solomon |
| Language | Hebrew; Greek by the grandson | Greek |
| Date | c. 190–175 BC; translation c. 132 (**reconstructed**) | late 1st c. BC – early 1st c. AD (**reconstructed**) |
| Survives in | Hebrew (Genizah, Masada, Qumran), Greek, Latin | Greek, Latin |
| Where Wisdom lives | in Israel, as the Torah (24) | in God, as radiance and image (7:25–26) |
| New Testament echo | Wisdom tenting in Jacob, beside John 1:14 | Heb 1:3; Col 1:15; Matt 27:43 |
| Jewish reception | quoted in the Talmud, not canonized | not preserved in rabbinic tradition |

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean): Sirach 24 and John 1:14.** *Literal sense first.* Ben Sira says God's wisdom has one home on earth, Israel's Law and Temple: a claim about the Torah, not a person. *Canonical reading second* ([canonical reading](../reference.md#canonical-reading)). John's Word "was made flesh, and dwelt among us", and the verb is *eskēnōsen*, "pitched his tent", Sirach's verb for Wisdom tenting in Jacob ([`new-testament` 2.5](../../new-testament/lessons/02-05-the-theology-of-john.md)). Read together, John does what Sirach did, giving Wisdom an address, and names a different one: a person, not a book. Sirach's own meaning stands.

**Example 2 (hard): "the firstborn before all creatures".** The Douay's Ecclesiasticus 24:5 has Wisdom call herself "firstborn before all creatures", which sounds like Colossians 1:15. *Where the method strains:* the phrase is absent from the Greek of 24:3, and no Hebrew survives for the verse; it is almost certainly a Latin-era expansion. Is it canonical? Trent received the books "entire with all their parts, as they have been used to be read in the Catholic Church, and as they are contained in the old Latin vulgate edition". It did not rule on which reading of a verse is original, and *Divino Afflante Spiritu* sent exegetes to the original texts. So mark the verse as a variant and take the Christology from Colossians itself. The Greek of the same chapter says "he created me" (*ektisen me*, 24:9), a phrase that fed the fourth-century fight over Proverbs 8:22 ([`christology` 1.4](../../christology/lessons/01-04-arius-and-the-divinity-of-the-son.md)).

## Watch out

- **"The Church teaches that Solomon wrote Wisdom."** It teaches that Wisdom is canonical. Authorship is a historical question, and "not Solomon" was the learned view in Augustine's day. The opposite slip, "not by Solomon, so not really Scripture", demotes a defined dogma.
- **"The Douay's Ecclesiasticus is Ben Sira's text."** It translates the longer Latin, numbered differently. Give the Greek number too (24:23 = DR 24:32–33).
- **"Wisdom 7 teaches the Trinity."** Its literal sense personifies God's wisdom. Reading it of the Son is a spiritual or canonical reading; label it as one.
- **"The Talmud quotes Ben Sira, so he was once canonical."** Quotation is not canonization; the same Talmud debates banning him ([6.1](06-01-how-the-old-testament-canon-formed.md)).

## One-liner

> Sirach gives Wisdom an address, the Torah; Wisdom of Solomon gives her Greek words for God's radiance, which Hebrews gives to the Son. Canonicity is dogma; dates and authors are history.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢)** *(Formal (a) · Exegetical (b))* The grandson came to Egypt "in the eight and thirtieth year" of Ptolemy Euergetes. Ptolemy III Euergetes reigned 246–222 BC. Ptolemy VIII Euergetes II counted his regnal years from 170 BC (year 1 = 170/169). (a) Which king is meant, and in what year did the grandson arrive? Show the arithmetic. (b) Name two internal features of Sirach that fit a date for the grandfather some two generations earlier, and mark each date in this problem *secure*, *reconstructed* or *disputed*. **Two sentences for (b).**

**P2 (🟡)** *(Exegetical)* Wisdom 7:24–27 (Douay–Rheims):

> "For wisdom is more active than all active things; and reacheth everywhere, by reason of her purity. For she is a vapour of the power of God, and a certain pure emmanation of the glory of the Almighty God: and therefore no defiled thing cometh into her. For she is the brightness of eternal light, and the unspotted mirror of God's majesty, and the image of his goodness. And being but one, she can do all things: and remaining in herself the same, she reneweth all things, and through nations conveyeth herself into holy souls, she maketh the friends of God and prophets."

Hebrews 1:3: the Son is "the brightness of his glory and the figure of his substance".

(a) Name the Greek word behind "brightness" in both texts, and one more word or image in Wisdom 7:25–26 that Hebrews 1:3 or Colossians 1:15 shares. (b) Name one thing Hebrews 1:3 says that Wisdom 7 does not. (c) State the literal sense of "she" in this passage in one sentence, and say what kind of reading makes it a text about the Son. **100 words in total.**

**P3 (🔴)** *(Exegetical)* Augustine, *City of God* XVII.20 (NPNF first series, vol. 2):

> "But it has been customary to ascribe to Solomon other two, of which one is called Wisdom, the other Ecclesiasticus, on account of some resemblance of style,—but the more learned have no doubt that they are not his; yet of old the Church, especially the Western, received them into authority".

(a) Augustine answers two different questions in this sentence. Name them. (b) Give each claim a status: **defined dogma**, **scholarly consensus**, **reconstructed date**, **spiritual-sense reading**, or **error**. One line each.

1. "Wisdom of Solomon is inspired Scripture."
2. "Solomon did not write Wisdom of Solomon."
3. "Wisdom was composed in Alexandria around the turn of the era."
4. "Since Solomon did not write Wisdom, its place in the canon is doubtful."
5. "Wisdom 2:12–20 prophesies Christ's passion."

<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Formal (a) · Exegetical (b))*

**Must hit, strict (a):** **Ptolemy VIII Euergetes II**, because Ptolemy III reigned only about 25 years (246 − 222 = 24, plus a partial year) and so never reached a 38th. With year 1 = 170/169, year 38 = 170 − 37 = **133/132 BC**. "132 BC" is the conventional answer.
**Must hit, strict (b):** any two of: the praise of the high priest **Simon son of Onias** (ch. 50, usually Simon II, about 200 BC); **no trace of Antiochus IV's persecution** (from 167 BC); the grandson's own "grandfather", which puts two generations back from 132. Statuses: 246–222 and 170 are **secure** regnal data. The arrival in 132 is **reconstructed** but firm, an arithmetic inference from a stated regnal year. The grandfather's date (about 190–175) and Simon's (about 200) are **reconstructed**. Antiochus's persecution (167) is **secure**.
**Wrong turns:** choosing Ptolemy III and computing 246 − 37 = 209. Counting year 38 as 170 − 38 = 132 with no account of year 1 (the right answer by the wrong route; credit it, but note the off-by-one). Calling Ben Sira's date "disputed". It is reconstructed, and the reconstructions agree closely.
**Model answer:** (a) Ptolemy VIII: 170 − 37 = 133, so year 38 is 133/132, conventionally 132 BC. Ptolemy III never had a 38th year. (b) Chapter 50 praises Simon son of Onias, probably Simon II (about 200 BC, reconstructed), and the book knows nothing of the persecution of 167 (secure). Both fit a grandfather writing about 190–175 (reconstructed); the regnal data are secure, and the arrival date is a firm reconstruction.

**P2** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** ***apaugasma*** ("brightness, radiance"). Plus one of: **"image" (*eikōn*)**, shared with Colossians 1:15 ("image of the invisible God"); or **"glory"** (Wis 7:25, "the glory of the Almighty"; Heb 1:3, "his glory").
**Must hit, strict (b):** one of: "**the figure of his substance**" (*charaktēr tēs hypostaseōs*), an exact impression of God's very being, which Wisdom does not say; Hebrews speaks of a **Son**, a person, where Wisdom speaks of "she"; in Heb 1:3 he upholds all things and makes purgation of sins.
**Must hit, strict (c):** literally, "she" is **God's own wisdom personified**, an attribute or emanation of God described in bold images within Jewish monotheism, not a second divine person in the author's mind. Reading it of the Son is a **spiritual or canonical reading** (Hebrews and the Fathers, e.g. Origen), and it should be labelled as one.
**Wrong turns:** saying the author of Wisdom taught the Trinity. Saying Hebrews "quotes" Wisdom. It borrows a word and an image, and it shifts "eternal light" to "his glory". Saying the Latin has the same word in both places. The Vulgate has *candor* in Wisdom and *splendor* in Hebrews, and only the Greek shares a word.
**Model answer:** (a) *Apaugasma*, "brightness", in both. Wisdom's "image of his goodness" answers Colossians' "image of the invisible God". (b) Hebrews adds "the figure of his substance" and says it of a Son. (c) Literally "she" is God's wisdom personified, a radiance of God and not a second person. Reading it of Christ is a spiritual, canonical reading, as in Hebrews and Origen.

**P3** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** **authorship** (who wrote the books: not Solomon, say "the more learned") and **authority** (whether the Church receives them: "received them into authority"). Augustine answers the two separately and differently.
**Must hit, strict (b):** (1) **Defined dogma**: Trent's canon, [`fundamental-theology` 3.3](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/03-03-the-canon-as-defined.md). (2) **Scholarly consensus**, with no magisterial ruling either way (already Augustine's "more learned"). (3) **Reconstructed date**, the scholarly majority. (4) **Error**: it makes canonicity depend on attribution, and canonicity rests on inspiration and the Church's reception. Denying the book's canonicity would contradict a defined dogma. (5) **Spiritual-sense reading**: Augustine's, echoed at Matt 27:43. Its literal sense is the persecuted righteous Jew. That the text points to Christ is a typological reading, not a defined one.
**Wrong turns:** marking (2) as "contrary to Church teaching": Trent names the book "Wisdom" and attributes it to no one. Marking (5) as defined dogma, or as the literal sense. Reading Augustine's "especially the Western" as a denial of canonicity. He is reporting reception history.
**Model answer:** (a) Authorship (not Solomon's) and authority (received by the Church). (b) 1: defined dogma, Trent. 2: scholarly consensus, no magisterial level. 3: reconstructed date, majority view. 4: error, since canonicity does not rest on attribution. 5: spiritual-sense (typological) reading, Augustine's; the literal sense concerns the righteous Jew.

## Flashback

**From Lesson [5.2](05-02-proverbs-ecclesiastes-and-the-song-of-songs.md) (Proverbs, Ecclesiastes, and the Song of Songs):** *(Exegetical.)* Ecclesiastes 9:7–11 (Douay–Rheims). In verse 9, both "unsteady life" and "thy vanity" render Hebrew *hebel*, "vapour, breath" (the Hebrew says "the days of your *hebel*" twice). In verse 10, "hell" renders *sheol*, the grave, the shadowy place of all the dead.

> Go then, and eat thy bread with joy, and drink thy wine with gladness: because thy works please God. At all times let thy garments be white, and let not oil depart from thy head. Live joyfully with the wife whom thou lovest, all the days of thy unsteady life, which are given to thee under the sun, all the time of thy vanity: for this is thy portion in life, and in thy labour wherewith thou labourest under the sun. Whatsoever thy hand is able to do, do it earnestly: for neither work, nor reason, nor wisdom, nor knowledge shall be in hell, whither thou art hastening. I turned me to another thing, and I saw that under the sun, the race is not to the swift, nor the battle to the strong, nor bread to the wise, nor riches to the learned, nor favour to the skilful: but time and chance in all.

(a) What does Qoheleth tell his reader to do, and on what grounds? Show from the passage what *hebel* means here. (b) Set verse 11 beside Proverbs 10:4 (Douay): "The slothful hand hath wrought poverty: but the hand of the industrious getteth riches." Does Qoheleth deny Proverbs' rule? (c) An invented study-Bible note says: "Here Solomon, writing in the years when he had fallen away, recommends worldly pleasure. The Church teaches that he wrote this book, and 'vanity' brands these delights as sinful." Name two errors, and give the correct level or reading for each. **Two sentences per part.**

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):** The passage commands **enjoying food, wine, festive dress and married love, and working "earnestly"**. It gives two grounds: these things are **God's gift and approved by him** ("thy works please God", "this is thy portion"), and **life is short and ends in *sheol***, where there is no work or wisdom. *Hebel* here means **fleeting, brief, breath-like**, not worthless or sinful. The context proves it: the reader is told to *enjoy* these days *because* they are *hebel*, and the joy is God-approved.
**Must hit, strict (b):** **No outright denial.** Qoheleth **presses the rule's exceptions**. Proverbs states what usually happens, and Qoheleth observes that "time and chance" can overturn it for the swift, the wise and the skilful alike. He does not deny that diligence and wisdom have value (v. 10 says to work earnestly, and 9:16–18 says that "wisdom is better than strength"). He denies only that they **guarantee** the outcome.
**Must hit, strict (c):** any two of: **(1) Solomonic authorship is not Church teaching.** The book is defined as canonical, not as Solomon's. Its authorship and date are **scholarly questions with no magisterial level**, and most scholars date it to the Persian or Hellenistic period (**disputed**). **(2) *Hebel* does not mean "sinful".** It means fleeting breath, and "vanity" pushing toward moral contempt is the Vulgate/Douay's colouring. **(3) The passage does not recommend sinful pleasure.** It commends these joys *as God's gift* ("thy works please God"). The "fallen Solomon" backstory is imported, not in the text.
**Wrong turns:** reading "neither work, nor reason ... in hell" as the Christian hell of punishment, when *sheol* is the common grave of all. Reading verse 10 as a doctrinal denial of any afterlife. The book asks and does not settle ("Who knoweth", 3:21). Calling Qoheleth a nihilist who rejects wisdom. Correcting the note by asserting that the Church teaches Solomon did *not* write the book, which is equally no teaching.
**Model answer:** (a) Eat, drink, dress for a feast, love your wife and work hard, because God approves these as your portion and because *sheol* ends all work. *Hebel* here is "fleeting breath": the days are to be enjoyed precisely because they pass, so the word cannot mean "sinful". (b) Not a denial. Qoheleth grants that effort and wisdom matter but observes that "time and chance" can defeat them, so Proverbs states a tendency, not a guarantee. (c) Solomonic authorship is a scholarly question with no magisterial level, since the Church defines the book's canonicity, not its author. And "vanity" (*hebel*) means transience, not sin: the passage calls these joys God's gift, not worldly vice.

</details>
