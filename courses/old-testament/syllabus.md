# The Old Testament — Syllabus

> Catholic Theology · Tier 1 · ~27 lessons · Prereqs: [fundamental-theology](../fundamental-theology/syllabus.md) · Roadmap id: `old-testament`

## Goal

This course reads the Old Testament the way the Church's own documents on exegesis ask it to be read: as the word of God in human words, so that faith and serious historical-critical scholarship both do real work, and neither replaces the other. You will finish able to read a passage in its literal sense, which means its genre, its setting and what its human author meant, and then say how the tradition reads the same passage spiritually and canonically. You will also be able to report a composition, dating or historicity question as the scholarly field actually argues it, name the evidence each side leans on, and say what inspiration does and does not require of the answer. The course moves through the whole Catholic Old Testament: how to read it; the Pentateuch (the creation accounts, the patriarchs, the Exodus, covenant and law, and how the five books were composed); the history from conquest to return; Isaiah, Jeremiah, Ezekiel and the Twelve; Psalms, the wisdom books and Job; how the canon formed and the deuterocanonical books; Daniel and apocalyptic; and the Old Testament read in light of the New. Jewish readings of the shared texts are presented as a living tradition's own interpretations, stated as its interpreters state them. It deliberately skips inspiration, inerrancy and the canon as doctrines (`fundamental-theology` Module 3), creation and the fall as dogma, messianic prophecy as an argument against Judaism, the Jubilee as theology, biblical Hebrew as a language (single words are translated when the word is the point), and Near Eastern archaeology beyond what a question needs.

## How practice works in this course

Problems follow [HUMANITIES-BUILD-BRIEF.md](../../HUMANITIES-BUILD-BRIEF.md). **Close reading (archetype 7) is the workhorse:** a passage of 150 words or fewer from the Douay–Rheims (Challoner), and the question is which reading the text supports and which words decide it. These parts are **exegetical** and graded strictly. So are statements of what a Church document teaches, at what weight, and what a named scholarly position or Jewish interpreter actually holds. Overstating a document's authority, or saying the scholarly field agrees on something it does not, is an exegetical error. **Evaluative** parts, verdict-neutral, cover questions that are genuinely open. Some are open among historians and exegetes: how the Pentateuch was composed, whether the patriarchs and the conquest are historical, the date of Daniel. Some are open among Catholic theologians: the *sensus plenior*, how far typology may go, how the historical-critical method should be weighed against canonical and patristic reading. A conclusion passes if the required moves are made. The student must state the rival positions, name the evidence each side leans on, and say what inspiration does and does not require of the answer. **Find the crux** (archetype 5) and **diagnose** (archetype 4: an invented sermon paragraph, study-Bible note or documentary voice-over) fill out each module. There are no apologetic parts: arguing the Catholic reading against Jewish or Protestant readings belongs to `apologetics-foundations` and `apologetics-catholic-claims`. Other public-domain sources: Aquinas (English Dominican Province); Augustine, Jerome and Origen in Schaff's *Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers* and *Ante-Nicene Fathers*; Josephus (Whiston); Luther's prefaces in pre-1930 translations. Modern Church documents (*Divino Afflante Spiritu*, Dei Verbum, the Biblical Commission's 1993 and 2001 documents, *Verbum Domini*) and modern scholars are paraphrased and cited by name.

## Dangerous Checklist

When you finish, you can:

- [ ] Read an Old Testament passage in its literal sense (genre, setting, the human author's meaning) and then give its spiritual senses, keeping the two distinct
- [ ] State what the Church's documents on exegesis ask of a Catholic reader, from *Providentissimus Deus* to *Verbum Domini*, and what they say the historical-critical method can and cannot do
- [ ] Weigh a Pontifical Biblical Commission statement correctly: what its early decrees bound, how later clarifications changed their force, and why its post-1971 documents are not magisterial acts
- [ ] Lay out the documentary hypothesis, why it broke down, and the models that replaced it, and say which Catholic claims do and do not depend on the answer
- [ ] Place a book or event on the timeline (patriarchs, Exodus, monarchy, 722, 587/586, 539, the Maccabean crisis) and mark which dates are secure, which are reconstructed and which are disputed
- [ ] Read Genesis 1–11, the Exodus and the conquest narratives against their Near Eastern parallels, and say what the parallels show and do not show
- [ ] Explain what a prophet is in Israel, and read an oracle of Isaiah, Jeremiah, Ezekiel or the Twelve in its historical setting before reading it in light of Christ
- [ ] State the Jewish and the Christian readings of a contested text (Isaiah 7:14, Isaiah 53, Daniel 7) as each tradition's interpreters would, and name the words each reading turns on
- [ ] Read a psalm by its genre and parallelism, find it under both the Hebrew and the Vulgate/Douay numbering, and read Job and the wisdom books on the question of retribution
- [ ] Tell the story of the Old Testament canon's formation from the evidence: the Septuagint, Qumran, Josephus, the Fathers' lists, Jerome and Augustine, the African councils, Luther. Separate what is attested from what is myth ("the Council of Jamnia")
- [ ] Say what each deuterocanonical book and Greek addition contributes, and where later doctrine has leaned on it
- [ ] Explain how the Church reads the Old Testament in light of the New without cancelling its own meaning, using Dei Verbum's chapter on the Old Testament and the Biblical Commission's 2001 document on the Jewish people and their Scriptures

## Modules

### Module 1: How the Church reads the Old Testament

What the book is, and the reading tools the rest of the course uses: the senses of Scripture, the historical-critical method with its limits, and canonical reading.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1.1 | What the Old Testament is | Say what books are in which Old Testament, in what order and languages, and which texts they survive in | Tanakh (Torah, Nevi'im, Ketuvim) vs the Christian fourfold order, Hebrew, Aramaic and Greek, the Masoretic Text, the Septuagint, the Dead Sea Scrolls, the Vulgate and the Douay–Rheims, the protocanonical/deuterocanonical terminology (the canon's history waits for 6.1) |
| 1.2 | The senses of Scripture | Give a passage its literal sense and its three spiritual senses, and say what grounds the spiritual ones | literal, allegorical, moral, anagogical (CCC 115–118; ST I q.1 a.10); "all the senses are founded on the literal"; typology vs allegory; Origen and the Antiochenes; Genesis 22 as the running case |
| 1.3 | The historical-critical method and its limits | Say what the Church's documents endorse in historical criticism, what limits they set, and how much authority each document carries | *Providentissimus Deus* (1893), the Biblical Commission's early decrees and their weight, *Divino Afflante Spiritu* (1943) and literary genres, Dei Verbum 12, *The Interpretation of the Bible in the Church* (1993), Ratzinger's 1988 Erasmus lecture on the method's limits, *Verbum Domini* (2010); the New Testament's own case study of the early responses is [`new-testament`](../new-testament/syllabus.md) 1.1, so this lesson uses the Old Testament ones (weights cited from [`fundamental-theology`](../fundamental-theology/syllabus.md) 4.5) |
| 1.4 | Canonical reading and the unity of the Testaments | Read a passage within the whole canon and the Church's faith, and say how that differs from harmonizing | Dei Verbum 12's three criteria (the content and unity of all Scripture, the living Tradition, the analogy of faith), Childs's canonical approach, "the New in the Old concealed, the Old in the New revealed" (Augustine, via Dei Verbum 16), the risk of flattening the Old Testament's own voice |

**Boss problem 1:** Read ST I q.1 a.10, "Whether in Holy Scripture a word may have several senses?" (English Dominican Province), and Genesis 22:1–14 (Douay–Rheims). (a) State Aquinas's account of the senses: what grounds the spiritual sense, why it does not make Scripture equivocal, and why argument is drawn from the literal sense alone. *(Exegetical.)* (b) Give the literal sense of Genesis 22 as historical-critical scholars read it, presenting at least two rival scholarly readings (for example, a narrative about testing and trust, or a polemic against child sacrifice) as rivals. Then give the typological reading the tradition draws from Isaac and the wood (Hebrews 11:17–19), and the place of the *akedah* in Jewish tradition. *(Exegetical.)* (c) An invented study-Bible note says "the real meaning of this story is simply the ancient rejection of human sacrifice." Diagnose the note using 1.3–1.4: what it gets right about the literal sense, and what claim about meaning it smuggles in. *(Exegetical.)* (d) In 150 words, assess Ratzinger's worry that the historical-critical method, used alone, cannot reach what the text says as the word of God. State the strongest reply a historical critic inside the Church would give. Any verdict passes if both sides get their best move. *(Evaluative.)*

| 2.3 | The primeval history: fall, flood, Babel | Read Genesis 3–11 as a unit with its own theology of sin spreading, against its Near Eastern parallels | the serpent and the garden (the doctrine of original sin ceded to `creation-grace-last-things`), Cain and Abel, the flood and *Atrahasis*/*Gilgamesh*, two flood strands interwoven, the Noahide covenant, Babel, genealogies as structure |
| 2.4 | The patriarchs | Follow the promise from Abraham to Joseph, and report the historicity debate honestly | the call and promise (Gen 12), the covenant rituals (Gen 15, 17), faith reckoned as righteousness (Gen 15:6), Jacob becomes Israel, the Joseph novella, Albright's "patriarchal age" vs Thompson and Van Seters (1974–75), what inspiration does not require of a historicity verdict |

| 2.6 | Covenant and law | Read the law codes as covenant law, compare them, and use the tradition's tools for asking what binds after Christ | the Covenant Code (Ex 21–23), the Holiness Code and sacrifice and purity in Leviticus, Deuteronomy as a renewed covenant, law development between the codes (the Hebrew slave law, Ex 21 vs Deut 15, whose legal setting is cited from [`history-of-debt`](../history-of-debt/syllabus.md) 1.3 and whose theology goes to `theology-of-debt`), Hammurabi's laws as a parallel, moral / ceremonial / judicial precepts (ST I-II q.99) |

**Boss problem 2:** (a) Close-read Genesis 1:24–27 and 2:18–22 (Douay–Rheims). List every difference between the accounts in the order of creation, the divine name, and how humanity is made, and say which words decide each. *(Exegetical.)* (b) Close-read Exodus 21:2–7 and Deuteronomy 15:12–17. List what the later law adds or changes about release, women and provision for the freed slave. Say what those differences are evidence *for* in the composition debate and what they do not settle. *(Exegetical.)* (c) State what the Biblical Commission's 1906 response on Mosaic authorship asserted and what its 1948 letter to Cardinal Suhard said about the Pentateuch's sources. Say what weight each carries today, without overstating either (1.3; [`fundamental-theology`](../fundamental-theology/syllabus.md) 4.5). *(Exegetical.)* (d) In 300–400 words, argue for the model of composition (documentary, supplementary, fragmentary, or a final-form literary unity with sources left open) that best explains (a) and (b). State the strongest datum against your model, and say explicitly which Catholic commitments your verdict leaves untouched. *(Evaluative.)*

**Boss problem 3:** (a) Close-read 2 Samuel 7:12–16 (Douay–Rheims). List the differences between the Chronicler's version and Samuel's, and say what the psalmist accuses God of after the dynasty's fall. Explain how the three texts, read canonically (1.4), produce a hope the plain historical record did not fulfil. *(Exegetical.)* (c) An invented documentary voice-over says "archaeology has proven King David was a myth." Diagnose it: state the minimalist and maximalist positions at their strongest, and name what the Tel Dan inscription does and does not establish. *(Exegetical.)* (d) In 150 words: does the historical reliability of Samuel–Kings matter for a Catholic reading of the Davidic covenant? Any verdict passes if it uses the distinction between the literal sense and historical reportage (1.3, 2.4) and states the strongest reason to answer the other way. *(Evaluative.)*

State the individual and collective readings of the figure "like the son of man" and the verse each leans on. *(Exegetical.)* (d) In 400–500 words: is the Maccabean dating of Daniel's visions compatible with the Catholic doctrine of inspiration, and does the answer change how Daniel 7 can be read in light of the New Testament (6.4)? This is freely disputed. Argue either side. Separate the historical question from the doctrinal one, state what inspiration does and does not require of a text's date and authorship, and meet the strongest point against you. *(Evaluative.)*

## Notes

- **Prerequisite hand-off.** [`fundamental-theology`](../fundamental-theology/syllabus.md) Module 3 is assumed throughout: inspiration (3.1), inerrancy and Dei Verbum 11–12 (3.2), the canon as defined at Trent (3.3), and Tradition (3.4). This course never re-argues those doctrines. It asks what they require of a particular reading, and every lesson that touches them links the owner. The ladder of authority (4.4–4.5) governs every statement about a document's weight.
- **Authority discipline for the Biblical Commission.** Its decrees of 1905–1915 (on Mosaic authorship, Isaiah, Genesis 1–3 and others) were issued with a binding force the lessons state accurately. Afterwards came the 1948 letter to Cardinal Suhard, *Divino Afflante Spiritu*, and statements in the 1950s by the Commission's officials about freedom with respect to decrees that do not touch faith and morals. After its 1971 reorganization the Commission is a consultative body, and its documents (1993, 2001) are not acts of the Magisterium. Lessons present that history and do not assign any composition or dating verdict a rung on the ladder beyond what the documents support. Overstating either the old decrees' current force or their irrelevance is an exegetical error.
- **Dating discipline.** Dates are marked as secure (the falls of Samaria and Jerusalem, Cyrus's decree, the Maccabean revolt, both within a year or two), reconstructed, or disputed (the patriarchs, the Exodus, the united monarchy's extent, the composition of the Pentateuch, Daniel's visions, Job). Lessons report scholarly majorities as majorities and minorities as live where they are. No lesson presents a composition theory as settled. Dates use BC.
- **Scripture conventions.** Quotations follow the Douay–Rheims (Challoner). The Douay follows Vulgate book names and Psalm numbering (for most psalms from Hebrew 10 to 147 the Douay number is one lower; 1–2 Kings are 3–4 Kings, and 1–2 Samuel are 1–2 Kings). Lessons give both references at first use, and the reference card carries the concordance table. A lesson may translate a single Hebrew or Greek word itself when the word is the point (*almah*, *go'el*, *herem*, *ehyeh asher ehyeh*).
- **Jewish readings.** Jewish interpretations (Rashi, the Targums, the midrash, modern Jewish scholarship) are stated as their own tradition states them and graded for fidelity, never set up to be refuted. The Biblical Commission's 2001 document is the course's model for this. Whether a Christian reading is *right* against them is argued in `apologetics-foundations`, not here.
- **The debt thread.** The release laws surface in 2.6, Amos's oracles against selling the poor in 4.1, and Nehemiah 5 in 3.4. Each is located and handed off: as law to [`history-of-debt`](../history-of-debt/syllabus.md) 1.2–1.3, as theology to `theology-of-debt`, which lists this course as a prerequisite.
- **Where this goes next.** Module 1's reading tools and 6.4 hand on to [`new-testament`](../new-testament/syllabus.md), whose 1.1 applies the same Church documents to the gospels and whose 6.6 continues the canon story of 6.1 for the New Testament. 2.1 and 2.3 are the texts `creation-grace-last-things` builds doctrine on. The Servant Songs, Isaiah 7 and Daniel 7 (4.2–4.3, 6.3) are the texts `apologetics-foundations` argues about. The canon history (6.1) is the record `apologetics-catholic-claims` argues from. Proverbs 8 (5.2) returns in `patristics` and `trinity-and-god` as an Arian proof text.
- **Lesson count.** The roadmap estimated ~24. The syllabus runs 27 (+12.5%, within the ±25% band). The Pentateuch needs six lessons because composition (2.2) and law (2.6) are separate questions from the narratives. The canon's history (6.1) needs its own lesson because this course owns it. The deuterocanon is split between late wisdom (5.4) and narrative books (6.2), so neither lesson crams seven books and the Greek additions. Merges made to hold the line: Amos and Hosea join the lesson on what a prophet is; Jeremiah and Ezekiel share a lesson; Proverbs, Ecclesiastes and the Song share a lesson; Ruth, Esther and Chronicles appear inside the history lessons rather than alone.
