# The Old Testament · Reference Card

> Open book. This card is meant to be open while you work — including during
> quizzes. Nothing here is something you're expected to recall cold.

Every claim in this course is one of two kinds, and every answer is graded strictly
on which kind it is. **Church teaching**, at its level: the canon of 46 books as
defined at Trent, inspiration and inerrancy (owned by `fundamental-theology`), and
what the Church's documents on exegesis actually ask. **Scholarly positions**, with
no magisterial standing either way: how the Pentateuch was composed, whether the
patriarchs, the exodus and the conquest can be reached as history, how large David's
kingdom was, Second Isaiah, the date of Daniel. Between the two sits the course's
hardest level question, **the Biblical Commission**: its 1905-1915 responses bound
Catholics when issued, their force was reinterpreted in 1948 and 1955, and since 1971
the Commission is consultative. This card is the lookup surface for all of it: the
Hebrew words that decide readings, the dates and how firm they are, the Douay's book
names and numbers, the positions as their holders would state them, and the weight of
every document the lessons use. Levels come from
[`fundamental-theology` 4.4](../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-04-the-ladder-of-doctrinal-authority.md),
which owns the ladder; where that course and this card differ, that course wins.

Scripture is quoted from the Douay-Rheims (Challoner), with modern chapter and verse
first and the Douay's in brackets where they differ
([the concordance](#book-names-and-numbering-concordance)). Translations of Hebrew
marked *(lesson's translation)* are the lessons' own. BC dates unless marked AD.

## Levels at a glance

The spine of the course. Each row is a claim, its standing, the act or evidence
behind it, and the lesson that grades it. Rung 1 is **defined dogma**, rung 2
**definitive**, rung 3 **authentic ordinary magisterium**, rung 4 **common
teaching**, rung 5 **freely disputed** (the ladder of
[`fundamental-theology` 4.4](../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-04-the-ladder-of-doctrinal-authority.md)).
"Hypothesis" means a scholarly reconstruction that sits on **no rung at all**: its
weight is the weight of its evidence.

### Church teaching vs hypothesis

**Two kinds of claim, kept apart, graded strictly both ways.** A claim either carries
a level on the ladder, because some act of the Church teaches it, or it is a
scholarly reconstruction whose only weight is its evidence. Every lesson ends its
argument with a **Mark the levels** paragraph that sorts its claims into the two kinds.

- **Promoting** a hypothesis to teaching: "the Church teaches Moses wrote the
  Pentateuch", "Catholics must hold Second Isaiah", "the Church teaches the Masoretic
  Text is the original", "the Church teaches Daniel wrote in the sixth century",
  "Frei's Persian authorization is Church teaching", "the 1909 decree requires six
  24-hour days".
- **Demoting** teaching to hypothesis: "scholars call Tobit deuterocanonical, so it is
  open for Catholics", "since 33:14-26 may be late it is not really Scripture",
  "not by Solomon, so not really Scripture", "the Greek additions are less Scripture".
- **Level-less claims are not low-level claims.** Dates, authorship, sources and
  historicity sit on no rung, so "rung 5" is wrong for them too. Rung 5 names questions
  Catholics dispute *under* the magisterium, such as how the royal psalms speak of
  Christ, or whether a *sensus plenior* exists.
- **Four habits.** Find the act and its genre (an encyclical, a conciliar
  constitution, a Commission response of 1905, a Commission study of 1993, a lecture, a
  translator's note). Separate a doctrine from the text it leans on (resurrection is
  defined; Job 19 is not its proof). Keep canonicity apart from authorship (Trent
  defines books, not pens: "the five books of Moses" is a title). Keep inspiration
  apart from commendation (Ezra 10 narrates a decision; the canon also holds Ruth).
- **A majority is not a result.** Give the scholarly majority where there is one, say
  so, and give the live minority its best evidence.

### The Biblical Commission and its weight

**Neither dogma nor a dead letter: name the response, its date, and which later step
changed its force.** Pius X's *Praestantia Scripturae* (1907) made the 1905-1915
responses binding in conscience as papally approved doctrinal decrees, not as
infallible definitions. Several had exits built in (sources and secretaries for
Moses; non-historical genre "where solid arguments prove it"). Their practical force
then changed in steps: *Divino Afflante Spiritu* (1943) made literary forms a duty; the
1948 letter to Cardinal Suhard called the 1905, 1906 and 1909 answers no hindrance to
further scientific study; the 1955 statements of Miller and Kleinhans freed questions
of authorship, date and literary criticism while keeping those touching faith and
morals. None was formally revoked. After *Sedula cura* (1971) the Commission is an
advisory body of scholars, and its documents (1993, 2001) are **not magisterial acts**,
however cited.

**Two errors, graded equally.** Overstating the old responses' present force ("Catholics
must hold Isaiah wrote all sixty-six chapters"; "the 1905 decree still requires Jonah to
be history"; "the 1934 decree still settles that all Israel was in Egypt"). Overstating
their irrelevance ("1906 was repealed"; "the early decrees are dead letters, so the
faith-bearing facts of Response III do not matter"). The faith points inside some
responses keep their weight from elsewhere: real prediction (*Dei Filius* ch. 3), the
reality of the original fault (Trent V), the history "in a true sense" of Genesis 1-11
(*Humani Generis* 38).

**Consultative documents quoted as teaching** is the post-1971 error: "the Church
teaches the Jewish reading is valid" (the 2001 document says "possible", and it is an
advisory body's judgment); "the Church endorses the canonical approach" (the 1993
document welcomes it and is not magisterial).

## Dates

### Dating discipline

**Every date gets a status.** *Secure*: fixed by independent records to a year or so
(722, 701, 597, 587/586, 539, the Maccabean dates). *Reconstructed*: computed from
regnal years, handwriting, radiocarbon or tradition, with a stated margin. *Disputed*:
serious scholars place it far apart (the patriarchs, the exodus, the united monarchy's
extent, the Pentateuch's sources, Daniel's visions, Job). Document dates are secure;
composition dates of biblical books are reconstructed at best. Majorities are
reported as majorities, live minorities as live, and no composition theory is
presented as settled.

## Book names and numbering concordance

**Convert before you argue.** The Douay-Rheims translates the Vulgate, so it uses the
Vulgate's book names, the Greek numbering of the Psalms, and here and there the Latin's
chapter and verse divisions. None of this is an error to "correct" in an answer: give
the modern reference first and the Douay's in brackets.

### Psalm numbering

**Douay = Hebrew minus one, for most psalms from 10 to 147.** The Hebrew and the
Septuagint divide the same 150 psalms at different points; the Vulgate Psalter is
Jerome's Greek-based revision, so the Douay numbers the Greek way. The Hebrew divides
where the Greek joins, and joins where the Greek divides:

| Hebrew (modern Bibles) | Douay / Vulgate / Septuagint | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| 1-8 | 1-8 | same |
| 9 and 10 | 9 (Heb 10 = Douay 9:22-39) | Greek joins two |
| 11-113 | 10-112 | Douay one lower |
| 114 and 115 | 113 (Heb 115 begins "Not to us, O Lord") | Greek joins two |
| 116 | 114 (vv. 1-9) and 115 (vv. 10-19) | Greek splits one |
| 117-146 | 116-145 | Douay one lower |
| 147 | 146 (vv. 1-11) and 147 (vv. 12-20) | Greek splits one |
| 148-150 | 148-150 | same |

**Verse numbers too.** Where a psalm has a title, the Douay counts it as verse 1 (or
1-2) and most modern English Bibles do not. The Douay's own note at 9:21 records the
Hebrew split and the 146-147 join.

## Reading Scripture: senses and methods

### The senses of Scripture

**Words signify things, and in Scripture the things can signify again, because the
author of the book also runs the history it tells.** The **literal** sense is what the
words signify; the **spiritual** sense is what the things signified signify, in three
kinds: **allegorical** (Christ and the Church), **moral** or tropological (how to live),
**anagogical** (the last things). John Cassian's miniature (*Conferences* XIV.8): one
Jerusalem is historically the city of the Jews, allegorically the Church, anagogically
the heavenly city, tropologically the soul. The medieval couplet, which the Catechism
cites (CCC 118) and the Biblical Commission's 1993 document credits to Augustine of Dacia
(thirteenth century; hedged):

> *Littera gesta docet, quid credas allegoria, / moralis quid agas, quo tendas anagogia.*

(Lesson's translation: the letter teaches what happened; allegory, what to believe; the
moral sense, what to do; anagogy, where you are heading. The 1993 document's version ends
*quid speras anagogia*.)

**Aquinas's argument** (ST I q.1 a.10, English Dominican Province): God can signify "not
by words only (as man also can do), but also by things themselves"; the spiritual sense
"is based on the literal, and presupposes it". Three consequences: no equivocation (one
word, one meaning; the *referent* means further); argument is drawn from the literal
sense alone (reply 1, after Augustine); and nothing is lost, since nothing necessary to
faith is contained only under a spiritual sense.

**The Church's control** (*Divino Afflante Spiritu* 26-27, paraphrased): expound the
spiritual sense only where God clearly intended it, with its signs in Christ's teaching,
the apostles, tradition and the liturgy. Figurative applications in preaching are
"extrinsic" and "accidental": the **accommodated sense**, not a sense of Scripture.

**Status:** that Scripture has a spiritual sense intended by God and founded on the
literal is authentic ordinary magisterium (DAS 26-27; CCC 115-117); the fourfold scheme is
an ancient tradition; Aquinas is theology; a Father's allegory carries that Father's
weight; typologies the New Testament draws belong to the literal sense of the New
Testament text.

from the lesson); Origen's *Homilies on Joshua* reading the wars as the soul's war on
vices (Eph 6:12), a spiritual sense that does not answer the historical question
([3.1](lessons/03-01-joshua-judges-and-the-land.md)); the Song's spiritual sense resting on its literal sense of human love, and the

reading Job historically, allegorically and morally, filing the resurrection reading of

### Literal sense

**What the human author meant, in the kind of writing he chose: not "literalist".** When
Scripture speaks of God's "arm", the literal sense is his power, not a limb (ST I q.1
a.10 ad 3). The 1993 document adds that the literal sense is what the human author
expressed, read by his genre's conventions, and that a story's literal sense need not be
a report of fact. Genre as a duty comes from *Divino Afflante Spiritu* (1943) and Dei
Verbum 12. The literal sense of a parable is its point; historicity is a separate
question of genre.

**Why it comes first.** It is the foundation of every spiritual sense (no literal sense,
no *thing* to signify further), the only base for argument, and the first step of
canonical reading. The typology of Matthew 2:15 *needs* Hosea 11:1's literal sense
(Israel's Exodus), or there is no pattern to repeat.

**Instances:** Kings as a theological verdict and Chronicles as a retelling, each read for
its own intent before comparing ([3.3](lessons/03-03-two-kingdoms-and-two-falls.md)); Ezekiel 10-11 as a priest's theodicy of exile
before any Johannine reading ([4.4](lessons/04-04-jeremiah-and-ezekiel.md)); Micah 5:2 as a Davidic king from David's clan
before the messianic readings ([4.5](lessons/04-05-the-book-of-the-twelve.md)); Hosea 11:1's "my son" = Israel, every verb past
([6.4](lessons/06-04-the-old-testament-in-light-of-the-new.md)).

### Typology

**A real person, event or institution prefigures another real one, and its literal
sense stays in place.** Jonah in the fish and Christ in the tomb; the bronze serpent;
the Red Sea as baptism (1 Cor 10:2). **Allegory** in the narrow sense reads a
narrative's details as code for truths of another order. Typology keeps the history;
allegory can spend it. Paul's "which things are said by an allegory" (Gal 4:24,
*allēgoroumena*) is his word, not the modern category: two real women prefigure two
covenants (typology), but Hagar is also "Mount Sina" (code). You can argue doctrine
from Galatians 4 (Paul's reading is the literal sense of Galatians), not from Genesis 16
and 21 alone.

### Sensus plenior

**A "fuller sense": a deeper meaning intended by God but not clearly by the human
author, seen when the text is read in light of later revelation.** The term goes back
to Andrés Fernández in the 1920s; Raymond Brown's dissertation (St Mary's, Baltimore,
1955) made it standard. It let an exegete say both that Isaiah wrote of a figure in his
own time and that God meant Christ. **Objections:** how can an intention the author did
not have be known, and is it just typology renamed (typology locates the pointing in
*realities*, a fuller sense in the *words*)? The debate is generally said to have faded
after the Council. The 1993 document (II.B.3) kept the term as a kind of spiritual
sense, valid only when a later biblical text or an authentic doctrinal tradition controls
it.

**Where it is used:** Isa 7:14 as a near sign to Ahaz's house (the 2001 document, 62:
the dynasty will endure) with a fuller or typological fulfilment in Christ, the
Christian perception of fulfilment being "retrospective" (21) ([4.2](lessons/04-02-isaiah-and-immanuel.md)); the Servant
Songs ([4.3](lessons/04-03-the-servant-of-the-lord.md)); Proverbs 8 and Wisdom 7 read of the Son ([5.2](lessons/05-02-proverbs-ecclesiastes-and-the-song-of-songs.md), [5.4](lessons/05-04-sirach-and-the-wisdom-of-solomon.md)); one of the rival
Catholic accounts of how Matthew 2:15 relates to Hosea 11:1 ([6.4](lessons/06-04-the-old-testament-in-light-of-the-new.md)).

**Status:** freely disputed (rung 5) whether it exists and how it works. Neither the
Council nor any definition adopted it; the 1993 document is consultative.

### Historical-critical method

**Recovering the literal sense historically: what did this text mean when written, and
how did it come to be?** Its tools: textual criticism (which words), source criticism
(what earlier documents), form criticism (what kinds of speech, used where), redaction
criticism (what the editors did). The Church's documents run through three phases:

1. **Suspicion of the operators (1893-1915).** *Providentissimus Deus* 17 calls criticism
   the "first means" of defence, and refuses "higher criticism" on two charges: an
   evidence rule (judging origin "from internal indications alone") and a philosophy
   (rationalism, eliminating "all prophecy and miracle"). The evidence rule was quietly
   passed by; the second charge became the permanent limit.
2. **Adoption with rules (1943-1965).** *Divino Afflante Spiritu*; Dei Verbum 12.
3. **Naming the limits (1988-2010).** Ratzinger's Erasmus Lecture called for a
   "criticism of criticism"; the 1993 document calls the method indispensable and no
   method fully adequate; *Verbum Domini* 34-36 sets out [two levels](#two-levels-of-interpretation).

**Form A and Form B** (the test for any late-dating argument): Form A says a prophet
cannot know the future, so the text is late (a premise Leo excluded). Form B says the
text's own evidence (its audience, its knowledge running out) places it (no premise
about miracles). Second Isaiah ([1.3](lessons/01-03-the-historical-critical-method-and-its-limits.md)) and Daniel ([6.3](lessons/06-03-daniel-and-apocalyptic.md)) are the course's cases.

*Introduced:* [1.3](lessons/01-03-the-historical-critical-method-and-its-limits.md); used at [4.2](lessons/04-02-isaiah-and-immanuel.md), [6.3](lessons/06-03-daniel-and-apocalyptic.md)

### Literary forms

**Genre is a historical fact to be discovered, and discovering it is a duty.**
*Divino Afflante Spiritu* (1943, 35-38, paraphrased): grammar alone does not settle an
ancient Eastern writer's literal sense; the interpreter must go back in spirit to that
world and learn its forms of speech, found only by studying ancient literature. Dei Verbum
12: truth is expressed differently in texts that are "variously historical", prophetic,
poetic, or of other kinds; attend to the ways of speaking and narrating of the writer's
time. **Genre fixes what a book asserts, not whether it is inspired.**

**Instances:** Joshua's totalizing victory reports (Younger's *Ancient Conquest
Accounts*, 1990) ([3.1](lessons/03-01-joshua-judges-and-the-land.md)); Chronicles' purpose ([3.3](lessons/03-03-two-kingdoms-and-two-falls.md)); Judith's opening anachronisms,
"Nabuchodonosor king of the Assyrians, who reigned in Ninive" (Greek 1:1 [DR 1:5]), read as
signals of a novella ([6.2](lessons/06-02-tobit-judith-baruch-and-the-maccabees.md)); Jonah's genre signals ([4.5](lessons/04-05-the-book-of-the-twelve.md)); Daniel's court tales vs
visions ([6.3](lessons/06-03-daniel-and-apocalyptic.md)); what Matthew's fulfilment claim is ([6.4](lessons/06-04-the-old-testament-in-light-of-the-new.md)).

**Watch:** a genre appeal made *only* where a spade bites is ad hoc; argue the genre from
the text ([3.1](lessons/03-01-joshua-judges-and-the-land.md)).

*Introduced:* [1.3](lessons/01-03-the-historical-critical-method-and-its-limits.md); used at [6.2](lessons/06-02-tobit-judith-baruch-and-the-maccabees.md), [6.4](lessons/06-04-the-old-testament-in-light-of-the-new.md); also [3.1](lessons/03-01-joshua-judges-and-the-land.md), [3.3](lessons/03-03-two-kingdoms-and-two-falls.md)

### Two levels of interpretation

**Historical-critical and theological: distinguished, never separated.** *Verbum Domini*
34-36 (Benedict XVI, 2010, paraphrased): stop at the first level and Scripture speaks only
of the past; leave out a hermeneutic of faith and a secularized one takes its place,
assuming God does not act in history; no criterion may rule out God's self-disclosure in
advance. This is Leo XIII's second charge restated with his evidence rule dropped.
**Status:** post-synodal apostolic exhortation, authentic ordinary magisterium (rung 3).
The Erasmus Lecture (1988) that anticipated it has no magisterial weight.

*Introduced:* [1.3](lessons/01-03-the-historical-critical-method-and-its-limits.md)

### Canonical reading

**Literal sense first, then the text within the whole canon: context added, tensions
kept.** Like a letter in a published correspondence: the collection does not change what
the letter meant when sent; it changes what it does.

| | Harmonizing | Canonical reading | Flattening |
|---|---|---|---|
| Asks | How can both be true? | What does the whole say by holding both? | What does the later text say this means? |
| The tension | removed | kept, and read | erased by the later text |
| Each text's literal sense | often bent | established first, then kept | replaced |
| Right when | the texts really address different cases | always, after the literal sense | never |

**Two programmes.** Brevard Childs (*Biblical Theology in Crisis*, 1970; *Introduction to
the Old Testament as Scripture*, 1979): the object is each book's **final form**; the
editors' shaping belongs to the meaning; he rejected the label "canonical criticism".
James A. Sanders (*Torah and Canon*, 1972) coined "canonical criticism" and studied the
**process** of reuse. The 1993 document (I.C.1) welcomes the approach as a complement,
warns that canons differ, and warns against emptying the Old Testament of its own meaning.
Augustine's formula, taken up in Dei Verbum 16, is a concessive clause inside a contrast
(fear marks the Old, love the New): *quamquam et in Vetere Novum lateat, et in Novo Vetus
pateat*, "although the New lies hidden in the Old, and the Old lies open in the New"
(*Questions on the Heptateuch* 2.73, on Ex 20:19; lesson's translation).

**Instances:** Proverbs 26:4-5 and Ecclesiastes' epilogue ([1.4](lessons/01-04-canonical-reading-and-the-unity-of-the-testaments.md)); 2 Chr 36:22-23 = Ezra
1:1-3, so the Hebrew order ends on "let him go up" and the Christian on Malachi, and Ruth
beside Ezra 9-10 ([3.4](lessons/03-04-exile-and-return.md)); Jonah's spared Nineveh beside Nahum's doomed one ([4.5](lessons/04-05-the-book-of-the-twelve.md));
Sirach 24's Wisdom tenting in Jacob beside John 1:14, where John gives Wisdom a different
address without cancelling Sirach's ([5.4](lessons/05-04-sirach-and-the-wisdom-of-solomon.md)).

**Status:** Dei Verbum 12's criteria are conciliar teaching; Childs and Sanders are
scholarly methods; every particular canonical reading is freely disputed.

*Introduced:* [1.4](lessons/01-04-canonical-reading-and-the-unity-of-the-testaments.md); used at [3.4](lessons/03-04-exile-and-return.md), [4.5](lessons/04-05-the-book-of-the-twelve.md), [5.4](lessons/05-04-sirach-and-the-wisdom-of-solomon.md)

### Three criteria of Dei Verbum 12

**After the historical criteria, read Scripture "in the Spirit in which it was written".**
Dei Verbum 12 asks for no less care for the **content and unity of the whole of
Scripture**, taking account of the **living Tradition of the whole Church** and the
**analogy of faith**. In the Latin the first is the main clause; the other two are what
it takes into account (*ratione habita*). The Catechism's gloss (CCC 112-114, paraphrased):
Scripture is one because God's plan is one, centred on Christ; Tradition carries the
Spirit's interpretation; the analogy of faith is the coherence of the truths of faith
with one another. (The official English renders *analogia fidei* in DV 12 as the harmony
between elements of the faith.) **Status:** conciliar teaching in a dogmatic
constitution, at least rung 3. It follows DV 12's historical criteria; it does not replace
them. The Council closed in 1965, before Childs's programme: "Dei Verbum endorses Childs"
is a level error.

*Introduced:* [1.4](lessons/01-04-canonical-reading-and-the-unity-of-the-testaments.md); listed in [`fundamental-theology` 3.2](../fundamental-theology/lessons/03-02-inerrancy.md)

## The collection and the text

Two questions, always separate: **which books** (24, 39 or 46, in an order that is
itself a reading), and **which words** (MT, LXX, Qumran, Vulgate). The Church has defined
the first and left the second to scholars.

### Tanakh and the Christian order

**Same Hebrew books, different counts and a different ending.** The **Tanakh** has three
parts. **Torah**: Genesis-Deuteronomy. **Nevi'im**: the Former Prophets (Joshua, Judges,
Samuel, Kings) and the Latter (Isaiah, Jeremiah, Ezekiel, the Twelve as one scroll).
**Ketuvim**: Psalms, Proverbs, Job, Song, Ruth, Lamentations, Ecclesiastes, Esther,
Daniel, Ezra-Nehemiah, Chronicles. So 5 + 8 + 11 = 24. In the Talmud's order (*Bava
Batra* 14b) and most printed Jewish Bibles, Chronicles comes last, ending on Cyrus's "let
him go up" (2 Chr 36:23); the great medieval codices (Aleppo, Leningrad) put Chronicles
**first** among the Writings, so the ending is a convention.

The **Christian order** follows the Greek codices by genre: **Law, History,
Wisdom/Poetry, Prophets**. Ruth moves beside Judges, Lamentations beside Jeremiah,
Daniel among the prophets. Protestant Bibles end with Malachi's Elijah (Mal 4:5 [Heb
3:23]); the Vulgate and the Douay put 1-2 Maccabees after the prophets.

**The counts.** Jewish 24 and Protestant 39 are the **same text**: split Samuel, Kings,
Chronicles and Ezra-Nehemiah (24 + 4) and count the Twelve separately (+ 11) = 39. The
Catholic 46 adds seven books plus the Greek parts of Esther and Daniel
([deuterocanonical books](#deuterocanonical-books)); Orthodox Bibles carry a few more (1
Esdras, 3 Maccabees among them). "Old Testament" is a Christian name (*testamentum* =
*diathēkē* = *berit*), assuming Jeremiah 31:31's new covenant; Melito (c. 170) is the
first Christian known to use it of a list of books; "Hebrew Bible" is the neutral scholarly
term.

**The Prologue to Sirach** (c. 132) names "the law, and the prophets" and never names
the third group ("others that have followed them", "other books", "the rest of the
books"): many scholars infer the Law and Prophets were closed and the Writings not yet
fixed. An inference, weighed in [6.1](lessons/06-01-how-the-old-testament-canon-formed.md).

*Introduced:* [1.1](lessons/01-01-what-the-old-testament-is.md); the ending read canonically at [3.4](lessons/03-04-exile-and-return.md)

### The Masoretic Text

**The standard vocalized Hebrew text, and the base of modern critical editions.** The
Masoretes, chiefly at Tiberias, c. AD 600-1000, added vowel points, accents and marginal
notes (*masorah*) to a consonantal text already standard. The **Aleppo Codex** (c. 930)
lost roughly two-fifths of its leaves after 1947, most of the Torah among them. The
**Leningrad Codex** (1008, dated by its own colophon) is the oldest complete Hebrew Bible.
Jerome used Hebrew manuscripts of the kind the Masoretes later standardized, not the MT
itself, which postdates him. **Ketiv and qere** (written vs read) are the Masoretes'
record of places where the reading tradition pronounced the consonants differently
(Isa 49:5, Job 13:15: *lo'* "not" vs *lo* "to him"). **Status:** no manuscript tradition
is defined as original; that is textual criticism.

*Introduced:* [1.1](lessons/01-01-what-the-old-testament-is.md)

### The Septuagint

**The Greek Old Testament, begun at Alexandria: the Pentateuch in the third century BC,
the rest over the following two centuries or so.** Most New Testament quotations follow
it; where it differs from the MT it is sometimes translating a different Hebrew text
(short Jeremiah). Dei Verbum 22 says the Church adopted it as her own. Sirach's grandson
already notes that the Law and the Prophets "have no small difference" in Greek.

**The legend.** The *Letter of Aristeas* (itself second century BC, reconstructed; read
as apologetic) claims a courtier of Ptolemy II (285-246): seventy-two elders, six from
each tribe, translated **the Law** only, and the Alexandrian community approved it.
Josephus (*Antiquities* XII.2) adds seventy-two days. Philo (*Life of Moses* II) makes the
translators inspired, all writing the same words. Irenaeus (*Against Heresies* III.21.2)
has the king separate them, and extends the miracle to all the books. Augustine held the
same Spirit was in the translators as in the prophets (*City of God* XVIII.43), a
theological proposal about two editions, not a defined answer ([4.4](lessons/04-04-jeremiah-and-ezekiel.md)).

**Not a canon.** "The Septuagint was the Alexandrian Jewish canon" is a hypothesis with
few defenders now: it is a translation tradition preserved in Christian codices, not a
Jewish list.

*Introduced:* [1.1](lessons/01-01-what-the-old-testament-is.md); the legend at [6.1](lessons/06-01-how-the-old-testament-canon-formed.md)

### The Dead Sea Scrolls

**Some two hundred biblical manuscripts from Qumran, found from 1947: every book of the
Hebrew Bible but Esther** (if Ezra-Nehemiah counts as one). The Great Isaiah Scroll
(1QIsa^a, second century BC) copies all 66 chapters as one book, about a thousand years
before Aleppo, and agrees with the MT in substance. **Textual plurality, not tampering:**
4QJer^b and 4QJer^d follow the shorter, Greek-type Jeremiah while 4QJer^a and ^c match the
MT; the Cave 11 Psalms Scroll (11QPs^a) orders the later psalms differently and adds others
(Psalm 151; Sir 51). Also found: Tobit in Aramaic and Hebrew (4Q196-200), Sirach fragments
(2Q18), a Greek fragment usually identified as the Letter of Jeremiah (7Q2), and books no
canon kept (*1 Enoch*, *Jubilees*). "Canon" is anachronistic here: the scrolls show what a
community copied and valued, not a closed list.

*Introduced:* [1.1](lessons/01-01-what-the-old-testament-is.md); used at [4.4](lessons/04-04-jeremiah-and-ezekiel.md), [5.4](lessons/05-04-sirach-and-the-wisdom-of-solomon.md), [6.1](lessons/06-01-how-the-old-testament-canon-formed.md), [6.2](lessons/06-02-tobit-judith-baruch-and-the-maccabees.md)

## The Pentateuch

**Composition models, described one by one.** The data every model must explain:
[doublets](#doublets), the divine names and Ex 6:3, post-Mosaic notices (Gen 12:6, 36:31,
Deut 34), and the codes' disagreements. The models ([2.2](lessons/02-02-who-wrote-the-pentateuch.md)'s map):

- **Mosaic, substantial.** Moses's work, with sources, secretaries and later inspired
  additions (the 1906 response's allowances); Ex 6:3 read as a claim about the name's
  meaning (Rashi).
- **Documentary** ([entry](#documentary-hypothesis)). Four continuous sources, combined by a redactor.
- **Supplementary / blocks** ([entry](#supplementary-hypothesis)). Blocks grown separately and joined by later compositional layers; today's European Priestly/non-Priestly model.
- **Neo-documentary** ([entry](#neo-documentary-hypothesis)). Four continuous sources revived, identified by plot.
- **Final form.** Read as designed, sources left open (Cassuto).

Which best explains the data is Boss problem 2(d): the card does not rank them.
**Status:** every model is a hypothesis, freely disputed; so is how much goes back to
Moses. **There is no consensus.** "Scholars agree it is composite" is close to true;
"scholars agree how" is false.

**The early history.** Ibn Ezra hinted at post-Mosaic verses; Spinoza (*Theological-Political
Treatise*, 1670, ch. 8) denied Mosaic authorship; the Oratorian Richard Simon (*Critical
History of the Old Testament*, 1678) proposed later public scribes compiling Moses's
material (Bossuet had it suppressed); Jean Astruc (*Conjectures*, 1753) sorted Genesis by
divine name as memoirs Moses combined; de Wette (1805) tied Deuteronomy to Josiah's book.
The tradition already allowed some post-Mosaic writing: the baraita in *Bava Batra*
14b-15a (Rabbi Yehuda: Joshua wrote the last eight verses; Rabbi Shimon: Moses wrote them
at God's dictation, with tears), Josephus (*Antiquities* IV.8.48: Moses recorded his own
death in advance), and Challoner's note giving Deut 34 to "Josue, or ... some of the
prophets".

### The two creation accounts

**Genesis gives creation twice: a seven-day liturgy and a potter's garden.** Genesis
1:1-2:4a is built on a pattern: days 1-3 **separate** regions (light and darkness, waters
above and below, sea and land), days 4-6 **fill** them (lights, fish and birds, animals and
humans), paired 1-4, 2-5, 3-6; "God saw that it was good" seven times; the seventh day
blessed. Its starting state is too much water (*tohu wa-bohu*, *tehom*), and God acts by
speaking (*bara*). Genesis 2:4b-25 is a story in one garden: its starting state is too
little water (no rain, no farmer), and the LORD God acts by forming (*yatsar*), breathing,
planting and building. The divine name changes at 2:4. The side-by-side comparison of
1:24-27 and 2:18-22 is Boss problem 2(a); [2.1](lessons/02-01-two-accounts-of-creation.md)'s synopsis is the place to work it.

**Genre, not cosmology.** The *raqia* (LXX *stereōma*, "a solid thing") holds up "the
waters that were above the firmament" (1:7): the account teaches that God made and
ordered the sky, not that the sky is a dome (*Providentissimus Deus*: the writers described
things as they appear). Challoner's note on the firmament is a harmonization with later
science, not the author's meaning.

**Readings.** Augustine (*City of God* XXII.17): the sleep of the man is the death of
Christ, and from his pierced side the Church is "built up" (a typology hung on the verb
"built", 2:22). Rashi on 1:1: *Elohim* is God's justice and the divine name his mercy,
joined at 2:4 because the world could not endure strict justice alone. Cassuto (*The
Documentary Hypothesis*, Hebrew 1941): the names differ by meaning, not by source.

**Status:** creation by God is doctrine (owned by `creation-grace-last-things`); two sources
behind the accounts (classically P for Genesis 1, J for Genesis 2) is a hypothesis; the 1909
responses are weighed in [the table](#the-biblical-commissions-documents-and-their-weight).
A Catholic may hold two sources, reject them, or read the final form as one deliberate
diptych.

*Introduced:* [2.1](lessons/02-01-two-accounts-of-creation.md); the source question at [2.2](lessons/02-02-who-wrote-the-pentateuch.md)

### Image and likeness

**Humanity as God's representative, male and female.** Gen 1:26-27 pairs *tselem* (image:
a carved or cast figure, the word used elsewhere for idols) and *demut* (likeness,
resemblance). Many scholars read it against royal custom: a king's statue marked the land
he ruled, and Genesis gives that office to every human, so dominion follows at once.
Irenaeus split the words: the image by our making, the likeness only "through the Spirit"
(*Against Heresies* V.6.1). Most modern exegetes treat them as near-synonyms. The literal
sense is representation and rule; the distinction is a later theological reading.

**"Let us make man" (1:26).** Literal options: plural of majesty; self-deliberation; the
heavenly court (Rashi: God consulted his court, to teach humility, and the singular
"created" of 1:27 answers anyone who reads many creators; parallels 1 Kgs 22:19 [DR 3
Kgs], Isa 6:8). The Fathers' and Challoner's Trinitarian reading is legitimate as a
canonical, spiritual reading and anachronistic as the literal sense
([`trinity-and-god` 2.1](../trinity-and-god/lessons/02-01-the-one-god-of-israel.md)).
Grammar cannot decide: the honest answer is a ranked list.

*Introduced:* [2.1](lessons/02-01-two-accounts-of-creation.md)

### Enuma Elish

**The Babylonian creation epic: shared picture, denied theology.** Marduk kills Tiamat,
the salt-water deep, splits her, and raises half as the sky with a guard posted "not to
let her water escape" (Budge's 1921 translation); humans are made from the slain Kingu's
blood to relieve the gods of labour. Composition date **disputed** (often late second
millennium); our copies are seventh-century.

**Shared with Genesis 1:** a watery beginning; a sky set up to hold back the upper waters.
**Pointed differences:** no battle; *tehom* is water, not a goddess; the sea monsters
(*tanninim*) are simply "created" (1:21); sun and moon go unnamed, mere "lights" (1:14-16);
humanity blessed and given rule, not made as slaves. **The *tehom* ~ Tiamat link is
contested:** Gunkel (*Schöpfung und Chaos*, 1895) derived *tehom* from Tiamat; many
Semitists now treat them as cognates from a common root. Shared cosmology is not literary
dependence; the comparison shows best where Genesis differs.

*Introduced:* [2.1](lessons/02-01-two-accounts-of-creation.md)

### Documentary hypothesis

**Four continuous sources, J, E, D and P, combined by a redactor, in that order.**
Julius Wellhausen gave it its classic form (*Geschichte Israels* I, 1878; retitled
*Prolegomena to the History of Israel*, 1883). **J** (Yahwist, Judah), **E** (Elohist,
north), **D** (Deuteronomy), **P** (Priestly). His decisive argument concerned worship:
the early historical books show many altars, Deuteronomy demands one chosen place, and P
simply assumes a single sanctuary, so P comes last and the finished law comes after the
prophets. **Classical dates** (all reconstructed, every one disputed): J tenth-ninth
century, E ninth-eighth, D seventh (Josiah, conventionally 622), P exilic or after.

**Textbook data:** the [two flood strands](#two-flood-strands) woven verse by verse; Ex 6:3
as P's independent claim contradicting J; Gen 35:9-15 (Bethel named and Israel renamed
again) as a P doublet.

**Where it stands:** in the 1970s the consensus broke; in Europe J and E have lost their
majority; in North America and Israel the four sources were revived in a new form. One
model among several, not the consensus. The divine-name criterion is the weakest
(the Douay's "Lord" hides which Hebrew word is meant).

*Introduced:* [2.2](lessons/02-02-who-wrote-the-pentateuch.md); used at [2.3](lessons/02-03-the-primeval-history.md); see also [2.1](lessons/02-01-two-accounts-of-creation.md), [2.5](lessons/02-05-exodus-and-sinai.md)

### Doublets

**The same story told twice or more with different details: data every composition model
must explain, not proof of sources by itself.** Two creation accounts; two flood strands
in one story; a patriarch passing his wife off as his sister three times (Gen 12, 20, 26);
Bethel named twice (28:19; 35:15); Jacob renamed Israel twice (32:28; 35:10); the
Decalogue twice (Ex 20, Deut 5). The method: identify the doublet, note the markers
(divine names, *El Shaddai*), state the rival explanations. Documentarians: separate
sources, each telling it once. Supplementary models: a later layer restating the earlier.
Final-form readers: deliberate repetition (the text's own "again", 35:9). All three explain
the data; they differ on whether the repetition was *made* or *found*.

*Introduced:* [2.2](lessons/02-02-who-wrote-the-pentateuch.md)

### Supplementary hypothesis

**No continuous J: blocks grew separately and later authors joined and extended them.**
John Van Seters (*Abraham in History and Tradition*, 1975) dated the Yahwist to the exile
and made him an author supplementing earlier material; H. H. Schmid (*Der sogenannte
Jahwist*, 1976) made a similar move; Rolf Rendtorff (1977) argued that the blocks
(primeval history, patriarchs, exodus, Sinai) grew separately and were joined late;
Erhard Blum (1984; 1990) described two post-exilic compositions, Deuteronomistic and
Priestly. Today's European **Priestly/non-Priestly model** descends from this: P is still
identified, the non-Priestly material is not divided into J and E, and many (Konrad
Schmid among them) argue that P first joined Genesis to the exodus story. **Status:** a
hypothesis. On this model Ex 6:3 is a problem: P wrote it knowing non-P texts.

*Introduced:* [2.2](lessons/02-02-who-wrote-the-pentateuch.md)

### Neo-documentary hypothesis

**Four continuous sources, revived and redefined by plot.** Baruch Schwartz, Joel Baden
(*The Composition of the Pentateuch*, 2012) and Jeffrey Stackert: contradictory accounts of
*what happened* mark separate documents; style and divine names are secondary; a compiler
preserved each source whole rather than rewriting. Ex 6:3 is the textbook case of a source
contradiction. **Status:** a hypothesis, strong in North America and Israel.

*Introduced:* [2.2](lessons/02-02-who-wrote-the-pentateuch.md)

### Protoevangelium

**Gen 3:15 read as the first announcement of a Redeemer** (CCC 410). In the Hebrew the
woman's offspring, *hu'* ("he" or "it", the seed), will strike the serpent's head; the
Septuagint has the masculine *autos*; the Vulgate *ipsa*, "she", which the Douay follows.
Genesis 3 never calls the serpent the devil: that identification comes later (Wis 2:24;
Rev 12:9). The literal sense tells how trust was lost; the doctrine of original sin is
built on it but not identical with it. **Status:** the Catechism's reading, at the weight of
its sources; the reality of the original fault is defined (Trent V); the Marian reading of
*ipsa* belongs to `ecclesiology-and-mariology`; Paul's Adam to
[`new-testament` 5.3](../new-testament/lessons/05-03-romans-5-11-adam-spirit-israel.md).

*Introduced:* [2.3](lessons/02-03-the-primeval-history.md)

### Near Eastern flood parallels

**Genesis retells a Mesopotamian story, and the retelling is its theology.** On 3 December
1872 George Smith read to the Society of Biblical Archaeology a flood story from a Nineveh
tablet: **Gilgamesh XI**, in which Utnapishtim, warned by Ea, builds a boat, rides out six
days and seven nights of storm, grounds on a mountain (Nimush; older readings Nisir),
sends out birds and sacrifices, and the gods crowd round like flies. **Atrahasis** (best
copy Old Babylonian, seventeenth century BC, reconstructed from a colophon) gives the
cause: humans made to labour for the gods, their noise keeping Enlil awake. **Genesis:**
one God floods the earth because it is "filled with iniquity" (6:11, *hamas*, violence),
grieves, and binds himself in a covenant. **What it shows:** a shared tradition reworked,
not copying from that tablet, and nothing either way about historicity or inspiration
(which works through human sources). There is no date for the flood; the geological record
shows no flood covering the whole earth.

*Introduced:* [2.3](lessons/02-03-the-primeval-history.md)

### Two flood strands

**Inside Genesis 6-9 the story says things twice, and differently.**

| | Strand A (non-Priestly, "J") | Strand B (Priestly, "P") |
|---|---|---|
| Animals | seven pairs of clean, one of unclean (7:2) | two of every kind (6:19-20; 7:15) |
| Timing | rain for forty days (7:4, 12) | waters prevail 150 days (7:24; 8:3); dated by Noah's age |
| Water | rain | the deep and the heavens burst open (7:11; 8:2) |
| Divine name | *YHWH* (7:1, 5) | *Elohim* (6:22; 9:1) |
| Ending | sacrifice of clean animals (8:20-22) | covenant and bow (9:1-17) |

**Readings:** the documentary reading takes two complete stories woven verse by verse;
supplementary models make one an expansion of the other; unity readers (Cassuto; Gordon
Wenham, "The Coherence of the Flood Narrative", *Vetus Testamentum*, 1978) read one
designed palistrophe rising to "God remembered Noe" (8:1). **Status:** two sources is the
majority view, Wenham a live minority, neither Church teaching. **Trap:** the Douay's "the
Lord" at 7:9 is *Elohim* in Hebrew.

*Introduced:* [2.3](lessons/02-03-the-primeval-history.md)

### The spread of sin pattern

**Sin, speech, mitigation, punishment, spreading from a couple to the whole race.** David
Clines (*The Theme of the Pentateuch*, 1978), after von Rad:

| Episode | Sin | Mitigation | Punishment |
|---|---|---|---|
| Eden (3) | the fruit | garments of skins (3:21) | expulsion |
| Cain (4) | fratricide | the mark (4:15) | exile (4:16) |
| Sons of God (6:1-4) | heaven and earth mixed | none clear | life limited to 120 years (6:3) |
| Flood (6-9) | violence | Noah, ark, covenant | the flood |
| Babel (11) | the tower and "a name" | **missing** | scattering |

The empty cell is the point: Babel's mitigation is Genesis 12, where God promises to
"magnify" Abram's name, giving as a gift what Babel tried to seize. The frame is the
*elleh toledot* formula (2:4, 5:1, 6:9, 10:1, 11:10, 11:27), narrowing from humanity to
Terah. Lamech's "seventy times sevenfold" (4:24, Hebrew seventy-seven) is often heard
reversed in Matt 18:22; Pentecost as Babel undone is typological. **Status:** a scholar's
structure that guides reading and settles no passage (6:1-4 fits only roughly).

*Introduced:* [2.3](lessons/02-03-the-primeval-history.md)

### Sons of God in Genesis 6

**Gen 6:1-4, three readings of *bene ha-elohim*.**

- **Angels.** Elsewhere the phrase means heavenly beings (Job 1:6); 1 Enoch 6-11's Watchers; Justin, *Second Apology* 5; most modern critics.
- **Sethites.** Augustine (*City of God* XV.23) doubts holy angels could fall so, and reads Seth's godly line marrying Cain's daughters; the Douay's note follows him.
- **Rulers.** Targum Onqelos, "sons of the great ones"; Rashi, sons of princes and judges (*elohim* as authority), recording the angels as a second opinion.

Whether 6:3's 120 years are a lifespan or a grace period is open (the Douay's note gives
both). On 1 Enoch in Jude, see [`new-testament` 6.4](../new-testament/lessons/06-04-peter-jude-and-the-letters-of-john.md).
**Status:** freely disputed.

*Introduced:* [2.3](lessons/02-03-the-primeval-history.md)

### The patriarchal promise

**Land, seed and blessing, promised at 12:1-3 and threatened by everything after.** One
command, "Go forth", carries the promises: a land to be shown, a great nation, a great
name, the nations blessed "in thee". Barren wives (Sarah, Rebekah, Rachel), famine, the
heir nearly lost twice, the favourite sold into Egypt: Genesis ends with seventy souls in
Egypt and the land unpossessed.

- **The covenant between the pieces** (Gen 15:7-21): animals cut in two; at nightfall "a smoking furnace, and a lamp of fire" pass between the halves (15:17). Jer 34:18 shows the logic: passing between the parts invokes their fate on oneself. Here **only God passes**: God takes the self-curse; Abram sleeps.
- **Circumcision** (Gen 17): Abram becomes Abraham, "father of many nations"; a "perpetual" covenant with a sign the household must keep. Most source critics assign 17 to P and 15 to non-P layers (a majority reading, not settled); that the two differ (unilateral promise vs obligation with a sign) is in the text.
- **Faith reckoned** (Gen 15:6): *he'emin* is trust in a person's word; *yachshevehah* has no named subject. Rashi: God counted Abram's trust to him as merit. Nachmanides: Abram counted the promise as God's righteousness. Paul's reading is [`new-testament` 5.1](../new-testament/lessons/05-01-galatians-faith-law-and-the-gentiles.md) and [5.2](../new-testament/lessons/05-02-romans-1-4-the-righteousness-of-god.md).
- **Jacob becomes Israel** (32:22-32): an etiology of "Israel" (*sarita*, "you strove"), Phanuel and the thigh-sinew custom; the "man" refuses his name; Hosea 12:3-4 calls him an angel.
- **Joseph** (37-50): a novella of hidden providence, "God turned it into good" (50:20); von Rad (1953) read it as wisdom-shaped, influential and contested.

**Status:** that God called Abraham and bound himself by promise is the content of
Scripture as the Church reads it (CCC 59, 145-146).

*Introduced:* [2.4](lessons/02-04-the-patriarchs.md)

### Historicity of the patriarchs

**Can Abraham, Isaac and Jacob be reached as history? Most historians say no; none can
say they did not exist.**

| Position | Representatives | Date of the patriarchs | Chief evidence | Main difficulty |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Patriarchal age | Albright, Bright, Speiser (1940s-60s) | early 2nd millennium (reconstructed) | Nuzi customs (a servant adopted as heir, a maid given to the husband), names, migrations | parallels not distinctive; anachronisms |
| Late composition, no recoverable history (now the majority) | T. L. Thompson (*The Historicity of the Patriarchal Narratives*, 1974), Van Seters (1975) | stories written in the 1st millennium | first-millennium features; the Nuzi critique | arguing from silence; old traditions may survive in late texts |
| Plausible core | K. A. Kitchen (*On the Reliability of the Old Testament*, 2003); Roland de Vaux, O.P., more cautiously | 2nd millennium (disputed) | slave prices (Joseph's "twenty pieces of silver", 37:28), treaty forms, name types | anachronisms must be explained as updating |

**The anachronisms:** "Ur of the Chaldees" (Chaldeans attested only in the first
millennium); Philistines (settled c. twelfth century); domesticated camels (Gen 12:16,
24:10; the Aravah copper-mine work of Sapir-Hen and Ben-Yosef, 2013, found them in the
southern Levant only around the late tenth century). A late detail dates the *telling*,
not necessarily the core; whether the details form a pattern is a judgment about
cumulative evidence ([inference to the best explanation](../philosophical-method/lessons/02-01-inference-to-the-best-explanation.md)).

**Status:** no magisterial act defines or dates the patriarchs; the 23 June 1905 response
set a burden of proof, since reweighed; historicity is freely disputed among Catholic
exegetes. "Unrecoverable" is the majority view; "refuted" overstates it.

*Introduced:* [2.4](lessons/02-04-the-patriarchs.md)

### Ehyeh asher ehyeh

**Ex 3:14, the name given at the bush.** *Ehyeh* is the first person imperfect of
*hayah*, "to be, become, happen", and the imperfect can point to the future. Three verses
earlier Moses asked "Who am I?" and God answered *ki-ehyeh immakh*, "I will be with thee"
(3:12): the same verb. The Douay: "I AM WHO AM ... HE WHO IS"; Challoner's note reads it as
"being itself, eternal, self-existent". Three readings:

- **Being.** LXX *egō eimi ho ōn*, Vulgate *ego sum qui sum*, Challoner, and Aquinas ("He Who Is" the most proper name of God, ST I q.13 a.11; [`thomistic-synthesis` 4.3](../thomistic-synthesis/lessons/04-03-the-names-of-god.md)).
- **Presence.** The name repeats 3:12's promise; Rashi, drawing on b. *Berakhot* 9b, has God say he will be with Israel in this trouble and later ones; many modern exegetes.
- **Reserve.** A name that cannot be used to gain power over God: I am who I am.

The Greek pushed the verse toward metaphysics; the Hebrew verb leans toward promise. They
can coexist; they are not the same. **Status:** Aquinas's reading is theology of great
weight, not a ruling on the literal sense. Hosea's *lo ehyeh lakhem* ("I will not be
yours", 1:9) is heard by many as an echo ([4.1](lessons/04-01-what-a-prophet-is-amos-and-hosea.md)).

*Introduced:* [2.5](lessons/02-05-exodus-and-sinai.md)

### Merneptah stele

**The earliest mention of Israel outside the Bible.** Found by Flinders Petrie at Thebes
in 1896; records a campaign in Merneptah's fifth year, usually put c. 1208 BC
(reconstructed). It names "Israel" among the defeated in Canaan, with the sign marking a
**people**, not a city or land. It constrains dates (Israel is in Canaan by then) and
settles no exodus position.

*Introduced:* [2.5](lessons/02-05-exodus-and-sinai.md); used at [3.1](lessons/03-01-joshua-judges-and-the-land.md)

### Positions on the exodus

**Not "exodus or no exodus" but how much history, and whose.**

| | Maximal | Smaller group | Critical majority |
|---|---|---|---|
| Representative | James Hoffmeier, *Israel in Egypt* (1997) | Richard Elliott Friedman, *The Exodus* (2017) | most historians and archaeologists; views range widely |
| Who left Egypt | Israel, much as told (some read *elef*, "thousand", as a clan or unit) | a few thousand, e.g. Levites, later merged with Israelites in Canaan | no large exodus; perhaps a small group, perhaps none |
| Leans on | Semites in the Delta, Egyptian names, the Ramesses city | only Levites bear Egyptian names (Moses, Phinehas, Hophni); oldest poems | silence of Sinai; local continuity in the highland villages |
| Weakest point | the silence of the record | tribal lists as tribal history | why a nation would invent a slave origin |

**The two biblical dates:** 1 Kgs 6:1 [DR 3 Kgs 6:1] puts the exodus 480 years before
Solomon's fourth year (c. 966), so c. 1446, the 480 often read as twelve generations of
forty; Ex 1:11's "Ramesses" points to Ramesses II (1279-1213) and the thirteenth century.
**The numbers:** Ex 12:37's "six hundred thousand men on foot, beside children" means two
million or more; no trace in Sinai. **The Song of the Sea** (15:1-18, 21): archaic poetry,
perhaps twelfth century (Cross and Freedman), date disputed.

**Status:** CCC 62 teaches that God freed Israel and gave the Sinai covenant (revelation, not
a date or head-count); every position is a hypothesis. The 1934 Schmidtke decree is weighed
through 1948 and 1955 ([table](#the-biblical-commissions-documents-and-their-weight)). The
Sea's two strands (a wind all night, 14:21; walls of water, 14:22) say how the text was
built, not whether anything happened; and a wind the Lord sends is not "merely natural".

*Introduced:* [2.5](lessons/02-05-exodus-and-sinai.md)

### Covenant treaty form

**Israel imagined its bond with God as a vassal's oath to a great king.** George
Mendenhall (*Biblical Archaeologist*, 1954) compared Sinai with Hittite vassal treaties
of the fourteenth-thirteenth centuries: preamble, historical prologue (the overlord's past
favours), stipulations, deposit and public reading, witnesses, blessings and curses. Ex
20:2 is preamble plus prologue ("I am the Lord thy God, who brought thee out"), and the
commandments follow as stipulations. Deuteronomy 28's curses are close to Esarhaddon's
succession treaty (672 BC, secure). Dennis McCarthy, S.J. (*Treaty and Covenant*, 1963):
the form stays much the same across two millennia, so it cannot date a text, and the full
form is clearer in Deuteronomy than in Exodus. **Status:** a comparison, not a dating tool;
no magisterial standing. Perlitt's thesis that covenant theology is itself late is in
[covenant lawsuit](#covenant-lawsuit).

*Introduced:* [2.5](lessons/02-05-exodus-and-sinai.md)

### The two Decalogues

**Ex 20:2-17 and Deut 5:6-21 differ, chiefly in the sabbath and coveting commandments.**
Deuteronomy frames itself as Moses restating the words given at Horeb (5:2-5), not
reproducing a transcript.

- **Sabbath:** "Remember" (*zakhor*, Ex) vs "Observe" (*shamor*, Deut); the motive is creation (Ex) vs deliverance from Egypt (Deut); Deuteronomy adds the servants' rest.
- **Coveting:** Exodus has *tachmod* twice, house first, the wife inside the household; Deuteronomy puts the wife first with *tachmod*, then *titavveh* ("desire") for house and goods, adding the field.
- **Numbering:** Augustine's division, which the Catechism follows (CCC 2066), as do the Lutheran confessions, joins images to the first commandment and counts coveting a wife (ninth) apart from goods (tenth), matching Deuteronomy's split; the Greek Fathers (Orthodox, Reformed) count images separately; Jewish tradition counts "I am the Lord thy God" as the first word. The rabbis also teach that "Remember" and "Observe" were one utterance (b. *Shevuot* 20b). Numbering is convention, not doctrine.

Most critics read Deuteronomy's version as the later reworking.

*Introduced:* [2.5](lessons/02-05-exodus-and-sinai.md)

### Covenant Code

**Ex 20:22-23:33: Israel's case law in the Near Eastern style.** Named from "the book of the
covenant" Moses reads aloud (Ex 24:7); headed *mishpatim*, "judgments" (21:1); casuistic,
"if ... then". Allows an altar "in every place where the memory of my name shall be"
(20:24). **Closest to Hammurabi** (c. 1792-1750, middle chronology, reconstructed; stele
found at Susa 1901-02, secure): same form and much of the same subject matter (injury,
slaves, oxen, deposits). **How the similarity got there is disputed.** David P. Wright
(*Inventing God's Law*, 2009): direct dependence on a written Hammurabi, adapted c. 740-640
BC. The more common view: a shared legal tradition taught by scribes over centuries
(Raymond Westbrook's work is the usual reference). **Date:** disputed, often placed in the
monarchy. Resemblance is agreed; direct literary ancestry is not; neither answer touches
inspiration.

*Introduced:* [2.6](lessons/02-06-covenant-and-law.md)

### Holiness Code

**Leviticus 17-26: holiness extended from the sanctuary to the whole people and the land.**
The name is usually credited to August Klostermann (1877). Refrain: "Be ye holy, because I
the Lord your God am holy" (Lev 19:2), and "I am the Lord" after command after command. It
puts moral, ceremonial and judicial precepts side by side: "love thy friend as thyself"
(19:18), no "different seeds" (19:19), "Let the balance be just" (19:36). It assumes a single
sanctuary. **Date:** disputed; Israel Knohl and Jacob Milgrom place it after the Priestly
core, which it edits. Leviticus's other blocks: sacrifice (1-7), purity (11-15), the Day of
Atonement (16).

*Introduced:* [2.6](lessons/02-06-covenant-and-law.md)

### Deuteronomy as renewed covenant

**Deuteronomy 12-26: preached law, a second covenant "beside" Horeb's.** Framed as Moses'
farewell speech on the plains of Moab (Deut 29:1), with motive clauses ("Remember that thou
also wast a bondservant", 15:15). It **revises** earlier law: one chosen place for sacrifice
(12:13-14) against Ex 20:24's many altars; the Hebrew slave law changes between Exodus 21
and Deuteronomy 15 (the comparison is Boss problem 2(b), so it is only named here; its legal
setting is [`history-of-debt` 1.3](../history-of-debt/lessons/01-03-release-laws-in-ancient-israel.md),
its theology [`theology-of-debt`](../theology-of-debt/syllabus.md)). Bernard Levinson
(*Deuteronomy and the Hermeneutics of Legal Innovation*, 1997): Deuteronomy deliberately
revises the Covenant Code while presenting the revisions as Moses' own. **Date:** its core
often tied to the late seventh century and Josiah (a disputed majority view; de Wette 1805);
see [Deuteronomistic History](#deuteronomistic-history).

*Introduced:* [2.6](lessons/02-06-covenant-and-law.md); Josiah's book at [3.3](lessons/03-03-two-kingdoms-and-two-falls.md)

### Lex talionis

**"Eye for eye" limits punishment by law; it is not licensed revenge.** Ex 21:23-25 (also
Lev 24:19-22, Deut 19:21). Hammurabi §196 (Johns, 1903): "If a man has caused the loss of a
gentleman's eye, his eye one shall cause to be lost." But Hammurabi applies talion only
between equals: a poor man's eye costs a mina of silver (§198), a servant's half his price
(§199), and §201 a poor man's tooth in silver. Exodus states talion without class, and
frees a slave who loses an eye or a tooth (21:26-27); Lev 24:22 demands "equal judgment ...
whether he be a stranger, or a native". **Jewish reading, as its tradition states it:** the
Mishnah (*Bava Kamma* 8:1) takes "eye for eye" as monetary compensation under five heads
(damage, pain, healing, loss of time, shame), which is what the verse has always required.
**Spiritual sense:** Matt 5:38-39 asks the disciple to give up even the limited claim; it
does not correct a barbaric rule. The dispute between the codes is over who counts as equal.

*Introduced:* [2.6](lessons/02-06-covenant-and-law.md)

### Moral, ceremonial and judicial precepts

**Aquinas's tool for what binds after Christ: ask where a precept's binding force comes
from, not what it is about.** ST I-II q.99 a.4 (English Dominican Province): "moral" precepts
"are dictated by the natural law"; "ceremonial" precepts "are determinations of the Divine
worship"; "judicial" precepts "are determinations of the justice to be maintained among
men". Judicial precepts bind "not from reason alone, but in virtue of their institution"
(q.104 a.1).

| Kind | Binds because | Examples (Aquinas's questions) | After Christ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Moral | reason dictates it (natural law) | the Decalogue sums them up (q.100 a.3) | last "for ever" (q.103 a.3 ad 1) |
| Ceremonial | divine institution determining worship | sacrifices, holy things, sacraments, observances (q.101-103) | ended with Christ; keeping them as binding would deny he came (q.103 a.3) |
| Judicial | divine institution determining justice | rulers, dealings, foreigners, household (q.104-105) | "dead indeed, because they have no binding force: but they are not deadly" (q.104 a.3) |

**The Sabbath** splits: moral "in so far as it commands man to give some time to the things
of God", ceremonial "as to the fixing of the time" (q.100 a.3 ad 2), which is how Christians
keep Sunday. **Strains:** the text does not divide itself (Leviticus 19 puts all three kinds
under one refrain); one verse can hold two kinds; Jewish tradition counts 613 commandments
(Rabbi Simlai, b. *Makkot* 23b), all binding Israel, with the Noahide laws for the nations.

**Status:** common theological teaching, not defined. Trent VI canon 19 defines that the
commandments bind Christians (and says nothing of the rest); Florence (1442) taught that the
legal ceremonies ended; CCC 1961-1964 calls the Old Law holy but imperfect. Natural law:
[`ethics` 4.4](../ethics/lessons/04-04-natural-law-aquinas.md).

*Introduced:* [2.6](lessons/02-06-covenant-and-law.md)

## History: conquest to return

### Herem

**The ban: what is devoted to God, withdrawn from human use, and in war destroyed.** The
Vulgate renders it *anathema*, hence the Douay's "let this city be an anathema" (Josh 6:17).
Deuteronomy distinguishes cases: distant cities are first offered peace (Deut 20:10-15);
the seven nations of the land are to be devoted (7:1-2; 20:16-18), the stated reason being
religious contamination, marriage and "strange gods" (7:3-4). **A Near Eastern practice:**
on the Mesha stele (mid-ninth century, found 1868) Moab's king says of Israelite Nebo, "for
I devoted them to Ashtar-Chemosh" (King's 1878 translation). A common critical view holds
Deuteronomy's ban was written when the Canaanites were long gone, as a charter of exclusive
loyalty rather than a record of policy. **Counter-voices in the text:** Rahab spared (Josh
2; 6:25; later in Matt 1:5 and Heb 11:31), the Gibeonites' treaty (Josh 9), the man of Bethel
let go (Judg 1:24-25).

**How the tradition read it.** Wisdom 12:8-10: God destroyed "by little and little", giving
"place of repentance". Augustine (*Reply to Faustus* 22.74): the wars showed "not ferocity
but obedience", a unique divine command. Origen (*Homilies on Joshua*, in Rufinus's Latin):
Joshua (*Iēsous*) figures Jesus, the Canaanite kings the vices to be driven from the soul
(against the Marcionites). *Deuteronomy Rabbah* 5:14: Joshua proclaimed that whoever wished
might leave, make peace or fight; the Girgashites left, the Gibeonites made peace. Maimonides
(*Mishneh Torah*, Kings and Wars 6:1-5): peace offered first even to the seven nations. None
read the ban as a policy to imitate.

**The last word of the prophets:** Mal 4:6 [Heb 3:24], "lest I come, and strike the earth
with anathema": *herem* again ([4.5](lessons/04-05-the-book-of-the-twelve.md)).

*Introduced:* [3.1](lessons/03-01-joshua-judges-and-the-land.md); used at [4.5](lessons/04-05-the-book-of-the-twelve.md)

### Conquest and settlement models

**How did Israel appear in the hill country? Four answers, and archaeology sits much closer
to Judges than to Joshua 1-12.**

| Model | Main names | Where Israel came from | Leans on | Where it strains |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Conquest | W. F. Albright | outside, by war | Late Bronze destruction layers (Hazor) | Jericho and Ai empty at the time; destructions spread over decades |
| Infiltration | Albrecht Alt (1925) | outside, peacefully | Judges 1; new highland villages | pottery continuous with Canaan |
| Revolt | George Mendenhall (1962), Norman Gottwald (*The Tribes of Yahweh*, 1979) | inside, by social upheaval | continuity; a YHWH covenant against the city-kings | little direct evidence of a revolt |
| Emergence (the majority in some form) | Israel Finkelstein (1988), William Dever (2003) | inside, gradually | village surveys, continuous pottery, no pig bones | why Israel remembered coming from Egypt |

**Test cases:** Jericho (Kathleen Kenyon, 1952-58: walled city destroyed in the sixteenth
century, little from the late thirteenth; Bryant Wood's fifteenth-century redating a
minority); Ai (if et-Tell, "the ruin": an Early Bronze city empty until a small village c.
1200); Hazor (a thirteenth-century burning, perpetrator disputed); hundreds of Iron I
highland villages (c. 1200-1000) with Canaanite-tradition pottery and no pig. **The text
itself:** Joshua's totalizing summaries (10:40; 11:16-23) are a genre (Younger, 1990), and
Joshua corrects its own "all the land" (13:1; 15:63, nearly Judg 1:21). **Status:** every
model is a hypothesis; a majority is not a result; no magisterial answer on scale or date.

*Introduced:* [3.1](lessons/03-01-joshua-judges-and-the-land.md)

### The dark passages

**Narratives of violence commanded or unrebuked: read them, in context, with the paschal
mystery as the key.** *Verbum Domini* 42 (Benedict XVI, 2010, paraphrased): revelation is
rooted in history and progressive; it met ancient peoples at their cultural and moral
level, so it sometimes narrates violence and massacre without explicitly condemning them;
the prophets then challenged every kind of violence; such passages should not be skipped
but read in their historical-literary context, with the paschal mystery as the final key.
**Status:** post-synodal apostolic exhortation, authentic ordinary magisterium (rung 3): a
reading principle, with no verdict on historicity. Whether God could command such a thing
is [`philosophy-of-religion`](../philosophy-of-religion/syllabus.md) Module 4's question.

*Introduced:* [3.1](lessons/03-01-joshua-judges-and-the-land.md)

### The Davidic covenant

**David wanted to build God a house; God promised to build David one.** Nathan's oracle,
2 Samuel 7 [DR 2 Kings 7], turns on *bayit* (temple, household, dynasty), with *hesed*
(covenant loyalty, not to be withdrawn as from Saul) and *ne'eman* (made firm) as its other
key words; the son may be punished "with the rod of men" (v. 14). The close reading of what is
promised, what is conditional and what is not, and the comparison with 1 Chronicles 17 and
Psalm 89 [DR 88], are Boss problem 3: work them from [3.2](lessons/03-02-david-and-the-house-of-david.md).

**How it was read.** The royal psalms (Ps 2:7's "Thou art my son" echoes 7:14; [5.1](lessons/05-01-the-psalms.md)) and
Isaiah's child-king hang on it. Christian reading takes the "for ever" of 7:13 and 7:16 as
fulfilled in Christ: Luke 1:32-33, Heb 1:5, and Augustine (*City of God* XVII.8) that whoever
thinks it fulfilled in Solomon "greatly errs" (typology: Solomon a shadow, the literal
promise to David's line intact). **Jewish reading, as its own:** the promise is still open;
the daily *Amidah* prays for the "sprout" of David; Maimonides (*Laws of Kings* 11) makes
Davidic descent a mark of the messianic king.

**Status:** that Jesus is the Christ, David's heir, belongs to the faith (CCC 436-439); the
literal sense (lasting favour to David's line) must be respected; the history of David's
kingdom is freely disputed and cannot settle what the text teaches.

*Introduced:* [3.2](lessons/03-02-david-and-the-house-of-david.md); used at [5.1](lessons/05-01-the-psalms.md)

### The Deuteronomistic History

**Deuteronomy through Kings read as one history, judging Israel by Deuteronomy's
standard.** Martin Noth (*Überlieferungsgeschichtliche Studien*, 1943): a single exilic
author compiled Deuteronomy-Kings (Ruth excluded) to explain the fall of Jerusalem; its last
event is Jehoiachin's release in exile (2 Kgs 25:27), c. 561. Frank Moore Cross (*Canaanite
Myth and Hebrew Epic*, 1973): two editions, one under Josiah and one in exile ("double
redaction"), probably the most widely held model. The Göttingen school (Rudolf Smend and
students): successive exilic layers. **Why it matters for reading:** whatever its sources, a
promise such as Nathan's reached its readers inside a history whose kings had ended, so it
had to be read as hope. **Status:** hypotheses, no magisterial standing. For how Kings
grades each reign, see [Deuteronomistic History](#deuteronomistic-history).

*Introduced:* [3.2](lessons/03-02-david-and-the-house-of-david.md); see also [3.1](lessons/03-01-joshua-judges-and-the-land.md), [3.4](lessons/03-04-exile-and-return.md)

### The Succession Narrative

**Who will sit on David's throne? A story with no miracles and God in three short notes.**
Leonhard Rost (1926) isolated 2 Samuel 9-20 and 1 Kings 1-2 [DR 2 Kings 9-20; 3 Kings 1-2]
as one story. God speaks through Nathan once; motives are left to the reader; it ends with
the ruthless Solomon enthroned. The three notes: David's act "was displeasing to the Lord"
(11:27), "the Lord loved" the infant Solomon (12:24), and "by the will of the Lord" Absalom's
best counsel failed (17:14). Rost read it as an apology for Solomon; others as a critique of
the dynasty. Rost's boundaries are still the starting point, rarely accepted whole.
**Status:** literary hypothesis.

*Introduced:* [3.2](lessons/03-02-david-and-the-house-of-david.md)

### Maximalists and minimalists

**How big was David's kingdom, and was there one?** A spectrum:

- **Minimalists:** the Copenhagen school (Niels Peter Lemche, Thomas L. Thompson) and Philip Davies (*In Search of "Ancient Israel"*, 1992): the united monarchy is a later literary creation; these texts are too late to be sources for the tenth century.
- **Low chronology:** Israel Finkelstein (from the 1990s) moves the "Solomonic" pottery and monuments down to the ninth-century Omrides; his David is historical but a small highland ruler.
- **Modified conventional chronology:** Amihai Mazar's framework, now the most widely used: a modest but real tenth-century polity. Yosef Garfinkel's fortified Khirbet Qeiyafa (radiocarbon c. 1000) is claimed as Judahite; Finkelstein reads it as early northern Israelite, others as Canaanite: identity disputed.
- **Maximalists:** Kenneth Kitchen: the biblical outline broadly reliable.

**Tel Dan inscription** (three Aramaic fragments, 1993-94; usually attributed to Hazael of
Damascus, who is not named; ninth century): *bytdwd*, read by most as "house of David". It
shows a dynasty named for a David was known abroad within a century or two; it says nothing
about the size of his kingdom. **Solomon's gates** (1 Kgs 9:15; Yadin's six-chambered gates at
Hazor, Megiddo, Gezer): the strata were dated partly by that verse, so they cannot
independently confirm it; Finkelstein redates them; radiocarbon has not ended the dispute.
Absence of monuments is weak evidence where later building destroyed the layers. **Status:**
freely disputed history; "archaeology proved David a myth" and "Tel Dan proved the Bible" are
both overreach.

*Introduced:* [3.2](lessons/03-02-david-and-the-house-of-david.md)

### Deuteronomistic History

**Kings as a verdict, not an archive.** Each reign gets a regnal formula (accession, length,
mother's name for Judah, a grade): "he did evil in the sight of the Lord" or, rarely, "he did
that which was right". Every northern king fails for "the sins of Jeroboam", the rival shrines
at Bethel and Dan with their golden calves (1 Kgs 12:28-30 [DR 3 Kgs]). The standard is
Deuteronomy's: one sanctuary, no other gods. Omri, whom Assyria still named a century later,
gets eight verses and a failing grade; Qarqar (853) is never mentioned. **The strain point:**
2 Kgs 23:25-27 [DR 4 Kgs] praises Josiah in the words of the Shema as the best king, yet the
fall is still decreed for Manasseh's sins two reigns back. Cross reads the seam as a Josianic
edition extended after 586; Noth's followers read one exilic author. The book says openly that
it selects ("are they not written in the book of the words of the days of the kings of
Juda?", e.g. 2 Kgs 21:17). **Status:** the Deuteronomistic History is a hypothesis; Kings'
inspiration is defined; inerrancy concerns what the author asserts, not what he omits. The
models are in [The Deuteronomistic History](#the-deuteronomistic-history).

*Introduced:* [3.3](lessons/03-03-two-kingdoms-and-two-falls.md)

### Chronicles as a second history

**The same kings retold from Judah's side for a community rebuilding its worship: a distinct
genre and purpose, not a correction slip for Kings.** 1-2 Chronicles [DR 1-2 Paralipomenon,
"things left out"; the ancient translators thought it supplied omissions, and it also makes
them] is post-exilic, with the Temple and the Levites at the centre and a sharper rule of
immediate reward and punishment. **Characteristic moves:**

- **omits** David's sin: 1 Chr 20:1 copies "But David stayed at Jerusalem" from 2 Sam 11:1 and goes straight to Rabbah's fall; his readers know Samuel, and he omits rather than denies;
- **adds** Manasseh's captivity and repentance (2 Chr 33:11-16);
- **supplies a cause** for Josiah's death at Megiddo (2 Chr 35:21-22: he "hearkened not to the words of Nechao from the mouth of God"), where Kings gives none (2 Kgs 23:29);
- **ends** almost word for word as Ezra begins (2 Chr 36:22-23 = Ezra 1:1-3).

Did the Chronicler know a tradition Kings omitted, or did his theology demand a cause? Both are
argued. What the method rules out: saying either book errs (each is judged by genre and purpose,
DV 12), and flattening the two into one. **Status:** whether Chronicles had sources beyond
Samuel-Kings is a hypothesis.

*Introduced:* [3.3](lessons/03-03-two-kingdoms-and-two-falls.md)

### The Cyrus Cylinder

**Evidence of a Persian restoration policy that fits Ezra 1, not the text of the Jewish
decree.** Akkadian, found at Babylon in 1879, now in the British Museum. It presents Cyrus
as chosen by Marduk, and says he returned gods' images to their sanctuaries and resettled
their peoples (paraphrase). The places it names are Mesopotamian: it **never mentions Judah,
Jews or Jerusalem**. "The first charter of human rights" is a modern slogan. The edict itself
survives in two forms, Hebrew (Ezra 1:2-4) and an Aramaic memorandum from Ecbatana (Ezra
6:3-5); Elias Bickerman (1946) argued both genuine (proclamation and archive docket); many
accept the Aramaic memo in substance and judge the Hebrew recast. **Isaiah 45:1** calls Cyrus
God's *mashiach*, the only foreign king so titled: b. *Megillah* 12a has God speak *to* his
anointed *about* Cyrus; the Septuagint's "to my anointed, Cyrus" (*tō christō mou Kyrō*)
became "to Christ my Lord" by a copyist's slip (*Kyrō* to *Kyriō*), read in Barnabas 12,
Cyprian (*Testimonies* I.21) and Novatian (*On the Trinity* 26): a real patristic reading of a
corrupt text, no warrant for the literal sense.

*Introduced:* [3.4](lessons/03-04-exile-and-return.md)

### Ezra and Nehemiah chronology

**Nehemiah's date is well supported; Ezra's is disputed.** Nehemiah came "in the twentieth
year of Artaxerxes" (Neh 2:1): most take Artaxerxes I, so **445**, supported by the Elephantine
petition of **407** naming Sanballat of Samaria as a father with grown sons. Ezra came "in the
seventh year of Artaxerxes" (Ezra 7:7): under Artaxerxes I that is **458**, and Ezra came first,
as the books say; under Artaxerxes II it is **398**, and Nehemiah came first (the Louvain
Catholic scholar Albin van Hoonacker, 1890s); others emend to the thirty-seventh year.
**Disputed**, no consensus. Around them: 539 Cyrus (secure), 538 the edict (reconstructed),
515 the Second Temple (some 516). **Status:** history, no magisterial level.

*Introduced:* [3.4](lessons/03-04-exile-and-return.md)

### Persian imperial authorization

**Did the Persian crown ratify the Torah as imperial law?** Peter Frei (1984) argued that
Persia ratified local law codes, the Pentateuch among them. Anchor text: Ezra 7:26, whoever
will not keep "the law of thy God, and the law of the king". James W. Watts's volume *Persia
and Torah* (2001) tested it, and many contributors doubted any formal ratification. The broader
claim, that the Torah reached substantially its final form in the Persian period, is a
majority view, not settled. **Status:** a debated hypothesis with no magisterial standing;
"the Church teaches the Pentateuch was finalized under Persian authority" is a level error.

*Introduced:* [3.4](lessons/03-04-exile-and-return.md)

## The prophets

### Nabi the prophet

**A prophet is someone else's mouth: forthtelling before foretelling.** *Nabi* is Israel's
usual word; most link it to a Semitic root "to call" (Akkadian *nabû*); whether it means "one
called" or "one who calls out" is disputed. Older title: "he that is now called a prophet, in
time past was called a seer" (1 Sam 9:9 [DR 1 Kgs 9:9]). The model: "Aaron, thy brother, shall
be thy prophet" (Ex 7:1), having been told he "shall be thy mouth" (4:16). Greek *prophētēs*
is "one who speaks for" as much as "beforehand". What eighth-century prophets foretell is
mostly what follows from the covenant if Israel keeps breaking it, and threats can be
revoked at intercession ("The Lord had pity upon this", Amos 7:3, 6). **Prediction is still
real:** Deut 18:22 makes an unfulfilled prediction the mark of a false prophet; *Dei Filius*
ch. 3 counts fulfilled prophecies among the "most certain proofs" of revelation. Aquinas,
who calls prophecy chiefly knowledge, lists "Deal thy bread to the hungry" among its objects
(ST II-II q.171 a.3). **Status:** "spake by the Prophets" is creedal (rung 1); theories of what
prophets mostly did are scholarship.

*Introduced:* [4.1](lessons/04-01-what-a-prophet-is-amos-and-hosea.md)

### Call narrative

**Authority from being sent, not from office.** Norman Habel's pattern: God confronts,
commissions, meets an objection, reassures, gives a sign, often at the **mouth**. Isaiah's lips
touched with a coal (Isa 6:5-8); Jeremiah's "I am a child" answered by "I have given my words
in thy mouth" (Jer 1:6-9); Ezekiel eats the scroll (Ezek 2:8-3:3); Amos's bluntest version,
"the Lord took me when I followed the flock" (Amos 7:15). **Status:** a form-critical
analysis, no magisterial standing.

*Introduced:* [4.1](lessons/04-01-what-a-prophet-is-amos-and-hosea.md)

### Messenger formula

**"Thus saith the Lord" (*koh amar YHWH*): how any ancient envoy began.** Jacob's servants
open with "Thus saith thy brother Jacob" (Gen 32:4); the Assyrian envoy with "Thus saith the
great king" (2 Kgs 18:19 [DR 4 Kgs]). Claus Westermann (*Basic Forms of Prophetic Speech*): the
typical oracle is a **judgment speech**, an **accusation** ("because ...") and an
**announcement** ("therefore ..."), with the formula marking whose verdict it is.

| Form | Marker | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Call narrative | commission, objection, sign at the mouth | Isa 6; Jer 1; Ezek 2-3; Amos 7:14-15 |
| Messenger formula | "Thus saith the Lord" | Amos 1:3, 2:6 |
| Judgment speech | "because ..." then "therefore ..." | Amos 5:11 |
| Covenant lawsuit (*rib*) | summons, witnesses, charges, verdict | Hos 4:1-3; Mic 6:1-8 |
| Woe oracle | "Woe to ..." | Amos 5:18: a funeral cry over the living |

The forms are flexible: name only the elements actually present (Amos 4:1-3 has no opening
"Thus saith"). **Status:** form-critical hypotheses; *Divino Afflante Spiritu* encouraged
the study of forms but endorsed no classification.

*Introduced:* [4.1](lessons/04-01-what-a-prophet-is-amos-and-hosea.md)

### Covenant lawsuit

**Some oracles take the shape of a trial.** *Rib* means a legal case or dispute. Hos 4:1:
"the Lord shall enter into judgment" (Douay), literally the Lord "has a case" against the land.
Micah 6:1-8 summons the mountains as witnesses, recites what God did, lets the people ask what
sacrifice would satisfy, and gives the verdict: "to do judgment, and to love mercy, and to walk
solicitous with thy God" (6:8). **Disputes:** Herbert Huffmon and G. Ernest Wright read a fixed
genre modelled on treaty or court procedure; Michael De Roche and others doubt *rib* names a
formal lawsuit form. Deeper: Lothar Perlitt argued that covenant (*berit*) theology is
Deuteronomistic, later than Amos and Hosea; others point to Hos 6:7 and 8:1 ("they have
transgressed my covenant") as early. **Status:** hypotheses, a live dispute, no magisterial
standing.

*Introduced:* [4.1](lessons/04-01-what-a-prophet-is-amos-and-hosea.md)

### Hesed

**Loyal covenant love, owed both ways: not pity.** The Douay usually renders it "mercy".
Hosea's key word: the land has "no mercy" (4:1); Israel's *hesed* is "as a morning cloud"
(6:4); "For I desired mercy, and not sacrifice" (6:6), quoted twice by Matthew (9:13; 12:7);
God will "espouse thee to me for ever ... in justice, and judgment, and in mercy" (2:19). It is
also what God will not withdraw from David's son (2 Sam 7:15). Hosea's marriage makes the
covenant a marriage and Baal worship adultery; his children's names are verdicts
(*Lo-ruhamah*, *Lo-ammi*). **Hosea's marriage, event or vision?** The Talmud (*Pesachim*
87a-b) takes it as event, God teaching Hosea why he will not exchange Israel; Maimonides
(*Guide* II.46): "All this passed in a prophetic vision"; many moderns: a real marriage
understood as a living parable. The point survives the dispute.

*Introduced:* [4.1](lessons/04-01-what-a-prophet-is-amos-and-hosea.md); also [3.2](lessons/03-02-david-and-the-house-of-david.md)

### The three parts of Isaiah

**One book on the shelf and, for most scholars, several prophets behind it.**

| Part | Chapters | Setting | Date |
|---|---|---|---|
| First Isaiah | 1-39 | Isaiah in eighth-century Jerusalem; call c. 740s | reconstructed |
| Second Isaiah | 40-55 | names Cyrus (44:28; 45:1), addresses exiles as present hearers; Isaiah unnamed after 39:8 | c. 550-539, reconstructed (majority) |
| Third Isaiah | 56-66 | a community back in Judah, arguing over the Temple (Duhm, 1892) | after 538, reconstructed |

The critical case is usually traced to J. C. Döderlein and J. G. Eichhorn (late eighteenth
century); the *New Jerome Biblical Commentary* holds it. **For unity:** "the Holy One of
Israel" runs through both halves (about two dozen times; rare outside Isaiah); the Great
Isaiah Scroll copies 66 chapters as one book; Sirach 48:24 [DR Ecclus 48:27] (c. 180) credits
Isaiah with comforting "the mourners in Sion" (the language of 40:1, 61:2-3). **Positions:**
one author and long-range prediction (O. T. Allis, *The Unity of Isaiah*, 1950; J. A. Motyer);
several hands but a deliberate literary unity (H. G. M. Williamson, *The Book Called Isaiah*,
1994: the exilic prophet edited the older collection; Brevard Childs and Christopher Seitz
canonically). For most current scholars "three authors" and "one book" are not opposites.

**Status:** the 1908 response (28 or 29 June) was binding when issued; today authorship of
40-66 is freely disputed: Second Isaiah is a majority position, not doctrine; single authorship
a live minority, not heresy. The faith point that God can reveal the future stands (*Dei Filius*
ch. 3), and holding Second Isaiah on Form B grounds does not deny it
([historical-critical method](#historical-critical-method)). Authorship changes *when* 45:1 was
said, not *what* it says.

*Introduced:* [4.2](lessons/04-02-isaiah-and-immanuel.md); used at [1.3](lessons/01-03-the-historical-critical-method-and-its-limits.md), [3.4](lessons/03-04-exile-and-return.md), [4.3](lessons/04-03-the-servant-of-the-lord.md)

### Syro-Ephraimite crisis

**The setting of Isaiah 7:14.** Around 734 BC Rezin of Damascus ("Rasin") and Pekah of Israel
("Phacee, son of Romelia") marched on Jerusalem to seat "the son of Tabeel" (7:6) and force
Judah into their anti-Assyrian coalition. Isaiah's counsel: trust, don't buy protection, with a
pun, *ta'aminu ... te'amenu*, "If you will not believe, you shall not continue" (7:9). Ahaz
sent silver and gold to Tiglath-pileser III instead (2 Kgs 16:7-9 [DR 4 Kgs]); Assyria took
Damascus in 732 (secure) and killed Rezin. Dates: the crisis c. 734-732, reconstructed within a
year or two. The sign's deadline (7:16) ties every literal reading to Ahaz's years.

*Introduced:* [4.2](lessons/04-02-isaiah-and-immanuel.md)

### Almah and parthenos

**The Hebrew is open, the Greek is narrower, and Matthew reads the Greek.** *Almah*: a young
woman of marriageable age; seven uses (Gen 24:43 Rebekah; Ex 2:8 Miriam; Ps 68:25 [DR 67:26];
Prov 30:19; Song 1:3; 6:8; Isa 7:14). It neither specifies nor rules out virginity. *Betulah*
is "virgin", and Gen 24:16 still adds "not known to man". Jerome (*Against Jovinianus* I.32):
an *almah* is "a hidden virgin", and no use of it for a married woman can be found. The Greek
Isaiah (second or first century BC, reconstructed) chose *parthenos*; Matt 1:23 quotes it;
Aquila and Theodotion put *neanis*, "young woman". *Parthenos* is the ordinary word for
"virgin", but Greek Genesis 34:3 uses it of Dinah after Shechem lay with her.

| | Hebrew (MT) | Greek Isaiah | Matthew 1:23 |
|---|---|---|---|
| the woman | *ha-almah*, the young woman | *hē parthenos* | *hē parthenos* |
| who names him | *qarat*, she will call | *kaleseis*, you (singular) will call | *kalesousin*, they will call |

**The readings in Ahaz's day:** a royal child (Trypho in Justin, *Dialogue* 67: Hezekiah);
Isaiah's own son (Rashi: the prophet's wife, from 8:3-4, rejecting Hezekiah on arithmetic,
since 2 Kgs 16:2 and 18:2 make him born nine years before Ahaz reigned); a woman already
pregnant (many moderns). **Status:** the virginal conception is dogma resting on Matthew and
Luke, not on winning the *almah* argument; CCC 497 sees Isa 7:14 fulfilled in it; what the
prophet foresaw and how fulfilment works are freely disputed; the Jewish readings are stated,
not refuted (the argument belongs to [`apologetics-foundations`](../apologetics-foundations/syllabus.md)).

*Introduced:* [4.2](lessons/04-02-isaiah-and-immanuel.md)

### Servant Songs

**Four poems in Isaiah 40-55 where "my servant" has a mission to Israel, suffers, and is
exalted.** Bernhard Duhm (*Das Buch Jesaia*, 1892) pulled them out: **42:1-4** (the chosen one
who brings justice quietly; "the bruised reed he shall not break"); **49:1-6** (the servant
speaks, is named "Israel", is sent to restore Israel and be "the light of the Gentiles");
**50:4-9** (the disciple who does not turn from "them that ... spit upon me"); **52:13-53:12**
(a servant despised, wounded "for our iniquities", "cut off out of the land of the living",
exalted). Some extend the first three. Duhm's separation from context is itself debated. Around
them, God's *'ebed* is plainly Jacob-Israel (41:8; 44:1, 21; 45:4), frightened and blind (42:19).

**The identifications, with their holders** (the verses each leans on are Boss problem 4(a),
worked in [4.3](lessons/04-03-the-servant-of-the-lord.md)):

- **Israel** (the nation, the faithful remnant, ideal Israel): Origen's Jewish interlocutor (*Against Celsus* I.55, c. 248); Rashi on 42 and 52:13-53 (Jacob, the righteous within Israel); much modern scholarship. The Septuagint already names Jacob and Israel at 42:1.
- **The prophet himself:** the eunuch's first guess (Acts 8:34); Rashi on 49:1-6; a modern minority (some read the fourth Song as his disciples' reflection).
- **Cyrus:** a minority, for the first Song only; Isaiah never calls Cyrus *'ebed*.
- **The Messiah, not suffering:** Targum Jonathan (final form early centuries AD, date disputed): "my servant the Messiah" (52:13), with the contempt and suffering largely transferred to Israel.
- **Jesus:** the New Testament (Matt 8:17; 12:18-21; Luke 22:37; Acts 8:30-35; 1 Pet 2:22-25) and the Fathers.

The first four are literal-sense claims; the fifth works at another level: a Catholic can hold
Israel or the prophet as the literal sense *and* Christ as fulfilment. **Status:** fulfilment in
Christ is Church teaching (CCC 601); the literal identity is a scholarly question; how the two
relate is freely disputed ([sensus plenior](#sensus-plenior), [typology](#typology)). Isaiah 53
read for sacrifice is [`christology` 5.2](../christology/lessons/05-02-ransom-christus-victor-and-sacrifice.md).

*Introduced:* [4.3](lessons/04-03-the-servant-of-the-lord.md)

### The new covenant

**The only place the Old Testament says "new covenant", and it promises the same law written
within.** Jer 31:31-34 (LXX 38): a covenant unlike Sinai's, "which they made void", with the law
put in their inward parts (*qereb*) and written on their heart (*lev*); still "my law" (*torati*);
and forgiveness. Ezekiel develops it: a new heart and spirit (36:26). The 2001 Commission
document (39): not a change of law but a new, interior relationship to it, "interiorization";
Qumran's Damascus Document already speaks of a "new covenant". **Readings:** Jewish: the renewal
of God's covenant with Israel and Judah, the Torah inscribed within. Christian: "the new covenant
in my blood" (Luke 22:20; 1 Cor 11:25); Hebrews 8 quotes the Greek and infers that "new" makes the
first old (8:13; [`new-testament` 6.2](../new-testament/lessons/06-02-hebrews.md)); Augustine reads
the writing on the heart as the Holy Spirit's presence, "the finger of God" (*On the Spirit and the
Letter* 36). How "old" (Heb 8:13) and "never revoked" (CCC 121) fit together is live among Catholic
theologians ([6.4](lessons/06-04-the-old-testament-in-light-of-the-new.md)). **Status:** fulfilment in Christ is the New Testament's own assertion, taught
by the Church (CCC 64, 580); the 2001 reading is consultative.

*Introduced:* [4.4](lessons/04-04-jeremiah-and-ezekiel.md); used at [6.4](lessons/06-04-the-old-testament-in-light-of-the-new.md)

### Individual responsibility in Ezekiel 18

**The exiles' proverb abolished: each generation answers for itself.** "The fathers have eaten
sour grapes, and the teeth of the children are set on edge" (18:2) means *we pay for our parents'
sins*. Ezekiel: "the soul that sinneth, the same shall die" (18:4); Jer 31:29-30 promises the same
for days to come. "Soul" is *nefesh*, the living person, not an immortal soul. The chapter is
addressed to the "house of Israel" (18:30-31): a summons to repent now, not modern
individualism. **Jewish reading:** R. Yose bar Hanina counts it among Moses' words a later prophet
revoked, Ezek 18:4 replacing the visiting of the fathers' iniquity (Ex 20:5) (b. *Makkot* 24a,
paraphrased); compare Onqelos restricting Ex 20:5 to children who continue their fathers' sins
([1.4](lessons/01-04-canonical-reading-and-the-unity-of-the-testaments.md)).

**Ezekiel's other visions:** the glory leaves the defiled Temple by the east gate (10:18-19; 11:23)
and returns only in the restored Temple (43:2-5): God left before Babylon destroyed; the dry bones
(37:1-14) are "the house of Israel", national restoration through *ruach*, read by Irenaeus as
bodily resurrection (*Against Heresies* V.15.1), by some rabbis literally, by one as truth and
parable at once (b. *Sanhedrin* 92b).

*Introduced:* [4.4](lessons/04-04-jeremiah-and-ezekiel.md)

### The two editions of Jeremiah

**Two Hebrew Jeremiahs coexisted before Christ.** The book reports its own growth: Baruch
rewrote the burned scroll and "there were added besides many more words than had been before"
(36:32).

| Feature | Masoretic (Hebrew; Vulgate, Douay) | Septuagint |
|---|---|---|
| Length | longer | roughly one-eighth shorter (some 2,700 words; some counts one-seventh) |
| Oracles against the nations | chapters 46-51 | after 25:13, different order |
| New covenant | 31:31-34 | chapter 38 |
| 33:14-26 (David's Branch, Levites) | present | absent |
| 10:6-8, 10 (hymn to God's greatness) | present | absent |
| Qumran support | 4QJer^a, 4QJer^c | 4QJer^b, 4QJer^d |

**Majority** (J. Gerald Janzen, Emanuel Tov): the Greek faithfully translates an earlier, shorter
Hebrew edition, expanded in the MT. **Minority** (Jack Lundbom): much of the shortfall is scribal
omission. **Status:** canonicity of the book "entire with all their parts", as in the Vulgate
(which follows the longer Hebrew), is defined; which edition is earlier has no magisterial level
(DAS 21: Vulgate authenticity juridical; DAS 16: go to the original texts). A late 33:14-26 is
still Scripture: lateness concerns authorship, not canonicity. Augustine's view that the same
Spirit spoke in prophets and translators (*City of God* XVIII.43) is a theological proposal.

*Introduced:* [4.4](lessons/04-04-jeremiah-and-ezekiel.md); foreshadowed at [1.1](lessons/01-01-what-the-old-testament-is.md)

### The Book of the Twelve

**The minor prophets are "minor" from brevity (Augustine, *City of God* XVIII.29), copied on one
scroll and counted as one book.** Ben Sira (c. 190-180) praises "the twelve prophets" (Sir 49:10
[DR Ecclus 49:12]); the Talmud (*Bava Batra* 14b) explains the single scroll (Hosea alone would be
lost, being small); Jerome counts "the book of the Twelve Prophets" eighth of the Prophets.

| Hebrew / Douay | Septuagint | Setting claimed or proposed | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 Hosea (Osee) | 1 | 8th c., northern kingdom | reconstructed |
| 2 Joel | 4 | none given; 9th-4th c. proposed | disputed |
| 3 Amos | 2 | 8th c., Jeroboam II | reconstructed |
| 4 Obadiah (Abdias) | 5 | after 587/586 | reconstructed |
| 5 Jonah (Jonas) | 6 | hero 8th c.; book usually post-exilic | disputed |
| 6 Micah (Micheas) | 3 | late 8th c. | reconstructed |
| 7-9 Nahum, Habakkuk, Zephaniah | 7-9 | 7th c. (Nahum before 612) | reconstructed |
| 10-11 Haggai, Zechariah | 10-11 | 520-518 (Zech 1-8); Zech 9-14 "Second Zechariah", disputed | reconstructed, tight |
| 12 Malachi | 12 | Persian period, 5th c. | reconstructed |

**Unity or anthology?** James Nogalski and others (since the 1990s): a designed whole with
catchwords across the seams; Ehud Ben Zvi and others: an anthology. Test case: the creed of Ex
34:6 quoted in Joel 2:13 and Jonah 4:2 (God "ready to repent of the evil"), echoed in Mic 7:18,
then turned in Nahum 1:3 ("will not ... acquit the guilty"): Jonah's spared Nineveh meets Nahum's
doomed Nineveh. **Key texts:** Mic 5:2 [Heb 5:1] (Bethlehem; Matthew's "not the least", "princes"
from *allufei* for *alfei*, and a line from 2 Sam 5:2); Zech 9:9 (one donkey named twice by
parallelism; b. *Sanhedrin* 98a sets it beside Dan 7:13); Joel 2:28-32 [Heb 3:1-5] at Pentecost
(Acts 2:16-21 changes "after this" to "in the last days"); Mal 4:5-6 [Heb 3:23-24] and Elijah (Sir
48:10; Luke 1:17; Matt 17:12; the Mishnah, *Eduyot* 8:7: Elijah comes to make peace). **Status:**
canonicity defined; order, unity, dates are scholarly questions.

*Introduced:* [4.5](lessons/04-05-the-book-of-the-twelve.md)

### The genre of Jonah

**History, parable or satire?** The hero is a real eighth-century prophet, "Jonas, the son of
Amathi" (2 Kgs 14:25 [DR 4 Kgs]).

- **History:** the book records events; the *Catholic Encyclopedia* article "Jonah" (1910) argued Jesus's sayings decide it ("Christ contrasts fact with fact, not fancy with fancy, nor fancy with fact").
- **Parable:** a didactic story about a real figure; genre signals: a city "of three days' journey" (3:3), an unnamed "king of Ninive", an empire converted in a day with cattle in sackcloth, a plant grown in a night, a book ending on God's unanswered question.
- **Satire:** every pagan repents faster than the prophet, who is furious that God is merciful (4:2).

**The sign of Jonah** (Matt 12:39-41; Luke 11:30 keeps only the preaching) does not settle it: a
speaker can cite a story's characters without ruling on its genre (as with the prodigal son), and
Josephus, cited for historicity, tells the fish with "It is also reported" and omits Nineveh's
repentance (*Antiquities* IX.10.2). **Dates:** the book usually post-exilic (disputed); Nineveh fell
in 612 (secure). **Status:** the 23 June 1905 response (non-historical reading only on solid proof,
"parable" named) bound when issued; the 1955 clarification freed such literary questions. Jonah's
genre is freely disputed (rung 5): not settled for "history" by the old decree, nor for "parable"
by the modern majority.

*Introduced:* [4.5](lessons/04-05-the-book-of-the-twelve.md)

### The cessation of prophecy

**A Second Temple claim that canonical prophecy had stopped: not a recorded date.** 1 Maccabees
marks events "since the day, that there was no prophet seen in Israel" (9:27); the defiled altar's
stones wait "till there should come a prophet" (4:46); Simon is high priest "till there should
arise a faithful prophet" (14:41). Ps 74:9 [DR 73:9]: "there is now no prophet". Josephus (*Against
Apion* I.8, Whiston) ranks later history lower "because there hath not been an exact succession of
prophets since that time", since Artaxerxes. The Tosefta (*Sotah* 13): when Haggai, Zechariah and
Malachi died, the holy spirit departed and the *bat kol* remained. Yet Josephus reports later
prophet-like figures, and Rebecca Gray (1993) argues what ceased was the classical,
scripture-writing kind. **The New Testament's answer:** John as Elijah (Mal 4:5-6), Joel's spirit
poured out at Pentecost. The claim also drives Josephus's canon ([the twenty-two books](#the-twenty-two-books)).

*Introduced:* [4.5](lessons/04-05-the-book-of-the-twelve.md); used at [6.1](lessons/06-01-how-the-old-testament-canon-formed.md)

## Psalms and wisdom

### Parallelism

**Hebrew poetry rhymes thoughts, not sounds: read the second line for what it adds.** Robert
Lowth (*De sacra poesi Hebraeorum*, 1753) named *parallelismus membrorum* in three kinds:
**synonymous** (the second line restates: "When Israel went out of Egypt, / the house of Jacob
from a barbarous people", Ps 114:1 [DR 113:1]); **antithetic** (the opposite: Ps 1:6);
**synthetic** (completes or extends, no echo: Ps 2:6). James Kugel (*The Idea of Biblical
Poetry*, 1981): "synonymous" misleads; his shorthand is *A, and what's more, B*. Robert Alter
(*The Art of Biblical Poetry*, 1985): the usual movement is intensification (laugh becomes
deride, anger becomes rage, Ps 2:4-5). **Application:** Zech 9:9's "upon an ass, and upon a
colt, the foal of an ass" names one animal twice; Matthew 21 reads two (treated in
[`new-testament` 2.4](../new-testament/lessons/02-04-john-and-the-synoptics.md)). **Status:**
scholarly analysis.

*Introduced:* [5.1](lessons/05-01-the-psalms.md); foreshadowed at [4.5](lessons/04-05-the-book-of-the-twelve.md)

### Gunkel's psalm genres

**Each psalm is a kind of prayer with a job to do; the genre tells you what each part is for.**
Hermann Gunkel's form criticism (commentary 1926; *Einleitung in die Psalmen*, completed by
Joachim Begrich, 1933) sorted psalms by recurring structure and asked where in worship each was
used (*Sitz im Leben*):

| Genre | Shape | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Hymn | call to praise, then the reason ("for he is good") | |
| Communal lament | the people after a disaster | |
| Individual lament (the largest group) | invocation, complaint, petition, statement of trust, vow of praise; often a sudden turn to confidence | Ps 13 [DR 12]; Ps 22 [DR 21], which turns to praise |
| Individual thanksgiving | a lament answered, retold | |
| Royal | sung for the king | Pss 2, 45, 72, 110 [DR 109] |
| Wisdom (smaller group) | instruction | Ps 1 |

The sudden change of mood in a lament is a convention of the form, not a psychological puzzle.
**The five books:** doxologies at 41:13, 72:18-19, 89:52, 106:48 [DR 40:14, 71:18-19, 88:53,
105:48]; the colophon "The praises of David ... are ended" (72:20 [DR 71:20]) followed by more
Davidic psalms shows collections joined over time. Individual psalms run from the monarchy to
after the exile (reconstructed); the Psalter's final shape is disputed (the Cave 11 Psalms
Scroll's different order). **Status:** scholarly analysis, no magisterial level. The liturgical
use is [`sacraments-and-liturgy`](../sacraments-and-liturgy/syllabus.md) 6.2.

*Introduced:* [5.1](lessons/05-01-the-psalms.md)

### Royal psalms

**Coronation songs for Israel's king, read after 587 as hope and by the New Testament as
fulfilled in Christ.** Psalm 2's literal sense is a coronation: the nations plot, God laughs, the
new king recites the decree, "Thou art my son, this day have I begotten thee": adoption language
as in 2 Sam 7:14, "this day" the coronation day. "His Christ" (2:2) is *meshicho*, "his
anointed". After the monarchy ended, a promise of worldwide rule to a throneless line became hope
for a future anointed one. **New Testament:** Acts 13:33 (the resurrection), Heb 1:5 (the Son);
Ps 110:1 [DR 109:1], among the most cited verses (Mark 12:35-37; Acts 2:34-35); 110:4 in Hebrews'
priesthood argument ([`new-testament` 6.2](../new-testament/lessons/06-02-hebrews.md)). Ps 110:3
[DR 109:3] is textually uncertain ([the Vulgate table](#where-the-douay-follows-the-vulgate-against-the-hebrew-or-greek)).
**Jewish readings:** Rashi records that the Sages expounded Ps 2 of the King Messiah (b.
*Berakhot* 7b), but gives its plain sense as David against the Philistines (2 Sam 5:17); he reads
Ps 110, with the rabbis and the Midrash on Psalms, as God's word to Abraham (addressed as lord in
Gen 23:6). **Status:** that the royal psalms speak of Christ is the New Testament's reading; *how*
(prediction, fuller sense, typology through the Davidic king) is freely disputed. The 1910 response
held Pss 2 and 110 Davidic; its authorship clauses are now freely disputed.

*Introduced:* [5.1](lessons/05-01-the-psalms.md)

### Imprecatory psalms

**Psalms that curse, and the three ways the tradition prays them.** Psalm 137 [DR 136] begins
"Upon the rivers of Babylon, there we sat and wept" and ends "Blessed be he that shall take and
dash thy little ones against the rock" (v. 9). Literal sense first: deportees who watched
Jerusalem fall ask God to remember Edom (v. 7) and repay Babylon like for like (v. 8).

- **Spiritually:** Origen (*Against Celsus* VII.22) and Augustine (on this psalm, §12): "What are the little ones of Babylon? Evil desires at their birth", to be dashed against the rock that is Christ.
- **Morally parsed:** Aquinas (ST II-II q.25 a.6 ad 3): scriptural imprecations "by way of prediction, not by way of wish", or as desire for God's justice, or for the sin's destruction "but that the man may live".
- **By omission:** the *General Instruction of the Liturgy of the Hours* (1971), 131, drops Pss 58, 83, 109 [DR 57, 82, 108] from the cycle and verses from others (137:7-9 among them), citing a psychological difficulty while noting the New Testament prays this way (Rev 6:10).

**Status:** the omissions are liturgical law about public prayer, not a judgment against
inspiration or the canon.

*Introduced:* [5.1](lessons/05-01-the-psalms.md)

### Wisdom literature

**Israel reasoning from experience, borrowing from its neighbours, and arguing with itself.**
Wisdom asks how to live well in the world as it is; its evidence is observation, not Sinai; the
genre was international (Egyptian and Mesopotamian instruction books long predate Israel). "The
fear of the Lord is the beginning of wisdom" (Prov 1:7). Three canonical voices: **confident**
(Proverbs: the world is ordered), **sceptical** (Qoheleth: the order is thin), **erotic** (the
Song). **Proverbs is an anthology** by its own headings: "proverbs of Solomon" (10:1), "the words
of the wise" (22:17), those "which the men of Ezechias ... copied out" (25:1), Agur (30:1), King
Lamuel (31:1). Solomon is the genre's patron (1 Kgs 4:30 [DR 3 Kgs]). Chapters 1-9 usually late
(Persian period), some of 10-29 possibly monarchic: reconstructed, not closely. Job's cycle of
retribution debate ([5.3](lessons/05-03-job.md)) and Sirach and Wisdom ([5.4](lessons/05-04-sirach-and-the-wisdom-of-solomon.md)) continue the tradition. **Status:**
the books are defined as canonical, not as Solomon's; no Commission response ruled on them (as far
as [5.2](lessons/05-02-proverbs-ecclesiastes-and-the-song-of-songs.md) found); dates and authorship have no magisterial level.

*Introduced:* [5.2](lessons/05-02-proverbs-ecclesiastes-and-the-song-of-songs.md)

### Instruction of Amenemope

**An inspired writer borrowed from a pagan instruction book, edited it, and put Israel's God at
its centre.** British Museum Papyrus 10474, acquired at Thebes by E. A. Wallis Budge in 1888,
published in full in 1923. Adolf Erman (1924) listed its parallels with Proverbs, mostly
22:17-23:11, and emended Prov 22:20 (written *shilshom*, read *shalishim*; Douay "three manner of
ways") to *sheloshim*, "thirty", after Amenemope's closing charge to look to its thirty chapters.
Date Ramesside, c. 1300-1075 (reconstructed).

| Proverbs (Douay) | Amenemope (paraphrase) | What changed |
|---|---|---|
| 22:17-18 "keep it in thy bowels" | keep the sayings in the casket of your belly | image kept |
| 22:20 "three manner of ways" (emended: thirty) | look to these thirty chapters | count kept, Hebrew later garbled |
| 22:22 "Do no violence to the poor" | beware of robbing the wretched | motive added: "the Lord will judge his cause" (22:23) |
| 22:28 "Pass not beyond the ancient bounds" | do not move the boundary markers | same law of land |

Verse 19 states the purpose: "that thy trust may be in the Lord". **Status:** most scholars since
the mid-twentieth century hold Proverbs depends on it, perhaps through an intermediary; a minority
argue a common source. Borrowing is no problem for inspiration: what the inspired author asserts
is the book's, whatever its source.

*Introduced:* [5.2](lessons/05-02-proverbs-ecclesiastes-and-the-song-of-songs.md)

### Lady Wisdom

**Proverbs 8: God's own wisdom personified as present before creation.** She speaks in the first
person, with God before the mountains, "playing before him at all times" (8:30). Verse 22 turns on
*qanani* (acquired, possessed, begot, created): the Vulgate *possedit* ("The Lord possessed me in
the beginning of his ways"), the Septuagint *ektisen*, "created". **Literal sense:** a
personification; nothing in the chapter asks the question Nicaea answered. **Christian reading:**
the New Testament calls Christ the wisdom of God (1 Cor 1:24); Col 1:15 echoes the chapter; the
Fathers read Proverbs 8 of the Son and referred "created" to the Incarnation. The Arians had the
*more literal* case from their own Bible; the dogma was never settled from this verse (Arius's use
and the Nicene reply: [`trinity-and-god` 2.2](../trinity-and-god/lessons/02-02-the-father-and-the-son-in-the-new-testament.md),
[`christology` 1.4](../christology/lessons/01-04-arius-and-the-divinity-of-the-son.md),
[`patristics` 3.1](../patristics/lessons/03-01-athanasius.md)). **Status:** the Christological sense is
a spiritual or fuller sense built on a personification, not a proof text. Genesis Rabbah 1:1 reads
8:30 as the Torah speaking ([wisdom identified with Torah](#wisdom-identified-with-torah)).

*Introduced:* [5.2](lessons/05-02-proverbs-ecclesiastes-and-the-song-of-songs.md); used at [5.4](lessons/05-04-sirach-and-the-wisdom-of-solomon.md)

### Hebel

**Qoheleth's key word: vapour, breath, real but insubstantial and quickly gone; not
"worthless".** More than thirty uses. *Havel havalim* is the construction of "song of songs" and
"holy of holies", a superlative: "sheer vapour". The Vulgate's *vanitas* gave the Douay "Vanity of
vanities" (1:2), pushing toward moral contempt, which the Douay headnote makes explicit; that is a
legitimate *moral* sense, a second step. **Qoheleth** is a participle from *qahal*, one who
addresses an assembly (Greek *Ekklesiastes*). Themes: death levels wise and fool, man and beast
(2:14-16; 3:19-20); God's work cannot be found out (3:11; 8:17); eat, drink and enjoy work as God's
gift (3:12-13; 9:7); "Who knoweth if the spirit of the children of Adam ascend upward?" (3:21). The
royal voice ("son of David", 1:1) is dropped after chapter 2. **The epilogue** (12:9-14) speaks of
Qoheleth in the third person and ends "Fear God, and keep his commandments"; most assign it to one
or two editors; whether it frames or domesticates the book is disputed (Childs: the editors'
instruction for use). **Date:** Persian or Hellenistic, often third century (late Hebrew; Persian
loanword *pardes*): disputed. The book neither denies God nor promises an afterlife.

*Introduced:* [5.2](lessons/05-02-proverbs-ecclesiastes-and-the-song-of-songs.md); the epilogue canonically at [1.4](lessons/01-04-canonical-reading-and-the-unity-of-the-testaments.md)

### Allegory of the Song

**A love song read by both traditions as a song of divine love, without making it stop being a
love song.** **Literal sense:** lyrics of desire, absence and union; God not clearly named (the
one candidate, *shalhevetyah*, "flame of Yah", 8:6, may be a superlative); date disputed, from
Solomon's century to the Hellenistic age. **Rabbinic status:** the Mishnah (*Yadayim* 3:5) records a
debate whether the Song and Qoheleth "defile the hands"; Rabbi Akiva: "all the Writings are holy,
and the Song of Songs is holy of holies" (lesson's translation). **Jewish allegory:** God and
Israel, the poem following Israel's history (Targum, midrash); Rashi on 1:2 hears the bride "in her
exile and her widowhood", and insists no verse loses its plain sense. **Christian:** Origen, the
Bridegroom the Word and the bride the Church or the soul; Jerome to Damasus (383): Origen "has in
the Song of Songs surpassed himself. He wrote ten volumes upon it"; the Greek mostly lost, the
first books in Rufinus's Latin, two homilies in Jerome's. Bernard of Clairvaux's sermons (some
eighty-six, unfinished at his death in 1153). **Theodore of Mopsuestia** the exception: a marriage
poem. **Status:** the Christological reading is a spiritual sense received through Fathers and
liturgy, not a definition; how far allegory may range is freely disputed; the Douay's headnotes
("Christ caresses his spouse") are a spiritual reading printed as headings.

*Introduced:* [5.2](lessons/05-02-proverbs-ecclesiastes-and-the-song-of-songs.md)

### Retribution theology

**The rule that the just prosper and suffering proves sin: Job's friends defend it and God
rejects them.** Eliphaz states it first: "who ever perished being innocent?" (Job 4:7). Baldad and
Sophar harden it cycle by cycle; by chapter 22 Eliphaz invents Job's sins to fit it; the third
cycle breaks down (Baldad's six verses in 25, no third speech from Sophar: design or damage,
disputed). **Elihu** (32-37) revises it: suffering as *discipline*, God opening "his ear in
affliction" (36:15; the idea already touched in Eliphaz 5:17); he appears in neither prologue nor
epilogue, God never answers him, his Hebrew differs: most take him as a later addition, others as a
deliberate foil (disputed). **The verdict:** "you have not spoken the thing that is right before
me, as my servant Job hath" (42:7). **The book's question** is the accuser's: does Job fear God
*hinnam*, "for nothing" (1:9)? God admits the suffering is *hinnam* (2:3). *Ha-satan* is "the
accuser", a title in the heavenly court (Zech 3:1); Challoner reads the devil.

| Section | Chapters | Retribution is... |
|---|---|---|
| Prologue | 1-2 | suspended: the suffering is *hinnam* |
| Three cycles | 4-27 | asserted by the friends, denied by Job |
| Wisdom poem | 28 | set aside: wisdom is God's alone; speaker disputed |
| Job's oath | 29-31 | turned into a lawsuit against God |
| Elihu | 32-37 | revised: discipline |
| Whirlwind | 38:1-42:6 | never mentioned |
| Epilogue | 42:7-17 | the friends rebuked; Job restored after being declared right |

**Status:** inerrancy covers what the sacred writer asserts, and the book asserts that the friends
were wrong: "the Bible teaches suffering is punishment (Job 4:7)" quotes Eliphaz. Whether the
whirlwind answers the problem of evil is [`philosophy-of-religion`](../philosophy-of-religion/syllabus.md)'s question.

*Introduced:* [5.3](lessons/05-03-job.md)

### Goel

**The *go'el* is the kinsman-redeemer: the relative who acts to restore family, land or blood.**
He buys back family land (Lev 25:25), avenges a killing (Num 35:19), or redeems a widow's line, as
Boaz does (Ruth 4). Job 19:25-27 names a *go'el* who "lives"; who he is (God, or a heavenly advocate
like the "witness ... in heaven" of 16:19 [DR 16:20]), what the Hebrew of verse 26 allows, and how
the Latin and Douay render the passage are Boss problem 5(b): work it from [5.3](lessons/05-03-job.md). Background the
lesson gives: Job 14 has no hope of return ("man when he is fallen asleep shall not rise again",
14:12), though Challoner's heading reads the chapter in light of later faith; the Greek Job adds a
closing note (42:17a) that Job will rise again; Gregory files his resurrection reading of 19:25 under
the literal (*Moralia* XIV.67-68). **Status:** bodily resurrection is defined dogma, not defined
*from* Job 19; the identity of the *go'el* is a scholarly question. Dei Verbum 15 calls some things in
the Old Testament "incomplete and temporary" within a true divine pedagogy.

*Introduced:* [5.3](lessons/05-03-job.md)

### Wisdom identified with Torah

**Sirach gives universal Wisdom an address: Israel, as the Law of Moses.** In Proverbs 8 Wisdom is
universal; Sirach keeps that ("in every nation" she had rule, DR 24:10) and then Wisdom is told to
pitch her tent in Jacob (Greek 24:8 [DR 24:12-13], *kataskēnoun*), serves in the Temple liturgy (DR
24:14-15), and is named: "All these things are the book of the covenant of God Most High, the law
which Moses commanded us" (Greek 24:23 [DR 24:32-33], lesson's translation). Baruch 4:1 does the
same ("the book of the commandments of God, and the law, that is for ever"). **Jewish carry-forward:**
Genesis Rabbah 1:1 reads Prov 8:30 as the Torah speaking: God looked into the Torah as an architect
consults his plan. **Christian:** John 1:14's *eskēnōsen*, "pitched his tent", gives Wisdom a
different address, a person, without cancelling Sirach's literal sense ([canonical reading](#canonical-reading)).
The Greek of the same chapter has "he created me" (*ektisen me*, 24:9), which fed the fight over
Proverbs 8:22; the Douay's "firstborn before all creatures" (24:5) is a Latin addition.

*Introduced:* [5.4](lessons/05-04-sirach-and-the-wisdom-of-solomon.md); Baruch at [6.2](lessons/06-02-tobit-judith-baruch-and-the-maccabees.md)

### Canonicity vs authorship

**Canonicity rests on inspiration and the Church's reception, not on the name attached.** Trent
lists "Wisdom, Ecclesiasticus" with Genesis, no second rank, and names Wisdom without attributing it
to Solomon. Non-Solomonic authorship is the scholarly consensus and was the learned view of
antiquity: the Muratorian fragment calls it "written by the friends of Solomon in his honour";
Augustine reports the learned view that it is not Solomon's (*City of God* XVII.20). The book speaks
as a king told to "build a temple" (9:8) and never names Solomon. The same rule governs the
Pentateuch ("the five books of Moses" is a title, [2.2](lessons/02-02-who-wrote-the-pentateuch.md)), Proverbs' named collections ([5.2](lessons/05-02-proverbs-ecclesiastes-and-the-song-of-songs.md)),
Isaiah ([4.2](lessons/04-02-isaiah-and-immanuel.md)) and Daniel ([6.3](lessons/06-03-daniel-and-apocalyptic.md)). **Two errors:** "the Church teaches Solomon wrote Wisdom"
(promoting), and "not by Solomon, so not really Scripture" (demoting a defined dogma).

**The two books:**

| | Sirach | Wisdom of Solomon |
|---|---|---|
| Author | Ben Sira, Jerusalem scribe, named (DR 50:29 [Greek 50:27]) | anonymous Alexandrian Jew |
| Language | Hebrew; Greek by the grandson | Greek |
| Date | c. 190-175; translation c. 132 (reconstructed, firm) | late 1st c. BC-early 1st c. AD (reconstructed; Winston, 1979: after 30 BC) |
| Survives in | Hebrew (Cairo Genizah, identified by Solomon Schechter 1896, about two-thirds; Masada scroll 39:27-44:17, found 1964; Qumran), Greek, longer Greek and Latin | Greek, Latin |
| Where Wisdom lives | in Israel, as the Torah (24) | in God, as radiance and image (7:25-26) |
| New Testament echo | tenting (John 1:14) | *apaugasma* (Heb 1:3), *eikōn* (Col 1:15), the just man (Matt 27:43) |
| Jewish reception | quoted by name in the Talmud (*Hagigah* 13a); *Sanhedrin* 100b debates whether reading him is forbidden; excluded by Tosefta *Yadayim* 2:13 | not preserved |

**Dating Ben Sira:** the grandson came "in the eight and thirtieth year" of Ptolemy Euergetes;
Ptolemy III reigned about 25 years, so the king is Ptolemy VIII Euergetes II, counting from 170: year
38 = 133/132, conventionally 132. Chapter 50 praises Simon II (c. 200); nothing of 167. **Wisdom's
themes:** immortality of the just (3:1-4, *athanasia*); the persecuted just man (2:12-20, *pais
kyriou*); Wisdom as "the brightness of eternal light" (7:26), read with Heb 1:3 by Origen (*De
Principiis* I.2.4-5: generation eternal as brilliancy from the sun). **Status:** that the Son is the
Father's radiance is Scripture and Creed; how Wisdom 2 and 7 relate to him is freely disputed.

*Introduced:* [5.4](lessons/05-04-sirach-and-the-wisdom-of-solomon.md); used at [6.2](lessons/06-02-tobit-judith-baruch-and-the-maccabees.md)

## Canon and deuterocanon

**Use came before lists, and lists before definitions.** Keep apart a collection in use, a count
with a reason, and a closed list backed by authority.

### Deuterocanonical books

**Seven books, plus the Greek parts of Esther and Daniel, in the Catholic Old Testament and not in
the Jewish canon.** Tobit, Judith, Wisdom, Sirach, Baruch (with the Letter of Jeremiah as its chapter
6), 1-2 Maccabees; Greek Esther (DR 10:4-16:24) and Greek Daniel (the Prayer of Azariah and Song of
the Three, DR 3:24-90; Susanna, DR 13; Bel and the Dragon, DR 14). The terms protocanonical /
deuterocanonical are usually credited to Sixtus of Siena's *Bibliotheca Sancta* (1566). They record
when acceptance was settled, **not rank**. Protestants call the seven "the Apocrypha".

| Book | Genre | Original language (what survives) | Date of writing |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tobit | edifying family tale | Aramaic or Hebrew (Qumran 4Q196-199 Aramaic, 4Q200 Hebrew, published by Fitzmyer 1995; Greek, two forms) | 3rd-early 2nd c., reconstructed |
| Judith | novella | probably Hebrew (Greek) | late 2nd c., reconstructed |
| Baruch 1-5 | penitential prayer, wisdom poem, consolation | disputed (Greek) | disputed, most often 2nd c. |
| Letter of Jeremiah (DR Bar 6) | satire on idols | disputed (Greek; 7Q2) | disputed |
| Greek Esther | six additions: Mordecai's dream, two edicts, prayers, the audience, the dream interpreted | mostly Greek | before 114 or 78/77 (colophon disputed) |
| 1 Maccabees | dynastic history, 175-c. 134 | Hebrew (Greek) | c. 100, reconstructed |
| 2 Maccabees | theological history, epitome of Jason of Cyrene's five books (2:24) | Greek | late 2nd c., reconstructed |
| Sirach; Wisdom | wisdom | see [canonicity vs authorship](#canonicity-vs-authorship) | |

**Their reception:** copied at Qumran (Tobit, Sirach); excluded by Josephus's prophetic cut-off and
the Tosefta; read but not canonical for Athanasius and Jerome; canonical for Augustine, Hippo and
Carthage, and Florence; defined at Trent (1546). **What they are cited for:** creation out of
nothing (2 Macc 7:28; CCC 297), resurrection (7:9, 14; CCC 992), prayer for the dead (12:46; CCC
1032): each doctrine defined on its own grounds and owned by
[`creation-grace-last-things`](../creation-grace-last-things/syllabus.md). Whether 7:28 already
means absolute nothing is disputed (Jonathan Goldstein's commentary; Gerhard May, 1978, dates the
technical formula to second-century Christians). Challoner calling 2 Macc 12 "evident and undeniable
proof" of purgatory is a translator's comment. Baruch 3:38 was read by the Fathers of the
Incarnation (Augustine, *City of God* XVIII.33, giving it to Jeremiah); in context the subject is
wisdom. Judith's opening anachronisms are read by most modern exegetes as genre signals
([literary forms](#literary-forms)).

**Status:** canonical with all their parts, defined (Trent IV); the label records a history of
dispute. Which Tobit (Vulgate, short or long Greek) its author wrote is textual criticism.

*Introduced:* [1.1](lessons/01-01-what-the-old-testament-is.md); used at [5.4](lessons/05-04-sirach-and-the-wisdom-of-solomon.md), [6.1](lessons/06-01-how-the-old-testament-canon-formed.md), [6.2](lessons/06-02-tobit-judith-baruch-and-the-maccabees.md), [6.3](lessons/06-03-daniel-and-apocalyptic.md)

### The twenty-two books

**The earliest explicit Jewish count, and a number the Fathers reused with different contents.**
Josephus, *Against Apion* I.8 (90s AD, Whiston): five books of Moses, thirteen of prophets "from the
death of Moses till the reign of Artaxerxes", four of hymns and precepts; later history "hath not
been esteemed of the like authority" for lack of "an exact succession of prophets". An apologetic
boast against the Greeks as well as a count; Josephus names no book after Moses, so the contents of
his 13 and 4 are reconstructed. **The rabbinic 24** (*Bava Batra* 14b) counts the same Hebrew books
differently; the Tosefta (*Yadayim* 2:13, numbered 2:5 on Sefaria) excludes "the books of Ben Sira
and all books written from then on" from defiling the hands. **Twenty-two = the Hebrew alphabet**
(Origen, Athanasius, Jerome), but the lists differ:

| Witness | Count | Esther | Deuterocanonical books |
|---|---|---|---|
| Josephus (90s AD) | 22 | probably (reconstructed) | none counted |
| Melito (c. 170; Eusebius IV.26) | none stated | absent | none ("Wisdom" there is Proverbs) |
| Origen (Eusebius VI.25) | 22 | in | Letter of Jeremiah inside Jeremiah; Maccabees "besides these" |
| Athanasius, Festal Letter 39 (367) | 22 | read, not canon | Baruch and Letter inside Jeremiah; Wisdom, Sirach, Judith, Tobit read by converts, not canon |

accurately. **Trap:** a book can sit inside another book (Baruch within Jeremiah), so the count alone
never tells you who accepted what.

### The Council of Jamnia myth

**There was no council, no list, and nothing about Greek books.** Heinrich Graetz (1871) proposed
that the rabbis at Yavneh (Jamnia) after AD 70 closed the canon; textbooks turned the proposal into a
"council of AD 90". Jack P. Lewis ("What Do We Mean by Jabneh?", *Journal of Bible and Religion*,
1964) showed how little the sources support it; David Aune (*JBL*, 1991) traced how the myth arose.
What the Mishnah records (*Yadayim* 3:5) is a debate whether the Song and Ecclesiastes "defile the
hands", settled, Ben Azzai says, "on the day" Rabbi Eleazar ben Azariah became head of the academy,
with Rabbi Akiva protesting that nobody ever doubted the Song. When the Jewish canon closed is
disputed; the Yavneh debates (c. 70-130) are reconstructed. **Level error:** "the Church teaches the
Jews closed their canon at Jamnia and so lost the right to define it": no document says it.

*Introduced:* [6.1](lessons/06-01-how-the-old-testament-canon-formed.md)

### Jerome and Augustine on the canon

**Two Fathers of the 390s, reading the same fact (the Church reads these books) toward different
conclusions.** Each position, described separately (setting them side by side with their crux is
Boss problem 6(a)-(b)):

- **Jerome.** His *Hebraica veritas* led to the "helmeted preface" to Kings (c. 391), which gives the twenty-two Hebrew books and sends everything else "amongst the Apocryphal writings" ([`patristics` 4.2](../patristics/lessons/04-02-jerome.md) reads it). His preface to the books of Solomon (c. 393) says the Church reads Judith, Tobit and Maccabees but does not admit them as canonical, and that Wisdom and Sirach may be read "for the edification of the people, not to give authority to doctrines of the Church". He translated Tobit and Judith anyway, and obelized Greek Daniel's additions while translating them. He did not translate Baruch, Wisdom or Sirach.
- **Augustine.** *De Doctrina Christiana* 2.8 (NPNF 1st ser. vol. 2) lets the "greater number of catholic churches" decide, and counts forty-four books including Tobit, Judith, the Maccabees, Wisdom and Sirach. He was present at the African councils.

**Status:** a Father's opinion is not the Church's definition; after Trent, Judith's canonicity is
defined, and Jerome's historical doubt stands untouched beside it.

*Introduced:* [6.1](lessons/06-01-how-the-old-testament-canon-formed.md)

### The African councils

**Regional synods that adopted a list: witnesses, not definitions.** Hippo (393) and Carthage (397),
with Augustine present. The list survives as canon 24 of the African code (419; NPNF 2nd ser. vol.
14): "That nothing be read in church besides the Canonical Scripture", and between Genesis and
Maccabees it names Tobit, Judith, "the five books of Solomon" (Wisdom and Sirach included) and 1-2
Maccabees, closing with a request that Boniface and the other bishops confirm it, "for these are the
things which we have received from our fathers to be read in church". Baruch is not named (most read
it under Jeremiah). For the African bishops, what is received to be read in church *is* the canon.
Dates secure.

*Introduced:* [6.1](lessons/06-01-how-the-old-testament-canon-formed.md)

### Luther's Apocrypha

**Separated, not deleted.** Luther's complete Bible of 1534 gathered Tobit, Judith, Wisdom, Sirach,
Baruch and 1-2 Maccabees, with the Greek parts of Esther and Daniel and the Prayer of Manasseh, into
a section between the Testaments, headed (the lesson's translation of the German) "Apocrypha: that
is, books which are not held equal to Holy Scripture, and yet are useful and good to read". He left
out 3-4 Esdras. That is Jerome's two-tier view, printed. Dropping the section from Protestant Bibles
was a later printers' practice. **Before him:** the Council of Florence's decree of union with the
Copts (session 11, 4 February 1442) professed one God as author of both Testaments and listed all
seven by name (teaching of an ecumenical council, without anathema). **After him:** Trent defined the
list (8 April 1546; [`fundamental-theology` 3.3](../fundamental-theology/lessons/03-03-the-canon-as-defined.md)).
The Protestant case is argued in [`apologetics-catholic-claims`](../apologetics-catholic-claims/syllabus.md).

*Introduced:* [6.1](lessons/06-01-how-the-old-testament-canon-formed.md)

### The Maccabean crisis

**The secure dates behind 1-2 Maccabees and Daniel's visions.** Antiochus IV Epiphanes accedes in
175 (Seleucid year 137). He sets "the abominable idol of desolation upon the altar" on 15 Casleu,
year 145 (1 Macc 1:54 [DR 1:57]): the Seleucid era counts from 312/311, so

$$312 - 145 = 167 \text{ BC}.$$

Judas rededicates the altar on 25 Casleu, year 148 = 164 BC (1 Macc 4:52), and decrees an
eight-day feast (4:59): Hanukkah. Antiochus dies in Persia late in 164 (year 149; 1 Macc 6).
Around them: 170/169 the first Egyptian campaign and the Temple treasury plundered (1 Macc 1:17-24
[DR 1:18-25]); 168 Rome's envoy Popilius Laenas orders him out of Egypt. **Three sides of
Hanukkah:** 1 Maccabees' dedication; Josephus, "they call it Lights" (*Antiquities* XII.7.7); John
10:22's "feast of the dedication"; and the Talmud's own tradition of the cruse of oil that burned
eight days (b. *Shabbat* 21b), not a gloss on 1 Maccabees, which is outside the Jewish canon. **The
books:** 1 Maccabees (Jerome found it "Hebrew"; Greek survives; c. 100) runs 175 to Simon's murder
(c. 134); 2 Maccabees (Greek, c. 180-161; prefixed letter dated 124) gives the martyrs (6-7) and
Judas's sin-offering for the dead (12:38-46; Greek two thousand drachmas, DR "twelve thousand").

*Introduced:* [6.2](lessons/06-02-tobit-judith-baruch-and-the-maccabees.md); used at [6.3](lessons/06-03-daniel-and-apocalyptic.md)

## Daniel and apocalyptic

**Daniel is two books sewn together, with seams running two ways.** By genre: court tales (1-6,
third person) and visions (7-12, first person). By language: Hebrew, then Aramaic from 2:4b (the
Chaldeans answer "in Syriac") to 7:28, then Hebrew from 8:1, so chapter 7 is an Aramaic vision. The
Greek additions (DR 3:24-90, 13, 14) Jerome translated because they were read everywhere, marking
them with an obelus: in Hebrew Daniel "contains neither the history of Susanna, nor the hymn of the
three youths, nor the fables of Bel and the Dragon" (Preface to Daniel, NPNF 2/6). In the Hebrew
canon Daniel sits among the Writings. **Daniel 7 read literally** has three live readings of the "one
like the Son of man" (*kebar enash*): the faithful people personified, an angelic leader (Michael;
Collins), an individual messianic king, with Rashi on 7:13, "he is the King Messiah" (lesson's
translation), and b. *Sanhedrin* 98a's clouds-or-donkey pairing with Zech 9:9. The verse each leans
on is Boss problem 6(c); Jesus answers at his trial with this verse (Mark 14:62). **Daniel 12:2** is
the first unambiguous statement of bodily resurrection to judgment in the Hebrew Bible (Isa 26:19
the nearest earlier text), tied to the martyrs of 11:33-35 (compare 2 Macc 7:9); resurrection is
defined dogma resting on the creeds, Dan 12:2 a text it leans on.

### Apocalyptic

**Revelation through an otherworldly mediator, disclosing a hidden world and a coming end.** John J.
Collins's definition (*Semeia* 14, 1979), paraphrased: a revelation, set in a narrative frame,
given to a human seer by an otherworldly being such as an angel, disclosing a transcendent reality
that is temporal (a final salvation) and spatial (a supernatural world behind this one). Daniel 7-12
is the first full Old Testament instance; *1 Enoch* is its neighbour at Qumran; Revelation is
[`new-testament` 6.5](../new-testament/lessons/06-05-revelation.md). The numbers: "a time, and times,
and half a time" (7:25; 12:7); 1,290 days (12:11) then 1,335 (12:12), read by critics as a
recalculation during the crisis and by Challoner of the Antichrist; they fit Antiochus's persecution
only roughly, so their weight is disputed.

*Introduced:* [6.3](lessons/06-03-daniel-and-apocalyptic.md)

### The date of Daniel

**Traditional sixth century vs the critical majority's 167-164 for the visions: a historian's
question, not the Church's.**

- **The setting the book claims:** Daniel deported "in the third year of the reign of Joakim" (1:1, c. 606/605), serving "even to the first year of king Cyrus" (1:21), last vision in "the third year of Cyrus" (10:1).
- **Porphyry** (third century AD) gave the twelfth book of *Against the Christians* to Daniel; Jerome's *Commentary on Daniel* (407) preserves his argument, in paraphrase: written in Judea under Antiochus IV, narrating the past rather than foretelling, accurate up to Antiochus and false beyond.
- **The modern case:** Daniel 11 sharpens toward 167 and then misses (below); Belshazzar was Nabonidus's son and never king in name; "Darius, the Mede" (5:31) is unknown outside the book; Greek instrument names in 3:5; Ben Sira's list of prophets (Sir 49 [DR Ecclus 49:9-12]) has no Daniel; the book sits among the Writings.
- **The traditional reply:** from 11:21 the passage looks past Antiochus to the Antichrist, "of whom this Antiochus was a figure" (Challoner, after Jerome); the tales were known to the Maccabees (1 Macc 2:59-60); Josephus has Alexander shown the book (*Antiquities* XI.8.5); Jesus cites "Daniel the prophet" (Matt 24:15). Critics answer: these show reception, not composition; many hold the court tales older than the visions.

**Status:** canonicity and inspiration of Daniel with all its parts defined (Trent IV); date and author
scholarly: Maccabean visions the critical majority, sixth century a live minority; no Commission

date fits the doctrine of inspiration is Boss problem 6(d).

*Introduced:* [6.3](lessons/06-03-daniel-and-apocalyptic.md)

### Vaticinium ex eventu

**"Prophecy from the outcome": history written in the form of prediction.** Detected where precision
stops: the text is exact up to the point a writer of 165 would stop knowing, then vague or wrong
(Dan 11:40-45). Keep the two arguments of [1.3](lessons/01-03-the-historical-critical-method-and-its-limits.md) apart. **Form A:** prediction is impossible, so the
text is late: Porphyry's premise and some moderns', excluded for a Catholic by *Providentissimus Deus*
17 (assuming prophecy impossible in advance). **Form B:** the text's own evidence dates it, with no
premise about miracles. Daniel's strongest late-date argument is Form B. "Critics date Daniel late
because they deny prophecy" misreports the argument actually made.

*Introduced:* [6.3](lessons/06-03-daniel-and-apocalyptic.md); the Form A / Form B distinction at [1.3](lessons/01-03-the-historical-critical-method-and-its-limits.md)

## Readings in Jewish tradition

### Jewish readings

**Stated as their own tradition states them, graded for fidelity, never set up to be
refuted.** Rashi (1040-1105), the Targums, the Talmud and midrash, Maimonides and modern Jewish
scholars are a tradition in their own right, not Christian readings gone wrong. Arguing Christian
against Jewish readings belongs to [`apologetics-foundations`](../apologetics-foundations/syllabus.md).
The 2001 Commission document calls the Jewish reading "a possible one" and the two readings
"irreducible" (22): an advisory body's judgment, not the Magisterium's, and less than "valid";
*Nostra Aetate* 4 (conciliar) forbids presenting the Jews as rejected or accursed as if Scripture
taught it.

## Pitfalls

### Level errors: promoting a hypothesis

- "The Church teaches Moses wrote the Pentateuch": Trent's "five books of Moses" is a title; 1906 is not dogma. *([2.2](lessons/02-02-who-wrote-the-pentateuch.md))*
- "Scholars have proved JEDP" / "two names means two authors": one model among several; the name criterion is the weakest. *([2.2](lessons/02-02-who-wrote-the-pentateuch.md))*
- "Two sources in Genesis 1-2 is Church teaching", or "forbidden": a hypothesis a Catholic may hold or reject. *([2.1](lessons/02-01-two-accounts-of-creation.md))*
- "The Church teaches the Masoretic Text is the original" / "the MT is the original Jeremiah": the book is defined, the edition is text criticism. *([1.1](lessons/01-01-what-the-old-testament-is.md), [4.4](lessons/04-04-jeremiah-and-ezekiel.md))*
- "The Church teaches Abraham lived around 1850 BC" / "the exodus happened in 1446 with two million people" / "the conquest happened as Joshua describes": no act dates or sizes any of them. *([2.4](lessons/02-04-the-patriarchs.md), [2.5](lessons/02-05-exodus-and-sinai.md), [3.1](lessons/03-01-joshua-judges-and-the-land.md))*
- "Catholics must hold David's empire historical because the Commission said so". *([3.2](lessons/03-02-david-and-the-house-of-david.md))*
- "Catholics must hold Second Isaiah" and "Catholics must hold Isaiah wrote all sixty-six chapters": both assign a composition verdict a rung it lacks. *([1.3](lessons/01-03-the-historical-critical-method-and-its-limits.md), [4.2](lessons/04-02-isaiah-and-immanuel.md))*
- "The Church teaches Isaiah 53 was written about Jesus and foresaw him": fulfilment is teaching; conscious foresight is one position. *([4.3](lessons/04-03-the-servant-of-the-lord.md))*
- "The Church teaches David wrote the Psalms" / "Solomon wrote Proverbs, Ecclesiastes, the Song, Wisdom": 1910 said *principal* author; the books are defined, not their pens. *([5.1](lessons/05-01-the-psalms.md), [5.2](lessons/05-02-proverbs-ecclesiastes-and-the-song-of-songs.md), [5.4](lessons/05-04-sirach-and-the-wisdom-of-solomon.md))*
- "The Church teaches Jonah is a parable" / "Judith is history" / "Daniel wrote in the sixth century": no act teaches any of these. *([4.5](lessons/04-05-the-book-of-the-twelve.md), [6.2](lessons/06-02-tobit-judith-baruch-and-the-maccabees.md), [6.3](lessons/06-03-daniel-and-apocalyptic.md))*
- "The Church teaches the Pentateuch was finalized under Persian authority" (Frei). *([3.4](lessons/03-04-exile-and-return.md))*
- "The moral/ceremonial/judicial division is dogma": common theological teaching. "Trent defined that only the Ten Commandments bind": canon 19 defines that they *do* bind. *([2.6](lessons/02-06-covenant-and-law.md))*
- "The four senses are defined dogma": a tradition the Catechism reports. *([1.2](lessons/01-02-the-senses-of-scripture.md))*
- "Dei Verbum endorses Childs": the Council predates his programme and names no method. *([1.4](lessons/01-04-canonical-reading-and-the-unity-of-the-testaments.md))*
- "The Church teaches the Jews closed their canon at Jamnia": the council is a myth. *([6.1](lessons/06-01-how-the-old-testament-canon-formed.md))*
- "The Church teaches the Jewish reading is valid": the 2001 document said "possible", and it is consultative. *([6.4](lessons/06-04-the-old-testament-in-light-of-the-new.md))*

### Level errors: demoting teaching

- "Scholars call Tobit deuterocanonical, so its status is open" / "the Greek additions are less Scripture" / "Judith is fiction, so not Scripture": canonicity is defined; the label records history, genre decides what a book asserts. *([1.1](lessons/01-01-what-the-old-testament-is.md), [6.2](lessons/06-02-tobit-judith-baruch-and-the-maccabees.md), [6.3](lessons/06-03-daniel-and-apocalyptic.md))*
- "Since 33:14-26 may be late, it is not really Scripture" / "Elihu is a later addition, so Catholics may set him aside": the canon is the books with all their parts. *([4.4](lessons/04-04-jeremiah-and-ezekiel.md), [5.3](lessons/05-03-job.md))*
- "Not by Solomon, so not really Scripture" / "if Moses didn't write it, it isn't inspired". *([5.4](lessons/05-04-sirach-and-the-wisdom-of-solomon.md), [2.2](lessons/02-02-who-wrote-the-pentateuch.md))*
- "Jerome rejected Judith, so Catholics may too": a Father's opinion is not the Church's definition. *([6.1](lessons/06-01-how-the-old-testament-canon-formed.md))*
- "The spiritual sense is pious decoration": understates *Divino Afflante Spiritu*. *([1.2](lessons/01-02-the-senses-of-scripture.md))*
- "Scholars date the Psalms late, so a Catholic can drop the messianic reading": the New Testament's reading of Pss 2 and 110 is Scripture's own; its mode is open. *([5.1](lessons/05-01-the-psalms.md))*
- "The 2001 document isn't magisterial, so a Catholic may call Jews cursed readers": *Nostra Aetate* 4 is conciliar. *([6.4](lessons/06-04-the-old-testament-in-light-of-the-new.md))*

### Weighing documents wrongly

- The early Commission responses as still binding ("the 1909 decree requires six 24-hour days": Response VIII says the opposite; "the 1905 decree still requires Jonah to be history"; "the 1934 decree settles that all Israel was in Egypt"). *([2.1](lessons/02-01-two-accounts-of-creation.md), [4.5](lessons/04-05-the-book-of-the-twelve.md), [2.5](lessons/02-05-exodus-and-sinai.md))*
- The early responses as void ("1906 was repealed"; "dead letters, so Response III's faith-bearing facts do not matter"): clarified and relativized, never revoked; say which step changed their force. *([2.1](lessons/02-01-two-accounts-of-creation.md), [2.2](lessons/02-02-who-wrote-the-pentateuch.md), [3.3](lessons/03-03-two-kingdoms-and-two-falls.md))*
- The 1993 document, the 2001 document, or the Erasmus Lecture quoted as magisterium; Ratzinger's preface does not make the 2001 document papal. *([1.2](lessons/01-02-the-senses-of-scripture.md), [1.3](lessons/01-03-the-historical-critical-method-and-its-limits.md), [4.3](lessons/04-03-the-servant-of-the-lord.md))*
- Challoner's notes and headings treated as teaching ("evident and undeniable proof" of purgatory; Job 14's heading; the Song's headnotes). *([5.2](lessons/05-02-proverbs-ecclesiastes-and-the-song-of-songs.md), [5.3](lessons/05-03-job.md), [6.2](lessons/06-02-tobit-judith-baruch-and-the-maccabees.md))*
- "The Church condemned historical criticism": Leo condemned a named method with a stated philosophy and commended criticism in the same paragraph. *([1.3](lessons/01-03-the-historical-critical-method-and-its-limits.md))*
- "Limits" misread as "ignore the evidence": the limit is on premises that decide the verdict in advance (Form A), not on internal evidence (Form B). *([1.3](lessons/01-03-the-historical-critical-method-and-its-limits.md), [6.3](lessons/06-03-daniel-and-apocalyptic.md))*
- The 1971 omissions from the Hours read as rejecting psalms: liturgical law, not a canon ruling. *([5.1](lessons/05-01-the-psalms.md))*

### Misreading the text

- "Literal" as wooden: the literal sense is what the author meant, in his genre; a parable's literal sense is its point. *([1.2](lessons/01-02-the-senses-of-scripture.md))*
- Accommodation passed off as a spiritual sense; a doctrine argued from a spiritual reading alone. *([1.2](lessons/01-02-the-senses-of-scripture.md))*
- Flattening: letting the New Testament answer Qoheleth's 3:21 *inside* Ecclesiastes; "the real meaning of Hosea 11:1 is Christ"; Hosea 11:1 read as a forecast (its verb is past). *([1.4](lessons/01-04-canonical-reading-and-the-unity-of-the-testaments.md), [6.4](lessons/06-04-the-old-testament-in-light-of-the-new.md))*
- Harmonizing mistaken for canonical reading; canonical reading mistaken for replacing historical criticism. *([1.4](lessons/01-04-canonical-reading-and-the-unity-of-the-testaments.md))*
- "Genesis copied Gilgamesh / Babylon" / "Amenemope proves Proverbs is just Egyptian wisdom" / "Hammurabi proves Moses copied": resemblance is not literary dependence, and borrowing is no problem for inspiration. *([2.1](lessons/02-01-two-accounts-of-creation.md), [2.3](lessons/02-03-the-primeval-history.md), [2.6](lessons/02-06-covenant-and-law.md), [5.2](lessons/05-02-proverbs-ecclesiastes-and-the-song-of-songs.md))*
- "Archaeology has disproved the patriarchs / Joshua / David", or "Tel Dan proved the Bible": reconstructions and anachronisms date tellings, not cores. *([2.4](lessons/02-04-the-patriarchs.md), [3.1](lessons/03-01-joshua-judges-and-the-land.md), [3.2](lessons/03-02-david-and-the-house-of-david.md))*
- Reading Paul back into Gen 15:6; treating Gen 15 and 17 as one covenant told twice. *([2.4](lessons/02-04-the-patriarchs.md))*
- *Ehyeh* only as metaphysics, or only as presence; say which sense you mean. *([2.5](lessons/02-05-exodus-and-sinai.md))*
- "Eye for eye was primitive vengeance": in both codes it limits punishment. *([2.6](lessons/02-06-covenant-and-law.md))*
- Reading the ban as a model; treating Origen's typology as the literal sense. *([3.1](lessons/03-01-joshua-judges-and-the-land.md))*
- "Church teaching requires Kings to be complete": inerrancy concerns what is asserted, not what is omitted. *([3.3](lessons/03-03-two-kingdoms-and-two-falls.md))*
- "Ezra 10 is inspired, so mixed marriages must be dissolved": inspiration guarantees assertions, not every narrated act; the canon also holds Ruth. *([3.4](lessons/03-04-exile-and-return.md))*
- "The Cyrus Cylinder is the decree of Ezra 1" / "a human-rights charter". *([3.4](lessons/03-04-exile-and-return.md))*
- "Prophets were fortune-tellers" (or, overcorrecting, prediction is unreal); "mercy" as pity instead of *hesed*. *([4.1](lessons/04-01-what-a-prophet-is-amos-and-hosea.md))*
- "*Almah* means virgin" or "means not a virgin": it leaves virginity open; the virginal conception does not depend on the word. *([4.2](lessons/04-02-isaiah-and-immanuel.md))*
- "Jeremiah was against the Temple"; "Ezekiel 18 invented individualism"; "the new covenant replaces the law" (it is "my law" written within). *([4.4](lessons/04-04-jeremiah-and-ezekiel.md))*
- "Micah names Jesus's birthplace word for word": Matthew's wording differs; fulfilment is not dictation. *([4.5](lessons/04-05-the-book-of-the-twelve.md))*
- Qoheleth as nihilist or as a hidden preacher of heaven; allegory set against the Song's literal sense. *([5.2](lessons/05-02-proverbs-ecclesiastes-and-the-song-of-songs.md))*
- "The Bible teaches suffering is punishment (Job 4:7)": that is Eliphaz, whom 42:7 rebukes. "Job 19:25 proves Israel believed in resurrection": the resurrection wording is the Latin's. *([5.3](lessons/05-03-job.md))*
- "Wisdom 7 teaches the Trinity"; the Douay's Ecclesiasticus treated as Ben Sira's text. *([5.4](lessons/05-04-sirach-and-the-wisdom-of-solomon.md))*
- "2 Maccabees proves purgatory"; a later dogma read back into 7:28. *([6.2](lessons/06-02-tobit-judith-baruch-and-the-maccabees.md))*
- Flattening Daniel 7 to one reading. *([6.3](lessons/06-03-daniel-and-apocalyptic.md))*

### Jewish readings and history of the canon

- "The Israel reading of the Servant is a medieval invention": Origen met it c. 248, and the Septuagint names Israel at 42:1. *([4.3](lessons/04-03-the-servant-of-the-lord.md))*
- "The Jewish reading of Isa 7:14 was invented to answer Christians": Trypho's Hezekiah reading is second-century; Rashi rejects Hezekiah himself. *([4.2](lessons/04-02-isaiah-and-immanuel.md))*
- "The *akedah* is a failed Christian reading." *([1.2](lessons/01-02-the-senses-of-scripture.md))*
- "Protestants removed seven books" / "Luther removed seven books": the 39 match the Jewish 24; Luther printed the seven apart. *([1.1](lessons/01-01-what-the-old-testament-is.md), [6.1](lessons/06-01-how-the-old-testament-canon-formed.md))*
- "The Septuagint was the Alexandrian Jewish canon": a translation tradition, not a list. *([6.1](lessons/06-01-how-the-old-testament-canon-formed.md))*
- "The Talmud quotes Ben Sira, so he was once canonical": quotation is not canonization. *([5.4](lessons/05-04-sirach-and-the-wisdom-of-solomon.md))*
- "Lamentations is by Jeremiah" / "De Wette discovered the book was Deuteronomy": the first is tradition (the Hebrew is anonymous); the identification is patristic, de Wette's claim was about when it was *written*. *([3.3](lessons/03-03-two-kingdoms-and-two-falls.md))*

### Dates and numbers

- 722, 701, 597, 587/586, 539, 175, 167, 164 are secure; the division (c. 930), Josiah's 622, Merneptah's c. 1208 are reconstructed; the patriarchs, exodus, Joel, Jonah, Job, Daniel's visions, Ezra's 458/398 are disputed. *([3.3](lessons/03-03-two-kingdoms-and-two-falls.md), [3.4](lessons/03-04-exile-and-return.md), [6.2](lessons/06-02-tobit-judith-baruch-and-the-maccabees.md))*
- "Prophecy ended in 400 BC": a Second Temple claim about canonical prophecy, not a date. *([4.5](lessons/04-05-the-book-of-the-twelve.md))*
- "The exile emptied the land": Jeremiah's own figures (4,600 in three deportations) tell against it. *([3.4](lessons/03-04-exile-and-return.md))*
- Psalm numbers unconverted ("Psalm 50" is the Hebrew 51); the Douay's divine names used to sort sources (Gen 7:9); the Douay's Ecclesiasticus verse numbers taken as the Greek's. *([5.1](lessons/05-01-the-psalms.md), [2.3](lessons/02-03-the-primeval-history.md), [5.4](lessons/05-04-sirach-and-the-wisdom-of-solomon.md))*

## Conventions

- **One card per course**, covering every lesson. The linter checks that every lesson file is cited somewhere on this card.
- **Intuition first**, as in the lessons: a plain-English line before the Hebrew or the document.
- **Headings are anchors.** Renaming a `###` breaks inbound lesson links. Headings are ASCII only: no parentheses, dashes, Hebrew, Greek or diacritics (*go'el* is "Goel"; *ehyeh asher ehyeh* is "Ehyeh asher ehyeh"). Two headings differ only by "The": [The Deuteronomistic History](#the-deuteronomistic-history) (the models) and [Deuteronomistic History](#deuteronomistic-history) (Kings' grading); both are linked from lessons.
- **No prose dollar signs** (see CLAUDE.md); the only math is Seleucid date arithmetic.
- **Boss problems are reserved.** Entries touching boss material stay at outline level: Genesis 22's rival literal readings and the *akedah* are left to 1.2 (Boss 1); Gen 1:24-27 and 2:18-22 are not compared, Ex 21 and Deut 15 are not tabulated, and the composition models are described without a verdict (Boss 2); 2 Samuel 7, 1 Chronicles 17 and Psalm 89 are not compared and the oracle's conditions are not analysed (Boss 3); the Servant's collective and individual identifications are listed by holder without their verses (Boss 4); Job 19:25-27's Hebrew and Latin are not set out (Boss 5); Jerome and Augustine are described separately without their crux, the readings of Dan 7:13 are named without their verses, and the Maccabean date is not weighed against inspiration (Boss 6).
- **Modern scholars and post-1930 documents are paraphrased**, cited by work and year and, for documents, section only where the lessons verified it. Quotations are public domain and short: Douay-Rheims (Challoner), Whiston's Josephus, NPNF and ANF, the English Dominican *Summa*, *Providentissimus Deus*, Johns's Hammurabi, Budge's *Enuma Elish*, King's Mesha stele.
- **No Denzinger numbers.** A DH number points into one edition of one handbook; a wrong one looks authoritative and cannot be caught by anyone who trusts it.
- Length is not capped the way a lesson's is: this is a lookup surface, not a read-through.
