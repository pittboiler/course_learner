# Apologetics I: God, Christ & the Religions — Syllabus

> Catholic Theology · Tier 2 · ~31 lessons · Prereqs: [`philosophy-of-religion`](../philosophy-of-religion/syllabus.md), [`new-testament`](../new-testament/syllabus.md) · Roadmap id: `apologetics-foundations`

## Goal

This course openly argues for the foundations of Catholic Christianity against the live non-Christian alternatives, and it is built so that the argument can lose. You will finish able to assemble a cumulative case for God from arguments [`philosophy-of-religion`](../philosophy-of-religion/syllabus.md) has already assessed, and answer the strongest atheist replies — Mackie, Oppy, Rowe's evidential argument from evil, Schellenberg on hiddenness — in the form their authors give them; to press naturalism on its own burdens (reason, consciousness, morality) while conceding where the naturalist answers are good; to say exactly what evolution, cosmology and quantum mechanics do and do not settle, and to correct the popular myths on the Catholic side as readily as on the secular one; to build and criticize a historical case for the resurrection of Jesus, with Ehrman, Lüdemann, Crossan and Dale Allison at full strength; to argue the Christian messianic claim while stating the Jewish reading of the contested texts as a Jewish reader would recognize it, and to say what the Church now teaches about the Jewish people and at what weight; to state the Islamic case — tawhid, prophethood, the Qur'an — as Muslim theologians state it, answer the charge that Trinitarian belief is tritheism, and handle the crucifixion (Qur'an 4:157) and the corruption charge (*tahrif*) with the textual evidence on both sides; and to place Catholic teaching on salvation outside the visible Church against Hick's pluralism without softening either the teaching or its history.

The discipline that keeps an argued course honest: **every opposing view is stated as its own best defenders state it, and in every apologetic problem that statement is graded first, strictly, before any reply is read.** Concessions are written into the syllabus, not left to the student's charity. Judaism and Islam are treated as living traditions with serious thinkers, not as positions to be knocked over.

It deliberately skips the assessment of the theistic arguments themselves ([`philosophy-of-religion`](../philosophy-of-religion/syllabus.md) owns every one of them, and this course cites its skeletons rather than rebuilding them), every intra-Christian dispute — papal primacy, *sola scriptura*, justification, the Marian dogmas, the Orthodox objections — which belongs to `apologetics-catholic-claims`, the resurrection and the Incarnation as doctrine ([`christology`](../christology/syllabus.md)), the Trinity as doctrine ([`trinity-and-god`](../trinity-and-god/syllabus.md)), the history of Christian–Jewish and Christian–Muslim relations as events ([`church-history`](../church-history/syllabus.md)), comparative religion as a descriptive discipline, and Hinduism, Buddhism and the new religious movements, which get a paragraph in 8.1 and no lesson.

## How practice works in this course

Problems follow [HUMANITIES-BUILD-BRIEF.md](../../HUMANITIES-BUILD-BRIEF.md), and this is one of the two courses in the library where the **apologetic** kind is the dominant one. The rule is mechanical and strict:

- **Every apologetic problem has two parts, and the first is exegetical.** Part (a) asks for the opposing view — Oppy's parity claim, Rowe's noseeum inference, Ehrman's methodological objection, Rashi on the Servant, the *shirk* charge against Trinitarian belief, Hick's hypothesis — stated as its own best defenders state it. It is graded **strictly, as exegesis**, against what that thinker or tradition actually holds, and a wrong statement fails the problem no matter how good the reply that follows. Caricature is the one error this course marks hardest.
- **Part (b) is the reply, graded on its quality, not on its conclusion.** A reply that concludes the objection succeeds passes if it is a good reply. Several problems require a **named concession**: the one thing the objection gets right, stated without qualification before anything is said against it.
- **Exegetical** parts are also strict for doctrinal weight. Saying that Nostra Aetate defined something, or that *Humani Generis* endorsed evolution, or that Dominus Iesus made a new definition, is an error of the same kind as misquoting Rowe. Levels come from [`fundamental-theology`](../fundamental-theology/syllabus.md) 4.4–4.5.
- **Evaluative** parts, verdict-neutral, are kept for questions genuinely open: how much weight a cumulative case can bear, whether the empty tomb is well attested, whether *Dei Filius* commits a Catholic to the soundness of any particular proof, how far the development from Florence's *Cantate Domino* to Lumen Gentium 16 can be called development. Any verdict passes if the moves are made.
- **Formal** parts are odds-form work: the likelihood ratio a piece of historical testimony carries, what a prior for a miracle report can be, and where a cumulative case multiplies and where it does not.

**Texts.** Scripture in the Douay–Rheims (Challoner), with the lesson's own translation of the Greek or Hebrew where the word decides the reading. Public-domain sources for close reading: Justin's *Dialogue with Trypho* and the *First Apology* in the *Ante-Nicene Fathers*; Newman's *Grammar of Assent* (1870) and *Apologia*; Hume, Paley, Clifford; Josephus in Whiston; Tacitus; Vatican I in Schaff's *Creeds of Christendom*; Maimonides's *Guide* in Friedländer. **The Qur'an is never quoted from a modern translation:** it is cited by surah and ayah and paraphrased, with the Arabic term given where the term is the point (*tawhid*, *shirk*, *tahrif*, *i'jaz*). Modern authors on every side — Oppy, Mackie, Plantinga, Swinburne, Habermas, Licona, Wright, Allison, Ehrman, Lüdemann, Crossan, Levenson, Wyschogrod, Ayoub, Nasr, Hick — are paraphrased and cited by work.

**Accuracy discipline.** No invented quotations, ever, on any side. No statistic, council canon, Denzinger number or document section that has not been checked; where a number is uncertain the document is named without one. Scholarly positions are attributed only where the attribution is certain, and "most scholars hold" is written only where that report is accurate and is always marked as a report, not a proof.

## Dangerous Checklist

When you finish, you can:

- [ ] Say what apologetics is for on the Catholic account — motives of credibility, preambles of faith, an argument that can make faith reasonable but cannot produce it — and name the three apologetic strategies and what each assumes
- [ ] Say who carries the burden of proof in a given exchange, build a cumulative case whose parts converge rather than chain, and say what it would take for the case to fail
- [ ] State Mackie's, Oppy's, Rowe's and Schellenberg's positions as they state them, and give the best Catholic reply to each while naming what each gets right
- [ ] Press naturalism on reason, consciousness and morality, with Plantinga's evolutionary argument and the argument from reason reconstructed exactly, and the naturalist replies (teleosemantics, illusionism, godless non-naturalism, Fitelson and Sober) given at full strength
- [ ] Say precisely what the Church has and has not taught about evolution, mark the weight of each text, and explain why the Thomist account of secondary causes makes a design inference unnecessary rather than merely optional
- [ ] Test a popular claim about science and religion against the record in both directions — the Galileo myth, and the claim that Big Bang cosmology proves creation
- [ ] Build the historical case for the resurrection from the minimal-facts approach and from the fuller evidential case, and state the strongest objection to each, including Dale Allison's objections from inside the field
- [ ] Run hallucination, legend and other rival hypotheses on the same evidence, say what each explains well, and put the comparison in odds form without hiding the prior
- [ ] Argue the Christian reading of Isaiah 53, Daniel 7 and Psalm 110 while stating the Jewish reading as a Jewish reader would recognize it, and name the criteria Maimonides gives for a messianic claimant
- [ ] State what Nostra Aetate and later Church texts teach about the Jewish people, at what level, what supersessionism means in each of its senses, and what the historical record requires a Catholic to concede
- [ ] Answer the charge that Trinitarian belief is *shirk*, stating *tawhid* as a Muslim theologian states it and the doctrine of the Trinity accurately enough that the tritheism charge misses
- [ ] Lay out the interpretations of Qur'an 4:157 on the crucifixion, weigh the *tahrif* charge against the textual evidence for both scriptures, and state the Muslim case for the prophethood of Muhammad before replying to it
- [ ] Place exclusivism, inclusivism and pluralism accurately, state Hick's hypothesis at full strength, and say what Lumen Gentium 16 and Dominus Iesus teach and at what weight
- [ ] Explain how *extra ecclesiam nulla salus* is held together with Lumen Gentium 16, and say honestly what makes that the hardest development in the course

## Modules

### Module 1: Giving a reason for the hope

What apologetics is, what it cannot do, how the three main strategies differ, and the rules — logical and ethical — that an argued course has to keep.

|---|---|---|---|
| 1.1 | What apologetics is, and what it isn't | Place apologetics inside the Catholic account of faith, and say what an argument can and cannot accomplish | 1 Peter 3:15–16 and the two halves of the verse ("with meekness and fear"); *apologia* in its ancient legal sense; apologetics vs evangelization vs theology; motives of credibility and preambles of faith ([`fundamental-theology`](../fundamental-theology/syllabus.md) 2.2), so that faith is neither blind nor a conclusion of reason ([`fundamental-theology`](../fundamental-theology/syllabus.md) 2.1, 2.3); grace and assent, and why no argument compels faith; Dignitatis Humanae on immunity from coercion and the duty to seek truth, with its level marked; the ethics of persuasion — no caricature, no invented quotation, no inflated number, no winning by embarrassing someone |

**Boss problem 1:** (a) Close-read 1 Peter 3:15–16 in the Douay–Rheims. State what the verse asks for, to whom it is addressed, and which words in verse 16 constrain *how* the reason is to be given; say what a reader who stops at "a reason of that hope" misses. *(Exegetical.)* (b) Three invented paragraphs are supplied: an apologetics blog post, a campus debate opening, and a parish bulletin note. For each, identify which of the three strategies of 1.2 it is using and which premise it needs but has not stated. *(Exegetical, archetype 4.)* (c) A skeptic says: "You are the one making the extraordinary claim, so the burden is entirely yours; until you discharge it the rational default is atheism." State Flew's version of this at its strongest, then reply, saying exactly what presumption you accept and what you deny. *(Apologetic: (a) of this part is the statement of Flew, graded strictly.)* (d) In 300–500 words, any verdict: can a cumulative case deliver more confidence than its strongest single argument, and if so under what condition? Use the independence condition from 1.3, and say what your answer implies for an apologist who includes an argument he privately thinks is weak. *(Evaluative.)*

### Module 2: The case for God, and the strongest replies

[`philosophy-of-religion`](../philosophy-of-religion/syllabus.md) checked the arguments one premise at a time and took no side. This module does what it refused to do — adds them up — and then meets the philosophers who have added them up and come out the other way.

**Boss problem 2:** (a) State Graham Oppy's strategy for comparing theism with naturalism, in his terms: what he takes the competing theories to be, what he compares them on, and what conclusion he draws. ### Module 5: Jesus and the resurrection as history

The hinge of the course. A case is built, tested against the best skeptical scholarship, put in odds form, and then judged for what it actually delivers.

## Notes

- **Why this course argues, and how it is kept honest.** Every other course this one depends on is verdict-neutral by design. [`philosophy-of-religion`](../philosophy-of-religion/syllabus.md)'s Notes say plainly that its skeletons must be ones a leading atheist and a leading theist would both sign, precisely because this course builds on them. That is the load-bearing arrangement: the assessment happens there, the argument happens here, and if the argument ever needs a skeleton that course would not sign, the argument is bad. Three rules enforce it in the lessons: the opposing view is stated first and graded first; each module contains at least one named concession written into the syllabus; and no lesson's rubric ever requires the student to reach the Catholic conclusion.
- **Ownership collisions found and resolved.** Five courses on disk explicitly cede material here, and each cession is honoured as written. [`philosophy-of-religion`](../philosophy-of-religion/syllabus.md) cedes "the resurrection as history, the argued case against naturalism, Christianity in dialogue with Judaism and Islam" (Modules 3, 5–7) and keeps every argument's assessment. [`new-testament`](../new-testament/syllabus.md) cedes "the resurrection as a historical case" and "Christianity and Judaism in dialogue" while keeping the texts, the criteria and the synopsis (Module 5 argues, and does not re-teach). [`old-testament`](../old-testament/syllabus.md) cedes "messianic prophecy as apologetics" while keeping the side-by-side presentation of the readings (6.2–6.3 argue what its 4.2–4.3 and 6.3 present). [`church-history`](../church-history/syllabus.md) cedes "Islam as a religion and Christian–Muslim dialogue" and keeps the conquests and the Church under Muslim rule; its Bridges note sends Lateran IV and 1096 here, and 6.4 takes them as the concession. [`philosophy-of-physics`](../philosophy-of-physics/syllabus.md) cedes "divine action and quantum indeterminacy" in its Notes, having considered and declined a theology lesson; 4.4 is that lesson. [`patristics`](../patristics/syllabus.md) cedes "Christian–Jewish relations and dialogue" while reading Justin and Chrysostom as texts, so 6.1 uses the *Dialogue* as method and cites its 1.4 for the text. Two boss problems already on disk are deliberately not reused: [`church-history`](../church-history/syllabus.md) Boss 7 (Bellarmine and the Letter to the Grand Duchess Christina) and [`old-testament`](../old-testament/syllabus.md) Boss 4 and Boss 6 (Isaiah 52:13–53:12; Daniel 7:13–14). 4.1 uses the Galileo affair only as a test of the conflict thesis, and 6.2 argues from the contested texts rather than close-reading the same passages. No built lesson in the library covers any of this course's topics.
- **The line against `apologetics-catholic-claims`.** This course argues only what separates Christians from non-Christians. The moment a question is one Protestants or Orthodox also press — papal primacy, *sola scriptura*, justification, the Marian dogmas, the canon, the patristic evidence for Catholic distinctives — it belongs to that course, which takes `ecclesiology-and-mariology` and `sacraments-and-liturgy` as prerequisites for exactly that reason. 7.2 and 7.3 state Trinitarian and Christological doctrine only as far as answering a Muslim objection requires, and hand the doctrine itself to [`trinity-and-god`](../trinity-and-god/syllabus.md) and [`christology`](../christology/syllabus.md).
- **Respect is a rubric item, not a tone note.** In Modules 6 and 7 the fidelity of the statement of the other tradition's position is graded before the reply is read, and a caricature fails the problem outright. Judaism and Islam are presented as living traditions with living thinkers: 6.3 and 7.1 both require a contemporary voice, not only a medieval one. The historical record — Chrysostom, 1096, Lateran IV, the expulsions, the Shoah, the polemical literature against Muhammad — is stated before any argument is made, never after it as mitigation.
- **Copyright and citation.** The Qur'an is never quoted from a modern translation: it is cited by surah and ayah and paraphrased, with Arabic terms transliterated in the body and kept out of reference-card headings. Modern scholars are paraphrased. No quotation is ever attributed to a real person unless it is verified in a public-domain source or checked against the work; invented material is confined to archetype 4 (op-eds, talks, bulletin notes), as the brief requires.
- **Claims deliberately softened or left out.** Denzinger numbers and document section numbers are given only where verified, so several texts are named without one. *Humani Generis* and John Paul II's 1996 message are described by content and date, not by paragraph. The 1951 Pius XII address and Lemaître's recorded unease are presented as what historians report, with the historiographical uncertainty named. Claims about how many scholars accept a given resurrection "minimal fact" are given as reports with their source, never as a count, and 5.2 makes the unreliability of such counts an object of study rather than a premise.
- **Lesson count.** The roadmap estimated ~26; the syllabus runs 31 (+19%, inside the ±25% band). The extra lessons are in Modules 5 and 7, and neither could be compressed without breaking the course's own rule. The resurrection needs five because the methodological question, the minimal-facts argument, the empty tomb, the appearances and the probabilistic weighing each require the opposing view stated in full before anything is argued, and stating Ehrman, Crossan, Lüdemann and Allison properly is most of a lesson each. Islam needs five because *tawhid*, the Incarnation, the crucifixion and the corruption charge, and the prophethood of Muhammad are four separate arguments with four separate literatures, and the lesson that states Islam on its own terms has to come first or the other four are polemic. Compression happened where it could: Module 1 is three lessons because [`philosophical-method`](../philosophical-method/syllabus.md) 4.2 already teaches burden and steelmanning; Module 2 is three because [`philosophy-of-religion`](../philosophy-of-religion/syllabus.md) has already assessed every argument, so this course only assembles and answers; and the apologetic treatment of cosmology is one lesson (4.3) rather than the two that fine-tuning and the kalam would take if they had to be rebuilt.
