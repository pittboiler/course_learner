# Apologetics I · Lesson 5.5: Weighing it: the Bayesian apologetic and its limits

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 5: Jesus and the resurrection as history · Builds on: [5.4 The appearances and the rival hypotheses](05-04-the-appearances-and-the-rival-hypotheses.md), [5.1 Can history establish a miracle?](05-01-can-history-establish-a-miracle.md), [1.3 Burden of proof and the shape of a cumulative case](01-03-burden-of-proof-and-the-shape-of-a-cumulative-case.md) · Unlocks: [6.1 The messianic claim, and how a prophecy argument works](06-01-the-messianic-claim-and-how-a-prophecy-argument-works.md)

## Why this matters

Four lessons have weighed the strands one at a time: the method question (5.1), the minimal facts (5.2), the tomb (5.3), the appearances against their rivals (5.4). This lesson puts them in one equation, because two serious philosophers have done exactly that. Richard Swinburne concluded that the probability that Jesus was God incarnate and rose from the dead is around 97 percent. Timothy and Lydia McGrew argued that the evidence is about $10^{44}$ times more expected if Jesus rose than if he did not. Both numbers get quoted far more than they get read. The point of reading them is to see what they actually claim, which term of the equation each one leans on, and what is left of the case once the critics have had their turn.

## The idea

Odds form is reloaded, not re-derived ([`philosophical-method` 2.4](../../philosophical-method/lessons/02-04-weighing-evidence-in-odds-form.md)):

$$\text{posterior odds} = \text{prior odds} \times L$$

Here $L$, the likelihood ratio, measures how much more expected the evidence is under the hypothesis than under its denial. A [Bayesian apologetic](../reference.md#bayesian-apologetic) is any argument that puts the resurrection into this shape. There are three terms, so there are three places to put the weight, and the two famous calculations put it in different places.

**Swinburne loads the prior.** He brings natural theology into the calculation as background, so before any Easter evidence is counted a resurrection is already not absurd, and he then asks the history for only a modest $L$. **The McGrews load the likelihood.** They build an enormous $L$ from the testimony and leave the prior largely to the reader. Each critic aims at the term the apologist leaned on: against Swinburne, *where did that prior come from?*; against the McGrews, *are those witnesses really independent?* Underneath both sits a more radical objection: that none of these numbers can be assigned at all.

## The other side

**Herman Philipse**, *God in the Age of Science? A Critique of Religious Reason* (2012), argues the case most directly against Swinburne's project, and he grants its form. If theism is a hypothesis, it should be weighed the way science weighs hypotheses. His objection is that it then fails on three counts.

1. **Meaning.** Our psychological predicates (wants, intends, acts) get their sense from embodied agents. Calling God an incorporeal person cancels the conditions under which those predicates apply, so it is unclear what the hypothesis even says.
2. **[Predictive power](../reference.md#philipse-on-predictive-power).** Suppose it does say something. We still have no background knowledge of what a bodiless omnipotent being would want, so theism predicts nothing specific. In odds form, nobody can fix $P(E \mid \text{God raised Jesus})$, and a Bayesian argument that needs that number has nothing to calculate with.
3. **Rivals.** The particular evidence apologists cite, the resurrection reports included, is on his view better explained by secular hypotheses (the territory of [5.4](05-04-the-appearances-and-the-rival-hypotheses.md)).

Philipse calls the result a dilemma, which he names "the Tension." Natural theology can borrow science's standards and fail them, or decline them and lose its claim to be rational. Notice what he does *not* say. He does not set the prior to zero by fiat, and he does not argue that miracles are impossible. The objection is about method, and it is stronger for that.

Two narrower objections come with it. Richard Otte's review of Swinburne (*Notre Dame Philosophical Reviews*) accepts the machinery and attacks the inputs. Swinburne's prior of one half that God, if he exists, becomes incarnate is unsupported when rival possibilities are open, and Swinburne's low probability for the evidence absent an incarnation leans on an appeal to God's not deceiving us that many philosophers will reject. Against the McGrews, critics press the oldest objection in the field ([`philosophy-of-religion` 3.5](../../philosophy-of-religion/lessons/03-05-miracles.md), Hume's Part II): the testimonies were not independent. Ehrman's version, that the historian cannot price a miracle at all, is [5.1](05-01-can-history-establish-a-miracle.md)'s.

## The argument

**[Swinburne's calculation](../reference.md#swinburnes-calculation)** (*The Resurrection of God Incarnate*, 2003, as summarized in Otte's review). The background $k$ is natural theology, which gives $P(\text{God} \mid k) = \tfrac12$. Given God, the probability of an incarnation of the kind Swinburne describes is $\tfrac12$. So the hypothesis $c$, that God became incarnate, has prior $\tfrac14$. The evidence $f$ is that Jesus met the "prior requirements" (a perfect life, moral teaching, claiming divinity, teaching an atonement, founding a church) together with the "posterior" one, a super-miracle that vindicates him: the resurrection evidence. Swinburne puts $P(f \mid c\,k) = \tfrac1{10}$, provisionally, and $P(f \mid \neg c\,k) = \tfrac1{1000}$. His conclusion is a probability of about 0.97.

**[The McGrews' Bayes factor](../reference.md#the-mcgrews-bayes-factor)** ("The Argument from Miracles: A Cumulative Case for the Resurrection of Jesus of Nazareth," in Craig and Moreland, eds., *The Blackwell Companion to Natural Theology*, 2009). There are three classes of testimony, each with its own factor: the women's report of the empty tomb at $10^{2}$, the disciples' testimony to the appearances at $10^{39}$, and Paul's at $10^{3}$. Multiplied, these give $10^{44}$, which they note would overcome a prior as low as $10^{-40}$ and leave a posterior above $0.9999$. The $10^{39}$ comes from counting the disciples' testimony witness by witness. The McGrews take up the dependence worry themselves and argue that collusion does not much weaken the factor.

The course's own argument, built from both:

- **P1.** The prior for "God raised Jesus" is not the frequency of corpses rising. It is fixed by the [total evidence](../reference.md#total-evidence) about whether there is a God who might act in this way, which is the case argued in Modules 2–4 (1.3). *In words:* Hume's base rate answers the wrong question.
- **P2.** On the historical data of 5.2–5.4 (the death, the appearance experiences including group ones, Paul, James, the tomb with its concession), the resurrection hypothesis carries a substantial $L$ against the best naturalistic combination.
- **P3.** That $L$ is far below $10^{44}$, because the strands share sources and the witnesses shared a community. Here the cable of 1.3 is partly a [chain](../reference.md#chain-and-cable).
- **P4.** For any prior in the range a reasonable theist can defend after Modules 2–4, the posterior is substantial. For a prior near zero, nothing historical moves it.
- **∴ C.** The resurrection case is a [motive of credibility](../reference.md#motives-of-credibility) ([`fundamental-theology` 2.2](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/02-02-credibility-and-the-preambles-of-faith.md)). It makes belief reasonable for someone whose total evidence keeps theism live. It compels no one, and the disagreement it leaves sits mostly in the prior.

*In words:* weaker than the calculation, stronger than agnosticism. The history does real work, but not all of it.

**Concession.** Nobody knows how to assign the prior. Swinburne's one half is a judgment summarizing a book of natural theology, not a measurement. The McGrews' $10^{39}$ is an artefact of multiplication. Philipse is right that no shared procedure fixes these inputs, and a critic who refuses them is breaking no rule of probability. What the numbers can do is locate the disagreement: they show which term each party denies and how much hangs on it. That is a real gain, and it is the only one claimed here.

**Where the argument is weakest.** The weak point is the likelihood under the resurrection hypothesis, the number Philipse says nobody can fix. Swinburne's $\tfrac1{10}$ rests on a theory of what God would do if he became incarnate. A Catholic who answered the problem of evil with [skeptical theism](../reference.md#skeptical-theism) (2.3), the view that God's reasons are beyond our ken, has to explain why those reasons become readable at Easter. The reply has to come from the content of revelation and of the Incarnation, which is not neutral ground.

## The odds

![Posterior probability against prior odds on a log scale from one in a million to even. With likelihood ratio 100 the curve rises through even odds at one in a hundred; Swinburne's prior of one to three gives 0.97; a prior for God of one in a thousand gives 0.05. A curve for ratio 10,000 sits two powers of ten to the left.](assets/05-05-fig1.svg)

Moving the prior one power of ten slides you along the curve by one unit. Changing $L$ shifts the whole curve sideways. The McGrews' $10^{44}$ would sit forty-two units to the left of the blue curve, off the chart, so it looks like certainty everywhere on this scale. That is exactly why everything rides on whether it is real.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: the base-rate objection met cleanly.** An objector says: "Of all the human dead, none has been seen to rise, so the prior is effectively zero." Run Swinburne's own numbers. The prior is $\tfrac14$, which is odds of $1:3$. The likelihood ratio is $\tfrac{1/10}{1/1000} = 100$. So the posterior odds are

$$\tfrac13 \times 100 = \tfrac{100}{3}, \qquad P = \tfrac{100}{103} \approx 0.971.$$

That reproduces his 0.97. Now lower only $P(\text{God})$, to $\tfrac1{10}$. The prior becomes $\tfrac1{20}$ (odds $1:19$), and the posterior is $100:19$, about $0.84$. At $P(\text{God}) = \tfrac1{1000}$ the prior is $1:1999$, and the posterior is $100:1999$, about $0.048$. The objector's base rate never appears, because the hypothesis is not "a corpse rose" but "God raised this man." Every bit of the remaining disagreement is disagreement about God, which is where [5.1](05-01-can-history-establish-a-miracle.md) said it would be. The objection is answered by relocating it.

**Example 2: where it strains, the McGrews' independence.** Recount the disciples as one community of testimony, worth an invented $10^{3}$ instead of $10^{39}$. The total becomes $10^{2} \times 10^{3} \times 10^{3} = 10^{8}$. At the McGrews' illustrative prior of $10^{-40}$, the posterior odds are now $10^{-32}$. Thirty-six orders of magnitude have vanished, and the question that did it was [`philosophical-method` 2.4](../../philosophical-method/lessons/02-04-weighing-evidence-in-odds-form.md)'s: would the second disciple's conviction have appeared if the first's had not? That lesson called independence the whole ballgame, and here it is. The apologist cannot simply say "independent." The disciples talked to one another, and 5.4's group-vision hypothesis is a claim about exactly that. Nor can the critic simply say "one witness," because Paul, a former persecutor, and James stood outside the circle. No agreed number lies between $10^{3}$ and $10^{39}$. The honest course is to argue the dependence structure strand by strand and report a range.

## Watch out

- **You might think the McGrews claim a 99.99 percent posterior. Actually** $10^{44}$ is a Bayes factor. The posterior figure is conditional on a prior they offer for illustration. Quoting it as their verdict misstates them.
- **You might think the Church teaches that the resurrection can be proved to 97 percent.** *Dei Filius* ch. 3 (rung 1, per [`fundamental-theology` 2.2](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/02-02-credibility-and-the-preambles-of-faith.md)) defines a capacity: revelation *can* be made credible by exterior signs, and miracles *can* rightly prove. It certifies no calculation, Swinburne's included, and faith does not rest on a posterior.
- **You might think Philipse simply assumes naturalism.** That caricature fails the problem. He accepts the Bayesian form and argues that theism cannot supply its inputs. Answer that, not a straw atheist.

## One-liner

> The number doesn't settle the resurrection; it shows that the dispute over the history is mostly a dispute over God, and over whether the witnesses were really many.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢) *(Formal.)*** Mara, an invented historian, grants that the resurrection evidence carries $L = 2{,}000$ against the best naturalistic rival.

(a) Compute the posterior odds and probability (to three decimals) for prior odds of $1:10$, $1:100$, $1:1{,}000$, $1:10{,}000$ and $1:100{,}000$.
(b) A second critic accepts Mara's prior of $1:100$ but argues that dependence among the witnesses inflates $L$ by a factor of 20. Compute the posterior. Then say in one sentence whether this critic or one who lowers the prior by a single power of ten moves the verdict more.

**P2 (🟡) *(Apologetic: (a) Exegetical, graded first · (b) reply.)*** (a) State Philipse's objection from predictive power as he would recognize it, including what it grants about the Bayesian form and which term of the odds equation it targets. (b) Reply, opening with a named concession. 150 words or fewer in total.

**P3 (🔴, optional) *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)*** An invented debate opening (nobody said this):

> "Bayes' theorem has settled the question. The McGrews proved the resurrection is 99.99 percent certain, and Swinburne got 97 percent. The Church itself teaches that miracles prove Christianity, so a Catholic who doubts these numbers is doubting the faith."

(a) Name three distinct errors, quoting the words that carry each. (b) Say in two sentences what this course's conclusion lets the speaker honestly claim instead.

**P1** *(Formal.)*

Posterior odds = prior odds $\times\, 2{,}000$:

| Prior odds | Posterior odds | Probability |
|---|---|---|
| $1:10$ | $200:1$ | $\tfrac{200}{201} \approx 0.995$ |
| $1:100$ | $20:1$ | $\tfrac{20}{21} \approx 0.952$ |
| $1:1{,}000$ | $2:1$ | $\tfrac23 \approx 0.667$ |
| $1:10{,}000$ | $1:5$ | $\tfrac16 \approx 0.167$ |
| $1:100{,}000$ | $1:50$ | $\tfrac1{51} \approx 0.020$ |

(b) $L = 2{,}000/20 = 100$, so at $1:100$ the posterior odds are $1:1$, a probability of $0.500$. Lowering the prior one power of ten instead (to $1:1{,}000$) gives $0.667$. So the dependence critic moves the verdict more, from $0.952$ to $0.500$, because a factor of 20 is more than one power of ten. Both moves are fights about inputs, and neither is a fight about the arithmetic.

**Wrong turns:** reporting the odds $200:1$ as a probability of $0.995$ without converting, or converting $2:1$ to $0.5$; dividing the prior instead of $L$ in (b).

---

**P2** *(Apologetic: (a) Exegetical, graded first · (b) reply.)*

**Must hit, strict (a), graded first:**

- Philipse grants the form: if theism is a hypothesis, it is to be weighed by the standards used for scientific hypotheses, Bayesian ones included.
- Theism lacks predictive power. We have no background knowledge of what an incorporeal omnipotent person would want or do, so it predicts nothing specific.
- So the likelihood under theism, $P(E \mid \text{God raised Jesus})$, cannot be fixed. The target is the likelihood term, not only the prior.
- Credit for naming the meaning objection behind it (psychological predicates applied to an incorporeal being), or "the Tension."

**Must hit (b):**

- A named concession. For example: no neutral procedure assigns these numbers, and Swinburne's $\tfrac1{10}$ is a judgment.
- A real reply. Candidates: the Christian hypothesis is not bare theism but a specific claim, God incarnate vindicating a particular life and teaching, and its content constrains the likelihood, much as a theory's auxiliary assumptions sharpen its predictions. Or: comparative judgments ("more expected on R than on not-R") can be defended without precise values. Or: the objection proves too much, since many historical and personal explanations also lack precise likelihoods.
- An acknowledgement that the reply's premise, that God's purposes can be read off revelation, sits uneasily with skeptical theism (2.3).

**Wrong turns:** stating Philipse as "assumes miracles are impossible" or "sets the prior to zero," which fails (a). Replying only that the prior can be raised, which misses that he targets the likelihood.

**Model answer, one of several:** (a) Philipse grants that theism, offered as a hypothesis, should be tested like one. It fails, he argues, because we know nothing of what a bodiless omnipotent person would want, so theism predicts nothing specific. That leaves $P(E \mid R)$ unassignable, and the calculation never gets started. (b) Concession: no neutral procedure fixes Swinburne's $\tfrac1{10}$. But the resurrection hypothesis is not bare theism. It is the claim that God vindicated this teacher, and that content predicts a vindication publicly connected to him, not just any outcome. Comparative judgments of this kind do not need exact values. The cost is real: an apologist who invoked inscrutable reasons on evil now owes an account of why they are legible here.

---

**P3** *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)*

**Must hit, strict (a):**

- **"The McGrews proved … 99.99 percent certain."** Their $10^{44}$ is a Bayes factor, and the 0.9999 is conditional on an illustrative prior. They report no unconditional posterior.
- **"Bayes' theorem has settled the question" / "proved."** A posterior is only as good as its inputs, and the inputs are exactly what is disputed (prior, independence, Philipse on the likelihood). The case is a motive of credibility, not a demonstration.
- **"The Church itself teaches that miracles prove Christianity, so a Catholic who doubts these numbers is doubting the faith."** *Dei Filius* ch. 3 defines a capacity, that miracles *can* rightly prove. It certifies no argument or number, so doubting Swinburne's inputs is not doubting a dogma. This overstates what the rung-1 text defines.

**Must hit, any verdict (b):** a claim scaled to the inputs. For example: on a prior a theist can defend after the natural-theology case, the history makes the resurrection credible, and the remaining disagreement is about God and about the independence of the witnesses.

**Wrong turns:** calling Swinburne's 97 percent invented (it is his figure). Treating the third error as "the Church says nothing about miracles," which overcorrects the level.

**Model answer (b), one of several:** For anyone whose total evidence leaves God's existence a live possibility, the historical evidence makes the resurrection a reasonable belief, more than agnosticism allows. How far it goes depends on a prior and on a dependence structure that reasonable people still dispute, and that disagreement is where the debate should be conducted.

## Flashback

**From Lesson [5.3](05-03-the-empty-tomb.md) (The empty tomb):** *(Exegetical: close reading · Evaluative.)* Mark 15:42–47 (DR):

> "And when evening was now come (because it was the Parasceve, that is, the day before the sabbath), Joseph of Arimathea, a noble counsellor, who was also himself looking for the kingdom of God, came and went in boldly to Pilate and begged the body of Jesus. But Pilate wondered that he should be already dead. And sending for the centurion, he asked him if he were already dead. And when he had understood it by the centurion, he gave the body to Joseph. And Joseph, buying fine linen and taking him down, wrapped him up in the fine linen and laid him in a sepulchre which was hewed out of a rock. And he rolled a stone to the door of the sepulchre. And Mary Magdalen and Mary the mother of Joseph, beheld where he was laid."

"Parasceve" is the day of preparation; "noble counsellor" renders the Greek *bouleutēs*, a member of a council. (a) Name three details in the passage, and for each say which of 5.3's premises it supports. One sentence each. (b) Any verdict: why can a reader who follows Crossan grant that Mark wrote verse 47 and still deny that it adds independent weight to the discovery, and what does that imply for entering the burial and the discovery as two strands in odds form? Two sentences.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):**

- **v.42, the eve of the sabbath:** supports **P1** (burial was possible). Jewish law required burial the same day (Deut 21:23), and the coming sabbath gave a local reason to take the body down at once, the point 5.3 draws from John 19:31.
- **v.43, Joseph a council member:** supports **P2**. An inventor would hardly give the burial to a member of the body that condemned Jesus.
- **v.47, the women saw where he was laid:** ties the burial witnesses to the discoverers of 16:1–8, so it supports the step from **burial in a known tomb** to **discovery** (and P3's women). It answers "nobody knew where the body lay" in the text's own terms.

**Must hit, any verdict (b):**

- Verse 47 is exactly what a narrative needs if the discovery is to follow, so a writer composing the discovery would supply it whether or not it was remembered. On its own it is about as expected on Crossan's hypothesis as on the apologist's, a likelihood ratio near 1, and its weight comes from whatever makes the burial probable.
- So burial and discovery are not two independent strands to be multiplied. The discovery's force is conditional on the burial, and entering both at full strength counts the burial evidence twice, the independence failure this lesson prices.

**Wrong turns:** reading v.43 as saying Joseph opposed the verdict (that is Luke 23:51, not Mark); treating v.47 as independent attestation of the tomb; using "Pilate wondered" (v.44) as evidence for the empty tomb (it bears on the death); citing CCC 640 or the creeds as if they settled the historical question.

**Model answer, one of several:** (a) The Parasceve (v.42) supports P1, since the coming sabbath and Deuteronomy's same-day rule gave Judeans a reason to bury at once. Joseph's being a council member (v.43) supports P2, as an awkward figure to invent. The women watching where he was laid (v.47) links the burial to the discovery and to P3's witnesses. (b) Whoever composed the discovery story needed exactly this link, so verse 47 is little more expected if the burial is remembered than if it is not, and it lends no weight of its own. In odds form the discovery enters conditional on the burial, not beside it, or the burial is counted twice.
