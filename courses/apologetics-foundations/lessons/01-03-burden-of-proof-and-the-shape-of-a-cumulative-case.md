# Apologetics I · Lesson 1.3: Burden of proof and the shape of a cumulative case

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 1: Giving a reason for the hope · Builds on: [1.2 Three strategies](01-02-three-strategies.md), [`philosophical-method` 4.2](../../philosophical-method/lessons/04-02-steelmanning-and-the-burden-of-proof.md), [`philosophical-method` 2.4](../../philosophical-method/lessons/02-04-weighing-evidence-in-odds-form.md) · Unlocks: [2.1 Assembling the case](02-01-assembling-the-case.md)

## Why this matters

Before Module 2 builds anything, two procedural questions have to be settled, and both are usually settled by sleight of hand. **Who owes the argument?** If the default is unbelief and the believer must prove, a draw counts as a loss. **What shape does the argument take?** If it is a chain, any broken link breaks it. If it is a cable, a few frayed strands do not. 1.2 said Catholic apologetics runs a cumulative case. This lesson says what that commits you to: arithmetic you cannot fudge, the one condition on which the whole case depends, and the item you are not allowed to leave out.

## The idea

A **chain** passes force from link to link: premise one, so two, so three. Its reliability is at most that of its weakest link and usually lower. Three links you trust at 80%, 85% and 90% give a conclusion you can trust at about 61% if they fail independently ($0.8 \times 0.85 \times 0.9 = 0.612$). A **cable** works the other way. Each strand bears on the conclusion directly. None has to carry the load alone, and the strands together can hold what no single one could. Peirce, writing in 1868, made this the method he wanted philosophy to use:

> "Its reasoning should not form a chain which is no stronger than its weakest link, but a cable whose fibers may be ever so slender, provided they are sufficiently numerous and intimately connected."
> (C. S. Peirce, "Some Consequences of Four Incapacities", *Journal of Speculative Philosophy*, 1868)

Read the proviso. "Numerous" is easy. "Intimately connected" can be misread. The strands must bear on the same conclusion, but their *failures* must not be connected. Five strands cut from one thread are one strand. That is the [chain and cable](../reference.md#chain-and-cable) distinction, and the lesson's formal work is all about that proviso.

## The other side

**Flew's presumption.** Antony Flew ("The Presumption of Atheism", *Canadian Journal of Philosophy*, 1972; reprinted in his book of that title, 1976) proposed reading "atheist" negatively, the *a-* as in "atypical": someone who is simply not a theist, not someone who asserts there is no God. A [presumption of atheism](../reference.md#presumption-of-atheism) is then a rule of method. Like the presumption of innocence in an English court, it is defeasible. It does not say the accused is innocent, and it does not stop the prosecution from proving guilt. It says that inquiry starts from non-belief, and that the one who affirms carries the onus. Flew pressed this as what any systematic inquiry into any existence claim requires, not as a special handicap for theists. Michael Scriven (*Primary Philosophy*, 1966) argued along related lines ([`philosophy-of-religion` 5.1](../../philosophy-of-religion/lessons/05-01-evidentialism-and-the-ethics-of-belief.md)). Plantinga's two replies, and the "double waiver" for getting out of a stand-off, are in [`philosophical-method` 4.2](../../philosophical-method/lessons/04-02-steelmanning-and-the-burden-of-proof.md), Example 2. They are reloaded here, not rerun.

**Flew's buckets.** Against the cumulative case itself, Flew offered an image in *God and Philosophy* (1966): if one leaky bucket holds no water, ten leaky buckets hold none either. At its strongest the objection is not "theistic arguments are bad." It is this: each argument, taken alone, is admitted not to establish its conclusion; adding failures to failures yields a heap of failures; and a case that needs many weak arguments advertises that it has no strong one. Its sharpest modern form is about dependence. The arguments usually fail *for the same reason*, a shared contested premise, so their failures are correlated and stacking them gives no more support than one.

## The argument

The Catholic apologist's answer, in two parts.

**On the burden.**

1. **P1.** Flew's presumption is procedural: it fixes who speaks first, not what is true ([`philosophical-method` 4.2](../../philosophical-method/lessons/04-02-steelmanning-and-the-burden-of-proof.md), B4).
2. **P2.** The Catholic account does not make the believer's faith wait on winning a debate. Faith rests on God's authority, and argument supplies [motives of credibility](../reference.md#motives-of-credibility), not the ground of assent ([`fundamental-theology` 2.2](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/02-02-credibility-and-the-preambles-of-faith.md)).
3. **P3.** But 1 Peter 3:15 asks for a reason whenever one is demanded ([1.1](01-01-what-apologetics-is-and-what-it-isnt.md)).
4. **∴ C1.** The apologist should take on the [burden of proof](../reference.md#burden-of-proof) in argument, while denying that losing the exchange would make atheism true or make unargued belief irrational.

*In words:* accept the onus as a method, and refuse it as a verdict.

**On the shape.** Write $G$ for theism and $N$ for its rival, and use odds form ([`philosophical-method` 2.4](../../philosophical-method/lessons/02-04-weighing-evidence-in-odds-form.md); conditionalization is [`epistemology` 5.3](../../epistemology/lessons/05-03-conditionalization.md)):

$$\frac{P(G \mid E_1 \ldots E_n)}{P(N \mid E_1 \ldots E_n)} = \frac{P(G)}{P(N)} \times L_1 \times L_2' \times \cdots \times L_n'$$

where $L_k' = \frac{P(E_k \mid G, E_1 \ldots E_{k-1})}{P(E_k \mid N, E_1 \ldots E_{k-1})}$ is item $k$'s ratio **given the items already counted**.

5. **P4.** A leaky bucket is a deductive argument with a false premise, and such arguments really do not add. An item with $L > 1$ is not leaky. It is real evidence, short of proof.
6. **P5.** Items whose ratios given each other stay above 1 multiply. So several items, none of which alone takes the odds past even, can do so together.
7. **∴ C2.** The bucket image is right about chains of proof and wrong about a [cumulative case](../reference.md#cumulative-case). Everything depends on the primed ratios.

**Concession.** Flew's buckets point at something true. A cumulative case can hide weak links behind numbers. Each ratio is a judgement presented as a figure, and multiplying many generous judgements gives a confident-looking product from soft inputs. Independence is usually where such cases fail, and those who build them tend not to check it. A case built on one contested premise, presented three times, is exactly a stack of leaky buckets.

**The total-evidence rule.** A posterior is only as honest as its list of items. The [total evidence](../reference.md#total-evidence) requirement (Carnap's name for it) says the product must include every relevant item, favourable or not. For theism that means suffering, which [`philosophy-of-religion` 4.2](../../philosophy-of-religion/lessons/04-02-the-evidential-problem-of-evil.md) states as Rowe and Draper state it. Swinburne's own case includes evil in the sum. An apologist who leaves it out has not made a weaker case. He has made a different calculation, about a world without suffering.

**What would falsify it.** The case is open to defeat term by term. If a strand's ratio is shown to be near 1 (a rival predicts the datum as well), it drops out. If two strands are shown to share a premise, one counts once. If suffering's ratio is shown to be far below 1, the total sinks. A case that no finding could lower is not a cumulative case.

**Where the argument is weakest.** In the primed ratios. Arguments for God are rarely independent in the needed sense. Several of them get their ratio from one value premise: that God would have reason to make embodied, conscious, moral agents. Show that a single premise is doing the work in each, and the case has fewer strands than it seems to.

## The odds

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (the cable meets the buckets).** Prior odds $G:N = 1:6$. Strand A is a fine-tuning argument with $L = 2.5$. Strand B is a moral argument with $L = 2$. Strand C is an argument from consciousness with $L = 1.8$. Alone, each leaves theism below even: $\frac{1}{6} \times 2.5 = 5:12$, $\frac{1}{6} \times 2 = 1:3$, $\frac{1}{6} \times 1.8 = 3:10$. Together, if independent:

$$\frac{1}{6} \times 2.5 \times 2 \times 1.8 = \frac{9}{6} = 3:2$$

That is 60%. No bucket held water and the cable held the load, so P5 is doing real work. Note what the case gets: more confidence than its strongest strand alone (29%), and only *because* the ratios multiply.

**Example 2 (where it strains).** Now the critic presses the independence proviso. Fine-tuning favours $G$ because embodied conscious agents are the sort of thing God would have reason to make. That same value premise is what makes consciousness expected on $G$. Given A, then, C's ratio falls. Say it falls to 1.2:

$$\frac{1}{6} \times 2.5 \times 2 \times 1.2 = 1:1$$

That is exactly 50%. Now apply total evidence. Suffering with an invented $L = 0.6$ takes the independent case to $\frac{3}{2} \times 0.6 = 9:10$ (47%) and the dependent one to $3:5$ (37.5%). For the independent case to stay at even odds or better, suffering's ratio must be at least $\frac{2}{3}$. The dependent case can stay at even only if suffering is no evidence against theism at all.

There is a second strain, internal to the tradition. *Dei Filius* canon 1 (1870) defines, at rung 1, that God **can be known with certainty** by natural reason from created things ([`fundamental-theology` 1.2](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/01-02-natural-knowledge-of-god.md)). A 60% posterior is not certainty. The canon is a capacity claim. It canonizes no argument and no method, so the cumulative case is not out of bounds. But it is not what the canon describes, either. The Catholic theory of certainty reached through converging probabilities is Newman's, and it is 8.3's.

## Watch out

- **You might think the presumption of atheism says God does not exist.** Flew's atheism is negative: non-belief, presumed as a method. Stating it as "atheists claim to know there is no God" is the caricature this course fails outright.
- **You might think *Dei Filius* made the cumulative case Church teaching.** Canon 1 defines that reason *can* know God with certainty. It names no argument and endorses no method. Reading "the council defined that these arguments prove God" into it is an overstated [level of authority](../reference.md#levels-of-authority).
- **You might think adding strands always helps.** Only if each strand's ratio is above 1 given the others. A dependent strand counts at 1, however impressive it looks alone, and an omitted item with a ratio below 1 is still in the true product.

## One-liner

> Accept the burden as a method and refuse it as a verdict; a cable beats a chain only while its strands fail separately, and suffering is a strand too.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢) *(Formal.)*** An invented debate opening: "Grant me prior odds of 1:7 for God. Three arguments: likelihood ratios 2.4, 2.5 and 1.5. None alone gets past even; together they do." (a) Give the posterior odds of each argument alone, then of all three if independent, with the probability. (b) The opponent shows that the third argument shares a premise with the first, so its ratio *given the first* is 1. Recompute. (c) What is the smallest ratio the third argument must keep, given the first, for the case to stay above even?

**P2 (🟡) *(Apologetic: (a) Exegetical, graded first · (b) reply.)*** (a) State Flew's leaky-bucket objection to cumulative arguments at its strongest, in two or three sentences, including the form that does not depend on any argument being deductive. (b) Reply in 150 words or fewer, opening with a named concession.

**P3 (🔴) *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)*** An invented campus-ministry handout:

> "No single argument for God is airtight, but put the five together and God's existence becomes certain. That is not just our opinion: the First Vatican Council defined that these arguments prove God. And leave the problem of evil out of it. Evil is a challenge to trust, not a piece of evidence about whether God exists."

(a) Name three errors, one per sentence-group, and correct each in a sentence. (b) Name one finding that would lower the handout's case below even odds, and say which term of the odds equation it moves. 100 words or fewer for (b).

<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Formal.)*

(a) Alone: $\frac{1}{7} \times 2.4 = 12:35$ (about 26%), $\frac{1}{7} \times 2.5 = 5:14$ (about 26%), $\frac{1}{7} \times 1.5 = 3:14$ (about 18%). All are below even. Together: $\frac{1}{7} \times 2.4 \times 2.5 \times 1.5 = \frac{9}{7}$, odds $9:7$, probability $\frac{9}{16} = 56.25\%$.

(b) Third ratio given the first is 1: $\frac{1}{7} \times 2.4 \times 2.5 = \frac{6}{7}$, odds $6:7$, probability $\frac{6}{13} \approx 46\%$. The case falls below even.

(c) Need $\frac{6}{7} \times x > 1$, so $x > \frac{7}{6} \approx 1.17$.

**Wrong turns:** adding ratios instead of multiplying them ($2.4 + 2.5 + 1.5 = 6.4$); dropping the *first* argument in (b) rather than discounting the third, which gives $\frac{1}{7} \times 2.5 \times 1.5$ and answers a different question; reading 9:7 as a probability of $\frac{9}{7}$.

---

**P2** *(Apologetic: (a) Exegetical, graded first · (b) reply.)*

**Must hit, strict (a), graded first:**

- The target: arguments each of which, by the arguer's own admission, fails to establish the conclusion alone.
- The claim: aggregating failures yields no success. Ten leaky buckets hold no more than one, and needing many weak arguments signals that there is no strong one.
- The non-deductive form: the arguments tend to fail *for a shared reason* (a common contested premise), so their weaknesses are correlated and stacking them adds little or nothing.

**Wrong turns:** stating it as the presumption of atheism (a different Flew claim, from 1972); stating it as "theistic arguments are all bad" (that is a verdict on each argument, not an objection to adding them); stating only the deductive form, which the reply then dispatches too easily.

**Accept:** any reply, for or against the cumulative case, that opens with a named concession and engages the correlated form of the objection.

**Must hit (b):**

- A named concession: numbers can hide weak links, or dependence is real and often unchecked.
- The deductive/evidential distinction: a valid argument with a false premise contributes nothing; an item with $L > 1$ contributes its ratio.
- The answer to the correlated form: grant it as the right test, then say what has to be shown, namely each strand's ratio *given the others*.

**Model answer (b), one of several:** Concession: Flew is right that a cumulative case can launder weak links through multiplication, and that strands sharing a premise count once, however many there are. But the image fits proofs, not evidence. A deductive argument with a false premise is a leaky bucket and adds nothing. An observation that is more expected on theism than on its rival is not leaky. It holds some water, and independent items multiply. So the correlated form of the objection is the right one to press, and it is a question about specific premises. Does the consciousness argument rest on the same value premise as fine-tuning? If it does, count it once. If it does not, the bucket image has no grip.

---

**P3** *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)*

**Must hit, strict (a):**

- **"Certain."** A cumulative case of probabilistic strands yields a posterior, not certainty. It can raise the odds without closing them.
- **"Vatican I defined that these arguments prove God."** *Dei Filius* canon 1 defines, at rung 1, that God *can be known with certainty* by natural reason from created things. It names no argument, endorses no method, and says "can be known", not "is proved". Overstating it is an exegetical error about level.
- **"Leave evil out."** The total-evidence rule requires every relevant item. Suffering bears on how probable $G$ is (Rowe, Draper), so its ratio belongs in the product. The handout may still argue that the ratio is near 1, but it may not omit the term.

**Wrong turns:** saying Vatican I taught nothing about knowing God (it defined the capacity); saying evil refutes the case (the error is the omission, not the conclusion); objecting to "five" as a number.

**Accept:** any finding that would really lower the odds, tied to the right term.

**Must hit, any verdict (b):** one concrete finding tied to one term. Examples: two strands shown to share a premise (a primed $L$ drops to 1); a rival shown to predict a strand's datum as well as theism does (that $L$ falls to 1); suffering's ratio shown to be well below 1 (a factor under 1 enters the product); or a defensible prior shown to be lower (the prior odds).

**Model answer (b), one of several:** Suppose the moral and consciousness strands were shown to rest on the same premise, that a good God would want embodied moral agents. Then one of them has a ratio of 1 given the other, and a primed $L$ disappears from the product. With suffering's ratio below 1 also in the product, the case could fall below even. The finding moves a likelihood term, not the prior.

## Flashback

**From Lesson [1.1](01-01-what-apologetics-is-and-what-it-isnt.md) (What apologetics is, and what it isn't):** *(Exegetical.)* Tomás, an invented convert, tells his RCIA group: "I believe because the arguments compelled me. I checked them the way I'd check a proof, and once I had checked them I had no choice." Two sentences per part. (a) On the account 1.1 gives (*Dei Filius* ch. 3 and its canons), name the two things in his self-description the account rejects, and say what it holds instead. (b) Name one thing the account grants him.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):**

- **"Because the arguments":** the motive of faith is God's authority in revealing, not the arguments. Arguments bear on the motives of credibility, the judgment that God has spoken, not on the ground of the assent.
- **"I had no choice":** the assent of faith is free, commanded by the will and moved by grace. *Dei Filius* ch. 3, can. 5 on faith anathematizes the claim that it is necessarily produced by the arguments of human reason (rung 1, and what it defines is that negative).

**Must hit, strict (b):**

- Any one of: his arguments can be real motives of credibility, grounding the judgment of credibility and even of credentity ("I ought to believe"); revelation *can* be made credible by outward signs (*Dei Filius* can. 3 on faith condemns the denial), so his route through arguments is legitimate; faith is not a blind movement of the mind (ch. 3).

**Accept:** for (b), any one of these with its reason.

**Wrong turns:** treating the account as calling his arguments worthless or his faith blind (that is the fideism the same chapter rejects). Saying "no choice" is fine because grace was doing it: grace moves the will, and the assent stays free. Calling Tomás dishonest: he may well have found the arguments compelling for the *judgment* of credibility; what the account denies is that the assent itself was necessitated.

**Model answer:** (a) Faith does not rest on the arguments but on God's authority; the arguments reached at most the judgment that God has spoken and that he ought to believe. And no argument necessitates the assent: *Dei Filius* can. 5 defines that it is free, commanded by the will under grace. (b) The account grants that his arguments can be genuine motives of credibility, since revelation can be made credible by outward signs (can. 3), so faith reached by checking arguments is not blind.
