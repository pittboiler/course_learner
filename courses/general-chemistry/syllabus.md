# General Chemistry — Syllabus

> Chemistry · Tier 0 · ~16 lessons · Prereqs: none · Roadmap id: `general-chemistry`

## Goal

Rebuild the working core of first-year chemistry: read the periodic table as a map of electron behavior, predict how atoms bond and what shapes result, and push reactions through the quantitative machinery of the mole, gases, energy, and equilibrium. You leave able to set up and reason about acids/bases, redox, and rates. This is the root of the whole Chemistry field — it deliberately skips deep quantum theory (that's `physical-chemistry` / `quantum-chemistry`) and organic reaction mechanisms.

## Dangerous Checklist

When you finish, you can:

- [ ] Write the ground-state electron configuration of any main-group atom or ion and read its trends off the periodic table
- [ ] Draw a valid Lewis structure and predict molecular geometry, hybridization, and polarity from it
- [ ] Convert freely between mass, moles, particles, and solution concentration
- [ ] Balance a chemical equation and find the limiting reagent, theoretical yield, and percent yield
- [ ] Classify an aqueous reaction as precipitation, acid–base, or redox — and balance the redox one by half-reactions
- [ ] Apply the ideal-gas law and explain each variable through kinetic-molecular theory
- [ ] Compute reaction enthalpy from calorimetry, Hess's law, or formation enthalpies
- [ ] Write an equilibrium expression and predict which way a stress shifts it via Le Châtelier
- [ ] Calculate pH for strong and weak acids/bases and design a buffer at a target pH
- [ ] Sketch and interpret an acid–base titration curve, locating the half-equivalence and equivalence points
- [ ] Read a rate law and use the Arrhenius relation to explain temperature dependence
- [ ] Diagram a galvanic cell and compute its standard cell potential from half-reaction potentials

## Modules

### Module 1: Atoms, the Periodic Table & Bonding

From the structure of a single atom to the shapes of molecules — why the periodic table is the master cheat sheet for everything that follows.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1.1 | The Quantum Atom | Describe an atom's parts and why electrons live in orbitals, not orbits | protons/neutrons/electrons, isotopes, energy levels, orbital shapes (s/p/d), quantum numbers (intuition only) |
| 1.2 | Electron Configurations & the Periodic Table | Write any main-group configuration and see it *as* the table's layout | Aufbau, Pauli, Hund, valence vs. core electrons, blocks, ions |
| 1.3 | Periodic Trends | Predict and rank atomic size, ionization energy, and electronegativity | effective nuclear charge, shielding, atomic/ionic radius, ionization energy, electron affinity, electronegativity |
| 1.4 | Ionic & Covalent Bonds and Lewis Structures | Draw a valid Lewis structure and classify a bond's character | octet rule, ionic vs. covalent vs. polar-covalent, lone pairs, formal charge, resonance, exceptions |
| 1.5 | Molecular Shape: VSEPR, Hybridization & a Taste of MO | Turn a Lewis structure into a 3-D shape and predict polarity | electron domains, VSEPR geometries, bond angles, sp/sp²/sp³ hybridization, molecular polarity, σ/π and bonding/antibonding MOs |

**Boss problem 1:** For $\mathrm{SF_4}$: draw the Lewis structure (account for all 34 valence electrons and any expanded octet), give the electron-domain geometry and the molecular geometry via VSEPR, state the sulfur hybridization, and decide whether the molecule is polar — justifying the dipole from the geometry. Then rank $\mathrm{F}$, $\mathrm{O}$, and $\mathrm{S}$ by electronegativity and explain the order using effective nuclear charge and periodic position.

### Module 2: Stoichiometry & Reactions

The accountant's module: the mole makes chemistry quantitative, and reaction types organize the zoo of what happens in water.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2.1 | The Mole, Molar Mass & Formulas | Convert among mass, moles, and particles; find a compound's formula from data | Avogadro's number, molar mass, percent composition, empirical vs. molecular formula |
| 2.2 | Stoichiometry & Limiting Reagents | Balance an equation and run mole-ratio calculations to a yield | balancing, mole ratios, limiting reagent, theoretical & percent yield |
| 2.3 | Aqueous Reactions: Precipitation, Acid–Base & Redox | Classify a reaction in water and balance a redox one by half-reactions | molarity & dilution, solubility rules, net ionic equations, oxidation numbers, half-reaction balancing |

**Boss problem 2:** $5.00\ \mathrm{g}$ of aluminum is added to $25.0\ \mathrm{mL}$ of $3.00\ \mathrm{M}$ $\mathrm{HCl}$, reacting as $2\,\mathrm{Al} + 6\,\mathrm{HCl} \to 2\,\mathrm{AlCl_3} + 3\,\mathrm{H_2}$. Identify the limiting reagent, compute the grams of $\mathrm{H_2}$ produced, and find the volume that $\mathrm{H_2}$ occupies at STP ($22.4\ \mathrm{L/mol}$). Then assign oxidation numbers to confirm this is a redox reaction and name what is oxidized.

### Module 3: Gases, Thermochemistry & Equilibrium

Reactions in the real world push against pressure, release or absorb heat, and settle into a balance you can steer.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3.1 | Gases: the Ideal-Gas Law & Kinetic Theory | Apply $PV = nRT$ and justify every variable microscopically | pressure, Boyle/Charles/Avogadro, ideal-gas law, kinetic-molecular theory, molar volume, real-gas deviations (van der Waals, intuition) |
| 3.2 | Thermochemistry: Enthalpy & Calorimetry | Measure heat flow and relate it to a reaction's enthalpy | system/surroundings, heat vs. work, enthalpy $\Delta H$, specific heat, calorimetry, exo/endothermic |
| 3.3 | Hess's Law & Enthalpies of Formation | Compute a reaction enthalpy you can't measure directly | state functions, Hess's law, standard formation enthalpies, enthalpy diagrams |
| 3.4 | Chemical Equilibrium: $K$ & Le Châtelier | Write an equilibrium constant and predict the response to a stress | dynamic equilibrium, $K_c$/$K_p$, reaction quotient $Q$, Le Châtelier's principle, ICE tables |

**Boss problem 3:** For the Haber process $\mathrm{N_2(g)} + 3\,\mathrm{H_2(g)} \rightleftharpoons 2\,\mathrm{NH_3(g)}$, $\Delta H^\circ = -92\ \mathrm{kJ}$: (a) $2.0\ \mathrm{mol}\ \mathrm{N_2}$ and $6.0\ \mathrm{mol}\ \mathrm{H_2}$ sit in a rigid $10.0\ \mathrm{L}$ vessel at $400\ \mathrm{K}$ — find the initial total pressure. (b) Predict the direction of shift when you (i) compress the vessel, (ii) raise the temperature, (iii) remove $\mathrm{NH_3}$, and (iv) add a catalyst. (c) Using $\Delta H_f^\circ(\mathrm{NH_3}) = -46\ \mathrm{kJ/mol}$ and Hess's law, verify the reaction enthalpy.

### Module 4: Acids, Bases & Intros to Kinetics and Electrochemistry

Where equilibrium meets everyday chemistry — proton transfer, buffering, and the two big "which way and how fast" questions: rates and electron flow.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4.1 | Acids & Bases: pH and Strength | Compute pH for strong and weak acids/bases | Brønsted–Lowry, autoionization, $\mathrm{pH}/\mathrm{pOH}$, $K_a$/$K_b$, strong vs. weak, conjugate pairs |
| 4.2 | Buffers & Titration | Build a buffer and read an acid–base titration curve | Henderson–Hasselbalch, buffer capacity, titration curves, half-equivalence & equivalence points, indicators |
| 4.3 | A Taste of Kinetics | Read a rate law and explain temperature dependence | reaction rate, rate law & order, integrated rate laws (intuition), half-life, activation energy, Arrhenius, catalysts |
| 4.4 | A Taste of Electrochemistry | Diagram a galvanic cell and compute its cell potential | oxidation/reduction half-cells, galvanic cells, standard reduction potentials, $E^\circ_\mathrm{cell}$, spontaneity link to $\Delta G$ |

**Boss problem 4:** You titrate $25.0\ \mathrm{mL}$ of $0.100\ \mathrm{M}$ acetic acid ($K_a = 1.8\times10^{-5}$) with $0.100\ \mathrm{M}$ $\mathrm{NaOH}$. Compute the pH (a) before any base is added, (b) at the half-equivalence point, and (c) at the equivalence point. (d) Explain in one sentence why the curve is steepest near the equivalence point, and which region acts as a buffer.

- Atkins & de Paula, *Physical Chemistry* — consulted only for the thermochemistry/equilibrium/kinetics tastes, kept deliberately light here.
