# Political Institutions · Lesson 5.1: Federal, unitary, devolved

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 5: Dividing power: federalism and courts · Builds on: [4.1 Bicameralism](04-01-bicameralism.md), [4.4 Party systems](04-04-party-systems.md) · Unlocks: [5.2 Decentralization in practice](05-02-decentralization-in-practice.md), [5.3 Judicial review compared](05-03-judicial-review-compared.md)

## Why this matters

Scotland's parliament makes the law for Scotland's schools. So does the Bavarian Landtag for Bavaria's, and the Texas legislature for Texas's. Yet the UK is a unitary state, while Germany and the US are federations. The difference is not how much a region does. It is **who can take the power away**. This lesson gives the test that sorts states into unitary, devolved and federal, runs it on the US, Germany and Switzerland, and reports Alfred Stepan's account of why some federations treat their units alike and others do not.

## The idea

A **[federation](../reference.md#federalism)** splits lawmaking between two levels, and the split is guaranteed by a constitution that neither level can rewrite alone. William Riker's classic definition (*Federalism: Origin, Operation, Significance*, 1964), paraphrased: two levels of government rule the same land and people; each has at least one area where it decides for itself; and that autonomy is guaranteed in some way. Daniel Elazar's shorthand is "self-rule plus shared rule": the units govern their own sphere (self-rule) *and* take part in governing the whole, typically through an upper chamber and a voice in constitutional amendment (shared rule).

A **unitary** state keeps sovereignty at the centre. Local and regional governments may do a great deal, but they do it by the centre's leave. France's Constitution describes the Republic as organised on a decentralised basis (Art. 1), yet its territorial communities run themselves through elected councils with regulatory powers, on terms set by national statute (Art. 72). This is Tocqueville's distinction at work ([`history-of-political-thought` 6.3](../../history-of-political-thought/lessons/06-03-tocqueville-tyranny-of-the-majority-and-soft-despotism.md)): a state can centralize *government* (who makes law) without centralizing *administration* (who runs things). The classification in this lesson is about government.

A **devolved** system is a unitary state that has handed real lawmaking power to a regional parliament by ordinary statute. The UK is the clean case. The Scotland Act 1998 creates the Scottish Parliament, and then says, at s.28(7):

> "This section does not affect the power of the Parliament of the United Kingdom to make laws for Scotland."

What one Act of Parliament grants, another can amend. The Scotland Act 2016 added a section declaring the Scottish Parliament and Government a permanent part of the UK's constitutional arrangements, not to be abolished except after a referendum in Scotland. On the orthodox doctrine of **[parliamentary sovereignty](../reference.md#parliamentary-sovereignty)** ([5.3](05-03-judicial-review-compared.md)), no Parliament can bind its successors, so that section is a political commitment written into statute, not a legal lock. The convention that Westminster will not *normally* legislate on devolved matters without Holyrood's consent is the **[Sewel convention](../reference.md#sewel-convention)**, and it belongs to [5.2](05-02-decentralization-in-practice.md).

One more neighbour: a **confederation** is a league of states that keep their sovereignty, like the US under the Articles of Confederation. Its centre acts through the member states, not directly on citizens.

## The mechanism

The **[test for unitary, devolved or federal](../reference.md#unitary-devolved-federal)** is three questions, asked in order. Every answer is a **mechanical** claim: it is read off a constitution's or statute's text.

1. **Is there regional lawmaking?** Does any regional body make its own statutes (primary law, not just regulations) in some field? If not, the state is unitary, however decentralized its administration.
2. **What is its source?** If the powers come from an ordinary statute, the system is devolved: the national parliament can change them by ordinary law. If they come from the constitution, go to step 3.
3. **Who holds the key?** Read the amendment rule. If the national level can change the division of powers alone, even by a supermajority, the units hold no key. If the units must consent, individually, collectively through a territorial chamber, or through their voters, the state is federal.

*In words:* a region's power is only as secure as the hardest rule someone must pass to take it away.

The three reference federations use three different locks.

- **United States.** Amendments need ratification by three-quarters of the states (Article V), and Article V adds that "no State, without its Consent, shall be deprived of its equal Suffrage in the Senate." Ratification is a collective key; the proviso is one each state holds alone. Powers not delegated to the Union are reserved to the states or the people (Tenth Amendment).
- **Germany.** Amendments need two-thirds of the Bundestag *and* two-thirds of the votes in the Bundesrat, the chamber of the Land governments (Basic Law Art. 79(2)). Art. 79(3) then puts some things beyond amendment altogether: "Amendments to this Basic Law affecting the division of the Federation into Länder, their participation in principle in the legislative process ... shall be inadmissible." Residual powers lie with the Länder (Art. 30).
- **Switzerland.** Every constitutional amendment goes to a mandatory referendum and needs a majority of voters *and* a majority of cantons (Federal Constitution Arts. 140 and 142). The cantons are sovereign except where the Constitution limits them (Art. 3).

Shared rule shows up again in the upper chamber, whose powers [4.1](04-01-bicameralism.md) compares:

| | Units | Upper chamber | How it represents the units (as of 2026) |
|---|---|---|---|
| US | 50 states | Senate | Two senators per state; directly elected since the Seventeenth Amendment (1913) |
| Germany | Länder | Bundesrat | Members of each Land government; 3 to 6 votes per Land by population, cast as a block (Art. 51) |
| Switzerland | 26 cantons | Council of States | 46 seats: two per canton, one each for six cantons; each canton sets its own election rules (Art. 150) |

A fourth, separate question is **symmetry**: do all units hold the same powers? A federation in which some units hold powers others lack is an **[asymmetric federation](../reference.md#asymmetric-federalism)**. The US, German and Swiss texts give their units the same constitutional powers; [5.2](05-02-decentralization-in-practice.md) takes up the asymmetric cases.

**Why federations differ: Stepan's three origins.** This part is **empirical**. In "Federalism and Democracy: Beyond the U.S. Model" (*Journal of Democracy*, 1999), Alfred Stepan argued that Riker's picture, sovereign units bargaining their way into a union, fits only one kind of federation. He distinguished three **[origins](../reference.md#coming-together-and-holding-together)**:

- **Coming-together** (the US, Switzerland, Australia): previously sovereign units pool sovereignty. Each could refuse to join, so they enter on equal terms. The pattern is symmetric powers and equal seats in the upper chamber, which constrains national majorities heavily.
- **Holding-together** (India, Belgium, Spain, which does not call itself federal): a unitary state devolves power, constitutionally, to keep territorially concentrated minorities inside it. The regions want different things, so the centre gives different regions different powers. Asymmetry is common.
- **Putting-together** (the Soviet Union): a coercive centre assembles units into a formally federal state. Outside a course that takes democracy as given.

How strong is the link from origin to symmetry? It is a tendency read off a small number of well-studied cases, not a tested regularity. International IDEA's constitution-building primer on federalism says asymmetry usually, but not necessarily, follows from holding-together origins. Measuring the effect properly belongs to [`empirical-political-economy`](../../empirical-political-economy/syllabus.md). Whether federalism is *good* is **normative**: Madison's "double security", power divided between nation and states and again within each ([`history-of-political-thought` 5.2](../../history-of-political-thought/lessons/05-02-the-federalist-faction-and-the-extended-republic.md)), the efficiency case in [`public-economics` 8.2](../../public-economics/lessons/08-02-assignment-spillovers-and-grants.md), and subsidiarity ([`political-philosophy` 6.4](../../political-philosophy/lessons/06-04-welfare-property-and-subsidiarity.md)) are cited, not argued here.

**Where the rule runs out.** The test tells you who must consent to *change* the division of powers. It does not tell you three things. First, who settles disputes about the *existing* division: a federal constitution needs an umpire, usually a court ([5.3](05-03-judicial-review-compared.md)). Second, how much autonomy units have in practice: a unit that raises little of its own revenue, or that mostly implements federal law, can be legally secure and practically dependent ([5.2](05-02-decentralization-in-practice.md)). Third, how far politics outruns law: a devolved parliament with no legal lock can be politically untouchable, and a locked federal unit can be hollowed out by conditional grants. The classification ranks legal entrenchment, not lived autonomy.

## The spectrum

*The categories differ in the lock, not in how much the regions do.*

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean): Rhodania.** An invented constitution. Rhodania has four provinces, Arlo, Bexa, Calder and Dunmore.

> Art. 4: The provinces legislate on schooling, policing and land use, and on every matter not assigned to the Union by Article 9.
> Art. 12: The Senate has twelve members, three chosen by each provincial legislature.
> Art. 40: This Constitution is amended by a two-thirds vote of the National Assembly, ratified by the legislatures of three of the four provinces.

Run the test. Step 1: yes, the provinces make law (Art. 4). Step 2: the source is the constitution. Step 3: the National Assembly cannot amend alone; it needs three provincial legislatures (Art. 40). **Rhodania is federal**, and the rule that decides is Art. 40. It is symmetric (Art. 4 names "the provinces", all alike), residual powers sit with the provinces as in the US, Switzerland and Germany, and Art. 12 adds shared rule through an equal, indirectly chosen Senate.

Notice what Art. 40 does *not* give: a key to each province. Three provinces can ratify an amendment that strips Dunmore alone of its powers. The US equal-suffrage proviso is the contrast case: it protects each state individually, but only its Senate seats. And the text cannot say whether the provinces were ever sovereign, though the equal Senate fits the coming-together pattern.

**Example 2 (hard): does France's Senate make it federal?** Run the test from step 3 first, as a careless reader might. France's Senate is elected indirectly, largely by local elected officials, and the Constitution charges it with representing the Republic's territorial communities (Art. 24). Every constitutional amendment must pass both houses in identical terms before going to a referendum or to a three-fifths vote of both houses sitting together (Art. 89). So a chamber of territorial origin holds a veto over every amendment, which looks like the units holding a key.

Now run steps 1 and 2. French communes, departments and regions govern themselves through elected councils with *regulatory* powers, on conditions set by national statute, under a state representative who checks legality (Art. 72). They have no lawmaking sphere the constitution reserves to them. So **France is unitary**, decentralized by its own description. The Senate's veto locks nothing, because there is no reserved sphere to lock. Compare the Bundesrat: its members are delegates of Land governments, voting as blocks to defend powers those governments themselves hold.

Here the definition strains. "Amendment needs the units' consent" can be met by a territorial chamber even where the units own no sphere of law. The consent test is necessary for federalism, not sufficient, which is why the test asks step 1 first.

## Watch out

- **You might think a federal region always does more than a devolved one, but actually the categories measure entrenchment, not scope.** A devolved parliament can legislate across more fields than some federal units. "How much do regions decide?" is a separate question, partly fiscal, for [5.2](05-02-decentralization-in-practice.md).
- **You might think "coming-together federations are symmetric" is a rule, but actually it is an observed tendency in a handful of cases.** Whether a given federation is symmetric is mechanical: read its constitution. Whether its *origin* explains that is the empirical claim, and the evidence for it is thin.
- **You might think a holding-together federation is just devolution, but actually the difference is the lock.** Holding-together describes how a federation *arose*. Once its regions' powers sit in a constitution they help amend, it is federal, whatever its origin.

## One-liner

> A state is federal not because its regions do a lot but because the centre cannot take their powers away alone: find the regional lawmaking, find its source, then find who holds the key.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢) *(Exegetical (a)-(b).)*** Diagnose. Set Example 1 aside: suppose instead that Rhodania's constitutional convention is choosing among three **invented** drafts.

- **Draft X:** "Regional assemblies, established by the Regions Act, legislate on education and health. Parliament may amend or repeal the Regions Act by ordinary law."
- **Draft Y:** "The regions legislate on education and health (Art. 5). Art. 5 may be amended by a two-thirds vote of Parliament." Parliament is a single chamber elected nationally.
- **Draft Z:** "The regions legislate on education and health (Art. 5). Every amendment requires a two-thirds vote of the National Assembly and a two-thirds vote of the Council of Regions, whose members are appointed and recalled by the regional governments."

(a) Classify each draft as unitary, devolved or federal, naming the clause that decides. One line each. (b) Name one change to Draft Y that would make it federal. One sentence.

**P2 (🟡) *(Exegetical (a)-(b).)*** Find the crux. Two **invented** advisers to Rhodania's convention disagree. Adviser A: "Give all four provinces identical powers and equal Senate seats; that is how federations work." Adviser B: "Dunmore, home to most of the country's Vellish-speaking minority, must control language and broadcasting, which the other provinces will not." (a) Using Stepan's typology, name the fact about how Rhodania's federation is being formed that each proposal presupposes, and state the crux between them. 80 words or fewer. (b) Name two pieces of evidence about Rhodania that would tell you which pattern to expect, and say how strong Stepan's pattern is as a prediction. 80 words or fewer.

**P3 (🔴, optional) *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)*** Apply to a hard case. Rhodania adopts Draft X, and Parliament, which is supreme as at Westminster, later passes a Regions (Amendment) Act adding: "The Dunmore Assembly is a permanent part of Rhodania's constitutional arrangements and shall not be abolished except after a referendum in Dunmore." Ten years on, a new parliamentary majority passes an Act abolishing the Assembly without a referendum. (a) Does the permanence clause make Dunmore's position federal, and what does the rule decide about the abolition Act's legal validity? Two sentences. (b) Say where the rule stops determining whether the Assembly is actually abolished. 100 words or fewer; any verdict on whether Parliament should proceed passes.

<details>
<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical (a)-(b), strict)*

**Must hit, strict (a):**

- **X: devolved.** The regional assemblies make law, but the source is an ordinary statute that Parliament may amend or repeal by ordinary law.
- **Y: unitary**, with constitutionally listed regional powers. The regions make law under the constitution (steps 1 and 2 pass), but Parliament alone can amend Art. 5 by two-thirds, so the regions hold no key (step 3 fails). A supermajority is not consent.
- **Z: federal.** The amendment rule needs two-thirds in a chamber of regional-government delegates, so the regions collectively hold a key, as in Germany's Art. 79(2).

**Accept (b):** any change that makes the regions' consent necessary to amend Art. 5.

**Model answer (b):** Require any amendment to Art. 5 to be ratified by a majority of the regional legislatures (or by a chamber of regional delegates, or by a majority of voters in a majority of regions).

**Wrong turns:** calling Y federal because the powers are written in the constitution, which skips step 3; calling Y devolved, which misses that its source is constitutional and the bar is a supermajority, not ordinary law. Accept "unitary, regionalized" or "quasi-federal" for Y only if the answer names the missing regional key as decisive; accept "unitary (devolved)" for X, since devolution happens inside a unitary state.

---

**P2** *(Exegetical (a)-(b), strict)*

**Must hit, strict (a):**

- A presupposes coming-together: the provinces were prior sovereign units, each able to refuse entry, so they bargain on equal terms and get symmetric powers and equal seats.
- B presupposes holding-together: an existing state is federalizing to keep a territorially concentrated minority inside, so it gives the region with distinct demands powers the others lack.
- Crux: whether the provinces enter as equal prior sovereigns or a centre is allocating powers to retain one discontented region.

**Must hit, strict (b):**

- Evidence (two of): whether the provinces were independent polities before the union and must each ratify it; whether Rhodania was previously unitary; whether a minority is concentrated in one province and presses distinct demands, such as language rights or a secession movement.
- Strength: a tendency across a small number of cases, not a law; a coming-together federation can still grant some asymmetry, and a holding-together one can choose symmetry.

**Wrong turns:** treating the typology as normative (which origin is better), when it is a claim about what origins tend to produce; saying that holding-together means the regions' powers are revocable, which confuses origin with the lock.

**Model answer:** (a) A assumes the provinces come together as former sovereigns, each with a veto on joining, so the bargain is struck on equal terms. B assumes a unitary Rhodania holding itself together by federalizing around a Vellish minority concentrated in Dunmore. The crux is which of those origins Rhodania has. (b) Ask whether the provinces were once separate polities that must each ratify, and whether Dunmore's minority presses distinct demands. Stepan's link is a tendency drawn from a few cases, not a reliable prediction.

---

**P3** *(Exegetical (a), strict · Evaluative (b), any verdict)*

**Must hit, strict (a):**

- Not federal: the clause is in an ordinary statute, so Dunmore holds no legal key; the system is still devolved.
- Under parliamentary supremacy, as stipulated, a later Act prevails over an earlier one, so on the orthodox view the abolition Act is valid despite the missing referendum. (Credit, not required: noting that a "manner and form" view holds that a parliament can bind its successors as to procedure, which would make validity a question for a court.)

**Must hit, any verdict (b):**

- The legal rule settles validity, not what happens: whether the majority actually abolishes the Assembly turns on political cost, such as reaction in Dunmore, the governing party's standing there, and the weight the referendum promise has acquired as a convention.
- Name what would decide in practice: for instance, whether the government can win a Dunmore referendum it chose to hold anyway, or whether a court entertains a manner-and-form challenge.

**Wrong turns:** treating "permanent" as entrenchment; concluding that the abolition Act is void without arguing that the stipulated supremacy has a procedural exception.

**Model answer (b), one of several:** The rule says only that the Act is valid. Whether the Assembly disappears depends on politics the rule does not reach: a promise written into statute raises the cost of breaking it, because Dunmore's voters and the opposition can call the abolition a breach of the constitution's spirit, and a government with few seats in Dunmore may pay little for that while one that needs them may pay a great deal. The referendum clause works as a commitment device only to the extent that breaking it is costly. A court might also be asked whether the clause binds as to procedure, which supremacy alone does not answer.

## Flashback

**From Lesson [4.3](04-03-parties-organization-and-discipline.md) (Parties: organization and discipline):** *(Formal (a)-(b) · Exegetical (c).)* Invented case. In the invented country of Calvara, the governing Kite party has 90 MPs. On a recorded division, 70 vote yes, 14 vote no and 6 abstain. (a) Compute Kite's Rice index. (b) For the next comparable vote, the whips aim for an index of at least 0.90, with every other Kite MP voting as before. What is the smallest number of the 14 no-voters who must (i) switch to yes, or instead (ii) abstain? (c) In two sentences or fewer, say what (b) shows about reading the Rice index as a measure of party discipline.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Worked arithmetic (a)-(b):**

The Rice index is $R = |Y - N|/(Y + N)$, with abstentions excluded.

(a) $R = |70 - 14|/(70 + 14) = 56/84 = 2/3 \approx 0.667$.

(b)(i) Each switch moves one vote from no to yes, so $Y + N$ stays 84 and the margin grows by 2. With $n$ no-voters left, $R = (84 - 2n)/84 \ge 0.9$ requires $n \le 4.2$, so $n = 4$: **10 must switch**, giving $R = 76/84 \approx 0.905$ (nine switches give $74/84 \approx 0.881$).

(b)(ii) Each abstention removes a no vote from the count, so $R = (70 - n)/(70 + n) \ge 0.9$ requires $7 \ge 1.9n$, so $n \le 3.68$ and $n = 3$: **11 must abstain**, giving $R = 67/73 \approx 0.918$ (ten abstentions give $66/74 \approx 0.892$).

**Must hit, strict (c):**

- Rebels who merely stay away raise the index almost as much as rebels brought into line (11 against 10), because the index drops abstainers.
- So a high index records cohesion among those who voted. It cannot tell members the whips overruled from dissenters who sat out, or either from members who agree: cohesion is an observed outcome, discipline a claim about sanctions.

**Wrong turns:** putting abstainers in the denominator, $56/90 \approx 0.622$; counting a switch as moving the margin by one vote rather than two; reading (b) as showing that abstention "doesn't count", when it raises the index.

**Model answer (c):** Ten switchers or eleven abstainers carry Kite to the same target, so an index that ignores abstentions rewards dissenters who stay home nearly as much as dissenters whipped into a yes. A Rice index of 0.9 therefore measures how united the voting members were, not whether discipline produced it.

</details>
