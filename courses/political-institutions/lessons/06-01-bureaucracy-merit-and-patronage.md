# Political Institutions · Lesson 6.1: Bureaucracy: merit and patronage

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 6: Delegation, direct democracy, and the whole design · Builds on: [3.1 Parliamentary government](03-01-parliamentary-government.md), [5.4 Weak-form review, appointments and independence](05-04-weak-form-review-appointments-and-independence.md) · Unlocks: [6.2 Delegation and oversight](06-02-delegation-and-oversight.md)

## Why this matters

When a US administration changes, the top layers of every department change hands. When a British government changes, the permanent secretary who ran a department on Friday runs it for the new minister on Monday. Most of what a state does is done by people nobody elected, so the rules for hiring and firing them decide whom they answer to. This lesson compares the two classic answers, patronage and merit, and the layered systems democracies actually run. Why legislatures hand these officials discretion, and how they police it, is [6.2](06-02-delegation-and-oversight.md)'s question.

## The idea

A civil service is defined by **two rules**, not one ([two rules of a civil service](../reference.md#two-rules-of-a-civil-service)):

1. **The appointment rule:** who chooses the official? A politician at discretion, or an open competition run by someone else?
2. **The removal rule:** can the official be dismissed at will, or only for cause through a protected procedure?

**[Patronage](../reference.md#patronage-and-merit)** combines discretion with removal at will. The official owes the job to a patron and keeps it by serving that patron. **Merit** combines open competition with removal only for cause. The official owes the job to no one in particular. A third variable is **depth**: how far down the hierarchy the political layer reaches.

Herbert Kaufman ("Emerging Conflicts in the Doctrines of Public Administration", 1956) named the values in tension. **Neutral competence** is expertise that serves any government. **Responsiveness** (his term was "executive leadership") is an administration that does what the elected executive wants ([neutral competence and responsiveness](../reference.md#neutral-competence-and-responsiveness)). An official the government cannot fire is hard to capture and also hard to steer. Every design picks a point on that trade-off.

Max Weber's ideal type of bureaucracy (officials appointed by qualification, salaried, on a career, bound by rules) is sociology, owned by [`social-theory`](../../social-theory/syllabus.md) 4.4. Here the same features are treated as design choices.

## The mechanism

The chain runs from rule to behaviour to outcome. Steps 1–2 are **mechanical**; steps 3–4 are **empirical**.

1. **The appointment rule fixes who the official owes the job to.**
2. **The removal rule fixes whose displeasure the official must avoid.** Mechanically, this is the rule that decides whom the official answers to day to day.
3. **Behaviour follows.** A patronage official works for the patron. Before 1883, US federal appointees raised campaign funds, and parties took a share of officials' fees. A merit official has reason to invest in skills that pay off under any government, and can afford frank advice.
4. **Outcomes follow:** competence, corruption, and responsiveness to elected leaders.

The four reference systems, as of 2026:

- **United Kingdom.** The Northcote–Trevelyan Report (published February 1854) recommended entry by competitive examination, and a Civil Service Commission was created in 1855. The rule is now statutory. The Constitutional Reform and Governance Act 2010, s.10(2), reads: "A person's selection must be on merit on the basis of fair and open competition." The civil service code must require officials to serve the administration of the day, whatever its political complexion (s.7(2)). The exception is **[special advisers](../reference.md#special-advisers)**. A minister selects one personally, the Prime Minister approves, and the appointment ends when that minister leaves office, or at the latest just after the next general election (s.15). Advisers are exempt from the duty of impartiality (s.7(5)), but by statute they may not authorise spending or manage civil servants (s.8(5)).
- **United States.** Under the nineteenth-century spoils system, jobs went to party loyalists. President Garfield was shot in 1881 by a disappointed office-seeker and died that September. The **[Pendleton Act](../reference.md#pendleton-act)** (signed 16 January 1883) required competitive examinations and banned firing on political grounds for covered posts. At first it covered about a tenth of federal jobs, and later presidents extended it. Today a career service sits beneath several layers of political appointees, and the most senior need Senate confirmation.
- **Germany.** Basic Law Art. 33(2): "Every German shall be equally eligible for any public office according to his aptitude, qualifications and professional achievements." Career officials (*Beamte*) normally serve for life, under the traditional principles of the professional civil service (Art. 33(5)). But the top two ranks of a ministry, state secretaries and directors-general, are **[political civil servants](../reference.md#political-civil-servants)**. The Federal President may send them into temporary retirement at any time (Federal Civil Servants Act, s.54).
- **France** recruits by competitive examination (the *concours*). Its school for senior officials, the ENA (founded 1945), was replaced by the INSP on 1 January 2022.

**Where the evidence is weakest.** The best-known cross-national study is Dahlström, Lapuente and Teorell, "The Merit of Meritocratization" (*Political Research Quarterly*, 2012). Across 52 countries, they found meritocratic *recruitment* associated with less corruption, even after controlling for many alternative explanations. Career stability, competitive salaries and internal promotion showed no significant effect. Evans and Rauch (*American Sociological Review*, 1999) associated "Weberian" features with faster growth in developing countries. These are robust correlations, but the direction of cause is contested: cleaner, richer states may be the ones that adopt merit. Stronger designs exist within single systems. Xu (*American Economic Review*, 2018) found that British colonial governors connected to their superior were promoted to higher-paid governorships and then raised less revenue and invested less, and the gap vanished once patronage appointments were abolished. Colonnelli, Prem and Teso (*AER*, 2020) found that in Brazil, political connections strongly determine public hiring and select less competent people. The other side has evidence too. Toral (*American Journal of Political Science*, 2024) found that Brazilian municipal patronage can *raise* accountability and service delivery, through monitoring and trust. On why merit gets adopted at all, Ting, Snyder, Hirano and Folke model insulation as insurance that incumbents buy when they expect to lose, and find partisan competition rising before US states adopted civil service reform. The evidence is weakest on the trade-off itself. Much of the corruption evidence comes from perception measures, much of the stronger evidence comes from developing or historical states, and no one has a good cross-national measure of responsiveness to weigh against competence. Whether those findings bear on the case these designs are best known from, rich democracies, is an open question. How they were identified belongs to [`empirical-political-economy`](../../empirical-political-economy/syllabus.md).

## The chain

![Three rows of four boxes, read left to right: appointment rule, removal rule, answers to, after an election. Patronage post, as in US federal posts before 1883: the party or patron picks freely; removal at will; answers to the patron and party; replaced by the winner's people. Career merit post, as in the UK, German and French career services: open competition by exam or commission; removal only for cause by set procedure; answers to the law and the government of the day; stays and serves the new government. Political layer, as in US appointees and UK special advisers: chosen by the minister or president; serves at pleasure with a term tied to the patron; answers to the elected executive; leaves with the outgoing government. ## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean): the morning after.** Invented case. Vespera's National Renewal party wins a general election. Three officials in the Ministry of Transport learn their fate.

| Official | Appointment rule | Removal rule | After the election |
|---|---|---|---|
| Harbour inspector, named by the outgoing minister | minister's discretion | at will | replaced by the new minister's nominee |
| Bridge engineer, hired through the Public Service Board's exam | open competition | only for misconduct, after a tribunal hearing | stays and serves the new minister |
| Deputy minister, chosen by the outgoing president | president's discretion | at pleasure | leaves with the old government |

The inspector's post is patronage, the engineer's is merit, and the deputy minister belongs to the political layer. In each case the removal rule decides who stays: only the engineer's job is protected by a procedure the new government cannot skip.

**Example 2 (hard): changing the removal rule without touching recruitment.** Vespera's Civil Service Act requires recruitment "on merit by open competition" and protects career officials from dismissal except for cause. It also lets the president except "positions of a policy-making character" from that protection. A new president excepts 6,000 posts held by exam-recruited analysts, then dismisses 400 of them.

Is Vespera now a patronage system? The appointment rule is untouched, so these posts are still filled by competition. The removal rule has changed for a band of posts that is wider than Germany's two top ranks. By step 2 of the mechanism, these analysts now answer to the president. **The rule runs out at the definition.** The Act never says what "policy-making character" means or who may contest a classification, so the line between the career service and the political layer is drawn by the executive it is supposed to constrain. The US has a live version of this case. Federal law exempts from removal protections positions "of a confidential, policy-determining, policy-making or policy-advocating character" (5 U.S.C. 7511(b)(2)). An October 2020 executive order created "Schedule F" on that basis. It was revoked in January 2021, reinstated in January 2025, later renamed Schedule Policy/Career, and given an implementing order in June 2026. Its effects are too recent to have been measured.

## Watch out

- **You might think merit means tenure, but actually there are two rules.** Germany's political civil servants are recruited by merit and removable at will. The cross-national corruption finding attaches to recruitment, not to job security.
- **You might think "merit systems are less corrupt" is part of what the rule does, but actually that claim is empirical.** Mechanically, the rule fixes only who chooses and who can fire. The corruption link is a robust cross-national correlation with a contested direction of cause.
- **You might think UK special advisers are Britain's version of US political appointees, but actually they differ in depth and power.** US appointees run agencies and direct career staff. By statute, special advisers advise and may not manage civil servants or authorise spending.

## One-liner

> A civil service is two rules, who appoints and who can remove; the removal rule decides whom officials answer to, and whether merit buys competence at the price of responsiveness is an empirical question with better evidence on the first half than the second.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢) *(Exegetical (a)-(b).)*** Diagnose. An **invented** news report from Vespera:

> "Three weeks after the Unity Front won the presidency, the new Minister of Works dismissed 1,100 road-depot managers and appointed replacements from party branch lists. Engineers in the same ministry, hired through the Public Service Board's examinations, keep their posts, but the minister has moved eleven of them from headquarters to remote provincial offices. The ministry's two directors-general were placed in early retirement on full pension the day after the inauguration, with no reason given."

(a) For each of the three groups, name the appointment and removal rules the report implies, and the kind of post (patronage, career merit, political layer or hybrid). One line per group. (b) The opposition calls the engineers' transfers "patronage by the back door". What would a rule have to cover to prevent them, and why does a removal protection alone not do it? Two sentences.

**P2 (🟡) *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)*** Find the crux. Two **invented** memos to Vespera's cabinet. Memo A: "Extend presidential appointment to the top three tiers of every ministry, so that the voters' choice reaches the machinery that carries it out." Memo B: "Confine political appointment to ministers and a few advisers with no management powers, so that expertise and frank advice survive every change of government." (a) Name the single trade-off the memos disagree about, and the empirical premise each must hold about how officials behave. Two sentences. (b) Name one piece of evidence that would move each side, and say how strong such evidence could be. 100 words or fewer; any verdict passes.

<details>
<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical (a)-(b), strict)*

**Must hit, strict (a):**

- Road-depot managers: appointment at the minister's discretion from party lists, removal at will; patronage.
- Engineers: appointed by open competition, protected from dismissal (they kept their posts); career merit.
- Directors-general: removable at any time without reason, but retired on pension rather than sacked, at the top of a ministry; a political layer, and if they were career-recruited, the German-style hybrid (political civil servants, s.54).

**Must hit, strict (b):**

- A protection against dismissal does not cover *where* an official serves. Punitive postings and blocked promotions are a second channel of political control.
- To close it, postings and promotions would need to be governed by rules or decided by an independent body (such as the Public Service Board), not by the minister's discretion.

**Wrong turns:** calling the whole ministry "patronage" because one group was purged; the report shows three regimes side by side. Calling the directors-general "dismissed for cause": no reason was given, which is the hallmark of removal at will.

**Model answer:** (a) Depot managers: discretionary appointment and removal at will, so patronage. Engineers: open competition and protection from dismissal, so career merit. Directors-general: removable at any time on pension, so the political layer, or a German-style hybrid if they were recruited by exam. (b) The engineers' protection covers only dismissal, so a minister who cannot fire them can still punish them by posting. Preventing that needs a rule that also takes transfers and promotions out of the minister's hands, for example by giving them to the Public Service Board.

---

**P2** *(Exegetical (a), strict · Evaluative (b), any verdict)*

**Must hit, strict (a):**

- The crux is responsiveness against neutral competence: how far the elected executive should be able to steer the administration, against how much expertise and impartiality should survive changes of government.
- Memo A must hold that career officials drift from, or obstruct, elected priorities, and that appointees can be competent enough.
- Memo B must hold that appointees are less competent or more corruptible, and that frank advice needs job security.

**Must hit, any verdict (b):**

- One piece of evidence per side, each bearing on that side's premise. Examples: performance of similar programmes run by appointees versus career managers, within one country; implementation speed after transitions; cross-national merit and corruption data; within-country patronage studies like those on colonial governors or Brazil.
- State the strength: cross-national correlations cannot settle the direction of cause, and within-country designs are stronger but may not travel to other states.

**Wrong turns:** stating a verdict ("merit is obviously better") instead of the trade-off. Treating the dispute as mechanical: both memos agree on what the rules do and disagree about how officials behave under them.

**Model answer (a):** The memos disagree about responsiveness against neutral competence: how far elected leaders should steer the machinery, against how much expertise and frank advice should outlast them. Memo A must believe career officials drift from or resist elected priorities while appointees are competent enough, and Memo B must believe appointees are less competent or more corruptible and that frank advice needs secure tenure.

**Model answer (b), one of several:** Memo A would be shaken by evidence that, within one country, programmes run by political appointees perform worse than comparable ones run by career managers. Memo B would be shaken by evidence that the career service delays or dilutes a new government's policies more than appointees do, or by findings like Toral's that political ties improve service delivery. Within-country comparisons of this kind are the stronger evidence, but each speaks for one state and period. Cross-national correlations travel further but cannot show which way the cause runs.

## Flashback

**From Lesson [5.3](05-03-judicial-review-compared.md) (Judicial review compared):** *(Formal (a) · Exegetical (b).)* Apply to a new case. Quillmar (invented) has a single-chamber Assembly of 312 members: Marram 130 and Wicker 54 form the governing coalition; Nettle 70, Pumice 40 and Tamarisk 18 are in opposition. The coalition passes a Data Retention Act, and all three opposition parties vote against it. A reform commission is choosing between two clauses, each of which would be the **only** route to Quillmar's constitutional court. **Clause F** copies France's Art. 61 referral as 5.3 describes it: 60 members may refer a passed bill before promulgation. **Clause G** copies Germany's abstract review: one quarter of all members may apply against a promulgated statute. (a) Under each clause, which opposition parties could bring the Act on their own, and which only in combination? Show the thresholds. (b) The Act is promulgated before any opposition party moves. Under each clause, can the parties from (a) still bring it, and what does (or would) a successful challenge do to the Act? Two sentences.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Worked arithmetic (a):**

Clause F needs a fixed 60 members, under a fifth of this chamber. Clause G needs a quarter of all members: $312/4 = 78$.

| Opposition group | Seats | F (at least 60) | G (at least 78) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nettle | 70 | yes | no |
| Pumice | 40 | no | no |
| Tamarisk | 18 | no | no |
| Nettle + Pumice | 110 | yes | yes |
| Nettle + Tamarisk | 88 | yes | yes |
| Pumice + Tamarisk | 58 | no | no |
| All three | 128 | yes | yes |

Under F, Nettle can refer alone; Pumice and Tamarisk cannot, even together (58, two short). Under G, no party can apply alone; Nettle with either partner can, and Pumice with Tamarisk never can. Under both clauses every qualifying group includes Nettle. The rule that decides is the standing threshold set against the opposition's seats.

**Must hit, strict (b):**

- Clause F: **no.** Its door is a priori and closes at promulgation, and F is the only route, so the Act can now never be reviewed. A timely referral that succeeded would have meant the Act was never promulgated or implemented.
- Clause G: **yes.** Abstract review lies against promulgated statutes, so Nettle with Pumice or Tamarisk can still apply, with no dispute in sight. A win voids the Act, or declares it incompatible and leaves the legislature to repair it, binding on every court and authority.
- The two clauses are both concentrated and both abstract; they differ only on the a priori versus a posteriori axis.

**Wrong turns:** taking a quarter of those voting, or of the opposition's 128, instead of all 312 members; counting Pumice + Tamarisk (58) as reaching 60; reading "abstract" as "before promulgation", which closes G's door by mistake (5.3's first Watch out); reaching for a QPC or a constitutional complaint, which the stem excludes and which belong to litigants and burdened persons, not to parliamentary parties.

**Model answer (b):** Under Clause F, no: the referral window closed at promulgation, so the Act is now beyond review, whereas a timely referral that succeeded would have kept it from ever being promulgated or applied. Under Clause G, yes: abstract review reaches statutes already in force, so Nettle with Pumice (110) or Tamarisk (88) can apply against the 78 needed, and a win voids the Act or declares it incompatible, binding on every court and authority.

</details>
