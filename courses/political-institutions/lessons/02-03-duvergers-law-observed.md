# Political Institutions · Lesson 2.3: Duverger's law, observed

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 2: What electoral systems do · Builds on: [1.1 Anatomy of an electoral system](01-01-anatomy-of-an-electoral-system-plurality-and-runoff.md), [2.1 Measuring outcomes](02-01-measuring-outcomes.md), [2.2 District magnitude and thresholds](02-02-district-magnitude-and-thresholds.md) · Unlocks: [2.4 Districts and electoral reform](02-04-districts-and-electoral-reform.md), [4.4 Party systems](04-04-party-systems.md)

## Why this matters

Ask why the United States has two parties and someone will say "Duverger's law". Maurice Duverger, in *Les partis politiques* (1951), proposed that plurality elections in single-member districts favour a two-party system, and said the relationship came close to a true sociological law. It is one of the best-known regularities in political science. It is also routinely "refuted" by Canada, India and the United Kingdom. Both can be true once you see what the law is about: **districts**, not nations, and two separate engines, one arithmetic and one human.

## The idea

Duverger named two forces, the **[mechanical and psychological effects](../reference.md#mechanical-and-psychological-effects)**. The mechanical effect is what the count does to a party's votes: under [first-past-the-post](../reference.md#first-past-the-post), a party that finishes third everywhere wins nothing, however many votes it piles up. The psychological effect is what people do once they expect that: voters who prefer the third party switch to the more acceptable of the two front-runners rather than waste their vote, and donors, activists and would-be candidates make the same calculation earlier.

Keep the kinds of claim apart. The mechanical effect is **mechanical**: given the votes, the seats follow by arithmetic. The psychological effect is **empirical**: a claim about how people respond, and it can fail. That plurality yields two parties *nationally* is a further empirical claim that needs a further step. Whether a system that wastes so many votes is unfair is **normative** and not argued here; Mill's complaint that winner-take-all districts shut every minority out is read in [`history-of-political-thought` 6.4](../../history-of-political-thought/lessons/06-04-mill-representative-government.md).

Invented Hesperia shows how the pieces come apart. It elects 100 MPs from 100 equal single-member districts. Hearth (H) and Forum (F) run everywhere; Lantern (L) also runs everywhere and is third almost everywhere; Boreal (B) runs only in the North, where it is strong. The figure below shows four parties nationally, an [effective number of parties](../reference.md#effective-number-of-parties) of 3.09 by votes, and yet only two parties in contention for the seat in any district.

## The mechanism

1. **The rule (mechanical).** One seat per district ($M = 1$); the candidate with the most votes wins ([1.1](01-01-anatomy-of-an-electoral-system-plurality-and-runoff.md)).
2. **The mechanical effect (mechanical).** In each district, every vote not cast for the winner elects no one. These are **[wasted votes](../reference.md#wasted-votes)**. For the psychological effect the ones that matter are votes cast for losing candidates, and this lesson counts only those; the convention [2.4](02-04-districts-and-electoral-reform.md) uses for districting also counts each winner's votes beyond what it needed. Summed across districts, the penalty falls on parties whose votes are *spread*: a party that is third in every district wins no seats, whatever its national share. A party whose votes are *concentrated* escapes the penalty and may be over-rewarded.

   *In words:* the count punishes being everywhere a little, not being small.

3. **The psychological effect (empirical).** Voters, and before them donors and candidates, anticipate step 2 and abandon parties they expect to lose *in their district*. The incentive is sharpest where a vote can change the result: the top two are close, and the third party's voters are numerous enough to decide between them.
4. **The district prediction (empirical).** Gary Cox (*Making Votes Count*, 1997) generalized this into the **[M plus 1 rule](../reference.md#m-plus-1-rule)**: in a district electing $M$ members, strategic desertion tends to leave at most $M+1$ viable candidates, so two under first-past-the-post. Why that is an equilibrium of strategic voting, and when it is not, is [`political-economy` 2.5](../../political-economy/lessons/02-05-strategic-voting-and-duvergers-law.md)'s to derive. Here it is an observed regularity: fairly robust at the district level, with known departures.
5. **The national step (empirical, and not supplied by the rule).** A national two-party system needs the *same* two parties to be the pair in every district. Cox called this cross-district coordination **[party linkage](../reference.md#party-linkage)**. Pradeep Chhibber and Ken Kollman (*The Formation of National Party Systems*, 2004) argue that it depends on how much power sits at the centre: the more the national government decides, the stronger the pull to join a national party. Nothing in the plurality rule fixes which pair forms where.
6. **The converse (empirical, weaker).** Duverger also proposed that PR and two-round elections favour multipartism. William Riker's review ("The Two-Party System and Duverger's Law", *American Political Science Review*, 1982) separated this "hypothesis" from the "law" and judged it the weaker claim. The usual reading now is that PR *permits* many parties rather than producing them. Malta elects its House by the [single transferable vote](../reference.md#single-transferable-vote) in 13 five-member districts (as of 2026) and has a nearly pure two-party system, Labour and the Nationalists. William Clark and Matt Golder (*Comparative Political Studies*, 2006) found that the number of parties rises with a society's social diversity only where the electoral system is permissive enough to let it.

*In words:* [Duverger's law](../reference.md#duvergers-law) is two engines acting on each district; whether the nation ends up with two parties is a separate question about linkage.

**Where the evidence is weakest.** At the psychological effect, and at the jump from district to nation. A third party's falling vote is what desertion predicts, but it is also what a plain loss of popularity predicts, and election results alone cannot tell the two apart; surveys that ask for first preferences are the usual tool, and estimates of how much strategic voting occurs vary across countries and elections. The district version of the law is the strong one; the national version has prominent exceptions, shown below. The arrow may also run backwards, since rulers who already lead two dominant parties have reasons to keep plurality, which [2.4](02-04-districts-and-electoral-reform.md) takes up. Identifying any of these effects causally belongs to [`empirical-political-economy`](../../empirical-political-economy/syllabus.md); this lesson reports how strong the evidence is, not how it is identified.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean): the mechanical effect in Hesperia.** Take the votes as given and run the count:

| Party | Vote % | Seats of 100 | Seat % ÷ vote % | Own votes cast for losers |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Hearth | 39.88 | 44 | 1.10 | 45.9% |
| Forum | 37.12 | 36 | 0.97 | 50.5% |
| Lantern | 12.00 | 0 | 0 | 100% |
| Boreal | 11.00 | 20 | 1.82 | 0% |

Each entry is arithmetic. Hearth's votes cast for losers are its share in the districts it lost, 37% in 36 southern districts and 25% in 20 northern ones:

$$36 \times 37 + 20 \times 25 = 1{,}832 \text{ of } 3{,}988 \text{ district-percent} = 45.9\%.$$

Lantern and Boreal carry the lesson: almost the same vote, and the spread party gets nothing while the concentrated party is over-represented by a factor of 1.82. **The rule that decides** is plurality in single-member districts; national vote share never enters it. Of all votes cast, 49.1% elected no one.

Real elections have the same shape. In the 2015 UK general election, UKIP won about one vote in eight across the UK and one seat, while the SNP, standing only in Scotland, won half the Scottish vote and 56 of Scotland's 59 seats. In Canada's 1993 election the Bloc Québécois, which runs candidates only in Quebec, won about half the Quebec vote, 54 seats and the status of Official Opposition. Concentration shelters a regional party from the mechanical effect.

**Example 2 (hard): where the law strains.** Nationally, Hesperia has three large caucuses under pure plurality (effective number 2.75 by seats). District by district it looks Duvergerian: effective number by votes 2.46, 2.43 and 2.59 in the three district types, with two contenders in each. The pair differs by region, Hearth–Forum in the South and Boreal–Hearth in the North. India is the standard real case of this pattern: many parties nationally, with state-level contests whose leading pair differs from state to state, a central case for Chhibber and Kollman.

Now press on step 3. In the North, Forum, a major national party, is the district's *fourth* party at 8%. The M plus 1 rule says Forum's and Lantern's northern voters should desert. The North's arithmetic gives them little reason: if every one of them moved to Hearth, Hearth would have $25 + 8 + 12 = 45$ against Boreal's 55. A vote that cannot change the result is wasted whichever way it is cast, so the wasted-vote logic predicts *weak* desertion exactly where Boreal is safest. Contrast the 44 southern districts Hearth wins, where Lantern's 12 points exceed the 10-point gap between Hearth and Forum: there Lantern's voters can decide the seat, and the pressure to desert is real. The rule settles each seat; it stops determining the party system at two points it does not control: voters' expectations about who is close, and a national party's choice to keep running where it cannot win.

Germany offers a test inside one booth. Its two-vote ballot ([1.5](01-05-mixed-systems-mmp-and-parallel.md)) gives each voter a constituency vote, counted by plurality, and a list vote. In the 2025 federal election (official results), the CDU's and the SPD's constituency-vote shares exceeded their list-vote shares by 2.9 and 3.7 points, while the Greens, the FDP and Die Linke each took a smaller share of the constituency vote than of the list vote. That is the pattern the psychological effect predicts, though candidates' personal appeal feeds it too.

## Watch out

- **You might think the mechanical effect is why a third party's vote falls, but actually the mechanical effect converts votes into seats *within* one election.** A vote that falls *between* elections is, if it is strategic, the psychological effect: people responding to the mechanical one. Conflating them is the commonest misreading of results.
- **You might think Canada or India refutes Duverger's law, but actually they refute its national version.** The district version, two serious candidates per seat, is the claim the evidence supports best, and regional parties can satisfy it in every district while the nation has many parties.
- **You might think the law says plurality *causes* two parties and PR *causes* many, but actually the first is a tendency and the second weaker still.** PR permits multipartism (Malta has two parties under STV), and the number of parties also tracks a society's divisions, which [4.4](04-04-party-systems.md) takes up.

## One-liner

> Plurality punishes spread-out parties by arithmetic and persuades their voters to leave by expectation; that yields two serious candidates per district, but only linkage makes them the same two parties everywhere.

## Problems

**P1 (🟡) *(Formal (a) · Exegetical (b)-(c).)*** Diagnose. Port Hesper, Hesperia's capital, elects its council from 20 equal wards by plurality. Lantern won both of its two stronghold wards in each of two elections. Invented results:

| Lantern's vote share | Election 1 | Election 2 |
|---|---|---|
| 2 stronghold wards (each) | 40% | 39% |
| 18 other wards (each) | 20% | 11% |

An **invented** report, not the words of any real person or outlet:

> "The ward system punished Lantern twice. First it turned 22 percent of the vote into 2 of the 20 seats. Then it cut Lantern's vote to 13.8 percent. Both are the mechanical effect of first-past-the-post."

(a) For each election, compute Lantern's citywide vote share, its seat share, seat share ÷ vote share, and the share of Lantern's votes cast in wards it lost. (b) Which of the report's two "punishments" is the mechanical effect, and what is the other one, if it is anything? Two sentences. (c) The ward pattern fits strategic desertion, but it also fits another explanation. Name that explanation and one piece of evidence that would separate the two. 80 words or fewer.

**P2 (🟡) *(Exegetical (a)-(b).)*** Find the crux. Two **invented** commentators on Hesperia's election, not the words of any real person:

> **Commentator 1:** "Duverger's law is a law. Plurality produces two parties, and Hesperia's districts show it."
>
> **Commentator 2:** "Hesperia has three large parties in parliament under pure plurality. The law is a tendency that regional concentration defeats."

(a) Name the single premise about the law that one commentator must affirm and the other deny. One sentence. (b) Say what evidence would move the dispute, and what evidence would count against the law even on the reading that saves it. 80 words or fewer.

<details>
<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Formal (a) · Exegetical (b)-(c))*

(a) With 20 equal wards, the citywide share is the average over wards.

| | Election 1 | Election 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Vote share | $(2 \times 40 + 18 \times 20)/20 = 22.0\%$ | $(2 \times 39 + 18 \times 11)/20 = 13.8\%$ |
| Seat share | $2/20 = 10\%$ | $2/20 = 10\%$ |
| Seat ÷ vote | $10/22.0 = 0.45$ | $10/13.8 = 0.72$ |
| Votes in lost wards | $360/440 = 81.8\%$ | $198/276 = 71.7\%$ |

The ratio *rises* in election 2 with the rule unchanged, because the remaining vote is more concentrated in the wards Lantern wins.

**Must hit, strict (b):**

- The first punishment, 22% of the votes becoming 10% of the seats, is the mechanical effect: the count converting a fixed set of votes into seats within one election.
- The fall from 22.0% to 13.8% is a change in votes between elections, so it is not mechanical; if it is strategic, it is the psychological effect, voters anticipating the mechanical one.

**Must hit, strict (c):**

- The pattern: Lantern lost 1 point (2.5% of its vote) in its strongholds and 9 points (45%) where it could not win, the signature of desertion.
- The rival explanation: a genuine fall in Lantern's popularity that happens to hit its weaker, softer areas hardest.
- One separating test, for example: a survey asking first preferences (deserters still rank Lantern first; disaffected voters do not); whether Lantern's lost votes went to the two front-runners in each ward; or Lantern's vote on a simultaneous proportional ballot, where desertion has no point.

**Wrong turns:** calling both changes mechanical because the rule "caused" both; reading the higher ratio in election 2 as the rule treating Lantern better, when only the geography of its vote changed; treating the ward pattern alone as proof of desertion.

**Model answer (c):** A national swing against Lantern, falling hardest on its softer supporters in weak wards, would produce the same numbers without anyone voting strategically. A survey asking voters which party they most prefer separates the two: if many voters who switched still put Lantern first, they deserted; if their first preference changed, Lantern lost support.

---

**P2** *(Exegetical (a)-(b))*

**Must hit, strict (a):**

- The crux is the law's unit: whether it predicts the number of serious candidates in each district (Commentator 1 must affirm this, since Hesperia's districts have two contenders each and its national three-partism then falls outside the law, as a linkage failure) or the national party system (Commentator 2 reads it so, which makes Hesperia a counterexample).

**Must hit, strict (b):**

- Evidence that moves it: district-level effective numbers of candidates in plurality countries with regional parties. If they sit near two almost everywhere, including regional strongholds, the district law stands and national exceptions need a linkage account (for instance, Chhibber and Kollman's centralization).
- Evidence against the law even at district level: persistent three-way races where voters can tell who is trailing, or third-party voters who stay put when pivotal, as Lantern's could be in Hesperia's 44 Hearth-won southern districts.

**Wrong turns:** making the crux a fact both accept (that Boreal exists, or that Hesperia has three caucuses); answering which electoral system is better, which is normative and not asked; treating national effective number of parties as settling the district claim.

**Model answer (b):** Count effective candidates district by district. Near two everywhere, with regional parties among the pairs, favours Commentator 1 and moves the national puzzle to linkage. Lasting three-way district races where polls show a clear third, or pivotal third-party voters who do not desert, would count against the law even on its district reading.

</details>

## Flashback

**From Lesson [2.1](02-01-measuring-outcomes.md) (Measuring outcomes):** *(Formal (a) · Exegetical (b).)* Diagnose. Invented numbers. Quillmark (invented) elects a 100-seat Assembly by plurality in single-member districts, so seats are percentages. In two elections the vote shares were identical: Moss 45, Flax 35, Sorrel 20 percent. Seats in the first election: Moss 55, Flax 35, Sorrel 10. In the second: Moss 40, Flax 45, Sorrel 15. An **invented** editorial, not the words of any real person or outlet:

> "Both elections scored a Loosemore–Hanby index of 10. The voting system distorted them equally, so it treated the parties the same way both times."

(a) Compute LH and LSq for each election. (b) In three sentences or fewer: which part of the editorial the LH figure supports, why LSq disagrees, and what the seat table shows that neither index records.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

(a) Gaps $d_i = s_i - v_i$:

| Election | Moss | Flax | Sorrel | LH | LSq |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| First | $+10$ | $0$ | $-10$ | $\tfrac12(10+0+10) = 10.00$ | $\sqrt{\tfrac12(100+0+100)} = 10.00$ |
| Second | $-5$ | $+10$ | $-5$ | $\tfrac12(5+10+5) = 10.00$ | $\sqrt{\tfrac12(25+100+25)} = \sqrt{75} \approx 8.66$ |

**Must hit, strict (b):**

- LH supports "distorted them equally" in its own sense: both times 10 of the 100 seats would have to move to make the result exact.
- LSq disagrees (10.00 against 8.66) because of its weighting: in the first election one party's 10-point gain is one other party's 10-point loss, the case where LSq equals LH; in the second, Flax's gain is offset by two 5-point losses, which squaring counts for less.
- Neither index sees who gained, so "treated the parties the same way" fails: in the first election Moss turned 45 percent of the votes into 55 seats, a majority (51 needed) to govern alone; in the second, Flax won more seats than Moss on fewer votes, and no party has a majority.

**Wrong turns:** calling the editorial's LH figure wrong (it is correct; the index simply has no direction); reading LSq's higher score for the first election as registering Moss's manufactured majority, when LSq would also be exactly 10.00 if Flax had taken 10 points of seats from Moss; dropping the halves (LH 20, or LSq $\sqrt{200} \approx 14.14$).

**Model answer:** LH is right that the two results are equally far from proportional in seats to be moved, 10 each time. LSq ranks the first election worse because its distortion is concentrated in one gain and one loss, while the second spreads the loss across two parties. But the table shows what neither number records: the first election handed Moss a majority on 45 percent, the second put Flax ahead of Moss in seats on fewer votes and left no one able to govern alone.

</details>
