# Political Institutions · Lesson 5.2: Decentralization in practice

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 5: Dividing power: federalism and courts · Builds on: [5.1 Federal, unitary, devolved](05-01-federal-unitary-devolved.md), [4.1 Bicameralism](04-01-bicameralism.md) · Unlocks: [5.3 Judicial review compared](05-03-judicial-review-compared.md)

## Why this matters

[5.1](05-01-federal-unitary-devolved.md) sorted states by the rule that protects their regions: a federal constitution cannot cut regional powers without the regions' consent or a supermajority that includes them, while a devolved settlement is an ordinary statute. That label leaves most of daily practice open. The UK is unitary in law, yet since 1999 Westminster has held back from legislating on Scottish devolved matters without Holyrood's consent, until in 2018 it went ahead without it. Spain is not formally federal, yet two of its regions levy most of the taxes raised on their own territory. Germany is federal, yet the rate of the income tax its Länder share is set by federal law. This lesson takes the three questions the label leaves open: does every region get the same deal, who administers the law, and who pays.

## The idea

Daniel Elazar described federalism as "self-rule plus shared rule": regions govern some things alone, and take part in governing the whole. Designs mix the two very differently. Scotland has wide self-rule in devolved fields and almost no shared rule beyond its MPs at Westminster. A German Land has little tax self-rule but strong shared rule, because its government sits in the Bundesrat ([4.1](04-01-bicameralism.md)).

Three dials, then, plus one question for devolved states:

- **Symmetry.** Do all regions hold the same powers? If not, the system is an **[asymmetric federalism](../reference.md#asymmetric-federalism)** (or asymmetric devolution).
- **Administration.** Does each level run its own programmes with its own officials, or do regions implement the centre's laws? That is **[administrative vs dual federalism](../reference.md#administrative-and-dual-federalism)**.
- **Money.** Who taxes and who spends? The gap at the regional level is **[vertical fiscal imbalance](../reference.md#vertical-fiscal-imbalance)**, bridged by transfers.
- **Protection without entrenchment.** If the settlement is a statute, what stops the centre rewriting it? In the UK, a convention.

## Source

Scotland Act 1998, s.28(7)–(8), subsection (8) inserted by the Scotland Act 2016, s.2 (legislation.gov.uk, Open Government Licence):

> (7) This section does not affect the power of the Parliament of the United Kingdom to make laws for Scotland.
>
> (8) But it is recognised that the Parliament of the United Kingdom will not normally legislate with regard to devolved matters without the consent of the Scottish Parliament.

Read the verbs. Subsection (7) is a rule of law: Westminster's power to legislate for Scotland is untouched, which is [parliamentary sovereignty](../reference.md#parliamentary-sovereignty) restated. Subsection (8) does not say "shall not". It says "it is recognised" that Parliament "will not normally" legislate, which reports a practice and adds an exception it never defines. In *Miller* (2017) the UK Supreme Court read these words exactly so: Parliament had recognised the **[Sewel convention](../reference.md#sewel-convention)** as a political convention, not turned it into a rule the courts could interpret or enforce, and policing it lies outside the judiciary's role. *(Mechanical: what the text says and how the court read it.)*

## The mechanism

1. **Devolution is a grant by statute.** Separate Acts set up the Scottish Parliament, the Welsh Senedd and the Northern Ireland Assembly, each with a different list of powers. England has no devolved legislature; Westminster legislates for it directly. *In words:* UK devolution is asymmetric by construction, because each settlement was negotiated separately.
2. **The settlement is held by convention.** Under Sewel, the UK government asks the devolved legislature for a legislative consent motion before Westminster legislates on a devolved matter. In practice the UK government has also sought consent for bills that change the devolved institutions' powers. The sanction for ignoring a refusal is political (relations with the devolved government, votes in Scotland), not legal.
3. **Asymmetry can be designed in.** Spain's 1978 Constitution created no regions; it let provinces opt into autonomy by a fast route to full powers (taken by the Basque Country, Catalonia, Galicia and Andalusia) or the slower route of Article 143. The result, as of 2026, is 17 autonomous communities and two autonomous cities. The First Additional Provision states that the Constitution protects and respects the historic rights of the foral territories (my translation). On that footing the Basque Country (through its three historic territories, under the Economic Agreement) and Navarre (under its own Economic Agreement) levy most taxes themselves and pay the state an annual quota for the services it still provides there. The other communities are financed through the common system of shared taxes and equalization. *Mechanical consequence:* the quota is computed from the cost of the state's services, not from what the territory collects, so the two bear the risk of their own tax take.
4. **Who administers.** In **dual federalism** (the US model), each level runs its own programmes through its own officials. The federal government has its own field agencies, and US courts hold that Congress may not commandeer state officers to carry out federal programmes (*Printz v. United States*, 1997; the doctrine belongs to [`constitutional-law`](../../constitutional-law/syllabus.md)). In **administrative federalism** (the German model), the Federation legislates and the Länder implement. Basic Law Article 83 (official translation): "The Länder shall execute federal laws in their own right insofar as this Basic Law does not otherwise provide or permit." *In words:* the US divides policies between levels; Germany divides functions, with legislation above and administration below. That is why Länder governments care so much about the Bundesrat's consent rights over laws they must carry out.
5. **Money.** Central governments typically raise more than they spend on their own programmes, and regions spend more than they raise. One standard measure of the regional gap is

$$\mathrm{VFI} = 1 - \frac{R}{E},$$

where $R$ is regional own-source revenue and $E$ is regional spending: the share of regional spending not paid for by regional taxes. Transfers close the gap, as unconditional block grants, conditional or matching grants, formula shares of national taxes, and equalization. Germany's Basic Law splits income and corporation tax revenue equally between the Federation and the Länder (Article 106(3)) and equalizes among the Länder through their shares of turnover tax (Article 107(2)). Any federal law on a tax whose revenue goes partly to the Länder needs the Bundesrat's consent (Article 105(3)). Why some taxes belong at the centre, how grants change local choices, and why grant money "sticks" are [`public-economics` 8.2](../../public-economics/lessons/08-02-assignment-spillovers-and-grants.md)'s; mobility and tax competition are [8.1](../../public-economics/lessons/08-01-tiebout-voting-with-your-feet.md) and [8.3](../../public-economics/lessons/08-03-tax-competition.md). *Empirical:* Jonathan Rodden's *Hamilton's Paradox* argues that regions which depend on transfers and can also borrow come to expect central bailouts (a soft budget constraint). The evidence is comparative case studies and cross-national correlations: suggestive, and contested on causal direction. The US Congress declined to assume the state defaults of 1841–42 ([`history-of-debt` 4.1](../../history-of-debt/lessons/04-01-hamiltons-assumption-plan.md)); the euro area wrote a no-bailout clause into its treaty ([`history-of-debt` 6.2](../../history-of-debt/lessons/06-02-the-eurozone-and-greece.md)).

**Where the rule runs out.** Each arrangement fixes a default and leaves the exception to politics. Section 28(8) fixes that consent is normally sought; "normally" has no definition and, after *Miller*, no judge. Spain's provision protects historic fiscal rights, but the size of the quota is negotiated between the two governments. A VFI figure fixes how far regions depend on transfers. It does not say whether a transfer is a formula entitlement or a discretionary grant, or whether "own revenue" includes a tax whose rate the region cannot touch, and that is where regional autonomy is actually decided.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean): Saldovia's gap.** Invented numbers, billion crowns per year. Regions spend $E = 220$ and raise $R = 66$; the centre raises 330 and spends 180 on its own programmes. The regions do not borrow, so grants are $220 - 66 = 154$, and the centre runs a deficit of $180 + 154 - 330 = 4$.

| | Raises | Spends (own programmes) | Share of revenue | Share of spending |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Centre | 330 | 180 | 83.3% | 45.0% |
| Regions | 66 | 220 | 16.7% | 55.0% |

$$\mathrm{VFI} = 1 - \frac{66}{220} = 0.70$$

Seven-tenths of regional spending is paid for by the centre. Now the arithmetic that matters for a regional voter. Suppose the region wants 10% more spending (22 more) and the grants are fixed. The whole increase must come from its own taxes, so they rise by $22/66 = 33.3\%$. In general a spending rise of $g$ requires own revenue to rise by $g/(1 - \mathrm{VFI})$. *In words:* the higher the imbalance, the more a small spending choice moves regional tax rates. That gearing is mechanical. What it does to accountability is the empirical dispute: defenders of large transfers stress that equalization lets poor regions fund services; critics stress that voters cannot tell who to blame. Neither side's claim follows from the ratio alone.

**Example 2 (hard): consent refused, 2018.** The UK government accepted that the bill which became the European Union (Withdrawal) Act 2018 engaged the Sewel convention, since it altered devolved powers. The Senedd consented after concessions. The Scottish Parliament voted to withhold consent in May 2018, and Westminster enacted the bill anyway. Run the rule:

- *Was it "with regard to devolved matters"?* The UK government accepted that it was. The rule applied.
- *Was consent given?* No.
- *Does s.28(8) then forbid enactment?* No. Subsection (7) preserves Westminster's power, "normally" allows exceptions, and *Miller* puts the convention's operation beyond the courts.

The rule settled the procedure: consent had to be asked. It stopped at the question that mattered, whether Brexit was a circumstance that was not "normal", and that was settled by a Commons majority and contested in Scottish politics. Compare Germany. A federal bill that needs Bundesrat consent and fails to get it simply does not become law. Shared rule there is a legal veto; in the UK, consent is a convention.

## Watch out

- **You might think "federal" means decentralized and "unitary" means centralized, but actually the classification records which rule protects the regions, not how much they do.** Navarre, in a state that is not formally federal, sets and collects taxes a German Land cannot.
- **You might think a high VFI shows a centralized state, but actually VFI measures financing, not control.** A region can spend mostly grant money under rules it writes itself (an unconditional formula grant) or a little grant money tied to central conditions. Read the grant design before reading centralization off the ratio, and keep the mechanical ratio apart from the empirical claims about accountability and bailouts.
- **You might think *Miller* held that Westminster may ignore the Sewel convention, but actually it held only that courts will not enforce it.** Whether overriding a refusal is constitutionally proper is a political and normative question the judgment left alone.

## One-liner

> Federal, unitary and devolved name the rule that protects regions; in practice decentralization is set by three dials (symmetry, who administers, who pays), and in each case the text fixes a default ("will not normally", a negotiated quota, a VFI ratio) while politics decides the exceptions.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢) *(Exegetical (a)–(b).)*** Use s.28(7)–(8) as quoted in the Source section. Three **invented** UK bills; for this problem take it as given that defence is reserved and that education and housing are devolved.

- Bill A changes the rules for recruiting to the UK armed forces.
- Bill B sets a new curriculum for Scottish secondary schools.
- Bill C moves housing from the Scottish Parliament's powers onto the reserved list.

(a) For each bill, does the text of s.28(8) call for the Scottish Parliament's consent? One sentence each. (b) The Scottish Parliament refuses consent to Bill B, and Westminster passes it anyway. Is the Act valid in law, and could a court strike it down for breaching s.28(8)? Name the words that decide. Two sentences.

**P2 (🟡) *(Formal (a)–(c) · Exegetical (d).)*** Invented numbers, billion crowns per year. Norvale, a region of Saldovia, spends 40, raises 10 in its own taxes, receives a block grant of 30 and does not borrow.

(a) Compute Norvale's VFI. (b) Norvale wants to raise spending by 10% with the grant held fixed. By what percentage must its own tax revenue rise? (c) Reform 1: Saldovia hands Norvale a new tax yielding 12, with full power to set its rate, and cuts the grant by 12. Recompute the VFI and the answer to (b). (d) Reform 2 instead gives Norvale a fixed share of a national tax, yielding 12, whose rate Saldovia sets, and cuts the grant by 12. A report counts the share as "own revenue" and prints the same VFI as in (c). In two sentences, say what that number hides, using your answer to (b).

**P3 (🔴) *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)*** Apply to a hard case. Saldovia's (invented) constitution provides:

> "The Constitution protects the historic fiscal rights of Vellar. Vellar levies the taxes raised on its territory and pays the State a contribution for the services the State provides there, fixed by a law agreed between the two governments."

The other nine regions are financed by shared taxes and an equalization fund. In a recession Vellar's tax take falls 15%, while the other regions' grants are maintained. The central government proposes a law, which Vellar has not agreed, raising Vellar's contribution to help fund equalization.

(a) Classify the arrangement, say who bears the recession's revenue loss, and say whether the proposed law satisfies the constitutional text. Three sentences. (b) A deputy from another region argues that Vellar should pay into the equalization fund. In 120 words or fewer, state the strongest case on each side, name the trade-off, and say where the text stops determining the outcome. Any verdict passes.

<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical (a)–(b), strict)*

**Must hit, strict (a):**

- Bill A: no. Defence is reserved, so the bill is not "with regard to devolved matters".
- Bill B: yes. Education is devolved, so the bill is squarely within (8).
- Bill C: the text is not decisive. The bill concerns housing, which is a devolved matter when the bill is introduced, so a reading that it is "with regard to devolved matters" is available. But (8) speaks of devolved *matters*, not of changes to the Parliament's *powers*. Consent for competence-changing bills rests on the convention as the UK government has operated it more than on the subsection's words.

**Must hit, strict (b):**

- Valid. Subsection (7) preserves Westminster's power to make laws for Scotland, and (8) is worded as recognition ("it is recognised") of what Parliament "will not normally" do, not as a prohibition. On *Miller*, that wording records a political convention the courts will not enforce, so no court strikes the Act down.

**Wrong turns:** treating (8) as a manner-and-form requirement whose breach voids the Act; answering Bill C with a confident "yes" from the text alone; saying consent comes from the Scottish Government rather than the Scottish Parliament.

**Model answer (b):** The Act is valid: s.28(7) leaves Westminster's power to legislate for Scotland intact, and s.28(8) only "recognises" that Parliament "will not normally" legislate without consent. Because those words describe a convention rather than impose a duty, no court will strike the Act down; the remedy for the refusal is political.

**P2** *(Formal (a)–(c), strict · Exegetical (d), strict)*

(a) $\mathrm{VFI} = 1 - 10/40 = 0.75$.

(b) 10% of 40 is 4, all from own taxes: $4/10 = 40\%$. Check with the gearing formula: $10\% / (1 - 0.75) = 40\%$.

(c) Own revenue $10 + 12 = 22$, grant $30 - 12 = 18$.

$$\mathrm{VFI} = 1 - \frac{22}{40} = 0.45$$

The same rise of 4 now needs $4/22 = 18.2\%$ more own revenue.

(d) Under Reform 2 the only revenue Norvale can raise at the margin is still its original 10, so a rise of 4 still needs a 40% increase in those taxes, exactly as before the reform. The printed VFI of 0.45 counts as "own" a revenue stream that behaves like a formula grant, so it overstates Norvale's fiscal autonomy.

**Must hit, strict:**

- (a) 0.75; (b) 40%; (c) 0.45 and 18.2%.
- (d) The tax-rate lever is what matters at the margin. With no rate power, the shared tax adds nothing to it, and the 40% gearing of (b) is unchanged.

**Wrong turns:** computing the VFI as grant over own revenue ($30/10$); answering (b) with 10%, as if the grant rose in proportion; in (d), saying the two reforms are the same because the totals are the same.

**P3** *(Exegetical (a), strict · Evaluative (b), any verdict)*

**Must hit, strict (a):**

- An asymmetric arrangement: one region holds a chartered fiscal regime (it levies its own taxes and pays a contribution) while the others are financed by the common system. This is the Basque–Navarrese pattern, here invented.
- Vellar bears the loss: its contribution is tied to the cost of the State's services, not to its collections, so a 15% fall in its tax take comes out of its own budget, while grant-financed regions are shielded.
- The proposed law fails the text, which requires the contribution to be "fixed by a law agreed between the two governments"; a unilateral law is not that. (Who would rule on the conflict is [5.3](05-03-judicial-review-compared.md)'s question.)

**Must hit, any verdict (b):**

- The case for Vellar: the regime is a protected constitutional bargain; Vellar carries its own revenue risk, as this recession shows; own taxation ties regional spending to regional voters.
- The case for the deputy: equal citizenship and inter-regional solidarity; a region outside equalization keeps the gains of its own tax base, and other regions may demand the same deal.
- The trade-off: fiscal autonomy and own-risk accountability against nationwide equalization.
- Where the text stops: it fixes *that* a contribution is paid and *how* it is set (by agreement), not its size, and not whether equalization counts as one of "the services the State provides there". That question is left to negotiation.

**Wrong turns:** treating (b) as settled by (a), as though the text's procedure answered whether the arrangement is fair; assuming Vellar gains in a recession; arguing as if the empirical claim that own taxation improves accountability were proven.

**Model answer (b), one of several:** Vellar's defenders say the regime is a constitutional bargain and that Vellar already carries its own risk: when its revenue fell 15%, nobody topped it up. Own taxation also keeps its government answerable to its own taxpayers. The deputy replies that citizens of one state should get comparable services wherever they live, and that a wealthy region standing outside equalization keeps the gains of its tax base while others share theirs. The trade-off is regional fiscal autonomy against nationwide equalization. The text settles the procedure, a contribution fixed by agreement, but not its size, and not whether equalization is a "service the State provides there". That is where the outcome passes to bargaining between the two governments.

## Flashback

**From Lesson [4.4](04-04-party-systems.md) (Party systems):** *(Formal (a) · Exegetical (b).)* Diagnose. An **invented** op-ed from the invented country of Ystral, not the words of any real person or outlet:

> "The Granary Party has just won its fifth straight single-party majority: 56 of 100 seats on 44% of the vote, against Osier (26% of the vote, 22 seats), Pewter (16%, 12) and Quince (14%, 10). Critics call Ystral a one-party state. Nonsense: our effective number of parties is above three, which proves we have a competitive multiparty system, not a dominant-party one."

(a) Compute the effective number of parties by votes and by seats, to two decimals. (b) The op-ed's last sentence assumes a high effective number rules out a dominant-party system. Say why it does not, and say what evidence separates the "one-party state" the critics allege from a dominant-party system. 80 words or fewer.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

(a) By votes: $\sum p_i^2 = 0.44^2 + 0.26^2 + 0.16^2 + 0.14^2$

$= 0.1936 + 0.0676 + 0.0256 + 0.0196 = 0.3064$, so $N_v = 1/0.3064 = 3.26$.

By seats: $\sum s_i^2 = 0.56^2 + 0.22^2 + 0.12^2 + 0.10^2$

$= 0.3136 + 0.0484 + 0.0144 + 0.0100 = 0.3864$, so $N_s = 1/0.3864 = 2.59$.

**Must hit, strict (a):**

- $N_v = 3.26$, $N_s = 2.59$ (slips of a few hundredths are fine if the method is right). "Above three" holds only by votes; by seats, which is where governments are made, it is 2.59.

**Must hit, strict (b):**

- The effective number describes how one election's shares are spread. The dominant-party (predominant) type is defined across elections: one party winning majorities repeatedly while elections remain free. A scattered opposition can coexist with one party's repeated majorities, so a high $N$ cannot rule dominance out; five straight single-party majorities meet even the strict test.
- Dominance is not hegemony. A dominant party wins free elections it could lose (the LDP lost office in 1993); a one-party state does not let it lose. The separating evidence is whether elections are free and whether the opposition could win and take office, not the share count.

**Wrong turns:** quoting only $N_v$ and missing that the seat figure is below three; treating the effective number as a classification, when Sartori counts parties by function and defines the predominant type by repeated majorities, which no single-election index captures; accepting "one-party state" because Granary keeps winning, when repeated victory is evidence of dominance, not of unfree elections.

**Model answer (b):** The effective number measures how one election's shares are spread; a dominant-party system is defined across elections, by one party winning majorities again and again under free competition. Five straight majorities meet even the strict test, however fragmented the opposition, so the index cannot rule dominance out. A one-party state is hegemonic: the party cannot lose. What separates the two is whether elections are free and the opposition could win office.
