# Political Institutions · Lesson 4.1: Bicameralism

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 4: Inside legislatures, and the parties that run them · Builds on: [3.1 Parliamentary government](03-01-parliamentary-government.md), [3.3 Presidential government](03-03-presidential-government.md) · Unlocks: [4.2 Committees and agenda control](04-02-committees-and-agenda-control.md), [5.1 Federal, unitary, devolved](05-01-federal-unitary-devolved.md)

## Why this matters

A government can win a lower-house majority and still fail to make law, because a second chamber said no. Whether it *can* say no, and for how long, differs enormously: the US Senate can kill any bill, the German Bundesrat can kill some bills and only delay others, the House of Lords can delay most bills by about a year, and the French Senate's resistance ends when the government decides it should. This lesson classifies second chambers on two axes and then finds, for each, the rule that ends a disagreement between the chambers.

## The idea

Ask two questions about any second chamber.

1. **Can it stop things?** That depends on its formal powers and on whether it has the democratic standing to use them. A directly elected Senate with an absolute veto can; an appointed chamber with a one-year delay mostly cannot.
2. **Is it different?** If both chambers are elected the same way by the same voters, the second mostly repeats the first. If it is chosen differently (by regions, by regional governments, by appointment), it brings different majorities.

A chamber that is both equal and different is a genuine second veto. An equal chamber that is the same is a duplicate. A weak chamber that is different is a revising chamber: it can make the first chamber think twice, but not refuse. A weak chamber that is the same does little.

Arend Lijphart (*Patterns of Democracy*, 2nd ed. 2012) formalized these as **[symmetry and asymmetry](../reference.md#symmetric-and-asymmetric-bicameralism)** (equal or only moderately unequal powers, plus comparable democratic legitimacy) and **[congruence and incongruence](../reference.md#congruent-and-incongruent-bicameralism)** (similar or different composition, for instance because one chamber over-represents small regions). His index of bicameralism runs from 4.0 (strong: symmetric and incongruent) to 1.0 (one chamber). These are **mechanical** classifications of rules on paper; how much a chamber actually blocks is an **empirical** question, taken up below.

## The mechanism

Every [bicameral](../reference.md#bicameralism) legislature must answer the same procedural question: **what happens when the two chambers pass different texts?** The procedure, step by step:

1. **Identical text.** A bill becomes law only when both chambers have passed the same words, unless a rule says otherwise. The US Constitution (Art. I, s. 7) requires passage by both Houses; France's Art. 45 sends each bill through both Houses with the aim of an identical text.
2. **The shuttle.** The bill goes back and forth with amendments; the French call this the **[navette](../reference.md#navette)**.
3. **A joint committee.** If the shuttle stalls, members of both chambers meet to draft a compromise: a US **[conference committee](../reference.md#conference-committee)**, Germany's Mediation Committee (Art. 77(2)), France's joint committee with equal numbers from each House (the *commission mixte paritaire*).
4. **The stopping rule.** If the compromise fails, one of three things happens: the bill dies; the lower house overrides, perhaps by a special majority; or someone else (the executive) decides whether the lower house gets the last word.
5. **Bill-type exceptions.** Stopping rules often differ by bill type: money bills, bills touching regional powers, constitutional amendments.

*In words:* the stopping rule settles who wins a deadlock, and the bill-type exceptions decide which stopping rule applies.

Run it on the four core cases, with rules as of 2026:

| | Second chamber | If the chambers disagree |
|---|---|---|
| **US** | Senate: two senators per state, directly elected | No override. Conference committee or amendments traded between chambers; otherwise the bill dies |
| **Germany** | Bundesrat: delegations of the 16 Land governments, 3 to 6 votes each (69 in all), each Land voting as a unit (Art. 51) | *[Consent bills](../reference.md#consent-and-objection-bills):* the Bundesrat has an absolute veto. *Objection bills:* it may object, and the Bundestag can override (Art. 77(4)) |
| **UK** | House of Lords: appointed life peers plus Church of England bishops | *Money bills:* law after one month whatever the Lords do. *Other public bills from the Commons:* law if the Lords reject the bill in two successive sessions and a year has passed since its Commons second reading ([Parliament Acts 1911 and 1949](../reference.md#parliament-acts-1911-and-1949)) |
| **France** | Senate: elected by indirect suffrage, representing the territorial communities (Art. 24) | After two readings in each House (one under the accelerated procedure), a joint committee; if that fails, the government may "ask the National Assembly to reach a final decision" (Art. 45) |

Three details carry the weight:

- **US.** The Senate is equal in power and in legitimacy (directly elected since the Seventeenth Amendment), and its equal representation of states is shielded by Article V even from ordinary constitutional amendment: "no State, without its Consent, shall be deprived of its equal Suffrage in the Senate." Strong bicameralism in its purest form.
- **Germany.** Which bills need consent is fixed provision by provision: for instance, laws on taxes whose revenue goes to the Länder (Art. 105(3)). Decisions need a majority of all its votes (Art. 52(3)), that is 35 of 69, so **an abstention counts as a no**. In practice, a Land whose governing coalition disagrees abstains. To override an objection, the Bundestag needs a majority of its Members; if the Bundesrat objected by two-thirds (46 of 69), the override needs two-thirds of the Bundestag, including a majority of its Members.
- **UK.** The Speaker certifies what is a money bill (Parliament Act 1911, s. 1(3)). The Parliament Acts exclude any bill extending a Parliament beyond five years, so there the Lords keep a full veto. The **Salisbury convention**, not law, holds that the Lords do not block government bills carrying out manifesto commitments. An Act of 2026 removed the last hereditary peers, so the House is now appointed.

**Where the rule runs out.** A stopping rule settles a deadlock only after the deadlock is formal, and most bargaining happens in its shadow. The Parliament Acts have been used only a handful of times; the Lords' one-year delay works as a threat a government weighs against its timetable, and a year costs more late in a Parliament than early. The Bundesrat's veto depends on which bills count as consent bills, and governments can shape that: the Federal Constitutional Court held in 2002 that splitting a measure into separate bills, so that only one part needs consent, is constitutionally unobjectionable. France's last word is the *government's* to grant ("may"), so whether the Senate is overridden is a political choice the text leaves open.

Two placements need comment. **Germany** scores 4.0, though the Bundesrat can only delay objection bills; Lijphart nevertheless codes it symmetric, and a reader who weighted the suspensive veto more heavily would place it lower. **Italy** shows that a congruent chamber can still matter: both Houses legislate together (Art. 70) and the government needs the confidence of both (Art. 94), so a government can hold a majority in one House and lose it in the other.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean): one German bill, two fates.** Invented alignment of the 69 Bundesrat votes: Länder governed only by the federal coalition's parties hold 27 votes; Länder governed only by opposition parties hold 20; mixed coalitions hold 22, and they abstain on this bill. (The real Land weights allow this split.)

- *If it is a consent bill:* yes votes are 27. The threshold is a majority of all votes, 35, and abstentions do not lower it. 27 < 35, so **the bill fails**. The government needs at least 8 more votes, which means winning over some mixed-coalition Länder, typically with concessions. *The rule that decides:* Art. 52(3)'s majority of all votes, together with block voting.
- *If it is an objection bill:* an objection itself needs 35 votes. The opposition-only Länder have 20 < 35, so if the mixed Länder abstain **no objection is possible, and the bill becomes law** (Art. 78). Even if every mixed Land joined, 20 + 22 = 42 is a majority but short of 46, so the Bundestag could override with a majority of its Members.

Same chamber, same votes; the bill's legal category decides everything.

**Example 2 (hard case): where France's asymmetry stops.** Suppose a French government bill on regional transport. The Senate twice amends what the Assembly passed; the joint committee cannot agree. Under Art. 45 the government may now hand the Assembly the final decision, so on ordinary legislation the Senate cannot block. That is asymmetry.

Now let the same reform require a constitutional amendment. Art. 89 requires both Houses to pass a constitutional bill in identical terms before it goes to a referendum or to Parliament in Congress. There is no last-word procedure, so the Senate has an absolute veto. The same holds for institutional acts relating to the Senate (Art. 46). France is asymmetric for ordinary law and symmetric for the constitution, and Lijphart's single score cannot show both. This is mechanical. Whether a government that will need senators for a future amendment hesitates to override them on ordinary bills is an empirical question; the text does not decide it.

## Watch out

- **You might think a chamber with weak formal powers has little influence, but that is an empirical claim, not a mechanical one.** Meg Russell (*The Contemporary House of Lords: Westminster Bicameralism Revived*) argues that the House became more assertive after most hereditary peers left in 1999, and that governments often accept or pre-empt its amendments. That is a detailed single-country study; influence exercised through anticipated reactions is hard to count.
- **You might think the Bundesrat is Germany's Senate, but its members are not elected to it at all.** They are ministers of Land governments, recalled at will, voting en bloc on the Land cabinet's instruction. The Bundesrat changes after every Land election and has no fixed term. The US Senate and the Bundesrat both represent territories; they differ in *who* the representative is (voters' senators vs regional executives).
- **You might think congruence means the same party controls both chambers, but it is about how the chambers are composed.** Divided control is more likely under incongruence, but congruent chambers can still split (Italy), and incongruent ones can agree for years.

## One-liner

> Classify a second chamber by whether it can stop things (symmetry) and whether it is different (congruence), then find its stopping rule: in the US no one overrides, in Germany it depends on the bill's category, in the UK a year's delay ends it, in France the government decides.

## Problems

Norvale (invented) has a Chamber of Deputies and a Council of Provinces made of provincial government delegations, each province voting as a unit. Today the Council has an absolute veto over every bill.

**P1 (🟢) *(Formal (a)-(c).)*** Council votes are set by population: every province 2 votes; more than 1 million inhabitants, 3; more than 3 million, 4; more than 6 million, 5. Decisions need a majority of *all* votes.

| Province | Population (millions) | Governed by |
|---|---|---|
| Ardel | 7.2 | Red-Blue coalition (abstains when its parties disagree) |
| Brisk | 4.1 | Red |
| Coren | 2.5 | Blue |
| Dunmore | 0.8 | Red |
| Elsby | 0.6 | Blue |

(a) Give each province's votes, the total, and the majority needed. (b) The national government (Red) brings a bill that Blue opposes. Does it pass if every province votes its governing party's line? Which *single* additional province would be enough to pass it? (c) How many times as many Council votes per inhabitant does Elsby have as Ardel?

**P2 (🟡) *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)*** Apply to a hard case. A reform commission proposes three stopping rules for ordinary bills. Sessions run January to December.

- **Rule A:** no override; a joint committee may propose a compromise, and if either chamber rejects it the bill dies.
- **Rule B:** if the Chamber passes a bill in two successive sessions and the Council rejects it each time (failing to pass it within a month counts as rejection), the bill becomes law, provided at least a year separates the Chamber's two passages.
- **Rule C:** once each chamber has read the bill twice without agreeing, the government may convene a joint committee; if that fails, the government may ask the Chamber to decide finally.

The government passes a Water Bill in the Chamber in March of year 1; the Council rejects it in April, and both chambers vote again by June, still disagreeing. Provinces administer national water law. (a) Under each rule, can the bill become law without the Council's consent, when at the earliest, and whose act is the last one? Two sentences per rule. (b) Under Rule C, should the government ask the Chamber to decide finally? Any verdict passes; 80 words or fewer.

**P3 (🔴, optional) *(Exegetical.)*** Find the crux. One Norvale reformer wants to keep the Council as a strong territorial chamber; another wants to replace it with an appointed revising chamber of experts that can only delay bills for a year. Name the single trade-off their disagreement turns on, and one kind of evidence that would move each side. 100 words or fewer.

<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Formal (a)-(c), strict)*

(a) Ardel 7.2 > 6 → **5**; Brisk 4.1 > 3 → **4**; Coren 2.5 > 1 → **3**; Dunmore 0.8 → **2**; Elsby 0.6 → **2**. Total $5+4+3+2+2 = 16$. Majority of all votes: $\lfloor 16/2 \rfloor + 1 = 9$.

(b) Yes votes from Red-only provinces: Brisk 4 + Dunmore 2 = 6. Ardel abstains, and its abstention counts against, since 9 of 16 are needed whatever the turnout. $6 < 9$: **the bill fails.** Adding one province:

| Added | Yes votes | Passes? |
|---|---|---|
| Ardel (5) | 11 | yes |
| Coren (3) | 9 | yes |
| Elsby (2) | 8 | no |

**Ardel or Coren** would be enough; Elsby would not.

(c) Votes per million inhabitants: Elsby $2/0.6 \approx 3.33$; Ardel $5/7.2 \approx 0.694$.

$$\frac{2/0.6}{5/7.2} = \frac{2 \times 7.2}{0.6 \times 5} = \frac{14.4}{3} = 4.8$$

Elsby has **4.8 times** Ardel's weight per inhabitant (Ardel has 47% of the population but 5/16, about 31%, of the votes).

**Must hit, strict:**

- (a) 5, 4, 3, 2, 2; total 16; majority 9.
- (b) 6 < 9, fails; abstention counts as no; Ardel or Coren suffices, Elsby does not.
- (c) 4.8.

**Wrong turns:** taking the majority of votes *cast* (then Ardel's abstention would leave 6 vs 5 and the bill would pass), which is exactly what a majority-of-all-votes rule like Art. 52(3) rules out; giving Coren 4 by misreading "more than 3 million".

---

**P2** *(Exegetical (a), strict · Evaluative (b), any verdict)*

**Must hit, strict (a):**

- **Rule A:** never without the Council; there is no override, so the Council's rejection is the last act and the bill dies (the US pattern).
- **Rule B:** yes, but not in year 1. The Chamber must pass it again in the next session at least a year after March of year 1, so no earlier than March of year 2; it becomes law when the Council rejects it again or lets a month pass. The last act is the Council's second rejection (or its lapse), which triggers the rule (the Parliament Acts pattern).
- **Rule C:** yes, possibly in year 1: two readings each are done by June, so the government may convene the joint committee and, if it fails, ask the Chamber to decide. The last act is the government's choice to invoke the rule, then the Chamber's vote (the Art. 45 pattern).

**Must hit, any verdict (b):**

- Note that the rule says *may*: it gives the government the option and does not settle whether to use it.
- Name a consideration for overriding (the Chamber's mandate; a deadline; the Council's position is purely partisan) and one against (provinces administer water law, so a law forced past their governments may be implemented grudgingly; the government may need the Council later).

**Wrong turns:** under Rule B, counting the year from the Council's rejection rather than from the Chamber's first passage; under Rule C, saying the Chamber can act on its own, when only the government can trigger the final reading.

**Model answer (b), one of several:** The rule lets the government override but does not tell it to. Overriding gets the bill on time and honours the Chamber majority. But the provinces will administer the law, and provincial governments overridden on water may implement it slowly; a joint-committee compromise buys their cooperation at the price of some content. I would seek the compromise first and override only if the Council's objections are partisan rather than administrative.

---

**P3** *(Exegetical, strict)*

**Must hit, strict:**

- The trade-off: **giving regions a veto over national majorities vs letting a national majority govern with only a delay and a second look.** The strong chamber protects territorial interests and forces negotiation at the cost of possible deadlock; the revising chamber adds expertise and time for second thoughts but cannot protect a region against a determined majority.
- Evidence for the strong-chamber side to weigh: how often vetoes have blocked policies the regions administer, and whether that produced deadlock or better-implemented laws. Evidence for the reformer: whether revising chambers' amendments are accepted (as is claimed for the Lords) and whether regions under such chambers lose out on policies that matter to them.

**Wrong turns:** making the crux "democratic vs undemocratic", which misses that the strong chamber is also chosen by elected governments; naming two trade-offs instead of one.

**Model answer:** The crux is whether provinces should be able to stop national legislation or only slow it. The territorial chamber's defender values a veto that forces national majorities to bargain with the provinces that administer the laws; the reformer values decisive national government plus expert revision. Evidence of chronic deadlock on necessary bills, or of provincial vetoes used for partisan rather than territorial ends, would move the first; evidence that revising chambers' amendments are routinely ignored, or that regions suffer under majorities they cannot stop, would move the second.

## Flashback

**From Lesson [3.3](03-03-presidential-government.md) (Presidential government):** *(Exegetical (a)-(b).)* Apply to new cases. Three invented draft constitutions for Ostavia, excerpted:

- **Draft 1:** "The President is elected by the people for five years. The Assembly may, by a majority of its members, declare that it no longer has confidence in the President, who shall then leave office."
- **Draft 2:** "The Assembly elects the President for four years. The President may be removed only on conviction for treason or bribery, and may not dissolve the Assembly."
- **Draft 3:** "The people elect the President for six years and the Assembly for four. The President may be removed only on conviction for treason or bribery by three-quarters of the Assembly, and may not dissolve it."

(a) By the lesson's two-part test, is each draft presidential? If not, name the feature it lacks. One sentence each. (b) Draft 3 adds: "If no budget law is in force when the fiscal year begins, the previous year's budget law continues to apply." Compared with the US rule and Chile's rule, can there be a shutdown, and whose bargaining position does this default strengthen? Two sentences.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):**

- **Draft 1:** not presidential; it has separate origin but **lacks separate survival**, since the Assembly can remove the President for losing its support, for no stated cause.
- **Draft 2:** not presidential; it has separate survival but **lacks separate origin**, since the Assembly, not the voters, chooses the President.
- **Draft 3:** **presidential**; both branches are elected separately on fixed terms, and removal only on conviction for named crimes is for misconduct, not a confidence vote. (Its unequal terms also make divided government possible.)

**Must hit, strict (b):**

- No shutdown: if no budget passes, spending continues at last year's levels.
- The default favours whichever side prefers last year's budget to the proposed change. A branch seeking new spending or cuts can no longer win by waiting, as the president does under Chile's rule, and a legislature can no longer threaten a shutdown, as under the US rule. The governing principle: whoever's preferred outcome is the default holds the stronger hand.

**Wrong turns:** calling Draft 1 presidential because the people elect the President (separate survival is the test); calling Draft 3 non-presidential because the Assembly can remove the President (removal for cause is impeachment, not confidence); calling Draft 2 presidential because the term is fixed; in (b), equating the rule with Chile's, whose default is the president's *new* bill, not last year's law.

**Model answer (b):** There is no shutdown, because last year's budget law simply carries over. That strengthens whichever branch is content with the status quo: the side proposing changes loses the leverage that Chile's rule gives the president and the US rule gives a legislature able to threaten a shutdown.

</details>
