# Political Institutions · Lesson 5.3: Judicial review compared

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 5: Dividing power: federalism and courts · Builds on: [5.1 Federal, unitary, devolved](05-01-federal-unitary-devolved.md), [`political-philosophy` 5.2](../../political-philosophy/lessons/05-02-majority-rule-vs-rights-judicial-review.md) · Unlocks: [5.4 Weak-form review, appointments and independence](05-04-weak-form-review-appointments-and-independence.md)

## Why this matters

"The court struck down the law" names very different events. In the United States, a trial judge can refuse to apply a statute to the defendant before her. In Germany, one court in Karlsruhe can void a statute because a quarter of the Bundestag asked it to, with no dispute in sight. In France before 2010, a law could be challenged only in the weeks before it took effect, and never afterwards. This lesson maps which courts review, at whose request, when, and with what effect. Whether courts should hold the power at all is the legitimacy question [`political-philosophy` 5.2](../../political-philosophy/lessons/05-02-majority-rule-vs-rights-judicial-review.md) owns; here it is taken as given.

## The idea

Every system of [constitutional review](../reference.md#judicial-review-models) answers four design questions:

1. **Which courts?** Every court (**diffuse** review) or one specialized constitutional court (**concentrated** review): [diffuse and concentrated review](../reference.md#diffuse-and-concentrated-review).
2. **Triggered how?** In a live dispute between parties (**concrete** review) or by a petition with no dispute at all (**abstract** review): [abstract and concrete review](../reference.md#abstract-and-concrete-review).
3. **When?** Before the law is promulgated (**a priori**) or after it is in force (**a posteriori**): [a priori and a posteriori review](../reference.md#a-priori-and-a-posteriori-review).
4. **With what effect?** The ruling binds the parties and spreads by precedent, or it voids the law for everyone.

Two historical templates fill these in differently. The **American** one grew out of ordinary adjudication. A judge must decide a case under all the law that applies, and the Constitution is law that outranks a statute. Hamilton's *Federalist* 78 put it plainly: "the Constitution ought to be preferred to the statute, the intention of the people to the intention of their agents." *Marbury v. Madison* (1803) is the canonical judicial statement; its doctrine belongs to [`constitutional-law`](../../constitutional-law/syllabus.md). Because the logic is that of deciding cases, every court has the power, and only inside a case.

The **European** template is Hans Kelsen's, built into Austria's 1920 constitution: give review to one dedicated court, outside the ordinary judiciary. Kelsen called such a court a "negative legislator": annulling a statute is legislation in reverse, so it belongs with a body built for it, not with every career judge. Germany's Federal Constitutional Court (sitting since 1951), Italy, Spain and many post-1989 Central European democracies followed this model.

The **United Kingdom** sits off the map. Under **[parliamentary sovereignty](../reference.md#parliamentary-sovereignty)**, no court may hold an Act of Parliament invalid. What British lawyers call "judicial review" is review of ministers, agencies and secondary legislation against the statutes that empower them. The UK's weak-form declarations are [5.4](05-04-weak-form-review-appointments-and-independence.md)'s subject.

In the vocabulary of [`political-philosophy` 5.2](../../political-philosophy/reference.md#strong-and-weak-judicial-review), the US, Germany and France all have **strong-form** review: a court may decline to apply a statute or modify its effect, and the legislature cannot simply re-enact its way past the ruling. The override is constitutional amendment (rules as of 2026): two-thirds of each US house plus three-quarters of the states (Article V); two-thirds of the Bundestag's members and of the Bundesrat's votes (Basic Law Art. 79(2)); in France, identical text from both houses, then a referendum or three-fifths of Parliament sitting in Congress (Art. 89).

## The mechanism

How a contested statute reaches a court, system by system. All of this is **mechanical**: it is what the texts say.

1. **United States: diffuse, concrete, a posteriori.** The statute is in force. Someone with **standing**, a concrete injury the statute causes, raises the constitutional claim in an ordinary case before any court, state or federal. If the court agrees, it declines to apply the statute *in that case*. A Supreme Court ruling binds lower courts through precedent, so the statute becomes unenforceable in practice, though it is never formally erased.
   *In words:* review is a by-product of deciding lawsuits.

2. **Germany: concentrated, with three doors.** All lead only to Karlsruhe. (A December 2024 amendment moved the jurisdiction clause from Art. 93 to Art. 94; older sources cite Art. 93.)
   - *Abstract review* (Art. 94(1) no. 2): the federal government, a Land government, or one quarter of the Bundestag's members may apply, with no case needed. An opposition with a quarter of the seats can take the majority's law straight to court.
   - *Concrete referral* (Art. 100(1)): "If a court concludes that a law on whose validity its decision depends is unconstitutional, the proceedings shall be stayed", and the question goes to the Constitutional Court. An ordinary judge may doubt a statute but may not set it aside herself.
   - *[Constitutional complaint](../reference.md#constitutional-complaint)* (Art. 94(1) no. 4a): any person alleging that public authority has infringed a basic right may file, normally after exhausting other remedies. A complaint directly against a statute must come within one year of its entry into force. Complaints make up the bulk of the court's caseload.

   A successful challenge voids the law, or declares it "incompatible" and leaves the legislature to repair it. Either ruling binds every court and authority (Art. 94(4)).

3. **France: concentrated, a priori, plus a posteriori since 2010.**
   - *Before promulgation* (Art. 61): organic laws go to the Conseil constitutionnel automatically. Ordinary statutes go only if the President, the Prime Minister, the president of either chamber, 60 deputies or 60 senators refer them. The 60-member route, added in 1974, gave the opposition the key. The Council has a month to rule, or eight days if the government declares urgency. A provision struck down "shall be neither promulgated nor implemented" (Art. 62).
   - *After entry into force*: the 2008 constitutional revision created the **[QPC](../reference.md#qpc-priority-question-of-constitutionality)** (*question prioritaire de constitutionnalité*, Art. 61-1), operating since 1 March 2010. A litigant claims that a provision applied in her case infringes constitutional rights and freedoms. The trial court passes the question to the Conseil d'État or the Cour de cassation. That court filters it: the provision must govern the dispute, the question must be serious, and the provision must not already have been upheld by the Council, unless circumstances have changed. A provision struck down is repealed from the decision's publication, or from a later date the Council sets.

4. **United Kingdom.** An Act of Parliament is applied by the courts, interpreted by them, and never set aside by them.

The rule that decides, in each system, is the **standing rule at each door**: who may knock, and when. A court can do nothing to a statute nobody is entitled to bring.

**Where the rule runs out.** The design fixes the doors. It does not fix how often anyone walks through them, or what the court does when they do. First, standing is partly court-made: US justiciability doctrine decides who has a "concrete injury", and that doctrine belongs to [`constitutional-law`](../../constitutional-law/syllabus.md). Second, a court can redefine its own mandate. In its 1971 *Liberté d'association* decision, the Conseil constitutionnel began reviewing statutes against the 1789 Declaration of the Rights of Man and the 1946 Preamble. That turned a body designed in 1958 chiefly to keep Parliament inside its domain into a rights court, and no text changed. Third, usage is **empirical**, and the same design produces very different records. Japan's 1947 constitution (Art. 81) copied the American diffuse design, and its Supreme Court held in 1952 that it cannot review in the abstract. Yet by most scholarly accounts it has set statutes aside only very rarely. The explanations are contested: one points to the long-dominant party's influence over appointments, another to the government's legal bureau screening bills before they pass. That is one case, not a law. Two more empirical claims, stated with their strength: Alec Stone Sweet (*Governing with Judges*, 2000) argues that abstract review pulls courts into lawmaking, as oppositions refer bills and governments draft with the court in mind (well documented in French and German case studies; the size of the effect is hard to measure). Tom Ginsburg (*Judicial Review in New Democracies*, 2003) reads strong review as "insurance" for drafters who expect to lose power (argued from a few Asian cases; contested as a general claim).

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean): one statute, four systems.** Invented case. Tavoria's legislature passes an Online Identity Act by 55 percent to 45. It requires real-name registration for every social-media account and sets a fine for anonymous posting. Imagine the identical Act enacted in each reference system, each with a constitutional free-speech guarantee.

| System | Who can bring it | When | Before whom | If it succeeds |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| US | a fined user with standing | once enforced or credibly threatened | any court, then up on appeal | not applied in her case; precedent spreads it |
| Germany | a quarter of the Bundestag; or a user; or a court hearing her fine case | any time after promulgation; a complaint against the Act itself within one year | the Constitutional Court only | void (or "incompatible"), binding on all |
| France | 60 deputies or senators, etc.; after 2010 a fined user via a QPC | before promulgation; or in later litigation | Conseil constitutionnel only | never promulgated; or repealed from publication |
| UK | nobody, against the Act itself | n/a | n/a | the Act stands |

The rule that decides is the standing rule. Only in Germany and France can the losing 45 percent's representatives bring the Act before a court before anyone has been fined.

**Example 2 (hard): the law nobody refers.** Invented case. Suppose Tavoria copies pre-2008 France: a priori review only, on referral by named officials or 60 legislators. It passes a Border Search Act by 410 votes to 12, with the government and the main opposition voting together. The 12 dissenters cannot refer it; nobody who can, wants to. The Act is promulgated unreviewed and can never be reviewed. Abstract review runs on disagreement among office-holders, so a law the major parties share escapes it, however hard it falls on a minority. The QPC answers this by giving the burdened litigant her own door.

Now add a QPC to Tavoria, and suppose the Council *had* upheld the Act a priori in 2027. A traveller searched in 2031 raises a QPC. The filter blocks a provision already upheld, unless circumstances have changed. The French Council has used that exception: in 2010 it re-examined police-custody rules it had once upheld, because their use had grown. Whether 2031 counts as changed circumstances is a judgment the text leaves to the courts; there the rule stops deciding.

## Watch out

- **You might think abstract review means a priori review, but actually these are different axes.** Abstract versus concrete asks whether a live dispute exists. A priori versus a posteriori asks whether the law is in force yet. Germany's abstract review targets promulgated statutes. France's a priori review is abstract *and* early.
- **You might think "concentrated courts strike down more laws" follows from the design, but actually that is an empirical claim the design is silent on.** The US and Japan share a design and differ sharply in use; counts also depend on how many statutes a country passes.
- **You might think a US ruling deletes the statute, as a German one does, but actually a US court only declines to apply it.** Precedent does the rest. A German or post-QPC French ruling removes the provision for everyone.

## One-liner

> Place a court by four answers (which courts, triggered how, when, with what effect); then find the standing rule at each door, because who may knock decides which laws are ever reviewed.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢) *(Exegetical (a)-(b).)*** Diagnose. An **invented** news report from the invented Republic of Tavoria:

> "The Constitutional Tribunal yesterday annulled the Housing Allocation Act eight days after the Assembly passed it and before the President could sign it. Seventy opposition senators had petitioned the Tribunal. Tavoria's ordinary courts have no power to rule on whether a statute is constitutional."

(a) Place Tavoria's review on the diffuse/concentrated, abstract/concrete and a priori/a posteriori axes, and name the reference system it most resembles. Two sentences. (b) Name one feature you would need to add to Tavoria's design to give a tenant, evicted under a later housing statute nobody petitioned against, a route to the Tribunal, and say which real system has that route. Two sentences.

**P2 (🟡) *(Exegetical (a)-(b).)*** Apply to a hard case. Invented case. A statute lets tax authorities publish online the names of anyone owing more than 50,000 euros in back taxes. It is enacted with cross-party support in Germany, France (in 2025, unreferred before promulgation) and the United States (with the threshold in dollars). Mr K, named on the list, says it violates a constitutional right to privacy. (a) For each country, say who can bring the challenge, before which court, and what happens to the statute if he wins. One line per country. (b) For one of the three countries, name the point where the review rule stops determining whether Mr K gets a ruling at all. Two sentences.

**P3 (🔴, optional) *(Evaluative.)*** Steelman and reply. Before 2008 France allowed review only before promulgation. In 150 words or fewer, give the strongest case for that a-priori-only design over adding a posteriori review, then the strongest reply. Any verdict passes.

<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical (a)-(b), strict)*

**Must hit, strict (a):**

- Concentrated: only the Tribunal reviews; ordinary courts cannot.
- Abstract: the trigger was a petition by legislators, with no dispute between parties.
- A priori: the ruling came before promulgation.
- Closest reference system: France's Conseil constitutionnel before the 2008 revision (Art. 61 referral by 60 deputies or senators).

**Must hit, strict (b):**

- Add a concrete, a posteriori route that a litigant can use: a court-referral procedure for statutes already in force.
- France's QPC (Art. 61-1, from 2010), or Germany's Art. 100 referral or individual constitutional complaint. Either answer passes if it is described correctly.

**Wrong turns:** calling it German because the report says "Constitutional Tribunal". Germany's abstract review can reach promulgated statutes, and the timing here is the French tell. Calling it diffuse because senators, not a court, started it: diffuse/concentrated is about which *courts* may review, not who petitions.

**Model answer:** (a) Tavoria has concentrated, abstract, a priori review, triggered by legislators before promulgation, which matches France's Conseil constitutionnel before 2008. (b) It needs a concrete route after promulgation, such as letting a trial court pass a litigant's constitutional question up to the Tribunal; France added exactly this with the QPC in 2008, in force in 2010.

---

**P2** *(Exegetical (a)-(b), strict)*

**Must hit, strict (a):**

- Germany: Mr K can file a constitutional complaint with the Federal Constitutional Court. He can file against the statute directly, within one year of its entry into force, or after exhausting remedies against the decision naming him. Alternatively, a court hearing his case can refer the question under Art. 100. Abstract review is open in principle, but the federal government, a Land government or a quarter of the Bundestag would have to want it, and with cross-party support none may. If he wins, the law is void or declared incompatible, binding on all.
- France: a priori review is gone, because nobody referred the statute before promulgation. Mr K can raise a QPC in litigation over his listing. It is filtered by the Conseil d'État or the Cour de cassation, whichever heads the court system hearing his case, and decided by the Conseil constitutionnel. If he wins, the provision is repealed from publication or from a later date the Council sets.
- US: Mr K sues, or defends, in an ordinary court, which needs his concrete injury. Any court may decline to apply the statute to him, and a Supreme Court ruling spreads by precedent.

**Must hit, strict (b), any one:**

- US: whether his injury suffices for standing is judge-made doctrine, which the review design does not settle.
- France: the filter's serious-question test, and its changed-circumstances exception if the provision had been upheld before, are judgments made by the filtering court.
- Germany: whether he must exhaust ordinary remedies first, or the court accepts the complaint for decision at all, is the court's call.

**Wrong turns:** saying the French statute is now immune. That was true only before 2010. Listing abstract review as Mr K's route in Germany: he has no standing to bring it. Saying the US Supreme Court "voids" the statute.

**Model answer:** (a) Germany: Mr K files a constitutional complaint in Karlsruhe, or a court hearing his case refers it; if he wins, the law is void or incompatible for everyone. France: with no a priori referral, he raises a QPC in his case; the Conseil d'État or Cour de cassation filters it, and the Conseil constitutionnel repeals the provision if he wins. US: he litigates in any court on a concrete injury; a win means the statute is not applied to him, and precedent carries the ruling further. (b) In France, whether his question is "serious" enough to pass the filter is a judgment the text leaves to the filtering court. If it says no, the Conseil constitutionnel never rules.

---

**P3** *(Evaluative, any verdict)*

**Must hit, any verdict:**

- Steelman: a priori review gives **legal certainty**. A law that survives is settled before anyone relies on it, and no statute is struck down after years of contracts, convictions and tax assessments rest on it. It also keeps the Council out of ordinary litigation and close to Kelsen's "negative legislator".
- Reply: a priori review depends on office-holders choosing to refer. Laws with cross-party support escape it, and harms that appear only in application are invisible in the abstract. A posteriori review gives the burdened person a door of her own.
- Name the trade-off either side accepts: certainty and speed against coverage and attention to application. Label any claim about how often unconstitutional laws slipped through as empirical.

**Wrong turns:** arguing whether review is legitimate at all, which is [`political-philosophy` 5.2](../../political-philosophy/lessons/05-02-majority-rule-vs-rights-judicial-review.md)'s question, not this design choice. A steelman that is only "it was simpler".

**Model answer, one of several:** A-priori-only review settles constitutionality once, before the law takes effect. Citizens, firms and officials can then rely on every promulgated statute, and no court unwinds years of transactions built on a law later voided. It keeps a non-judicial council out of everyday cases and confines it to a narrow, fast check. Reply: the check runs only when office-holders choose to pull the trigger. A law that both major parties support is never referred, however heavily it falls on a minority, and many defects show only in application, which no one can foresee within a month. Adding a posteriori review trades some certainty for coverage; France built a filter (the top courts' screening, and the bar on re-examining upheld provisions) to limit that cost. Which matters more depends on how often referral failed to catch real defects, which is an empirical question.

## Flashback

**From Lesson [5.1](05-01-federal-unitary-devolved.md) (Federal, unitary, devolved):** *(Exegetical (a)-(b).)* Apply to a new case. The invented state of Veloria has a single-chamber National Assembly, elected nationally, and five cantons: Aster, Brume, Cairn, Dorrel and Eskel. Its invented constitution says:

> Art. 3: The cantons legislate on schooling, policing and language, and on every matter not assigned to the Union by Article 8.
>
> Art. 30: This Constitution is amended by a two-thirds vote of the National Assembly, approved in a referendum by a majority of voters nationwide and by a majority of voters in at least four of the five cantons.

An invented op-ed argues: "Veloria has no chamber of the cantons, so it cannot be a federation." (a) Run the three-step test on Veloria, name the clause that decides, and say whether the op-ed's premise matters to the test. Three sentences or fewer. (b) Could an amendment take away Eskel's power over language even though Eskel's voters reject it? Say what kind of key Art. 30 gives the cantons. Two sentences.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):**

- Step 1: the cantons make their own law (Art. 3). Step 2: the source is the constitution. Step 3: the Assembly cannot amend alone, because the cantons' voters must approve (Art. 30). **Federal; Art. 30 decides.**
- The premise does not matter to the test: step 3 asks whether the units must consent, by any route (individually, through a territorial chamber, or through their voters). A territorial upper chamber is a typical channel of shared rule, not a requirement; Veloria's lock works through the cantons' voters, as in Switzerland's double majority.

**Must hit, strict (b):**

- Yes: if voters nationwide and in the other four cantons approve, Eskel's rejection does not block the amendment.
- The key is **collective**, not individual: any two cantons whose voters reject can block, but no canton alone can. (Contrast the US Article V proviso, which protects each state individually, but only its equal suffrage in the Senate.)

**Wrong turns:** calling Veloria unitary because there is no chamber of the cantons; treating the Assembly's two-thirds rule as the lock, when a national supermajority is not the units' consent; saying in (b) that "the cantons must consent" means each canton holds a veto.

**Model answer:** (a) The cantons legislate under the constitution, and Art. 30 makes every amendment need majorities in four of the five cantons, so the national level cannot change the division of powers alone and Veloria is federal. The test asks only whether the units' consent is required, by whatever route; a chamber of the cantons is one route, and a cantonal referendum majority is another. (b) Yes: four approving cantons and a national majority suffice, so Eskel can be outvoted. Art. 30 gives the cantons a collective key, which any two of them can turn together, but not an individual one.

</details>
