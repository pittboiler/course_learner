# Political Institutions · Lesson 4.4: Party systems

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 4: Inside legislatures, and the parties that run them · Builds on: [2.1 Measuring outcomes](02-01-measuring-outcomes.md), [2.3 Duverger's law, observed](02-03-duvergers-law-observed.md), [4.3 Parties: organization and discipline](04-03-parties-organization-and-discipline.md) · Unlocks: [5.1 Federal, unitary, devolved](05-01-federal-unitary-devolved.md), [6.4 Majoritarian and consensus democracy](06-04-majoritarian-and-consensus-democracy.md)

## Why this matters

Two parliaments can each hold six parties and behave nothing alike. In one, governments alternate between two stable blocs that fight over the centre; in the other, a permanent centre coalition faces hostile parties on both flanks, each outbidding it. Counting parties ([2.1](02-01-measuring-outcomes.md)) tells you how fragmented a system is. This lesson adds the second coordinate, how far apart the parties stand, and asks where a country's parties came from in the first place.

## The idea

A party system is the pattern of interaction among a country's parties, not just their list. Place it by two numbers: **fragmentation** (how many parties matter) and **polarization** (how far apart the ones that matter stand). Then explain it by two forces: the social conflicts that parties grew out of, and the electoral rules that filter how many of those conflicts become parties ([2.2](02-02-district-magnitude-and-thresholds.md), [2.3](02-03-duvergers-law-observed.md)).

Keep three kinds of claim apart. That a vote distribution has an effective number of 3.48 parties is **mechanical**: arithmetic. That party systems in Western Europe stayed frozen for decades, or that polarized systems are unstable, is **empirical**, and the strength of the evidence varies. Whether a fragmented system is better or worse is **normative** and not argued here.

## The mechanism

1. **Count by size.** The [effective number of parties](../reference.md#effective-number-of-parties) (Laakso and Taagepera, *Comparative Political Studies*, 1979) weighs each party by its share $p_i$ (votes or seats, as fractions):
$$N = \frac{1}{\sum_i p_i^2}$$
*In words:* $N$ is the number of equal-sized parties that would give the same concentration; it is computed in full in [2.1](02-01-measuring-outcomes.md).

2. **Count by function.** Giovanni Sartori (*Parties and Party Systems*, 1976) counted differently. A party is one of the [relevant parties](../reference.md#relevant-parties) if it has **coalition potential** (it is, or plausibly could be, needed for a governing majority) or **blackmail potential** (its presence changes how the governing-oriented parties compete, typically because it is large and refuses to govern with them). A small party that keeps entering cabinets counts in full; a large party nobody can govern without or around also counts.

3. **Measure distance.** Place the relevant parties on a left–right scale (in practice from expert surveys or manifesto coding) and take the distance between the two most extreme. Ask also whether any relevant party is one of the [anti-system parties](../reference.md#anti-system-parties): in Sartori's sense, a party that undermines the legitimacy of the regime itself, not merely of the incumbents.

4. **Classify.** Sartori's types for competitive systems:

| Type | Relevant parties | Distance | Direction of competition |
|---|---|---|---|
| Predominant | one party wins majorities repeatedly | any | elections remain free |
| Two-party | 2 | usually small | centripetal |
| Moderate pluralism | about 3 to 5 | small | centripetal; two blocs alternate |
| Polarized pluralism | about 6 or more | large, with anti-system parties | centrifugal |

[Moderate and polarized pluralism](../reference.md#moderate-and-polarized-pluralism) differ in mechanics, not only in number. Under **polarized pluralism** relevant anti-system parties sit on *both* flanks (bilateral oppositions), the centre is occupied by a party or bloc that governs more or less permanently, and the flanks, never expecting to govern, can promise freely and outbid it. Competition then pulls outward. Sartori argued that the count is a symptom; the distance does the work. His leading cases were Weimar Germany and post-war Italy. That polarized pluralism destabilizes democracies is an empirical claim resting on a handful of cases.

A [dominant-party system](../reference.md#dominant-party-system) is the democratic one-winner case. Japan's Liberal Democratic Party governed without interruption from its founding in 1955 until 1993; Sweden's Social Democrats led every government from 1932 to 1976 apart from a few months in 1936, sometimes in coalition. Definitions differ: a strict test asks for repeated single-party majorities, a looser one (T. J. Pempel's comparative volume *Uncommon Democracies*, 1990) asks for long dominance in votes, seats and office. Dominance is not hegemony: the LDP lost office in 1993, which a hegemonic party cannot.

5. **Trace the origins.** Seymour Martin Lipset and Stein Rokkan (in *Party Systems and Voter Alignments*, 1967) traced Western European parties to [cleavages and the freezing hypothesis](../reference.md#cleavages-and-the-freezing-hypothesis). The **national revolution** (state-building) produced two cleavages: **centre–periphery** (the nation-building core against regions with their own language or identity) and **state–church** (secular state against the church over schooling and morals). The **industrial revolution** produced two more: **land–industry** (agrarian against urban commercial interests, giving farmers' parties) and **owner–worker** (the class cleavage, giving socialist and labour parties). Their **freezing hypothesis**, paraphrased: the party systems of the 1960s still reflected, with few exceptions, the cleavage structures of around the time mass suffrage arrived. The mechanism: when the vote was extended, parties already organized along these lines captured the new voters and left little unattached electorate for newcomers.

6. **Filter through the rules.** Cleavages supply potential parties; electoral rules decide how many survive. Octavio Amorim Neto and Gary Cox (*American Journal of Political Science*, 1997) found that the number of parties rises with social diversity mainly where district magnitude is high: many cleavages *and* permissive rules. That interaction is a well-replicated cross-national pattern, though "social diversity" is crudely measured.

7. **Watch the thaw.** Since about the 1970s, many established democracies show weaker party attachment, more vote-switching, and weaker class and religious voting: **[dealignment](../reference.md#dealignment)** (Russell Dalton and Martin Wattenberg, eds., *Parties without Partisans*, 2000). New parties grew in the gap: green and new-left parties, linked to the value change Ronald Inglehart described in *The Silent Revolution* (1977), and populist radical-right parties (Cas Mudde, *Populist Radical Right Parties in Europe*, 2007). The decline of partisanship is a broad, well-documented trend whose size varies a lot by country. Whether the new parties express a *new* cleavage, which would be realignment (Liesbeth Hooghe and Gary Marks call theirs a "transnational" cleavage: openness against national closure), or no stable cleavage at all, is contested.

**Where the evidence is weakest.** Three places. First, the causal arrow between cleavages and parties: Lipset and Rokkan read parties off social structure, but parties also mobilize and even manufacture divisions, and incumbent parties chose the electoral rules (Rokkan himself, and later Carles Boix in the *American Political Science Review*, 1999, argued that threatened incumbents adopted PR as socialist parties rose). So "PR countries have many parties" is partly "countries with many parties chose PR". Second, polarized pluralism's danger rests on a few historical cases. Third, distance depends on where parties are placed, and placements differ by method.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean): Quillon, Election I (invented).** A 100-seat national list, Sainte-Laguë, 5% legal threshold.

| Party | Position | Votes % | Seats |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tide | 0.8 | 3.0 | 0 |
| Union | 3.2 | 36.2 | 37 |
| Field | 5.4 | 11.8 | 12 |
| Hearth | 6.0 | 18.9 | 20 |
| Market | 6.8 | 30.1 | 31 |

*Fragmentation.* By votes, $\sum p_i^2 = 0.1310 + 0.0906 + 0.0357 + 0.0139 + 0.0009 = 0.2722$, so $N_v = 3.67$. By seats, $0.1369 + 0.0961 + 0.0400 + 0.0144 = 0.2874$, so $N_s = 3.48$.

*Relevance.* Market and Hearth together hold 51 seats; Union and Hearth hold 57. Hearth is pivotal both ways, and Field has sat in centre-right cabinets (stipulated), so both count. Tide holds no seats and no leverage. **Four relevant parties.**

*Distance.* Union at 3.2 to Market at 6.8: 3.6 points, no relevant anti-system party.

*Verdict.* **Moderate pluralism**: two blocs, one led by Union and one by Market, compete for the centre parties, so competition is centripetal. **The rule that decides** is Sartori's relevance rule plus the distance between the relevant extremes. Note that the effective number (3.48) and Sartori's count (4) disagree: Field counts in full for Sartori but only fractionally for $N$.

**Example 2 (hard): Quillon, Election II (invented), a generation later.** Dealignment has done its work.

| Party | Position | Votes % | Seats |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tide | 0.8 | 8.9 | 9 |
| Union | 3.2 | 22.1 | 22 |
| Leaf | 3.8 | 13.0 | 13 |
| Field | 5.4 | 7.9 | 8 |
| Hearth | 6.0 | 10.1 | 10 |
| Market | 6.8 | 19.9 | 20 |
| Banner | 9.2 | 18.1 | 18 |

By seats, $\sum s_i^2 = 0.1622$, so $N_s = 6.17$ ($N_v = 6.16$). Majorities: Union + Leaf + Hearth + Field = 53, a broad centre coalition. Market + Hearth + Field = 38, so the right can govern only with Banner (56). Banner thus has at least blackmail potential. Tide, which also refuses to govern, leaves the moderate left (Union + Leaf = 35) far short, which is why the centre coalition must reach across to Hearth and Field. Seven relevant parties, distance 8.4, a centre coalition facing oppositions on both flanks: by the numbers, **polarized pluralism**.

Here the classification strains. Sartori's type needs *anti-system* flanks. Banner attacks judges and "the political class" but contests elections and promises to keep the constitution: is it anti-system (against the regime) or anti-establishment (against the incumbents)? That is a judgement about the party, not a count. And the decisive symptom, centrifugal competition, shows up only over several elections: do Union and Market drift outward to stem their losses, or does Market bring Banner into a coalition and turn the system bipolar again? **Where the rule runs out:** the numbers place Quillon; whether it *behaves* as polarized pluralism is an empirical question one election cannot settle.

## Watch out

- **You might think the effective number and Sartori's count measure the same thing, but actually** $N$ weighs parties by size while Sartori counts them by function. A 5% party that makes and breaks governments is a whole party to Sartori and about a twentieth of one to $N$.
- **You might think fragmentation and polarization go together, but actually** they are separate coordinates. Many parties packed close together are fragmented but not polarized; three parties, one of them a large anti-system party, are less fragmented but more polarized.
- **You might think "the system is frozen" is a definition, but actually** it is an empirical claim about a period (the 1920s to the 1960s), and dealignment since is a separate empirical claim. A new party arising on an old cleavage is consistent with the cleavage model even when the old alignments have broken.

## One-liner

> Place a party system by how many parties matter and how far apart they stand; explain it by the cleavages it grew from and the rules that filtered them, and remember that the polarized case is defined by its centrifugal behaviour, which no single election shows.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢) *(Formal (a) · Exegetical (b)-(c).)*** Quillon, Election III (invented). A 100-seat list election with a 5% threshold:

| Party | Position | Votes % | Seats |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tide | 0.5 | 4 | 0 |
| Union | 3.5 | 38 | 40 |
| Field | 5.0 | 8 | 8 |
| Hearth | 5.5 | 18 | 19 |
| Market | 6.5 | 32 | 33 |

Field has sat in two of the last three cabinets; Tide rejects the constitution. (a) Compute the effective number of parties by votes and by seats, to two decimals. (b) Apply Sartori's counting rules: which parties are relevant, and what is the distance between the relevant extremes? Then classify the system. Two sentences. (c) In one sentence, say why your two numbers from (a) and your count from (b) differ.

**P2 (🟡) *(Exegetical (a)-(b).)*** Diagnose. An **invented** news report, not the words of any real person or outlet:

> "The Strand League, founded four years ago, took 9% of the national vote in Quillon yesterday, but almost all of it in the three western provinces, where it won nearly half the vote. The League demands control of fishing licences and schooling in the western Ness language. Its leader is a former dockworkers' union official, and it promises to cut fuel duty. A columnist writes: 'The League's rise refutes Lipset and Rokkan once and for all.'"

(a) Name the Lipset–Rokkan cleavage behind the League's rise, and the evidence in the report that decides it over the rival readings. Two sentences. (b) Assess the columnist's claim: what did the freezing hypothesis assert, and what does this case show about it? 80 words or fewer.

<details>
<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Formal (a) · Exegetical (b)-(c))*

(a) By votes: $\sum p_i^2 = 0.38^2 + 0.32^2 + 0.18^2 + 0.08^2 + 0.04^2$

$= 0.1444 + 0.1024 + 0.0324 + 0.0064 + 0.0016 = 0.2872$, so $N_v = 1/0.2872 = 3.48$.

By seats: $\sum s_i^2 = 0.40^2 + 0.33^2 + 0.19^2 + 0.08^2$

$= 0.1600 + 0.1089 + 0.0361 + 0.0064 = 0.3114$, so $N_s = 1/0.3114 = 3.21$.

**Must hit, strict (a):**

- $N_v = 3.48$, $N_s = 3.21$ (arithmetic slips of a few hundredths are fine if the method is right).

**Must hit, strict (b):**

- Relevant: Union, Market, Hearth and Field (Field by coalition potential: it keeps entering cabinets). Tide has no seats and no leverage, so it is not relevant even though it is anti-system.
- Distance 6.5 − 3.5 = 3.0 points; no relevant anti-system party. **Moderate pluralism**: four relevant parties, close together, two blocs (Union-led, Market-led; Market + Hearth = 52 seats) competing for the centre.

**Must hit, strict (c):**

- $N$ weighs parties by size (the seat figure is lower because Tide's votes vanish and the large parties gain), while Sartori counts by function, so the 8% Field counts as a whole party.

**Wrong turns:** counting Tide as relevant because it is anti-system (relevance needs coalition or blackmail potential, and Tide has neither); taking the distance from Tide at 0.5 (8 points full scale, not the relevant spread); calling it polarized because an anti-system party exists.

**Model answer (b)-(c):** Four parties are relevant (Union, Market, Hearth, Field), spanning 3.0 points with no relevant anti-system party, so Quillon is a moderate pluralist system with centripetal two-bloc competition. The effective numbers are fractional because they weigh each party by size and the seat figure drops Tide, while Sartori counts each party that can make or block a government as one.

---

**P2** *(Exegetical (a)-(b))*

**Must hit, strict (a):**

- **Centre–periphery.** The deciding evidence is territorial concentration (almost all its vote in three provinces) together with demands for regional control and a minority language: a periphery defending its identity and autonomy against the national centre.
- The distractors are not decisive: a trade-unionist leader and a fuel-duty pledge do not make it an owner–worker party; neither appeals to a class constituency across the country.

**Must hit, strict (b):**

- The freezing hypothesis was an empirical claim about a period: that 1960s party systems still mirrored the cleavages present when mass suffrage arrived. It was not a prediction that no party could ever rise.
- The League mobilizes one of Lipset and Rokkan's own four cleavages, so its rise is consistent with the cleavage model, though it does show the old party alignment has thawed. One case cannot refute a cross-national regularity in any event.

**Wrong turns:** naming owner–worker because of the leader's union past; reading the fuel-duty promise as land–industry; treating "frozen" as a timeless law, so that any new party refutes it.

**Model answer (b):** Lipset and Rokkan claimed that 1960s party systems still reflected the cleavages of the era of mass suffrage, not that new parties could never rise. The League rose decades later, which fits the evidence of unfreezing, but it rose on centre–periphery, one of their own four cleavages. Far from refuting the model, it is a case of an old cleavage finding a new party, and one case could not overturn a cross-national pattern anyway.

## Flashback

**From Lesson [4.2](04-02-committees-and-agenda-control.md) (Committees and agenda control):** *(Exegetical (a)-(b).)* Diagnose. An **invented** report on the Chamber of the invented Republic of Brindmoor, not about any real legislature, party or person:

> (i) "Of the 15 members of the Fisheries Committee, 13 sit for coastal districts, though coastal districts hold under a fifth of the Chamber's seats; its bills reliably raise subsidies to the fleet." (ii) "Bills from the Budget Committee, whose members' views mirror the whole Chamber's, usually pass the floor unamended because deputies defer to its long hearings." (iii) "Last month the Fisheries chair reported a quota bill that most of her own majority party opposed. It passed with opposition votes, and the majority caucus then stripped her of the chair."

(a) Match each of (i)–(iii) to the reading of committees it best fits (distributive, informational or partisan), one sentence each. (b) Does episode (iii) count for or against the cartel theory? Say what the theory predicts, and what one episode can and cannot show. 80 words or fewer.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):**

- (i) **Distributive:** deputies whose districts care most about a policy sit on the committee that controls it, and its bills deliver benefits to those districts.
- (ii) **Informational:** a committee representative of the floor does the specialist work, and the floor accepts its product; the floor is buying information.
- (iii) **Partisan:** the majority party treats the chair as its agent and removes one who opened the gate to a bill most of the party opposed.

**Must hit, strict (b):**

- The quota bill's passage is a **roll** of the majority party (a bill passing over the opposition of most of its members), which the cartel theory says gatekeeping makes rare.
- The caucus's sanction is the theory's mechanism: members delegate agenda power to leaders and chairs, and discipline an agent who misuses it.
- One episode shows the mechanism can operate; it cannot show how often gatekeeping works. That needs roll rates across many votes, and a low roll rate is itself open to the floor-median reading.

**Wrong turns:** calling (ii) partisan because the floor follows the committee; reading (iii) as refuting the cartel theory because a roll happened (the theory predicts rolls are rare, not impossible); treating one sanction as proof of the theory.

**Model answer (b):** Both ways. The bill's passage was a roll, a gate failure of the kind the cartel theory says should be rare. The caucus's response is the theory's mechanism at work: members delegate agenda power to chairs and punish one who uses it against most of the party. One episode can show that mechanism exists, not how often it decides outcomes; that needs roll rates over many votes, and even those admit the floor-median reading.

</details>
