# Political Institutions · Lesson 4.3: Parties: organization and discipline

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 4: Inside legislatures, and the parties that run them · Builds on: [1.3 List PR I](01-03-list-pr-quotas-and-largest-remainders.md), [3.1 Parliamentary government](03-01-parliamentary-government.md), [3.3 Presidential government](03-03-presidential-government.md), [4.2 Committees and agenda control](04-02-committees-and-agenda-control.md) · Unlocks: [4.4 Party systems](04-04-party-systems.md)

## Why this matters

Every model of a legislature so far has treated parties as blocs: a government "has a majority", a cartel "controls the agenda". But a legislature is a few hundred individuals who each want to keep their seat, and many want promotion. Why do they vote together at all? In some chambers a member who votes against her party once makes the news; in others, members cross party lines and nobody treats it as a revolt. This lesson finds the rules behind that difference: who picks the candidates, what the ballot rewards, and what a lost vote costs the government.

## The idea

A legislator votes with her party when the people who control her future want her to. So ask three questions about any member:

1. **Who decides whether she runs again?** Her party leader, local members, or voters in a primary.
2. **What gets her elected?** The party's label, or her own name, the **personal vote**.
3. **What does a lost vote cost?** If defeating a bill can bring down her own government and trigger an election, rebellion is expensive. If it costs nothing beyond the bill, rebellion is cheap.

Where all three answers point to the party leadership (leaders control renomination, voters choose parties, and the government's survival rides on each vote), expect near-perfect bloc voting. Where they point away (primary voters renominate, voters choose people, and nothing falls when a bill fails), expect a legislature of individual entrepreneurs who usually cooperate with their party.

Keep the claim types apart. *Mechanical*: what a selection rule, ballot or confidence rule does by its text. *Empirical*: how cohesive legislators are observed to be. *Normative*: whether a member *ought* to follow her party, her conscience or her voters. That question belongs to [political-philosophy](../../political-philosophy/syllabus.md); Madison's worry that parties are factions is [`history-of-political-thought` 5.2](../../history-of-political-thought/lessons/05-02-the-federalist-faction-and-the-extended-republic.md)'s.

## The mechanism

1. **The selectorate.** **[Candidate selection](../reference.md#candidate-selection)** is done by a *selectorate*. Reuven Hazan and Gideon Rahat order selectorates by inclusiveness: the party leader, a delegates' convention, all party members, or every voter willing to take part in a primary. *Mechanical:* a member who wants renomination must satisfy whoever selects. Germany fixes this in statute: as of 2026, the Federal Elections Act (sections 21 and 27) requires a party's constituency candidates, and the order of its Land list, to be chosen by secret ballot in an assembly of members or of their elected delegates. National leaders cannot simply write the list. In the US, nominees for Congress are chosen mostly in primaries, and no party organization can deny a nomination to someone who wins one.

2. **The ballot.** John Carey and Matthew Shugart ("Incentives to cultivate a personal vote", *Electoral Studies*, 1995) rank electoral systems by how much a candidate's own reputation is worth, using four variables:
   - **Ballot:** do leaders control who gets on the ballot and in what order?
   - **Pool:** do a candidate's votes count for the whole party list, or only for her?
   - **Vote:** does the voter choose a party, or one candidate over her co-partisans?
   - **District magnitude:** as magnitude rises, the personal vote is worth *more* where voters choose among co-partisans, and *less* where they choose only parties.

   In words: a **[closed list](../reference.md#closed-open-and-flexible-lists)** makes a member's seat depend on her rank, and her rank on the selectorate. An open list or [STV](../reference.md#single-transferable-vote) makes it depend on beating her own colleagues for votes, which takes a local name the leader cannot give or take away. The ranking is a *deduction*. Evidence that legislators actually respond (more constituency service, more defection under candidate-centred rules) is supportive in many single-country studies but uneven across countries.

3. **The constitution.** Under parliamentary government the cabinet survives only while it holds the chamber's confidence ([3.1](03-01-parliamentary-government.md), [confidence and no-confidence](../reference.md#confidence-and-no-confidence)). Leaders can tie any bill to it. Germany's Basic Law, Article 68(1), as of 2026:

   > "If a motion of the Federal Chancellor for a vote of confidence is not supported by the majority of the Members of the Bundestag, the Federal President, upon the proposal of the Federal Chancellor, may dissolve the Bundestag within twenty-one days."

   In the UK, the Dissolution and Calling of Parliament Act 2022 revived the prerogative power of [dissolution](../reference.md#dissolution), which the prime minister asks the monarch to use. The chain: a rebel who defeats a confidence bill may bring down her own party's government, hand office to the other side, and face an early election. Daniel Diermeier and Timothy Feddersen (*American Political Science Review*, 1998) model why this binds: once bills double as votes on who keeps control of the agenda, a governing coalition's members vote together even on issues that would otherwise split them. Under presidential government ([3.3](03-03-presidential-government.md)) the executive and the assembly survive separately: a president's bill can fail and no one falls, and there is no dissolution. The career ladder differs too. A parliamentary minister is normally drawn from the leader's own MPs. The US Constitution (Article I, section 6) bars a member of Congress from holding executive office while serving.

4. **The whips' toolkit.** **[Party discipline](../reference.md#party-discipline)** is enforced by whips, who count votes in advance and apply the leadership's sanctions: promotion or its denial, committee posts, and in Westminster systems withdrawal of the whip. An MP who loses the whip stays in the Commons but sits as an independent, and is outside the party until it is restored. Money is a lever too. **Party finance** in outline: whoever raises the money holds the lever. Germany's Basic Law (Article 21) requires parties to account publicly for their funds, and the Political Parties Act (section 18) funds them partly from the state budget, allotted by votes won, members' and office-holders' contributions, and donations raised. US congressional candidates raise most of their money through their own campaign committees, so the money builds a personal organization rather than a party one.

5. **Measuring the outcome.** Cohesion on a recorded vote is usually measured by the **[Rice index](../reference.md#rice-index)**:
   $$R = \frac{|Y - N|}{Y + N},$$
   where $Y$ and $N$ are the party's members voting yes and no (abstentions excluded). $R = 1$ is a unanimous party; $R = 0$ is an even split. *In words:* the party's margin as a share of its voting members.

**Empirical.** Parties in parliamentary systems are observed to vote more cohesively than those in presidential systems. This is a widely reported cross-national pattern in roll-call studies, but with large overlaps. US congressional voting has also become far more party-line since the 1970s, with no change to the constitution.

**Where the evidence is weakest.** Three places. *Cohesion is not discipline:* a Rice index of 0.98 cannot tell members who obeyed from members who agreed, and the US rise since the 1970s tracks parties whose members grew ideologically apart, with no new sanctions. Keith Krehbiel's "Where's the party?" (1993) pressed this: shared preferences can mimic party strength. *The votes are selected:* leaders call the votes they expect to win ([negative agenda control](../reference.md#negative-agenda-control), [4.2](04-02-committees-and-agenda-control.md)), so splits that never reach the floor go unrecorded. *The variables travel together:* presidential systems often also use primaries or open lists, so "parliamentarism causes cohesion" and "party-controlled ballots cause cohesion" are hard to separate in the cross-national data.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean): Pellamar's pension bill.** Invented country and numbers. Pellamar is a parliamentary democracy with a 150-seat chamber elected from closed lists. The governing party, Harbour, holds 80 seats; the opposition's 70 vote as a bloc against Harbour's bill raising the pension age. Fourteen Harbour MPs from older districts dislike the bill.

*Free vote.* If the leadership leaves the vote free, 66 Harbour MPs vote yes and 14 no. Rice $= |66 - 14|/80 = 0.65$. The bill fails, 66 to 84.

*Confidence vote.* The prime minister declares the bill a matter of confidence. Now each of the 14 is choosing between the bill and her own government, a possible early election, and a list rank set by Harbour's selectorate. Eleven vote yes, two abstain, one votes no. Rice $= |77 - 1|/78 \approx 0.974$; the bill passes, 77 to 71.

Same members, same preferences, same bill. **The rule that decided:** the confidence attachment, which turned a pension vote into a vote on who governs. A presidential constitution has no such switch, which is why the same 14 legislators would face a far cheaper defection there.

**Example 2 (hard): Harbour's perfect record.** In a later session Harbour's average Rice index over 120 recorded divisions is 0.97, and commentators call it the most disciplined party in Pellamar's history. The whips' private counts showed two further bills would have drawn 25 Harbour votes against (Rice $= |55 - 25|/80 = 0.375$ on each), so the leadership withdrew them. Had they been called, the average over 122 divisions would be $(120 \times 0.97 + 2 \times 0.375)/122 \approx 0.96$.

The arithmetic changes little; the inference changes a lot. The record shows a party that votes together *on the votes it holds*, and the 0.97 hides two policies the party could not pass. The recorded index cannot tell discipline (members overruled) from agreement (members persuaded) from selection (disagreements kept off the floor). **Where the rule runs out:** the confidence rule explains why Harbour members voted together once a vote was called. It cannot say which votes the leadership dared to call. That depends on the whips' counts, which are private.

## Watch out

- **You might think a high Rice index proves strong party discipline, but actually it is an empirical measure of an outcome.** Discipline is a claim about sanctions. Agreement and agenda selection produce the same number, as Example 2 shows.
- **You might think Germany's free mandate makes Bundestag members free agents, but actually Article 38(1) bars binding "orders or instructions", meaning legal compulsion.** It leaves untouched the political levers: renomination by the party's assemblies, list rank, promotion and the Chancellor's confidence question. Party groups in the Bundestag nonetheless usually vote as blocs. The mechanical rule (no binding mandate) and the observed behaviour (cohesion) answer different questions.
- **You might think open lists strip the party of all ballot control, but actually the selectorate still decides who appears on the list at all.** What passes to voters is the *order*. Losing that lever weakens a leader, but deselection remains; only primaries hand access itself to voters.

## One-liner

> Legislators vote with their party when the party controls their renomination, their ballot fate and their government's survival; roll-call cohesion measures the result, but cannot by itself say whether it came from discipline, agreement or the votes leaders chose not to call.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢) *(Exegetical (a)-(b).)*** Diagnose. An **invented** news report from the invented country of Pellamar, not about any real party:

> "Two years ago Pellamar replaced its closed lists with open lists: preference votes alone now order each party's list. This month 19 MPs of the governing Harbour party voted against the government's fuel-tax bill, most from rural districts, against 3 rebels on a comparable bill under the old rules. A Harbour whip admitted that the threat to 'move rebels down the list' no longer frightens anyone. Open lists, the report concludes, cause rebellion."

(a) Name the institutional change that disarmed the whip's threat, using Carey and Shugart's variables, and name one lever Harbour's leadership still holds. Three sentences or fewer. (b) The final sentence is a causal claim. Say what kind of claim it is, and name two features of this comparison that could explain the rise in rebels without the ballot change. 80 words or fewer.

**P2 (🟡) *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)*** Apply to a hard case. Pellamar's other large party, Ridge, now in opposition, adopts a reform. Until now its five-member executive decided who appeared on its open lists. From the next election, every Ridge list will be chosen by a postal ballot of all the party's members, and the executive's veto over candidates is abolished.

(a) Classify Ridge's selectorate before and after, and name which leadership sanction the reform removes and two that it leaves untouched. Three sentences or fewer. (b) If Ridge enters government after the next election, should we expect its MPs to vote more or less cohesively than under the old rule? Answer in 120 words or fewer, saying where the rules stop determining the answer. Any verdict passes.

<details>
<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical (a)-(b), strict)*

**Must hit, strict (a):**

- The **Ballot** variable changed: leaders (through the selectorate) no longer control list *order*, so "moving rebels down the list" is no longer something they can do. The **Vote** variable changed with it: voters now choose candidates over their co-partisans, so a rural MP's seat depends on her own local reputation, which defying an unpopular fuel tax may *raise*.
- At least one remaining lever: the selectorate still controls *access* (it can leave a rebel off the list entirely); the government can make the bill a matter of confidence; promotion to office; withdrawal of the whip.

**Must hit, strict (b):**

- It is an **empirical** (causal) claim, not a mechanical one: the rule change is mechanical, but whether it produced the rebellion is a matter of evidence.
- Two rival explanations from the comparison itself, for instance: the fuel tax may divide rural from urban districts more than the earlier bill did; the vote may not have been made a matter of confidence; Harbour's majority may differ (a large majority makes rebellion cheap because the bill passes anyway); one pair of votes is a single comparison, not a pattern.

**Wrong turns:** saying open lists leave the leadership with *no* ballot lever (access remains with the selectorate); treating "19 versus 3" as proof of the causal claim; answering (a) with district magnitude alone, which did not change.

**Model answer:** (a) Moving to open lists took list order away from the leadership (Carey and Shugart's Ballot variable) and made voters choose among Harbour's own candidates (Vote), so a rural MP now keeps her seat by her local name, and opposing a fuel tax may help it. The leadership still controls who appears on the list at all, and can tie a bill to confidence. (b) It is an empirical causal claim. A single before-and-after pair cannot separate the ballot change from the bill (a fuel tax splits rural MPs in a way the earlier bill may not have) or from whether the vote carried the government's survival.

---

**P2** *(Exegetical (a), strict · Evaluative (b), any verdict)*

**Must hit, strict (a):**

- Before: an exclusive selectorate (a small party executive, near the leader end of Hazan and Rahat's scale). After: an inclusive one (all party members).
- Removed: the executive's power to deny renomination (deselection or a veto over access).
- Untouched, any two: the confidence question once Ridge governs; ministerial promotion; withdrawal of the whip; committee posts. (List order was already set by preference votes, so it was never the executive's lever.)

**Must hit, any verdict (b):**

- Run the mechanism in both directions: losing the deselection threat weakens the leadership, which predicts *less* cohesion; but if members punish MPs who stray from the party line, discipline is transferred to the members rather than lost, which predicts cohesion around *the members'* positions.
- Name what still binds once Ridge governs: the confidence rule fuses MPs' votes with the government's survival whatever the selectorate.
- Say where the rules stop: the answer turns on whether members' preferences sit near the leadership's or away from it, and on what members reward (loyalty or independence). The selection rule does not settle that. Name evidence that would: how members vote in the first ballots, or cohesion in parties that made a similar switch.

**Wrong turns:** treating "more inclusive" as automatically "less cohesive"; forgetting that the confidence rule still operates once Ridge governs; claiming the reform changes list order, which open lists already gave to voters.

**Model answer (b), one of several:** Less, at the margin, but not much. The executive loses its deselection threat, which was its sharpest pre-election weapon. But once Ridge governs, every major bill can be made a confidence question, and that binds MPs whatever the selectorate. Members may also act as a new enforcer: a membership more ideological than the leadership would punish MPs who back compromise deals, producing cohesion around the members' line and trouble for the leaders who must bargain. The rules stop at preferences. If members sit near the leadership, little changes; if they sit away from it, expect fights between MPs and ministers. The first two membership ballots would show which.

</details>

## Flashback

**From Lesson [4.1](04-01-bicameralism.md) (Bicameralism):** *(Formal.)* Caldermoor (invented) has a Chamber of 400 deputies and a Council of Regions made of regional government delegations, each region casting all its votes as a unit. The Council has 55 votes: regions governed only by the national governing parties hold 17, regions governed only by opposition parties hold 25, and two mixed-coalition regions, Lenholt (8 votes) and Sarrow (5 votes), abstain unless stated. Every Council decision needs a majority of *all* 55 votes. The rules:

- **Consent bills** become law only if the Council approves them.
- **Objection bills** become law unless the Council objects. The Chamber overrides an objection by a majority of all deputies; but if the objection carried at least two-thirds of all Council votes, the override needs two-thirds of the deputies voting, including a majority of all deputies.

The governing parties hold 214 seats; all 400 deputies vote on party lines. (a) Does a consent bill pass with both mixed regions abstaining? Which mixed regions must the government win over to pass it without opposition regions? (b) For an objection bill, say whether the Council objects, by what majority, and whether the bill becomes law, when: (i) both mixed regions abstain; (ii) Lenholt joins the objection; (iii) both join. (c) In case (iii), how many opposition deputies must vote with the government for the bill to become law? Name the rule that decides, in one sentence.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

Council thresholds: a majority of all votes is $\lfloor 55/2 \rfloor + 1 = 28$; two-thirds of all votes is $\tfrac{2}{3}\times 55 \approx 36.7$, so at least **37**.

(a) Yes votes $17 < 28$: **fails**, and the abstentions count against it.

| Mixed regions won | Yes votes | Passes? |
|---|---|---|
| Lenholt | 25 | no |
| Sarrow | 22 | no |
| Both | 30 | yes |

The government needs **both** mixed regions.

(b) Chamber thresholds: a majority of all deputies is 201; two-thirds of 400 voting is $\tfrac{2}{3}\times 400 \approx 266.7$, so at least 267.

| Case | Objection votes | Council objects? | Override needs | Becomes law? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| (i) | 25 | no ($25 < 28$) | nothing | **yes**, no objection |
| (ii) | $25+8=33$ | yes, simple majority ($28 \le 33 < 37$) | 201 | **yes**, $214 \ge 201$ |
| (iii) | $25+8+5=38$ | yes, two-thirds ($38 \ge 37$) | 267 | **no**, $214 < 267$ |

(c) $267 - 214 = 53$: **53** opposition deputies (with 52 the yes side has 266 of 400, short of two-thirds). The rule that decides is the two-thirds clause: an objection carried by at least two-thirds of the Council raises the Chamber's override bar from a majority of all deputies to two-thirds of those voting.

**Wrong turns:** counting a majority of votes *cast*, which in (b)(i) lets the opposition's 25 votes object against no one; the majority-of-all-votes rule is what makes abstention help the government on objection bills and hurt it on consent bills. Rounding two-thirds of 55 down to 36, which would also put a 36-vote objection over the line. Treating a two-thirds objection as an absolute veto: it only raises the override bar, and 53 crossers would clear it.

**Model answer:** (a) Fails, 17 of the 28 needed; Lenholt or Sarrow alone is not enough, both together give 30. (b) (i) No objection, 25 < 28, so the bill is law. (ii) A simple-majority objection (33), overridden by the government's 214 ≥ 201. (iii) A two-thirds objection (38 ≥ 37); the override needs 267 and the government has 214, so the bill dies. (c) 53 opposition deputies; the two-thirds objection clause decides.

</details>
