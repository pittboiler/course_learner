# Political Institutions · Lesson 6.3: Direct democracy

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 6: Delegation, direct democracy, and the whole design · Builds on: [6.2 Delegation and oversight](06-02-delegation-and-oversight.md), [4.1 Bicameralism](04-01-bicameralism.md) · Unlocks: [6.4 Majoritarian and consensus democracy](06-04-majoritarian-and-consensus-democracy.md)

## Why this matters

[6.2](06-02-delegation-and-oversight.md) followed authority downward, from legislature to agency. Direct democracy runs the other way: voters decide a question themselves instead of leaving it to their representatives. The one word "referendum" covers very different devices. No Swiss constitutional amendment can pass without a popular vote. Californians write statutes their legislature cannot freely amend. An Italian repeal vote can fail even though most of those who vote say Yes. And the UK's 2016 vote changed no law, yet set the country's course. This lesson classifies the devices, then computes how two decision rules, the turnout quorum and the double majority, change outcomes.

## The idea

Ask three questions of any popular vote.

1. **Who puts it on the ballot?** The constitution (automatically), citizens (by collecting signatures), or the authorities (a government or parliament choosing to ask).
2. **What is on the ballot?** A text the legislature has just passed, a text citizens drafted, or a law already in force.
3. **What happens after the count?** Either the result changes the law by itself (binding), or it goes back to the representatives (advisory).

The answers give the four standard [referendum types](../reference.md#referendum-types). Rules as of 2026:

| Type | Trigger | On the ballot | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mandatory | the constitution | a measure of a listed kind | Switzerland: every constitutional amendment (Art. 140) |
| Optional (facultative) | signatures, after passage | a law just passed | Switzerland: 50,000 voters or eight cantons within 100 days of publication (Art. 141) |
| Citizens' initiative | signatures | citizens' own text | Switzerland: 100,000 voters within 18 months (Art. 139); California |
| Abrogative | signatures or regions | a law in force, to repeal it | Italy: 500,000 voters or five Regional Councils (Art. 75) |

A fifth kind, the **authorities' (ad hoc) referendum**, is called by a government or parliament on a question it chooses: the UK's three national votes, in 1975, 2011 and 2016. All of this is **mechanical**: the texts answer it. The deepest line in the table separates the optional referendum from the initiative. The first is a citizens' **veto**: a No keeps the status quo. The second is citizens' **agenda-setting**: a Yes writes new law.

Defenders, stated at full strength, say direct democracy lets citizens check representatives on questions where politicians share an interest against voters, such as their own pay or electoral rules. It also gives constitutional change a legitimacy no legislature can supply. Critics reply that single-issue votes break the cross-issue bargaining legislatures do, reward framing and money, and expose minorities to majorities without a forum for compromise. The **empirical** record is mixed. An influential reading of Swiss history, associated with Leonhard Neidhart, holds that the threat of an optional referendum pushed parliament toward broad pre-legislative compromise and all-party government. That is a single case, persuasive to many, hard to test. Barbara Gamble found that US ballot measures restricting minorities' civil rights passed unusually often; Todd Donovan and Shaun Bowler argued that the pattern weakens in larger jurisdictions. That evidence is contested. Whether direct democracy is *better* is **normative**: see [political-philosophy 5.3](../../political-philosophy/lessons/05-03-deliberative-vs-aggregative-democracy.md) on aggregation versus deliberation.

## The mechanism

Run any popular vote through five steps.

1. **Trigger.** Signatures within a deadline, or an automatic requirement. California's initiative statutes need signatures equal to 5% of the votes cast for governor at the last election; initiative constitutional amendments need 8% (Cal. Const. art. II, s. 8(b)).
2. **Gatekeeping.** Someone may keep a proposal off the ballot. Switzerland's Federal Assembly declares an initiative invalid if it lacks unity of form or subject matter, or breaks mandatory international law (Art. 139(3)). Italy's Art. 75 excludes laws on taxes, the budget, amnesty and pardon, and treaty ratification, and the Constitutional Court rules on admissibility.
3. **The decision rule.** The default is a majority of those voting. Three common additions:
   - A **[double majority](../reference.md#double-majority)**. Swiss votes of the People and Cantons pass only "if a majority of those who vote and a majority of the Cantons approve them" (Art. 142(2)). Each canton's popular result decides its vote, and six cantons count half, so there are 23 cantonal votes and 12 are needed.
   - A **[turnout quorum](../reference.md#turnout-quorum)**. Italy's abrogative referendum is carried "if the majority of those eligible has voted and a majority of valid votes has been achieved" (Art. 75). Italy's confirmatory referendum on constitutional amendments (Art. 138) has no quorum: one country, two rules.
   - An **[approval quorum](../reference.md#approval-quorum)**: Yes must reach a set share of the *whole electorate*.
4. **Legal effect.** Whether a vote is [binding or advisory](../reference.md#binding-and-advisory-referendums) is read off the authorizing text. The UK's Parliamentary Voting System and Constituencies Act 2011 obliged a minister to bring the alternative-vote provisions into force after a Yes (once a boundary order was made) and to repeal them after a No (s. 8). The European Union Referendum Act 2015 required a vote and fixed the question, but said nothing about consequences. So in *Miller* (2017) the Supreme Court held that the 2016 result did not authorize withdrawal without legislation: until Parliament acted, its force was "political rather than legal".
5. **Afterlife.** Can the representatives undo the result? The California legislature may amend or repeal an approved initiative statute only with the voters' approval, unless the initiative itself permits otherwise (art. II, s. 10(c)).

*In words:* the trigger decides who can ask, the gatekeeper what may be asked, the decision rule what counts as Yes, and the legal-effect and afterlife clauses how long a Yes lasts.

The two quorums create opposite incentives, by arithmetic alone. Under a turnout quorum, if Yes leads, an opponent who votes No *helps* the vote reach the quorum, while one who stays home pushes it toward invalidity, so opponents' best tactic is a boycott. Under an approval quorum a No vote never helps Yes, so abstaining gains opponents nothing; the cost is that a measure can win a clear majority of voters and still fail. The Venice Commission's *Code of Good Practice on Referendums* (2007) advises against both kinds for exactly these reasons. Defenders of quorums answer that without one, a mobilized minority can change the law on a low turnout. That dispute is normative. The mechanism is not in dispute.

**Where the rule runs out.** The rules settle the count, not what the count means. Three gaps remain. *Wording:* the decision rule says nothing about how the question is framed, and framing can move votes. *Implementation:* when Swiss voters approve an initiative drafted only as a general proposal, the Federal Assembly then drafts the bill (Art. 139(4)), so the text guarantees a bill but not its content. *Advisory force:* *Miller* settles that an advisory result binds no one in law, but how far it binds politically is decided by representatives who must face the voters.

Read it right to left. As more opponents stay home, the Yes share *rises* and the measure *fails*. The quorum turns each abstention into a No.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean): a boycott in Yarrowmere.** Yarrowmere (invented) has an abrogative referendum: a law is repealed if more than half of the electorate votes and Yes beats No. Electorate 2,000,000. Invented polls: 760,000 will vote to repeal, 540,000 oppose, 700,000 never vote.

- *Everyone votes sincerely:* turnout $1{,}300{,}000 / 2{,}000{,}000 = 65\%$; Yes $= 760{,}000 / 1{,}300{,}000 = 58.5\%$. **Repealed.**
- *Opponents boycott entirely:* turnout $760{,}000 / 2{,}000{,}000 = 38\%$. **Invalid; the law stands**, although every vote cast said Yes.
- *The threshold:* the quorum needs turnout above 1,000,000, so No votes above $1{,}000{,}000 - 760{,}000 = 240{,}000$. A boycott by **300,000 or more** of the 540,000 opponents (5/9, about 56%) defeats repeal. At exactly 300,000, turnout is exactly half, which is not "more than half".

Without a quorum, staying home would only widen the Yes margin. *The rule that decides:* the quorum clause, which changes both the threshold and the opponents' best strategy.

**Empirical:** Italy's record fits the prediction. Since the late 1990s most abrogative referendums have been declared invalid for low turnout, often with lopsided Yes majorities among those who did vote. Evidence strength: a long run of cases, but in one country. How many would have passed without a quorum cannot be read off the results, because the quorum itself changed who turned out.

**Example 2 (hard): when the two majorities disagree.** Yarrowmere copies Switzerland's Art. 139b. Voters may approve both a citizens' initiative and parliament's counter-proposal, and a third, deciding question asks which they prefer if both pass. If the People prefer one and the cantons the other, the proposal with the higher sum of its two percentages on the deciding question wins: its share of the popular vote plus its share of the cantonal votes. Yarrowmere has 5 full cantons and 2 half-cantons: 6 cantonal votes.

An initiative for 30 weeks' parental leave and a counter-proposal of 20 weeks both win double majorities. On the deciding question, 51.8% of voters prefer the initiative, but only Aldon and Brevik do, with 2 of 6 cantonal votes (33.33%).

| | People | Cantons | Sum |
|---|---|---|---|
| Initiative | 51.80 | 33.33 | 85.13 |
| Counter-proposal | 48.20 | 66.67 | 114.87 |

**The counter-proposal comes into force.** In general, if $p$ and $c$ are the initiative's popular and cantonal percentages, it wins when

$$p + c > (100 - p) + (100 - c), \quad \text{that is,} \quad p + c > 100.$$

*In words:* with only 2 of 6 cantonal votes, the initiative needed more than 66.67% of the people.

The strain: two separate majority tests have been turned into one weighted sum. One Yarrowmere cantonal vote is worth 16.67 points of the national vote (one Swiss full canton, 1/23, is worth about 4.35). And the rule still runs out: Art. 139b's text does not say what happens if the sums are equal. California settles a clash between two approved measures differently: the one with more Yes votes prevails (art. II, s. 10(b)).

## Watch out

- **You might think the optional referendum and the initiative are one device because both need signatures, but they are opposites.** The optional referendum lets citizens veto what parliament passed, so the status quo wins on No. The initiative lets citizens set the agenda, so new law wins on Yes.
- **You might think "advisory" means "inconsequential", but that confuses a mechanical claim with an empirical one.** Legal effect is read off the authorizing statute (the 2015 Act provided none). Political effect is an observed consequence, and 2016 shows it can be decisive.
- **You might think turnout and approval quorums are two strengths of one rule, but their incentives are opposite.** A turnout quorum rewards opponents for abstaining. An approval quorum gives them no reason to, but can defeat a measure that most voters supported.

## One-liner

> Classify a popular vote by who triggers it, what is on the ballot and what the result legally does; then find the decision rule, because a turnout quorum makes abstention a No and a double majority lets the cantons outvote the people.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢) *(Exegetical.)*** Diagnose. An **invented** news digest from Yarrowmere; not the words of any real outlet.

> (i) "The amendment to the electoral article goes to voters automatically in May." (ii) "Opponents of the broadcasting law passed in March filed 61,000 signatures in time, so the law cannot take effect until a vote." (iii) "A tenants' group has collected enough signatures to put its own rent-cap text on the ballot." (iv) "Hunters are gathering signatures to repeal the 1998 game law." (v) "The government will ask voters whether to join a customs union; ministers say parliament will take the result into account."

Name the type of each vote. For (v), say in one sentence whether the result is binding and what would make it so.

**P2 (🟡) *(Formal.)*** Yarrowmere amends its constitution by a vote needing a majority of voters and a majority of cantonal votes (a canton's popular result decides its vote; half-cantons count half).

| Canton | Cantonal vote | Yes | No |
|---|---|---|---|
| Aldon | 1 | 372,000 | 228,000 |
| Brevik | 1 | 261,000 | 189,000 |
| Corrin | 1 | 66,000 | 84,000 |
| Dunlow | 1 | 49,000 | 71,000 |
| Esk | 1 | 46,000 | 54,000 |
| Fenn Upper | 1/2 | 19,000 | 21,000 |
| Fenn Lower | 1/2 | 23,000 | 17,000 |

(a) Does the amendment pass? Give the popular totals and the cantonal count. (b) What is the smallest number of voters who, by switching from No to Yes in a single canton, would have made it pass? Name the canton.

**P3 (🔴, optional) *(Formal (a)-(b) · Evaluative (c).)*** Apply to a hard case. A Yarrowmere abrogative referendum (the Example 1 rule: more than half the electorate must vote, and Yes must beat No). Electorate 3,000,000; invented polls: 1,200,000 for repeal, 500,000 against.

(a) Is the law repealed if everyone votes sincerely? The No campaign calls a boycott and 60% of its supporters stay home: what are turnout and the Yes share now, and is the law repealed? How many opponents must stay home, at minimum, to defeat repeal? (b) Suppose the rule were instead an approval quorum: Yes must beat No and reach 35% of the electorate. Can the boycott defeat repeal? (c) After the outcome in (a), the Yes side says a boycott that defeats a 6-to-1 majority of voters is illegitimate; the No side says the quorum worked as designed. Name what each side assumes abstention means. Any verdict; 100 words or fewer.

<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical, strict)*

**Must hit, strict:**

- (i) **Mandatory**: the constitution requires the vote for an amendment.
- (ii) **Optional (facultative)**: signatures against a law just passed, before it takes effect; a citizens' veto.
- (iii) **Citizens' initiative**: citizens put their own text on the ballot.
- (iv) **Abrogative**: signatures to repeal a law long in force.
- (v) **Authorities' (ad hoc) referendum**, called by the government. As described it is **advisory**: binding force must come from the authorizing law, which would have to state the consequence of each result (as the UK's 2011 Act did for the alternative vote).

**Wrong turns:** calling (ii) an initiative because it used signatures, when what is on the ballot is parliament's text, not the citizens'; calling (iv) optional, when the law is decades old rather than just passed; treating (v) as binding because the government called it.

---

**P2** *(Formal, strict)*

(a) Popular vote:

$$\text{Yes} = 372 + 261 + 66 + 49 + 46 + 19 + 23 = 836 \text{ thousand}$$

$$\text{No} = 228 + 189 + 84 + 71 + 54 + 21 + 17 = 664 \text{ thousand}$$

Yes has $836{,}000 / 1{,}500{,}000 = 55.7\%$: **the people say Yes.** Cantons: Yes in Aldon (1), Brevik (1), Fenn Lower (1/2), so 2.5; No in Corrin, Dunlow, Esk (3) and Fenn Upper (1/2), so 3.5. A majority of 6 cantonal votes needs more than 3. **The cantons say No, so the amendment fails.**

(b) Switching $s$ voters moves the margin by $2s$:

| Canton flipped | No minus Yes | Switchers needed | Cantonal Yes after | Passes? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Fenn Upper | 2,000 | 1,001 | 3 of 6 | no: 3 is not a majority |
| Esk | 8,000 | 4,001 | 3.5 of 6 | yes |
| Corrin | 18,000 | 9,001 | 3.5 of 6 | yes |
| Dunlow | 22,000 | 11,001 | 3.5 of 6 | yes |

**Esk, 4,001 voters**: $46{,}000 + 4{,}001 = 50{,}001 > 49{,}999$.

**Must hit, strict:**

- 836,000 to 664,000 for Yes; 2.5 to 3.5 cantonal votes; fails.
- Esk with 4,001; Fenn Upper's cheaper flip produces 3 of 6, a tie, which is not a majority.

**Wrong turns:** answering Fenn Upper with 1,001 (it ties the cantons and still fails); answering 4,000, which leaves Esk tied at 50,000 each; counting the half-cantons as full votes, which turns the count into 3 to 4 of 7 and hides the trap.

---

**P3** *(Formal (a)-(b), strict · Evaluative (c), any verdict)*

(a) Sincere: turnout $1{,}700{,}000 / 3{,}000{,}000 = 56.7\%$, above half; Yes $1{,}200{,}000/1{,}700{,}000 = 70.6\%$. **Repealed.** With 60% of opponents at home, No votes are $500{,}000 \times 0.4 = 200{,}000$, so turnout is $1{,}400{,}000 / 3{,}000{,}000 = 46.7\%$ and Yes is $1{,}200{,}000 / 1{,}400{,}000 = 85.7\%$. **Invalid; the law stands.** The quorum needs turnout above 1,500,000, so No votes above 300,000. **At least 200,000 opponents (40%) must stay home**; at exactly 200,000, turnout is exactly half, which fails.

(b) The approval threshold is $0.35 \times 3{,}000{,}000 = 1{,}050{,}000$. Yes has 1,200,000 (40% of the electorate) and beats No however many opponents vote. **The boycott cannot defeat repeal**; staying home only shrinks No.

**Must hit, strict (a)-(b):**

- Repealed when all vote; 46.7% and 85.7%, invalid, under the boycott; 200,000 minimum; under the approval quorum, repeal succeeds whatever opponents do.

**Must hit, any verdict (c):**

- The Yes side treats abstention as **indifference**, so only votes cast measure the popular will.
- The No side treats abstention as a **choice for the status quo**, which a turnout quorum deliberately counts with No.
- Note that the rule settles validity but not this dispute: whether the quorum *should* exist is normative.

**Wrong turns:** in (a), using a 50% boycott (250,000) as the threshold; in (b), thinking that opponents' abstention lowers turnout and so matters, when an approval quorum looks only at Yes; in (c), declaring a winner without naming what each side assumes abstention means.

**Model answer (c), one of several:** The Yes side assumes those who stay home have no view, so 85.7% of votes cast is the people speaking. The No side assumes staying home is a considered vote for the status quo, and that the quorum exists to stop a mobilized minority, here 40% of the electorate, from repealing a law the rest would keep. The quorum rule decides validity; it cannot settle which reading of abstention is right.

## Flashback

**From Lesson [6.1](06-01-bureaucracy-merit-and-patronage.md) (Bureaucracy: merit and patronage):** *(Exegetical (a)-(b).)* Counterexample. An **invented** cabinet memo from the invented country of Corriemont, not the words of any real person or agency: "Since the Tenure Act, no official in our ministries can be dismissed except for misconduct proven before a tribunal. We therefore have a merit civil service, and corruption will fall." The same Act leaves hiring to each minister's discretion, with no examination or open competition. In the four years before the last election, ministers filled 3,000 posts this way, mostly with members of their own party. (a) Name the appointment and removal rules for these posts, say why the memo's first inference fails, and say what happens to the 3,000 officials when the opposition wins the election. Three sentences. (b) A cross-national study found meritocratic *recruitment* associated with less corruption, while job security showed no significant effect. Does it support the memo's prediction, and how strong would such evidence be even where it applies? Two sentences.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):**

- Appointment at the minister's discretion (the patronage rule); removal only for cause through a tribunal (the merit rule). The posts are a hybrid, the mirror image of Germany's political civil servants, who are recruited by merit but removable at will.
- The memo reads "merit" off the removal rule alone. A merit post needs both rules, open competition *and* removal only for cause; these 3,000 posts are the counterexample, protected yet filled by patronage.
- The removal rule decides who stays: the new government cannot skip the tribunal, so all 3,000 stay, owing their jobs to the outgoing ministers' party. Patronage is entrenched rather than replaced.

**Must hit, strict (b):**

- No. The Act changes only the removal rule; the finding attaches to recruitment, which the Act leaves discretionary, and the feature the Act does change, job security, is the one that showed no significant effect.
- Strength: a robust cross-national correlation, but the direction of cause is contested (cleaner, richer states may be the ones that adopt merit), and much corruption evidence rests on perception measures. What the Act does is mechanical; "corruption will fall" is an empirical prediction.

**Wrong turns:** calling the posts plain patronage, when patronage pairs discretion with removal at will and the point here is that no one can remove them; agreeing with the memo because the officials are protected; reading the study as showing that tenure reduces corruption, or as proving that merit recruitment causes clean government.

**Model answer:** (a) The posts pair discretionary appointment with removal only for cause, a hybrid that mirrors Germany's merit-recruited, freely removable political civil servants. The memo fails because merit needs both rules, and these officials were chosen by ministers from their own party, not by open competition. When the opposition wins, the removal rule keeps all 3,000 in post, still owing their jobs to the defeated party, so patronage is locked in rather than swept out. (b) No: the Act touches only job security, which the study found made no significant difference, and leaves untouched the recruitment rule that the finding is about. Even where it applies, the evidence is a robust correlation whose direction of cause is contested, since cleaner states may be the ones that adopt merit.

</details>
