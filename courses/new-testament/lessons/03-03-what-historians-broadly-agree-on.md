# The New Testament · Lesson 3.3: What historians broadly agree on

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 3: The historical Jesus · Builds on: [3.1 The quests](03-01-the-quests.md), [3.2 Sources and criteria](03-02-sources-and-criteria.md) · Unlocks: [3.4 The passion and resurrection narratives](03-04-the-passion-and-resurrection-narratives.md)

## Why this matters

Do the quests and criteria produce results? Yes: there is a short list of facts about Jesus that historians of almost every stripe accept. Past that list the disagreements start, and the largest is over what Jesus expected God to do next. Know where the line runs: people claim "scholars agree" for things they do not, and the historians' list is easily confused with the creed, which overlaps it in one place on a different foundation.

## The idea

Think of three bins.

1. **Near-consensus.** Claims that survive every serious method, because the evidence is multiple, early, and sometimes awkward for the people who handed it on.
2. **Live dispute.** Claims about the same public figure where competent historians read the same evidence differently.
3. **Beyond the method.** Claims that history cannot reach at all, as opposed to claims it has tested and rejected. "Jesus had a reputation as an exorcist" goes in bin 1. "Jesus cast out demons by the Spirit of God" goes in bin 3. The historian can report the reputation, not certify its cause.

Most confusion comes from moving a claim between bins unnoticed.

## Source

Two sayings that carry most of the weight on both sides of the big dispute. Douay-Rheims (Challoner); the verse numbers are those of the Greek text, and Mark 9:1 is 8:39 in the Douay.

> And after that John was delivered up, Jesus came into Galilee, preaching the gospel of the kingdom of God, and saying: The time is accomplished and the kingdom of God is at hand. Repent and believe the gospel. (Mark 1:14–15)

> But if I by the finger of God cast out devils, doubtless the kingdom of God is come upon you. (Luke 11:20; cf. Matthew 12:28)

Two verbs decide the reading. In Mark 1:15, *ēngiken* is the perfect of *engizō*, "draw near": my translation is "the kingdom of God **has come near**", close but not yet here. In Luke 11:20, *ephthasen* is "has **arrived** upon you": in some sense it is already present in the exorcisms. The whole debate over Jesus's eschatology (his teaching about the last things) sits in the space between those two verbs. C. H. Dodd (*The Parables of the Kingdom*, 1935) read *ēngiken* in the light of *ephthasen* and made the kingdom already arrived. Most exegetes now keep both: the kingdom is future and also already breaking in.

## The argument

1. **Sanders's list.** In *Jesus and Judaism* (1985), E. P. Sanders began from deeds rather than sayings, with eight facts he judged almost beyond dispute. Paraphrased: Jesus (1) was baptized by John; (2) was a Galilean who preached and healed; (3) called disciples and spoke of there being twelve; (4) kept his work within Israel; (5) was involved in a controversy over the Temple; (6) was crucified outside Jerusalem by the Roman authorities. After his death (7) his followers continued as an identifiable movement, and (8) at least some Jews persecuted at least parts of it (Paul says he did, Galatians 1:13). This is [Sanders's list](../reference.md#sanders-list-of-secure-facts). *In words:* start from what is hardest to deny, and build out.
2. **Why these hold.** Each clears several of the [criteria](../reference.md#criteria-of-authenticity) from [3.2](03-02-sources-and-criteria.md). The crucifixion is attested by all four gospels, by Paul, by Josephus, and by Tacitus, who says "Christus ... suffered the extreme penalty during the reign of Tiberius at the hands of one of our procurators, Pontius Pilatus" (*Annals* 15.44, Church and Brodribb). The Twelve appear in Mark, in John, in the saying behind Matthew 19:28 that the disciples will sit on twelve seats judging the twelve tribes, and in the tradition Paul passes on at 1 Corinthians 15:5. The Greek there reads "the twelve" even after Judas's defection; the Douay's "the eleven" follows the Vulgate's correction. A group that kept its number with a traitor in it was not a later invention. *In words:* early, multiple, and sometimes awkward evidence.
3. **Healer and exorcist.** Sanders's item 2 is stronger than it looks. The opponents in Mark 3:22 do not deny the exorcisms; they explain them: "He hath Beelzebub, and by the prince of devils he casteth out devils." Hostile concession is the best evidence a reputation can have. Josephus calls Jesus "a doer of wonderful works" (*Antiquities* 18.3.3, Whiston). The phrase sits inside the disputed Testimonium, but many scholars who cut its Christian additions keep this phrase as Josephus's own. John P. Meier (*A Marginal Jew*, vol. 2, 1994) concludes that Jesus did deeds that he and his contemporaries took to be miracles. He also holds that whether they *were* miracles is a question history cannot answer.
4. **Table fellowship.** The "Q" saying (Matthew 11:19 / Luke 7:34) preserves an insult: "a man that is a glutton and a wine drinker, a friend of publicans and sinners." No follower would invent that, and its fact is widely accepted: Jesus ate with people the pious avoided. Its *meaning* is disputed. Sanders argued that the scandal was his offering the kingdom to the wicked without first requiring the usual repentance and restitution. Critics, Dunn among them, think that reads the Pharisees too thinly and Jesus's call to repent (Mark 1:15) too lightly. The same list-then-dispute pattern holds for the Temple incident: that it happened is item 5; what it meant (a cleansing, a symbolic destruction, a prophecy of a new Temple) is contested.
5. **The big dispute: an eschatological prophet?** Albert Schweitzer (1906) made Jesus a prophet who expected the imminent, God-wrought end of the present age: [the eschatological prophet reading](../reference.md#the-eschatological-prophet-reading). Sanders, Dale Allison (*Jesus of Nazareth: Millenarian Prophet*, 1998) and Bart Ehrman (*Jesus: Apocalyptic Prophet of the New Millennium*, 1999) defend versions of that view. Allison's strongest argument is the sandwich. Jesus's teacher, John, preached imminent judgment. The movement after Jesus, Paul included, expected the Lord's return soon (1 Thessalonians 4:15–17). The simplest bridge between an eschatological start and an eschatological sequel is an eschatological Jesus. Sayings like Mark 9:1 (some standing here will not die before they see the kingdom come in power) and 14:25 (Jesus will not drink wine again until he drinks it new in the kingdom) add weight.
6. **The rivals.** (a) *Non-eschatological.* John Dominic Crossan (*The Historical Jesus*, 1991), Marcus Borg and the majority of the Jesus Seminar make Jesus a teacher of a kingdom present in how people live now, a sapiential rather than apocalyptic kingdom. They argue that Jesus broke with John, and that the church's end-time expectation came after Easter, not from Jesus. Luke 17:21 is their showpiece: *entos hymōn*, which the Douay renders "within you" following the Vulgate's *intra vos*, and which many translate "among you" or "in your midst". (b) *Restoration of Israel.* N. T. Wright (*Jesus and the Victory of God*, 1996) keeps Jesus a prophet of imminent crisis. But he reads the cosmic language as Jewish metaphor for events inside history: the end of Israel's exile, God's return to Zion, the fall of the Temple, Jesus's vindication. *In words:* all three camps grant the kingdom sayings; they disagree over what "the end" meant and whether the imminent-deadline sayings go back to Jesus.
7. **How Catholic exegetes handle the deadline.** If Jesus set a deadline that passed, a theological question follows. In July 1907 the Holy Office decree *Lamentabili* condemned the proposition that any unprejudiced reader must choose between Jesus erring about an immediate messianic coming and most of his gospel teaching being inauthentic ([*Lamentabili* on the immediate coming](../reference.md#lamentabili-on-the-immediate-coming)). The condemnation refuses the dilemma. It does not settle which historical reconstruction is right. Meier, in the same volume, keeps a future kingdom at the centre of Jesus's teaching but judges the explicit deadline sayings (Mark 9:1, 13:30, Matthew 10:23) more probably early-church formulations. Others read them with Wright, or as prophetic foreshortening.

**Mark the levels.** Sanders's list is a **historians' consensus**: a majority judgment, strong but not a proof, and without magisterial standing either way. The eschatological-prophet reading and all its rivals are **scholarly hypotheses**, freely disputed. That Jesus suffered under Pontius Pilate is also **defined dogma** (rung 1 of [the ladder](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-04-the-ladder-of-doctrinal-authority.md)): it is an article of the creed. But the Church holds it on the apostolic witness, not because Tacitus confirms it. The gospels' [historical character](../reference.md#historical-character-of-the-gospels) is Church teaching: Dei Verbum 19 says, in paraphrase, that the Church firmly and unhesitatingly asserts it. It is taught by an ecumenical council in a dogmatic constitution but newly defines nothing; read its weight with [`fundamental-theology` 4.5](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-05-reading-a-documents-weight.md). It endorses no reconstruction. *Lamentabili* is a Holy Office decree approved by Pius X. It condemned its propositions as a set and assigned no note to each, so read it as a **judgment against a dilemma**, not a definition of Jesus's eschatology. Faith does not rest on the historians' list, and it does not need historians to agree ([church teaching vs hypothesis](../reference.md#church-teaching-vs-hypothesis)).

## The map

| Near-consensus | Live dispute | Beyond the method |
|---|---|---|
| Baptized by John | Why he was baptized, and whether he broke with John | That the Father spoke at the baptism |
| Galilean preacher of the kingdom | Kingdom future, present, or both (*ēngiken* vs *ephthasen*) | That the kingdom is God's reign in Christ |
| Reputation as healer and exorcist | Which particular stories go back to him | That the cures were acts of God |
| Called disciples; spoke of twelve | Whether the Twelve meant a restored Israel | — |
| Ate with "publicans and sinners" | What the scandal was (Sanders vs his critics) | — |
| Temple controversy | Cleansing, symbolic destruction, or prophecy | — |
| Crucified under Pilate by Roman authority | Why, and who pressed for it ([3.4](03-04-the-passion-and-resurrection-narratives.md)) | That his death was a sacrifice for sins |
| Followers continued as a movement | What they experienced after his death ([3.4](03-04-the-passion-and-resurrection-narratives.md)) | That he rose |
| — | Eschatological prophet, sage, or restoration prophet | — |

Read across a row: the left column never settles the right one.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean): "Jesus chose twelve disciples."** Bin 1. The evidence is multiple (Mark, John, the Q saying behind Matthew 19:28, Paul's 1 Corinthians 15:5) and early. It also contains embarrassment: that the betrayer was one of the twelve (Mark 14:10) is not a detail a church would invent. What it does *not* establish is what the number meant. Twelve tribes suggests a restored Israel, and Sanders and Wright both argue so, but that inference is a bin-2 claim riding on a bin-1 fact. Report them separately.

**Example 2 (where it strains): "Jesus predicted the end within a generation."** Here the bins blur. That Jesus preached a coming kingdom is bin 1. That he expected it *soon* is the majority reading, which Allison's sandwich supports, and it is still bin 2: Crossan and Borg deny it, Wright redefines "the end", and Meier questions the deadline sayings. That he was *mistaken* is a further step still. It needs the deadline sayings to be authentic *and* to be read literally. *Lamentabili* bars a Catholic from the flat dilemma, not from the history. Honest summary: a majority for an eschatological Jesus, a real minority against, and an error claim resting on two further disputed premises.

## Watch out

- **"Scholars agree" as a trump card.** The eschatological-prophet reading is probably the majority view, but it is not on Sanders's list, and a majority is not a proof.
- **Hypothesis promoted to teaching, or teaching demoted to hypothesis.** Dei Verbum 19 affirms the gospels' historical character; it does not canonize Sanders, Meier or Wright. Conversely, the crucifixion under Pilate is a creedal article *and* a historians' consensus. It is not "only" a scholarly result that the Church happens to share.
- **Bin 3 is not failed bin 1.** "History cannot show the exorcisms were divine" does not mean "history shows they were not." Meier's line (the reputation is secure, the cause lies beyond the method) is a limit on method, not a verdict.

## One-liner

> Historians agree on the frame (John's baptism, a Galilean healer preaching the kingdom, the Twelve, sinners at table, the Temple, a Roman cross, a movement that did not die) and disagree about what he expected; the creed shares one line of that list, and holds it on other grounds.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢)** *(Exegetical)* Put each claim in a bin: **near-consensus**, **live dispute**, or **beyond the method**. Where a claim is also Church teaching, say so and give its level. One line each.

1. Jesus was baptized by John.
2. Jesus expected God to end the present order within his hearers' lifetimes.
3. Even Jesus's opponents conceded that he performed exorcisms.
4. Jesus cast out demons by the Spirit of God.
5. Jesus was crucified by Roman authority under Pontius Pilate.
6. Jesus's table fellowship scandalized people because he did not require sinners to repent first.
7. Jesus's death was a sacrifice for sins.

**P2 (🟡)** *(Exegetical)* Two positions, paraphrased:

> **Allison:** John the Baptist preached imminent judgment, and the movement after Easter expected the end soon. The best explanation of that continuity is that Jesus himself was an eschatological prophet; the sayings about the kingdom's coming belong to his core message.

> **Crossan:** Jesus began under John but broke with his apocalyptic message, teaching a kingdom present in a way of life. The early church's end-time expectation was a post-Easter development, read back into his sayings.

(a) Name the single premise one side must deny for the other to win. (b) Say what kind of evidence would move a neutral reader on it, with one example text. **Two sentences per part.**

**P3 (🔴)** *(Exegetical)* An **invented** parish-newsletter column:

> "Scholarship has settled the question: every serious historian now agrees Jesus was an apocalyptic prophet who predicted the world would end within a generation, as Mark 9:1 proves. Since Dei Verbum commits the Church to historical criticism, the Church must accept this result, and the old claim that Christ taught no error is finished. Still, historians do confirm his miracles: even his enemies admitted he cast out demons."

Name **three** distinct errors, and for each say what the lesson's evidence or the Church's teaching actually supports. **Two sentences per error.**

<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict:** (1) **Near-consensus** (Sanders's item 1). (2) **Live dispute.** It is the majority reading but is denied by Crossan, Borg and the Jesus Seminar, and redefined by Wright. (3) **Near-consensus**: Mark 3:22 and the Q parallel concede the deeds and dispute their source. (4) **Beyond the method.** The historian can report the reputation but not certify its cause. (5) **Near-consensus** (Sanders's item 6, with Tacitus) **and** Church teaching: **defined dogma**, rung 1, as an article of the creed, held on the apostolic witness. (6) **Live dispute.** The meals are consensus; this explanation is Sanders's thesis, which Dunn and others contest. (7) **Beyond the method**, and Church teaching (its level and texts belong to [`christology` 5.2](../../christology/lessons/05-02-ransom-christus-victor-and-sacrifice.md)). The historian can say the early church so interpreted it (1 Corinthians 15:3).

**Wrong turns:** putting (2) in near-consensus because it is the majority view. Putting (4) or (7) under "live dispute", as if historians were split on it, when their method cannot test it. Marking (5) as consensus only, or dogma only. Putting (6) in near-consensus because the meals are secure; the claim is about their meaning.

**Model answer:** 1 consensus; 2 dispute; 3 consensus; 4 beyond; 5 consensus and defined dogma (the creed); 6 dispute (Sanders's thesis); 7 beyond the method, and Church teaching.

**P2** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** the crux is **continuity with John and the later church, and whether Jesus broke from it**. Allison's inference needs Jesus to stand in line between two eschatological ends. Crossan must deny that premise, holding that Jesus broke with John and that the post-Easter expectation was new. Credit an equivalent formulation: whether the imminent-kingdom sayings are Jesus's own or the church's retrojection.

**Must hit, strict (b):** evidence about the **dating and stratification of the sayings**, meaning whether the future-kingdom sayings appear in the earliest, multiply attested layers (Mark 1:15, 14:25, Luke 11:20) or only later. Or evidence of a **break with John**: sayings contrasting Jesus's practice with John's, such as eating and drinking in Matthew 11:18–19.

**Wrong turns:** naming "whether miracles happen", which neither side's argument turns on. Treating Luke 17:21 as decisive by itself, when *entos hymōn* is ambiguous. Giving a verdict instead of a crux.

**Model answer:** (a) The crux is whether Jesus broke with John's imminent-judgment message. Allison needs continuity from John through Jesus to Paul, and Crossan must deny it, making the church's expectation a post-Easter return to John. (b) What would move a reader is whether the future-kingdom sayings lie in the earliest multiply attested layer or were added later. Mark 14:25 (he will drink wine again only in the kingdom) is a test case: authentic, it favours Allison; a church formulation, it weakens him.

**P3** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict:** any three of these. (1) **Consensus overstated.** The eschatological-prophet reading is a majority view with a serious minority against it (Crossan, Borg, the Jesus Seminar; Wright redefines "the end"), and it is not on Sanders's list. A majority is not a proof. (2) **One text "proves" it.** Mark 9:1's authenticity and sense are disputed; Meier judges the deadline sayings probably early-church formulations. (3) **Teaching misdescribed.** Dei Verbum 19 affirms the gospels' historical character; it does not bind the Church to any scholar's reconstruction, and scholarly results carry no magisterial standing. *Lamentabili* (1907) refused the dilemma the column asserts. (4) **Reputation confused with deed.** The enemies' concession (Mark 3:22) secures a reputation as exorcist; whether the acts were divine lies beyond the historian's method, so historians do not "confirm miracles".

**Wrong turns:** answering that historians are simply wrong about the eschatological reading. The task is to diagnose the column's confusions, not to settle the dispute. Saying the Church "rejects" historical criticism. Treating *Lamentabili* as a definition of Jesus's eschatology.

**Model answer:** (1) "Every serious historian agrees" is false: the apocalyptic-prophet reading is a majority view, contested by Crossan, Borg and Wright, and a majority is not a proof. (2) Mark 9:1 cannot "prove" it, since whether the deadline sayings go back to Jesus is itself disputed (Meier doubts it). (3) Dei Verbum 19 affirms the gospels' historical character, not any historian's reconstruction, and *Lamentabili* condemned exactly the either-Jesus-erred-or-the-gospels-are-inauthentic dilemma. (4) The enemies' concession secures his reputation as an exorcist; whether the deeds were acts of God lies beyond the historian's method.

</details>

## Flashback

**From Lesson [3.1](03-01-the-quests.md) (The quests):** *(Exegetical.)* The stilling of the storm, Mark 4:37–41 (4:37–40 in the Douay numbering), and Psalm 107:28–29 (106:28–29 in the Douay), Douay–Rheims:

> And there arose a great storm of wind, and the waves beat into the ship, so that the ship was filled. ... And rising up, he rebuked the wind, and said to the sea: Peace, be still. And the wind ceased: and there was made a great calm. And he said to them: Why are you fearful? have you not faith yet? And they feared exceedingly: and they said one to another: Who is this (thinkest thou) that both wind and sea obey him?

> And they cried to the Lord in their affliction: and he brought them out of their distresses. And he turned the storm into a breeze: and its waves were still.

Three **invented** readings:

1. "A squall on the lake died down, as squalls do, just as Jesus spoke; the frightened disciples took a coincidence for a command."
2. "The first believers confessed that the Lord who stills the sea in the psalms was present in Jesus, and they told that faith as a story about a boat."
3. "Whether any storm ever stopped is beyond recovery and beside the point. The story confronts the hearer with 'have you not faith yet?' and demands a decision now."

(a) Name the phase or figure from 3.1 whose working assumption produces each reading, and state the assumption. **One line each.** (b) Which reading does the psalm parallel serve, and what does the parallel *not* show by itself? **Two sentences.**

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):** (1) The **rationalist** explanation, a real natural event misunderstood. It assumes that events happened but the miracles did not. This is the view Strauss rejected alongside the supernaturalist one. (2) **Strauss**: myth, the early Church's faith and Old Testament patterns told as narrative. It assumes that the gospel form itself is faith, not report. (3) **Bultmann**: form-critical scepticism plus theological indifference. It assumes that the units are community proclamation, that the history is out of reach, and that faith needs only the kerygma's call to decision.
**Must hit, strict (b):** It serves **reading 2**. The psalm gives the pattern (the Lord stills the storm for those who cry to him), and the disciples' question "who is this that both wind and sea obey him?" points to the psalm's answer. By itself the parallel does **not** show that the story was generated from the psalm: an evangelist telling a remembered event could echo Scripture just as well. An echo fits both the myth reading and the memory reading.
**Wrong turns:** giving (1) to Reimarus, whose charge was deliberate fraud, not honest misunderstanding. Giving (3) to Kähler; his preached Christ is close, but the form-critical scepticism about the history is Bultmann's. Treating the psalm echo as proof of invention.
**Model answer:** (a) 1: the rationalists; the events were real and natural, and the miracle is a misreading of them. 2: Strauss; the story is the Church's faith, shaped on Old Testament patterns, told as history. 3: Bultmann; the story is proclamation, its history is unrecoverable, and faith needs only the call to decide. (b) The psalm parallel serves Strauss, since it supplies the pattern of a Lord who stills the sea. But an echo of Scripture is equally what a writer telling a remembered event might produce, so the parallel alone cannot decide between myth and memory.

</details>
