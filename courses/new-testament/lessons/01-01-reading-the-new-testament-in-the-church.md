# The New Testament · Lesson 1.1: Reading the New Testament in the Church

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 1: The world of the New Testament and the making of the gospels · Builds on: [`fundamental-theology` 3.2 Inerrancy](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/03-02-inerrancy.md), [4.5 Reading a document's weight](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-05-reading-a-documents-weight.md) · Unlocks: [1.4 The Synoptic problem](01-04-the-synoptic-problem.md), [1.5 From oral tradition to written gospel](01-05-from-oral-tradition-to-written-gospel.md)

## Why this matters

Every lesson in this course does two things. It reads the gospels as the Church reads them, as faithful witness to what Jesus did and taught. It also reads them as critics do, asking who copied whom, when each was written, and what each evangelist changed. Confuse the two and either every hypothesis sounds like an attack on the faith, or the faith sounds like one more hypothesis. This lesson draws the line once, from the documents themselves, so the rest of the course can work on both sides of it.

## The idea

Picture a court that rules that four witness statements are truthful. It has not ruled that they agree word for word, that witness B never saw A's statement, or on which day each was signed. Those remain questions of evidence.

The Church's teaching on the gospels has this shape. It affirms **what the gospels are**: faithful, historical witness to Jesus. It maps **how they came to be** in three stages: Jesus himself, the apostles' preaching, the evangelists' writing. And it leaves **the mechanics** of the third stage to scholars. Which gospel came first, whether there was a source called Q, and the date of each gospel are left open. The map is teaching; what goes on inside each stage is evidence.

## Source

Luke 1:1–4, Douay–Rheims (Challoner). Dei Verbum 19 itself cites these verses.

> Forasmuch as many have taken in hand to set forth in order a narration of the things that have been accomplished among us, according as they have delivered them unto us, who from the beginning were eyewitnesses and ministers of the word: it seemed good to me also, having diligently attained to all things from the beginning, to write to thee in order, most excellent Theophilus, that thou mayest know the verity of those words in which thou hast been instructed.

Read it for its three layers. **The things accomplished** (*pragmata*) come first: events. Next, those who were **eyewitnesses and ministers of the word** *delivered* them. The Greek *paredosan*, "handed on", is the technical verb of tradition. Last comes a writer who has followed everything closely (*akribōs*, "carefully") and now writes **in order** (*kathexēs*). "Verity" renders *asphaleia*, which means firmness or certainty rather than bare truth. Theophilus has already been taught (*katēchēthēs*, "catechized"); the book is meant to make him sure of what he was taught. Luke describes from the inside the process the Church later mapped. [2.3](02-03-luke.md) reads the prologue for Luke himself.

## The argument

1. **The frame (reloaded).** Dei Verbum 11 teaches that Scripture has God as author through human authors who were true authors, and that it teaches without error the truth God willed for our salvation ([`fundamental-theology` 3.1](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/03-01-inspiration.md), [3.2](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/03-02-inerrancy.md)). Dei Verbum 12 says that to find what God meant, you must find what the human writer meant, and that requires attention to literary forms. *Divino Afflante Spiritu* (1943) had already made literary forms a method. *In words:* before you ask whether a text errs, find out what kind of claim it makes.

2. **Dei Verbum 19 (1965), paraphrased.** The Church has held firmly and constantly that the four gospels, whose [historical character](../reference.md#historical-character-of-the-gospels) she affirms without hesitation, faithfully hand on what Jesus really did and taught for our salvation until his Ascension. *In words:* the gospels are not myth or edifying fiction; what they report of Jesus is true.

3. **The three stages** (the same paragraph, labelled as in CCC 126). *Stage 1, the life and teaching of Jesus.* *Stage 2, the oral tradition*: after the Ascension the apostles handed on what he said and did, with the fuller understanding they gained from the Easter events and from the Spirit. *Stage 3, the written gospels*: the evangelists selected some things from what had been handed on orally or in writing, reduced others to a synthesis, and explained others "with an eye to the situation of the churches" (CCC 126's rendering). Throughout, they kept the form of preaching, and what they told us about Jesus was true and honest. *In words:* [three stages](../reference.md#the-three-stages-of-gospel-formation), each shaping the material and none of them inventing it.

4. **Where the map came from.** Dei Verbum 19 footnotes [*Sancta Mater Ecclesia*](../reference.md#sancta-mater-ecclesia), the Biblical Commission's instruction on the historical truth of the gospels, which Paul VI approved on 21 April 1964. It set out the three stages the Council took up. It also said two things the Council compressed. First, the truth of the gospels is not compromised when the evangelists report Jesus's words and deeds in different orders, or report his words not literally but in varied forms that keep the sense. Second, exegetes may use the sound elements of form criticism, but must reject the philosophical assumptions often bundled with it. Among those assumptions are denying the supernatural and exaggerating the early community's creativity at the expense of the apostles as witnesses. *In words:* the three stages permit shaping and forbid fabrication.

5. **What "historical character" does not require.** It does not require verbatim reports, as the two forms of the Our Father show (Example 1). The words over the cup show it too. Mark 14:24 reads "This is my blood of the new testament"; Paul's form in 1 Corinthians 11:25, which Luke 22:20 closely resembles, reads "This chalice is the new testament in my blood." Nor does it require one chronology (Example 2). *In words:* history in the ancient sense, faithfully reported, not a transcript.

6. **The weight cases: the early responses.** Between 1905 and 1915 the Commission answered a series of *dubia* (formal questions put for an official yes-or-no answer). The response on John (1907) affirmed apostolic authorship. The one on Matthew (19 June 1911) said the apostle wrote first, in the language of Palestine's Jews. Two responses of 26 June 1912 followed. One affirmed the traditional authorship of Mark and Luke. The one on the Synoptic question let exegetes discuss dependence hypotheses freely, but said they were not free to advocate the two-document hypothesis (Mark plus a sayings collection). Pius X required the same submission to these responses as to other papally approved curial decrees (so Fitzmyer summarizes). In 1955 the Commission's secretary, A. Miller, O.S.B., and its under-secretary, A. Kleinhans, O.F.M., each reviewed the new edition of the *Enchiridion Biblicum*. In parallel wording they distinguished responses touching faith and morals from those on authorship, date, literary criticism and historicity. On the second kind, they said, scholars may pursue their research with full freedom. Fitzmyer calls this a "semiofficial" clarification. J. E. Steinmueller disputed it, holding that the responses had not been tacitly revoked. *In words:* the [early responses](../reference.md#the-early-biblical-commission-responses) were real acts of authority on literary questions, and their current force is the textbook case of reading a document's weight ([`fundamental-theology` 4.5](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-05-reading-a-documents-weight.md)).

7. **The method's documents, recapped** (they belong to [`old-testament` 1.3](../../old-testament/syllabus.md)). *Providentissimus Deus* (1893) defended inerrancy against the critics; *Divino Afflante Spiritu* (1943) opened the door with literary forms. Since 1971 the Commission has been an advisory body of scholars. Its [*The Interpretation of the Bible in the Church*](../reference.md#the-interpretation-of-the-bible-in-the-church) (1993) calls the historical-critical method indispensable for the scientific study of ancient texts. Cardinal Ratzinger's preface says the Commission is not an organ of the teaching office but a commission of scholars that office trusts. *In words:* the 1993 document weighs what its arguments weigh.

**Mark the levels.** [Church teaching vs hypothesis](../reference.md#church-teaching-vs-hypothesis): the **historical character of the gospels** (Dei Verbum 19) is **conciliar teaching in a dogmatic constitution**. It is not a new definition. It is at least authentic magisterium at its heaviest ([rung 3](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-04-the-ladder-of-doctrinal-authority.md)), and it presents itself as what the Church has always held, so there is a case for more. The **three-stage map** is part of the same teaching. ***Sancta Mater Ecclesia*** was a papally approved instruction, and the Council received its core. The **early responses** were disciplinary-doctrinal decisions, owed submission when issued. How far they bind today is a question of weight this course does not settle beyond what the 1955 clarification says. The **1993 document** is not magisterial. **Markan priority, Q, the gospels' dates and authorship questions** are **scholarly hypotheses with no magisterial standing either way**.

## The three stages

| Stage | Dei Verbum 19 / CCC 126 | Luke 1:1–4 | Who studies it in this course |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Jesus | what he really did and taught | "the things that have been accomplished among us" | historical-Jesus research ([3.1](03-01-the-quests.md), [3.2](03-02-sources-and-criteria.md)) |
| 2. Apostolic preaching | handed on with fuller post-Easter understanding | "eyewitnesses and ministers of the word" *delivered* them | form criticism, oral tradition, memory ([1.5](01-05-from-oral-tradition-to-written-gospel.md)) |
| 3. The written gospels | selected, synthesized, explained for the churches | "to write to thee in order" | source and redaction criticism, the Synoptic problem ([1.4](01-04-the-synoptic-problem.md)) |

The left two columns are teaching. The right column is where hypotheses live.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean): the Our Father.** Matthew 6:9 opens "Our Father who art in heaven" and has seven petitions. Luke 11:2 opens with "Father" alone and, in the Douay (following the Vulgate's shorter text), has five petitions. Matthew asks forgiveness of "debts", Luke of "sins". Run the stages. *Stage 1:* Jesus taught his disciples a prayer addressing God as Father, with petitions for the kingdom, bread, forgiveness and protection from trial. Both gospels bear witness to that. *Stages 2–3:* the prayer was prayed in communities before it was written, and each evangelist sets it where it serves his book. Matthew sets it in the Sermon on the Mount, Luke in answer to a disciple's request. Dei Verbum 19 commits you only to the gospels faithfully handing on what Jesus taught. It leaves open which wording is closer to his. Joachim Jeremias influentially argued that Luke keeps the original length and Matthew often the older wording. Whether Jesus himself taught it in two forms on two occasions is also left open. *Sancta Mater Ecclesia* says outright that reporting his words in varied forms, not literally, costs the gospels nothing.

**Example 2 (hard): the Temple incident.** In John (2:13–22), Jesus drives the traders from the Temple at the first Passover of his ministry. In Mark (11:15–17), followed by Matthew and Luke, it happens in the last week and helps set the Passion in motion. Three readings have been defended. *Two incidents*: the traditional harmonization. *One incident, placed early by John for theological reasons*: most critical scholars think the Synoptic placement is the historical one. *One incident, placed late by Mark*: Mark has only one Jerusalem visit, so any Jerusalem event has to go at the end. The teaching fixes none of these; *Sancta Mater Ecclesia*'s rule on order covers the last two. The strain lies elsewhere. Where does "arranging in a different order" end and "narrating an event that did not happen" begin? The documents give the principle, faithful witness and no fabrication, but no line drawn case by case. Drawing it is the exegete's work, by inference to the best explanation ([`philosophical-method` 2.1](../../philosophical-method/lessons/02-01-inference-to-the-best-explanation.md)), and subject finally to the Church's judgment (Dei Verbum 12). [2.4](02-04-john-and-the-synoptics.md) takes up the case.

## Watch out

- **Hypothesis promoted to teaching.** "The Church teaches that Matthew wrote first" treats a 1911 response of disputed present force as doctrine. "Catholic scholarship holds Markan priority" makes a majority view into a Church position. Neither is Church teaching; a majority is evidence, not proof.
- **Teaching demoted to hypothesis.** "Historical character is one scholarly view among others" is also an error. It is conciliar teaching. The hypotheses operate *inside* it.
- **"Historical" misread as "stenographic."** A reader who finds two forms of a saying and concludes that one gospel errs has assumed a standard the Church explicitly disclaimed.
- **Stage 2 misread as invention.** The three stages permit interpretation in the light of Easter. They reject the thesis that the community created the tradition.
- **The 1993 document quoted as magisterium.** Its own preface denies it is a teaching act.

## One-liner

> The Church vouches for *what* the gospels hand on and maps *how* in three stages; who copied whom inside stage three is left to the evidence.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢)** *(Exegetical)* Assign each claim its standing: Church teaching (with its level and the act that carries it), a document of lesser or disputed weight (say what weight), or a scholarly hypothesis. **One or two sentences each.**

1. "The four gospels faithfully hand on what Jesus really did and taught."
2. "Exegetes are not free to advocate the two-document hypothesis." (Biblical Commission response, 26 June 1912)
3. "The historical-critical method is indispensable for the scientific study of ancient texts." (*The Interpretation of the Bible in the Church*, 1993)
4. "Mark was the first gospel written, and Matthew and Luke used it."

**P2 (🟡)** *(Exegetical)* An **invented** study-bible note on Luke 11:2–4:

> "Luke's shorter Our Father raises a question of faith. The Church teaches that the gospels are historical, so Jesus must have taught both forms word for word, on two separate occasions; otherwise one evangelist would be in error. In any case the Biblical Commission settled in 1911 that Matthew wrote first, and its 1993 document made the historical-critical method Catholic doctrine."

Name the three errors. For each, give the correction and the document that supplies it. **Two sentences per error.**

<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict:**
- (1) **Church teaching**: Dei Verbum 19, a dogmatic constitution of Vatican II, restating what the Church says she has always held. It is **not a new definition**. Mark it at least authentic magisterium at its heaviest (rung 3); noting the case for a higher rung is fine.
- (2) **An early Biblical Commission response.** It was papally approved and owed submission when issued (Pius X), but it is not a definition. The 1955 semi-official clarification put such literary questions among those scholars may now pursue with full freedom, and that reading was itself contested (Steinmueller). Do not call it dogma. Do not call it formally revoked either.
- (3) **Not magisterial.** Since 1971 the Commission is an advisory body of scholars, and Ratzinger's preface says it is not an organ of the teaching office. The sentence weighs what its argument weighs.
- (4) **Scholarly hypothesis** (Markan priority), with no magisterial standing either way. It is the majority view among critical scholars, and a majority is not a proof.

**Wrong turns:** calling (1) "defined dogma of 1965": Vatican II defined nothing new. Calling (1) "a hypothesis the Church favours". Treating (2) as either binding doctrine or a dead letter, when its present force is the disputed point. Giving (3) magisterial weight because Cardinal Ratzinger signed the preface. Calling (4) "freely disputed doctrine": it is not a doctrinal question at all.

**Model answer:** (1) Church teaching, Dei Verbum 19. It restates the constant faith in a dogmatic constitution, so it is at least rung 3 at its heaviest, and it is no new definition. (2) An early Commission response, papally approved and owed submission when issued. Its literary judgments fall among those the 1955 clarification said scholars may now treat freely, a reading some contested. (3) A non-magisterial commission of scholars, weighty only as argument. (4) A scholarly hypothesis, the majority view, with no magisterial standing.

**P2** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict:**
1. **"Historical" is misread as verbatim.** *Sancta Mater Ecclesia* (endorsed in substance by Dei Verbum 19) says the gospels' truth is not compromised when Jesus's words are reported not literally but with the same sense. So neither evangelist errs, and two occasions are a *permitted* explanation, not a *required* one.
2. **The weight of the 1911 response is overstated.** The Matthew response is a disciplinary-doctrinal decision, not a definition. The 1955 clarification by the Commission's secretaries said such authorship and dating questions may now be researched with full freedom. "Settled" is wrong.
3. **The 1993 document is not doctrine.** Ratzinger's preface says the post-conciliar Commission is not an organ of the teaching office. Its endorsement of the method carries the weight of scholarship, not of a teaching act.

**Wrong turns:** "correcting" error 1 by denying that Jesus could have taught the prayer twice (that is allowed; it just is not required). Replacing error 2 with "the 1911 response was revoked", which is also more than the evidence shows. Letting the first sentence pass: it makes a stage-3 variation into a "question of faith".

**Model answer:** (1) The note equates historical with word for word. *Sancta Mater Ecclesia*, which Dei Verbum 19 cites, says the evangelists may report Jesus's words not literally but with the same sense, so neither form errs and two occasions are optional. (2) The 1911 response did not "settle" anything as doctrine. The Commission's secretaries said in 1955 that on authorship and date scholars now have full freedom. (3) The 1993 document is a scholars' commission's study. Its own preface says the Commission is not an organ of the teaching office, so it made nothing doctrine.
