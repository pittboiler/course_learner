# The New Testament · Lesson 4.3: Paul at work: Thessalonica, Corinth, Philippi

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 4: Acts and Paul's churches · Builds on: [4.1 Acts as history](04-01-acts-as-history.md), [4.2 Paul's life and letters](04-02-pauls-life-and-letters.md) · Unlocks: [5.1 Galatians: faith, Law, and the Gentiles](05-01-galatians-faith-law-and-the-gentiles.md)

## Why this matters

Paul never wrote a treatise. Every doctrine in his letters arrived as an answer to a problem: someone died before the Lord came back, a church split into fan clubs, the rich ate while the poor went hungry. Read the answers without the problems and you misread both. This lesson reads five passages from three [undisputed letters](../reference.md#undisputed-and-disputed-letters) ([4.2](04-02-pauls-life-and-letters.md)) as pastoral replies. One of them, the Philippians hymn, sits at the centre of the liveliest modern debate about how early Christians came to worship Jesus.

## The idea

A letter is half of a conversation, and you rebuild the other half from what Paul says. Scholars call this [mirror-reading](../reference.md#mirror-reading): the answer mirrors the question. It is indispensable and risky. John Barclay's well-known essay on Galatians warns that not every sentence implies an opponent. The safest mirror-reading uses what Paul *says* he was told ("by them that are of the house of Chloe", 1 Cor 1:11) and what he quotes back ("How do the dead rise again?", 15:35).

So, three steps for each passage. What was the problem? What is Paul's answer? Which word carries it?

## Source

1 Thessalonians 4:13-18, Douay-Rheims. The Douay text used here numbers these verses 4:12-17, because it joins the standard 4:11-12. The references below use the standard numbers.

> And we will not have you ignorant brethren, concerning them that are asleep, that you be not sorrowful, even as others who have no hope. For if we believe that Jesus died and rose again: even so them who have slept through Jesus, will God bring with him. For this we say unto you in the word of the Lord, that we who are alive, who remain unto the coming of the Lord, shall not prevent them who have slept. For the Lord himself shall come down from heaven with commandment and with the voice of an archangel and with the trumpet of God: and the dead who are in Christ shall rise first. Then we who are alive, who are left, shall be taken up together with them in the clouds to meet Christ, into the air: and so shall we be always with the Lord. Wherefore, comfort ye one another with these words.

The Greek words that decide it (the translations are the lesson's own):

- ***hēmeis hoi zōntes hoi perileipomenoi*** (4:15, 4:17): "we, the living, the ones left over."
- ***parousia*** (4:15): "arrival", the ordinary word for a ruler's state visit.
- ***ou mē phthasōmen*** (4:15): "we shall certainly not get ahead of." Douay's "prevent" is the old sense, from Latin *praevenire*, "come before."
- ***eis apantēsin*** (4:17): "to a meeting", the word for a city's delegation going out to greet an arriving dignitary and escort him in. Erik Peterson's much-repeated argument is that this implies a return to earth with the Lord. Others deny the word carries that technical sense here.

## The argument

1. **Thessalonica: the grief.** Most scholars date 1 Thessalonians to about 50-51, from Corinth, which makes it probably the oldest Christian writing we have. Mirror-read 4:13: believers have died, and the church fears they will miss the Lord's arrival. Paul's answer is an order of events. The dead rise *first*, the living join them, and nobody gets a head start (*phthasōmen*). Its ground is the creed of 4:14: Jesus died and rose, so God will bring the dead with him. *In words:* Jesus's resurrection is the pattern, and the Christian dead are inside it.

2. **Corinth: the fan clubs (1 Cor 1:10-31).** "I am of Paul; and I am of Apollo; and I of Cephas; and I of Christ" (1:12): a status contest, with teachers as badges. Paul does not referee. He changes the scale: *ho logos ho tou staurou*, "the message of the cross" (1:18), folly to one audience and scandal to another. The social data follows in 1:26, "not many wise ... not many mighty, not many noble", which Gerd Theissen's social reading of Corinth builds on. *In words:* a church competing for status is answered with a crucified Lord.

3. **Corinth: the Supper (1 Cor 11:17-34).** At the common meal "one indeed is hungry and another is drunk" (11:21). Paul answers by reciting a tradition: "I have received of the Lord that which also I delivered unto you" (11:23). *Parelabon ... paredōka*, "I received ... I handed on", is the vocabulary of [handing on tradition](../reference.md#handing-on-tradition); the same pair introduces the creed of 15:3. Paul "handed on" (*paredōka*) the account of the night Jesus "was being handed over" (*paredideto*). The words of institution stand closer to Luke 22 than to Mark 14, which suggests a liturgical formula already fixed before Paul wrote in the mid-50s. *In words:* Paul corrects the meal by quoting the meal. What the Supper *is* belongs to [`sacraments-and-liturgy`](../../sacraments-and-liturgy/syllabus.md).

4. **Corinth: the body (1 Cor 15:35-49).** Some say "there is no resurrection of the dead" (15:12) and ask "with what manner of body shall they come?" (15:35). Paul answers with a seed: what is sown is not the plant that comes up. Then: "It is sown a natural body: it shall rise a spiritual body" (15:44). The Greek is *sōma psychikon* / *sōma pneumatikon*, "a soul-type body" and "a spirit-type body"; the first echoes Adam made "a living soul" (*psychēn zōsan*, 15:45, quoting Genesis 2:7). The [spiritual body](../reference.md#spiritual-body) is disputed. N. T. Wright argues that *-ikos* adjectives name what *animates* a thing, not its material: a body driven by the Spirit. Dale Martin and Troels Engberg-Pedersen argue from ancient physics that *pneuma* was a fine material stuff: a body made of spirit-matter. *In words:* all agree Paul says "body"; they dispute what "spiritual" says about it.

5. **Philippi: the hymn (Phil 2:6-11).** Writing from prison (1:13), Paul urges humility on a church with rivalries (2:3-5) and cites Christ as the example. Since Ernst Lohmeyer's study of 1928, many scholars take [the Philippians hymn](../reference.md#the-philippians-hymn) as pre-Pauline, with "even the death of the cross" (2:8) as Paul's addition, since it breaks the rhythm. Gordon Fee answered that it is elevated Pauline prose, not a hymn. The structure is plain either way: in 2:6-8 Christ is the subject and empties himself; in 2:9-11 God is the subject and exalts him. The doctrine of the emptying belongs to [`christology` 3.3](../../christology/lessons/03-03-kenosis.md). Of the disputed *harpagmos* (2:6), name only the three options: a prize not yet held, to be seized; a possession clung to; or, after Roy Hoover's idiom study, something to *exploit* for one's own advantage.

6. **The Isaiah echo and the debate.** In Greek Isaiah 45:23, God says that to him "every knee shall bend" (*kampsei pan gony*); Paul quotes the line of God in Romans 14:11 with "every tongue shall confess" (*exomologēsetai*; the Hebrew, and the Greek text in Brenton's edition, have "swear"). The verse before is God's "I am God, and there is no other" (45:22). In Phil 2:10-11 the knees bend "in the name of Jesus" and the tongues confess *Kyrios Iēsous Christos*. This is where the [early high Christology](../reference.md#early-high-christology) debate turns. Paraphrased:
   - **Larry Hurtado** (*Lord Jesus Christ*): within two decades, Jewish believers gave Jesus a pattern of devotion without parallel for any other figure: hymns, invocation (*Marana tha*, 1 Cor 16:22), baptism in his name, his Supper, the confession "Lord." A mutation inside Jewish monotheism, not a departure from it.
   - **Richard Bauckham** (*Jesus and the God of Israel*): Jewish monotheism defined God by his unique identity as sole Creator and Ruler; Phil 2 puts Jesus *inside* that identity with Isaiah's most monotheistic text. The earliest Christology was already the highest.
   - **James Dunn**: the hymn is an Adam Christology ("form of God" echoing "image of God") that need not imply pre-existence (*Christology in the Making*); worship in the full sense went to God *through* Jesus (*Did the First Christians Worship Jesus?*), and the hymn ends "in the glory of God the Father."
   - **Bart Ehrman** (*How Jesus Became God*): a pre-existent but lesser heavenly being, exalted to a higher status than before.
   *In words:* nobody disputes that the text is early; the dispute is what it shows.

**Mark the levels.** That the dead will rise bodily is **defined dogma**, rung 1 of [the ladder](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-04-the-ladder-of-doctrinal-authority.md), confessed in the creeds (owner: [`creation-grace-last-things`](../../creation-grace-last-things/syllabus.md)); what a *sōma pneumatikon* is made of is **freely disputed**. Christ's divinity is defined dogma (owner: [`christology`](../../christology/lessons/01-02-true-god-and-true-man.md)); *when* Christians came to worship Jesus, and whether Phil 2:6-11 is pre-Pauline, are **scholarly hypotheses with no magisterial standing either way**. An early date does not prove the dogma, and a late one would not refute it. Eucharistic doctrine is defined and ceded. That Paul wrote these letters is a near-consensus of scholars, not Church teaching. The Biblical Commission's 1915 response on the parousia (Example 2) is a Roman commission's reply, not a definition; weigh it with [`fundamental-theology` 4.5](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-05-reading-a-documents-weight.md). See [Church teaching vs hypothesis](../reference.md#church-teaching-vs-hypothesis).

## The case

| Church | Problem (mirror-read) | Paul's answer | The word that carries it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Thessalonica | the dead will miss the Lord's arrival | the dead rise first; nobody is ahead | *phthasōmen*, *apantēsis* |
| Corinth (1) | status contest among teachers | the cross reverses status | *logos tou staurou* |
| Corinth (2) | the rich eat, the poor go hungry | recite the tradition of the Supper | *parelabon / paredōka* |
| Corinth (3) | "no resurrection of the dead"; "what body?" | seed and plant; animated by the Spirit | *sōma pneumatikon* |
| Philippi | rivalry, self-seeking | Christ emptied, God exalted | *Kyrios*, Isaiah 45:23 |

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean): what did the Corinthian deniers deny?** Mirror-read 15:12 with 15:35. They accept that Christ is preached as risen (15:12 opens with it; 15:11, "so you have believed"). What they deny is the resurrection of the dead in general, and 15:35 shows what bothered them: what body could a corpse have? The best-supported reconstruction is Greek distaste for a revived corpse. So Paul must do two things: tie Christ's resurrection to everyone's ("the firstfruits", 15:20), and redefine the body as neither a revived corpse nor a bodiless soul.

**Example 2 (hard): "we who are alive."** Did Paul expect to be alive at the parousia? Most critical scholars say yes, because he puts himself in the "we." The best case against a firm expectation is the next paragraph: the day comes "as a thief in the night" (5:2), so "we who are alive" may name a category, *whichever of us are still living*. Two things strain the question. First, a Latin rendering shifts the evidence: the Greek of 1 Cor 15:51 says "we shall not all fall asleep, but we shall all be changed", still placing Paul among the living, while the Douay, following the Vulgate, reads "We shall all indeed rise again: but we shall not all be changed." Second, [the Biblical Commission](../reference.md#the-early-biblical-commission-responses) in 1915 denied that the apostles, while teaching no error, voiced human views in which error could hide, and denied that one may reject the traditional reading of 1 Thess 4:15-17 as not making Paul count himself among the survivors. Its first answer touches [inerrancy](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/03-02-inerrancy.md), which is Church teaching; its third rules on how to read a verse. The commission's secretaries clarified in 1955 that responses on literary and historical questions left scholars free, while those touching faith and morals stood, but that does not sort each answer for you. State the weight question; this course leaves it open.

## Watch out

- **"The Church teaches Phil 2:6-11 is an early hymn, so Christ's divinity is historically proven."** Two level errors: the hymn's origin is a hypothesis, and the dogma does not rest on a dating.
- **"Spiritual body" means no body.** Paul says *sōma*; both scholarly readings keep a body.
- **Mirror-reading everything.** Not every exhortation implies a faction. Use what Paul says he was told.
- **"Paul was simply wrong about the end, as the Church admits."** No Church document says so. "Paul expected to survive" is a scholarly reading; "Paul taught an error" collides with inerrancy as taught.

## One-liner

> Every doctrine in Paul arrived as an answer: rebuild the question first, then find the one Greek word that carries the answer.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢)** *(Exegetical)* 1 Corinthians 15:12-18 (Douay-Rheims):

> Now if Christ be preached, that he arose again from the dead, how do some among you say that there is no resurrection of the dead? But if there be no resurrection of the dead, then Christ is not risen again. And if Christ be not risen again, then is our preaching vain: and your faith is also vain. Yea, and we are found false witnesses of God: because we have given testimony against God, that he hath raised up Christ, whom he hath not raised up, if the dead rise not again. For if the dead rise not again, neither is Christ risen again. And if Christ be not risen again, your faith is vain: for you are yet in your sins. Then they also that are fallen asleep in Christ are perished.

(a) Which proposition does Paul assume his opponents already *accept*, and which do they *deny*? Cite the words. (b) Set out the argument as numbered premises and a conclusion, and name its form. (c) Which verse shows that the problem at Corinth is not the same as the one at Thessalonica? **Two sentences per part.**

**P2 (🟡)** *(Exegetical)* Two positions, paraphrased:

- *Hurtado:* From the earliest years, believers gave Jesus hymns, invocation ("Our Lord, come"), baptism in his name, a meal in his name, and the confession "Jesus is Lord." Judaism gave that pattern of devotion to God alone. It amounts to worship of Jesus within a monotheism that had been reshaped.
- *Dunn:* Devotion to Jesus is real, but in the New Testament worship in the full sense is normally directed to God and offered *through* Jesus. Jesus is more the means of worship than its object, so "they worshipped Jesus" overstates the evidence.

(a) Name the crux: the single premise one side must deny. (b) Say in one sentence how each would read Phil 2:9-11, using a phrase from the text. (c) Name one kind of evidence that would move the question. **150 words or fewer in total.**

**P3 (🔴)** *(Exegetical)* An **invented** study-bible note on 1 Thessalonians 4:

> "Here Paul teaches the rapture: believers will be snatched up to heaven, leaving the earth behind, before the end. Paul expected this within his own lifetime ('we who are alive'), and since it did not happen the Church now admits that Paul's teaching on the end was mistaken. Scholars agree this is Paul's earliest letter, which the Church has defined."

Find **three** errors, and correct each by the text or by the correct level. **One or two sentences each.**

<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** They **accept** that Christ is preached as risen: "if Christ be preached, that he arose again" (v. 12, backed by v. 11, "so you have believed"). They **deny** "resurrection of the dead" as a general event: "some among you say that there is no resurrection of the dead."

**Must hit, strict (b):** P1. If there is no resurrection of the dead, Christ is not risen (vv. 13, 16). P2. Christ is risen (the premise they accept, and 15:20). ∴ C. There is a resurrection of the dead. The form is **modus tollens**. Paul spells out what follows if Christ is not risen (vain preaching, false witnesses, still in sins, the dead perished) to make P1's cost vivid. Credit a reconstruction that also includes vv. 14-18 as a *reductio*: denying the general resurrection empties the faith they hold.

**Must hit, strict (c):** v. 12 (or v. 18). At Thessalonica believers *hoped* for the resurrection and feared that the dead would miss out. At Corinth some *denied* the resurrection of the dead. Verse 18, "they also that are fallen asleep in Christ are perished", is the Thessalonian fear, which Paul now uses as a consequence of the Corinthian denial.

**Wrong turns:** saying the Corinthians denied Christ's resurrection, which v. 12's opening excludes. Reading the argument as affirming the consequent. Turning this into a historical case for the resurrection, which is ceded to [`apologetics-foundations`](../../apologetics-foundations/syllabus.md).

**Model answer:** (a) They accept that Christ rose ("if Christ be preached, that he arose again") and deny "resurrection of the dead" in general. (b) P1 if the dead do not rise, Christ is not risen; P2 Christ is risen; ∴ the dead rise. This is modus tollens, with vv. 14-18 listing what denying it would cost. (c) Verse 12: the Corinthians deny the general resurrection, while the Thessalonians believed it and only feared the dead would be left out.

**P2** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** The crux is **what counts as worship**. Hurtado must hold that the devotional pattern (hymn, invocation, confession, rites in his name) is itself worship of the kind Judaism reserved for God. Dunn must deny that devotion *through* or *about* Jesus amounts to worship *of* him in that sense. Credit the related form: whether any other Jewish intermediary figure received the same pattern.

**Must hit, strict (b):** Hurtado: every knee bends "in the name of Jesus" and every tongue confesses him *Kyrios*, which is the homage Isaiah 45:23 gives to God, now given to Jesus. Dunn: God does the exalting and the giving of the name, and the confession ends "in the glory of God the Father", so the homage ends in the Father.

**Must hit, strict (c):** Any one of these: evidence of Second Temple Jews giving comparable cultic devotion (prayer, hymns, invocation) to an angel or exalted patriarch, which would weaken Hurtado's claim that it had no parallel; or clear early texts of prayer or sacrifice-language addressed *to* Jesus rather than through him, which would weaken Dunn.

**Wrong turns:** making the crux the *date*, when both agree the evidence is early. Treating the dispute as whether Jesus is divine; that is dogma and not the historians' question.

**Model answer:** The crux is whether the devotional pattern counts as worship proper: Hurtado says it does, and Dunn denies it, reserving full worship for God reached through Jesus. Hurtado reads "in the name of Jesus every knee should bow" as Isaiah's homage to God given to Jesus. Dunn stresses that God exalts and gives the name, and that all ends "in the glory of God the Father." A Second Temple parallel of hymns and invocation directed to an angel would weaken Hurtado. Clear early prayer addressed to Jesus would weaken Dunn.

**P3** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict:** any three of these:
1. **"Leaving the earth behind"**: the text says they are taken up "to meet Christ" (*eis apantēsin*), and says nothing of staying in heaven. On one reading the "meeting" is the civic delegation that escorts the arriving ruler *back*. At the least the text does not settle the direction.
2. **"The Church now admits Paul was mistaken"**: no Church document says so. The 1915 Biblical Commission response rejected the claim that the apostles, on the parousia, voiced human views in which error could lie. Whether Paul personally expected to survive is a **scholarly** question.
3. **"Paul expected this within his lifetime"** is stated as fact. It is the **majority scholarly reading**, not certain: 5:2's "thief in the night" supports a categorial "we."
4. **"Earliest letter ... which the Church has defined"**: the date and priority of 1 Thessalonians are a **scholarly hypothesis** (a broad majority view) with no magisterial standing. The Church defines the canon, not the dates or sequence of books.

**Wrong turns:** fixing the rapture error by asserting that *apantēsis* certainly means a return to earth; that is Peterson's reading, not a settled fact. Correcting (2) by saying the Church *teaches* that Paul expected no imminent end; the 1915 response is a commission's reply, and its weight is the question.

**Model answer:** (1) Paul says "to meet Christ," not "leaving the earth": *apantēsis* can mean going out to escort an arriving ruler in, so the direction is at least open. (2) No Church document says Paul's teaching erred; the 1915 Biblical Commission reply rejected that claim, and whether he personally expected to live is a scholarly question. (3) That 1 Thessalonians is the earliest letter is a majority scholarly dating, not something the Church has defined.

## Flashback

**From Lesson [4.1](04-01-acts-as-history.md) (Acts as history):** *(Exegetical.)* Find the crux. Paul's farewell to the elders of Ephesus at Miletus, Acts 20:33–35 (Douay–Rheims):

> I have not coveted any man's silver, gold or apparel, as You yourselves know. For such things as were needful for me and them that are with me, these hands have furnished. I have shewed you all things, how that so labouring you ought to support the weak and to remember the word of the Lord Jesus, how he said: It is a more blessed thing to give, rather than to receive.

Compare Paul's own letters: "we labour, working with our own hands" (1 Cor 4:12); "working night and day, lest we should be chargeable to any of you" (1 Thess 2:9). The speech falls inside the second "we" section of Acts (20:5–21:18). Two readings, paraphrased:

- **Reader D (Dibelius's line):** like the other speeches in Acts, this is Luke's composition in the Thucydidean convention: what a departing apostle's farewell demanded, in Luke's own style.
- **Reader B (the line of Bruce and Hemer):** the wording is Luke's, but the substance preserves what Paul actually said; its themes match letters that Acts never mentions, and the narrator's "we" puts him on the scene.

(a) Name the single premise on which they divide. (b) Say what each makes of the parallel with 1 Cor 4:12 and 1 Thess 2:9. (c) Name one fact that, if established, would move the question. **150 words or fewer in all.**

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):** the crux is **where the speech's Pauline content came from**: from memory of Paul's words (the narrator's own, or a source's), or from Luke's own composition out of what he knew of Paul. Both agree the wording is Luke's and that ancient speeches kept to the general sense at best, so the dispute is not "transcript or not". Credit the sharper form: whether Luke knew Paul's letters.
**Must hit, strict (b):** B takes the parallel as evidence of **independent access to Paul**. If Luke did not use the letters, the match on self-support is best explained by memory of Paul himself. D takes it as **what the convention predicts**. An author composing a fitting farewell for Paul would give him Paul's known habits, which were also public knowledge in the churches, so the match proves no speech was remembered.
**Must hit, strict (c):** any one. Proof that Luke used Paul's letters would weaken B's inference. Evidence that the self-support theme is a stock feature of ancient farewell speeches would help D. Establishing that the "we" narrator was at Miletus would help B.
**Wrong turns:** reading D as calling the speech fiction or false; the Thucydidean convention adheres to the general sense. Treating B's case as proof of a verbatim record. Settling it by inspiration or inerrancy: Dei Verbum 12 sends the reader to ancient literary forms, and both readings are free.
**Model answer:** They divide on the source of the speech's Pauline content. B says it comes from memory of Paul, at first or second hand; D says Luke composed what the occasion called for from what he knew of Paul. For B, the match with 1 Corinthians and 1 Thessalonians, letters Acts never cites, shows access to Paul independent of them. For D, it is exactly what a well-informed composer produces, since Paul's working for his keep was widely known. Proof that Luke read Paul's letters would undercut B; evidence that the narrator was present at Miletus would support B.

</details>
