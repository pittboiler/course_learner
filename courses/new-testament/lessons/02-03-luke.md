# The New Testament · Lesson 2.3: Luke

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 2: The four gospels · Builds on: [1.4 The Synoptic problem](01-04-the-synoptic-problem.md), [2.1 Mark](02-01-mark.md), [2.2 Matthew](02-02-matthew.md) · Unlocks: [2.4 John and the Synoptics](02-04-john-and-the-synoptics.md), [4.1 Acts as history](04-01-acts-as-history.md)

## Why this matters

Luke is the only evangelist who tells you how he worked, and the only one who wrote a sequel. His prologue is the nearest thing the New Testament has to a methods section: it admits earlier written accounts, distinguishes the author from the eyewitnesses, and promises order and accuracy. Dei Verbum 19 cites it when it teaches the gospels' historical character. That same careful author also dates Jesus's birth by a census that collides with Josephus, the hardest dating problem in the gospels. You need both facts together, and the skill of saying exactly what each one does and does not show.

## The idea

Read Luke as the first volume of a two-volume history. Volume one runs from the Temple in Jerusalem (Zechariah at the altar, 1:5-23) to the Temple again (the disciples praising God, 24:53). Volume two, Acts, runs from Jerusalem to Rome. The hinge is the Spirit, who overshadows Mary, descends on Jesus while he prays, and falls on the Church at Pentecost.

Two threads run through volume one. **Reversal:** God lifts the lowly and sends the rich away empty. Luke announces it before Jesus is born and repeats it in the Sermon on the Plain and in the parable of Lazarus. **Reach:** the story widens from a priest in the Temple to shepherds, Samaritans, tax collectors, prodigals and, in Acts, the nations. Jesus's first sermon at Nazareth (4:18-19, reading Isaiah 61) states the programme: the Spirit anoints him to bring good news to the poor and to proclaim "the acceptable year of the Lord."

## Source

Luke 1:1-4 (Douay-Rheims, Challoner):

> Forasmuch as many have taken in hand to set forth in order a narration of the things that have been accomplished among us, according as they have delivered them unto us, who from the beginning were eyewitnesses and ministers of the word: it seemed good to me also, having diligently attained to all things from the beginning, to write to thee in order, most excellent Theophilus, that thou mayest know the verity of those words in which thou hast been instructed.

The Greek words that carry the weight, in this lesson's translation: *diēgēsis*, "an orderly account" (v. 1); *autoptai*, "eyewitnesses", and *hypēretai tou logou*, "servants of the word" (v. 2); *parēkolouthēkoti anōthen*, "having followed everything from the start" (v. 3); *akribōs*, "carefully, accurately"; *kathexēs*, "in sequence"; *asphaleia*, "firmness, security" (v. 4). The Douay's "verity" renders *asphaleia*: Luke's goal is that Theophilus's instruction stand on solid ground.

## The argument

1. **Luke is not an eyewitness and says so.** The "many" wrote before him. The eyewitnesses and servants of the word handed the tradition down "to us", and the "us" includes Luke. He places himself at the receiving end of a chain. *In words:* the prologue describes Dei Verbum 19's third stage from the inside: an author working from tradition and earlier writings. On [Markan priority](../reference.md#markan-priority), Mark is one of the "many". That is a hypothesis about which predecessors he meant, and the prologue names none.

2. **What he promises is care and order, not transcript.** *Akribōs* claims careful investigation. *Kathexēs* claims a sequence, but Luke rearranges Mark freely: he moves the rejection at Nazareth to the start of the ministry (4:16-30) as a programmatic scene. So "in order" means an ordered narrative, not strict chronology. *In words:* judge Luke by the standard he set himself, which is a well-researched, well-arranged account.

3. **What kind of preface is it?** The majority reads it as a historian's preface. Loveday Alexander (*The Preface to Luke's Gospel*, 1993) argued its closest analogues are the prefaces of technical and scientific treatises. On her reading Luke presents himself as a transmitter of a tradition more than as a Thucydides. Both readings agree he claims research. They differ on how much his genre promises about critical sifting of sources.

4. **[Luke-Acts](../reference.md#luke-acts) is one work.** Acts opens by addressing Theophilus again and summing up its "former treatise" (Acts 1:1). Henry Cadbury's *The Making of Luke-Acts* (1927) made the hyphenated name standard. That one author wrote both is close to unanimous. Whether they form one narrative and one genre was questioned by Mikeal Parsons and Richard Pervo (*Rethinking the Unity of Luke and Acts*, 1993). Hans Conzelmann (*Die Mitte der Zeit*, 1954) read the whole as salvation history in three epochs: Israel up to John the Baptist, Jesus as "the middle of time", and the Church. Many now think his scheme is too neat. The tradition names the author Luke, Paul's companion; Irenaeus says so (*Against Heresies* III.1.1). Brown and most critical scholars date the gospel around 80-90. Authorship and date are argued in [1.5](01-05-from-oral-tradition-to-written-gospel.md) and [4.1](04-01-acts-as-history.md).

5. **[Reversal in Luke](../reference.md#reversal-in-luke).** Mary's canticle: "He hath put down the mighty from their seat and hath exalted the humble. He hath filled the hungry with good things: and the rich he hath sent empty away" (1:52-53). The Sermon on the Plain pairs four blessings with four woes (6:20-26): *woe to you that are rich, that are filled, that now laugh, when all speak well of you.* In the parable only Luke tells, the rich man and Lazarus change places after death, and Abraham explains it by what each received in life (16:25). *In words:* in Luke, the kingdom inverts the social order, and the inversion is announced before it is enacted.

6. **The Spirit and prayer.** Only Luke has Jesus praying at his baptism (3:21), all night before choosing the Twelve (6:12), and at the transfiguration (9:28-29). One small change carries a lot: where Matthew's Father gives "good things" to those who ask, Luke's gives the Spirit (Example 1). *In words:* on either Synoptic hypothesis, a Lukan hand has made the Spirit the Father's gift par excellence, as Acts 2 will show.

7. **The L parables.** The Good Samaritan (10:29-37) and the Prodigal Son (15:11-32) appear in no other gospel. Scholars call material unique to Luke "L". That is a label for a body of material, not proof of a single written source. Both parables turn on the outsider or the lost being received, which is Luke's reach made into story.

**Mark the levels.** That the gospels, Luke included, faithfully hand on what Jesus really did and taught is [the historical character of the gospels](../reference.md#historical-character-of-the-gospels). Dei Verbum 19 teaches it in a dogmatic constitution as what the Church has always held. It is conciliar teaching to be held, not a new definition of 1965. That Scripture teaches without error is doctrine at the highest weight ([`fundamental-theology` 3.2](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/03-02-inerrancy.md)); *how far* that guarantee extends to incidental historical detail is **freely disputed**. Everything else in this lesson is a **scholarly hypothesis** with no magisterial standing either way: Markan priority, L as a source, Alexander's reading of the preface, Conzelmann's epochs, the date 80-90, and each solution to the census problem below ([church teaching vs hypothesis](../reference.md#church-teaching-vs-hypothesis)).

## The two infancy narratives

Luke's and Matthew's birth stories share no scene. They agree on a core, and each explains the Bethlehem-to-Nazareth problem differently. Brown's *The Birth of the Messiah* (1977; updated 1993) builds much of its analysis on this pattern. The Marian dogmas the texts later fed belong to [`ecclesiology-and-mariology`](../../ecclesiology-and-mariology/syllabus.md).

| | Matthew 1-2 | Luke 1-2 |
|---|---|---|
| Point of view | Joseph: dreams, decisions | Mary: annunciation, canticle, "kept all these words" |
| Shared core | Mary betrothed to Joseph, of David's house; conception by the Holy Spirit, not by Joseph; angel gives the name Jesus; born at Bethlehem in Herod's days; raised at Nazareth | same |
| Home before the birth | Bethlehem (implied) | Nazareth |
| Why Bethlehem, then Nazareth | born at home; flight to Egypt; avoids Archelaus, settles in Nazareth (2:22-23) | the census brings them to Bethlehem; they return home after the presentation |
| Visitors | magi from the east, a star | shepherds, angels |
| Scripture | five fulfilment quotations | canticles in scriptural idiom (Magnificat, Benedictus, Nunc dimittis) |
| Temple | absent | frames the whole: Zechariah, presentation, the boy Jesus at twelve |

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean): Luke 11:13 against Matthew 7:11.** The frame is identical: "If you then being evil, know how to give good gifts to your children". Only the last clause differs: Matthew's "good things", Luke's "the good Spirit". Direction: "good things" matches the "good gifts" of the frame, so it is the natural original. "Spirit" breaks the parallel and matches a theme Luke carries into Acts. On the [Two-Source hypothesis](../reference.md#two-source-hypothesis), Matthew preserves Q and Luke edits. On Farrer-Goodacre, Luke edits Matthew directly. Either way the change is Luke's. The method works cleanly because a redactional theme and a broken parallel point the same way.

**Example 2 (hard): [the census of Quirinius](../reference.md#the-census-of-quirinius).** Luke 2:1-2: a decree from Augustus that "the whole world should be enrolled", and "this enrolling was first made by Cyrinus, the governor of Syria." Luke 1:5 sets the story "in the days of Herod"; so does Matthew 2:1. Josephus (*Antiquities* 17.13.5, 18.1.1) puts Quirinius's census after Archelaus was deposed, when Judea was annexed to the province of Syria. He dates the end of the taxings to "the thirty-seventh year of Cæsar's victory over Antony at Actium" (18.2.1), which is AD 6-7. Herod died in 4 BC on the usual reckoning (a minority argue 1 BC). That leaves roughly a decade between the two dates. Luke knows the later census too: Gamaliel's speech mentions Judas of Galilee "in the days of the enrolling" (Acts 5:37), Josephus's Judas who resisted Quirinius. Three kinds of solution:

- **Read *prōtē* as "before".** The Greek is *hautē apographē prōtē egeneto hēgemoneuontos tēs Syrias Kyrēniou*. Some argue *prōtē* with a genitive can mean "prior to", as *prōtos mou* means "before me" in John 1:15. On that reading the census happened *before* Quirinius governed. Some conservative commentators defend it, and N. T. Wright has floated it. *Cost:* it is an unusual construction here, and it leaves unexplained why Luke would date an event by a governor who had not yet arrived.
- **An earlier census or an earlier Quirinius.** Tertullian (*Against Marcion* 4.19) says a census had been taken in Judea under Sentius Saturninus, legate of Syria late in Herod's reign. Others suggest an earlier Quirinius command, or an oath or registration under Herod. *Cost:* no source attests a Roman census in Herod's client kingdom. Tertullian's governor is not Luke's.
- **Luke has dated it wrongly.** Brown (*Birth of the Messiah*) and Fitzmyer (Anchor Bible *Luke*) judged no harmonization convincing. They held that Luke attached the famous census of AD 6 to the birth, which the tradition placed under Herod. This is the majority among critical scholars. A majority is not a proof.

Where it strains: this is a dispute about a detail's history, not about the gospel's historical character. Dei Verbum 19 commits you to Luke faithfully handing on what Jesus did and taught. It does not decide which census stands behind 2:2. Whether an incidental dating error would fall under inerrancy's guarantee is exactly the freely disputed scope question in [`fundamental-theology` 3.2](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/03-02-inerrancy.md). The restrictive reading can grant an error in a detail with no salvific bearing. The non-restrictive reading keeps the census as an open problem.

## Watch out

- **Hypothesis promoted to teaching:** "the Church teaches Luke used Mark", or "the Church teaches the census happened as Luke describes." Neither is taught. The first is a scholarly hypothesis. The second makes a harmonization into doctrine, when the Church has not settled any of the three solutions.
- **Teaching demoted to hypothesis:** "the census problem shows the gospels' historical character is just a scholarly opinion." Dei Verbum 19 is conciliar teaching, and one disputed date does not touch it.
- ***Kathexēs* is not a promise of chronology.** Luke visibly rearranges Mark. Reading "in order" as "in strict time order" manufactures contradictions he never claimed to avoid.
- **"L" is not a document.** It names material only Luke has, whatever its origin.

## One-liner

> Luke tells you he is a careful second-hand historian writing a two-volume story of the Spirit reaching the poor and the nations; read him by his own standard, and keep the census a dispute.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢)** *(Exegetical)* Read these three texts.

> Luke 1:5 (DR): "There was in the days of Herod, the king of Judea, a certain priest ..."
>
> Luke 2:1-4 (DR): "... there went out a decree from Caesar Augustus that the whole world should be enrolled. This enrolling was first made by Cyrinus, the governor of Syria. And all went to be enrolled, every one into his own city. And Joseph also went up from Galilee, out of the city of Nazareth, into Judea, to the city of David ..."
>
> Josephus, *Antiquities* 18.1.1 and 18.2.1 (Whiston), excerpted: "Cyrenius ... came at this time into Syria ... being sent by Cæsar ... to take an account of their substance ... Cyrenius came himself into Judea, which was now added to the province of Syria ... when the taxings were come to a conclusion, which were made in the thirty-seventh year of Cæsar's victory over Antony at Actium ... Herod and Philip had each of them received their own tetrarchy."

Luke makes four claims about the census: (i) Augustus ordered it for "the whole world"; (ii) Quirinius governed Syria when it was made; (iii) each person registered in his ancestral city; (iv) it applied to Joseph, a resident of Galilee. Mark each claim **consistent with**, **in tension with**, or **not addressed by** the Josephus excerpt, and give the words that decide it. **One line per claim.**

**P2 (🟡)** *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b))* The Beatitudes in parallel (DR). Rows are aligned by content, not by order (Luke has hunger before weeping). The Douay's order of Matthew 5:4-5 follows the Vulgate; the Greek has "mourn" before "meek".

| Luke 6:20-23 | Matthew 5:3-12 |
|---|---|
| Blessed are ye poor: for yours is the kingdom of God. | Blessed are the poor in spirit: for theirs is the kingdom of heaven. |
| — | Blessed are the meek: for they shall possess the land. |
| Blessed are ye that weep now: for you shall laugh. | Blessed are they that mourn: for they shall be comforted. |
| Blessed are ye that hunger now: for you shall be filled. | Blessed are they that hunger and thirst after justice: for they shall have their fill. |
| — | merciful · clean of heart · peacemakers · persecuted for justice' sake |
| Blessed shall you be when men shall hate you, and when they shall separate you and shall reproach you and cast out your name as evil, for the Son of man's sake. | Blessed are ye when they shall revile you, and persecute you, and speak all that is evil against you, untruly, for my sake. |
| (6:24-26: four woes follow) | — |

(a) List **five** differences in form or content. (b) Which version is closer to the wording of the common source, or of Jesus? Argue either way in **150 words or fewer**, naming the Synoptic hypothesis you assume, and deal with the strongest point against you.

**P3 (🔴)** *(Exegetical)* An **invented** study-bible note on Luke 2:2:

> "Luke promises in his prologue to write *akribōs*, 'with exact precision', and Vatican II's Dei Verbum 19 defined that the gospels are historical. The census under Quirinius therefore took place before Herod's death, exactly as Luke says, and Catholics must hold that Josephus is mistaken. Scholars who say Luke erred are contradicting a dogma."

Name **three** errors, and for each give the correction, marking the level of any Church teaching involved. **150 words or fewer.**

<details>
<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict:**
- (i) **Not addressed.** Josephus describes Quirinius's assessment of "Syria" and "Judea" only ("take an account of their substance"), and says nothing of an empire-wide decree. Credit: "being sent by Cæsar" is consistent with Augustus as its authority; the "whole world" scope is what goes unattested.
- (ii) **In tension**, not on Quirinius's office but on the date. Josephus agrees Quirinius made the census. He dates it after Archelaus's deposition, "the thirty-seventh year ... Actium" (AD 6-7), while Luke 1:5 places the story "in the days of Herod".
- (iii) **Not addressed.** Josephus's census is an "account of their substance", an assessment of property, and says nothing of registering in one's ancestral city.
- (iv) **In tension.** Josephus's census covers Judea, "now added to the province of Syria"; Galilee stayed Herod Antipas's tetrarchy ("Herod and Philip had each of them received their own tetrarchy"), so a Galilean would not fall under it.

**Wrong turns:** marking (ii) "contradicted" on the ground that Quirinius was never governor; Josephus confirms that he was. Missing (iv) entirely, which is the least-noticed tension and is visible in the excerpt.

**Model answer:** (i) Not addressed: Josephus knows only Syria and Judea, and "sent by Cæsar" fits Augustus as the authority. (ii) In tension: same governor, but "thirty-seventh year ... Actium" is AD 6-7, not "the days of Herod." (iii) Not addressed: Josephus describes an "account of their substance", not travel to ancestral towns. (iv) In tension: Judea was "added to the province", but Galilee was Antipas's tetrarchy.

**P2** *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b))*

**Must hit, strict (a):** any five of the following. Luke uses the second person ("ye"), Matthew the third ("they") until 5:11. "Poor" in Luke against "poor in spirit" in Matthew. "Hunger now" against "hunger and thirst after justice". "Weep now ... laugh" against "mourn ... comforted". Luke has four blessings, Matthew eight in the third person plus one in the second. Luke adds four woes. "Kingdom of God" against "kingdom of heaven". "The Son of man's sake" against "my sake". "Separate you" (exclusion) against "persecute you". The "now" in Luke's blessings.

**Must hit, any verdict (b):** name the hypothesis (Two-Source, or Farrer-Goodacre with Luke using Matthew). Explain at least two differences by the editing it posits. Meet the best counterpoint: for a pro-Luke answer, that Luke's "now" and woes fit his own reversal theme, so he edited too; for a pro-Matthew answer, that "justice" (*dikaiosynē*) is a favourite Matthean word (seven times in Matthew, once in Luke, never in Mark), so Matthew's longer forms look like his hand. Mark the question as a hypothesis, not teaching.

**Wrong turns:** treating either version as "the real Sermon" on doctrinal grounds; the question is exegetical and freely disputed. Calling one version "wrong" because it differs: Dei Verbum 19 expects the evangelists to shape what they hand on.

**Model answer (b), one of several:** On the Two-Source hypothesis Luke is closer to Q. Luke's short, second-person blessings addressed to a crowd ("ye poor") are the harder, more concrete form. Matthew's "in spirit" and "after justice" add his own favourite word, *dikaiosynē*, and turn material conditions into dispositions. That fits a Jewish-Christian catechesis (2.2). The strongest point against me is that Luke's "now" and the woes are Luke's own reversal theme, so Luke edited as well. I grant it: Luke added the woes and the "now". But adding a frame is easier to see than removing "in spirit", so I still judge the bare "poor" original. This is a hypothesis about sources; the Church teaches neither form as the more original.

**P3** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict:** (1) **Mistranslating *akribōs*** as "exact precision". It means carefully or accurately investigated, a claim about research. Luke also rearranges Mark, so the prologue cannot be read as a guarantee of every detail. (2) **Overstating Dei Verbum 19.** It teaches the gospels' historical character in a dogmatic constitution as what the Church has always held; it did not *define* a dogma in 1965. It concerns what Jesus did and taught, and it settles no census date. (3) **Promoting a harmonization to teaching.** The Church has settled none of the census solutions. Whether an incidental dating error would fall under inerrancy is the freely disputed scope question ([`fundamental-theology` 3.2](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/03-02-inerrancy.md)). So scholars who hold Luke erred (Brown, Fitzmyer) contradict no dogma, and Catholics need not hold that Josephus is wrong.

**Wrong turns:** answering that the Church teaches Luke erred (overcorrection). Calling Dei Verbum 19 a mere hypothesis.

**Model answer:** *Akribōs* means "carefully", a claim about research, and Luke visibly reorders Mark, so it guarantees no detail. Dei Verbum 19 teaches, without a new definition, that the gospels faithfully hand on what Jesus did and taught. That is conciliar teaching to be held, but it does not date the census. The solutions ("before Quirinius", an earlier census, Luke's error) are hypotheses. Whether inerrancy covers such incidental details is freely disputed, so neither holding Luke wrong nor holding Josephus wrong is required of a Catholic.

## Flashback

**From Lesson [2.1](02-01-mark.md) (Mark):** *(Exegetical.)* Four commands to silence in Mark (Douay-Rheims; the bracketed settings are added):

> **1:24–25** [an unclean spirit cries out:] I know who thou art, the Holy One of God. And Jesus threatened him, saying: Speak no more, and go out of the man.
>
> **5:43** [after raising Jairus's daughter:] And he charged them strictly that no man should know it: and commanded that something should be given her to eat.
>
> **7:36** [after healing a man deaf and dumb:] And he charged them that they should tell no man. But the more he charged them, so much the more a great deal did they publish it.
>
> **8:26** [after healing a blind man:] And he sent him into his house, saying: Go into thy house, and if thou enter into the town, tell nobody.

"Speak no more" is *phimōthēti*, literally "be muzzled". (a) For each command, say what it hides (who Jesus is, or a miracle) and whether Mark shows it kept, broken, or does not say. **One line each.** (b) Which of the four fits Wrede's explanation of the secret best, and which strains it most? **Two sentences.** (c) What do most scholars now keep from Wrede, what do they drop, and what standing does either have? **Two sentences.**

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):** **1:24–25** hides **who Jesus is** ("the Holy One of God"); **kept**, since the spirit is silenced and driven out. **5:43** hides a **miracle** (a raising); Mark **does not say** whether it was kept. **7:36** hides a **miracle**; **broken** at once and the more for being given. **8:26** hides a **miracle**; Mark **does not say**.
**Must hit, strict (b):** **Best fit: 1:24–25**, where a true statement of Jesus's identity is suppressed, which is what a secret covering his messiahship until Easter requires. **Most strain: 7:36**, a command broken as soon as it is given, so it keeps nothing secret, and it protects a healing, not a title. Credit 5:43 or 8:26 as the strain if the reason given is that they protect miracles, not titles.
**Must hit, strict (c):** most scholars **keep** Wrede's discovery that secrecy is a deliberate **Markan theme**, and **drop** his explanation that it was built to cover an unmessianic tradition (a Jesus wary of a politically loaded title is plausible history). Both the theme reading and Wrede's theory are **scholarly hypotheses** with no magisterial standing. Dei Verbum 19 allows an evangelist to arrange material around a theme and does not choose between Wrede and his critics.
**Wrong turns:** marking 5:43 or 8:26 "broken"; the text reports no breach. Reading the secret as Mark doubting that Jesus is Messiah, when 1:1 announces it. Treating the current majority view ("keep the theme, drop the theory") as Church teaching.
**Model answer:** (a) 1:24–25 hides his identity, and it is kept: the spirit is muzzled and cast out. 5:43 hides a raising, outcome not told. 7:36 hides a healing and is broken at once. 8:26 hides a healing, outcome not told. (b) 1:24–25 fits Wrede best, because it silences a true confession of who Jesus is before Easter. 7:36 strains his theory most, since a command broken on the spot conceals nothing and concerns a miracle, not a title. (c) Scholars keep the secret as a real Markan theme and drop Wrede's claim that it covers an unmessianic history. Both are hypotheses without magisterial standing, and Dei Verbum 19 settles neither.

</details>
