# The New Testament · Lesson 1.2: Second Temple Judaism

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 1: The world of the New Testament and the making of the gospels · Builds on: [1.1 Reading the New Testament in the Church](01-01-reading-the-new-testament-in-the-church.md) · Unlocks: [1.3 Under Rome](01-03-under-rome.md), [1.4 The Synoptic problem](01-04-the-synoptic-problem.md)

## Why this matters

The gospels assume a reader who knows what a Pharisee is, why a Sadducee would mock the resurrection, and what "the kingdom of God" meant before Jesus said it. You must reconstruct it, and the reconstruction changes what the texts say. [Lesson 1.1](01-01-reading-the-new-testament-in-the-church.md) showed that the Church asks for exactly this: Dei Verbum 12 tells the interpreter to attend to the ways of perceiving, speaking and narrating that were customary in the sacred writer's time (paraphrase). This lesson maps that time, from the Temple's rebuilding (515 BC) to its destruction (AD 70).

## The idea

Picture Judaism in Jesus's day as a people with a shared core and a few noisy minorities. Most Jews belonged to no party. They kept the Sabbath, circumcised their sons, avoided pork, went up to the Temple for the feasts, and said the Shema. E. P. Sanders called this [common Judaism](../reference.md#common-judaism) in *Judaism: Practice and Belief, 63 BCE–66 CE* (1992): one God, one chosen people, one Law, one Temple, shared by priests and people.

On that core sat a handful of **groups**: small, argumentative and learned, which is why they fill the sources. Josephus, a priest turned historian in Rome, presented them to Greek readers as philosophical schools, like Stoics and Epicureans. Hold that label loosely.

Two things held the people together: the **Temple** in Jerusalem, the one place of sacrifice, run by priests under a high priest; and the **Torah**, the Law of Moses, the covenant charter every group claimed to keep. The groups differed on how to keep it, who could interpret it, and what God would do next.

## Source

Josephus, *Antiquities of the Jews* 18.1.3–4 (Whiston's translation, public domain), written in the 90s AD:

> [The Pharisees believe] that under the earth there will be rewards or punishments, according as they have lived virtuously or viciously in this life; and the latter are to be detained in an everlasting prison, but that the former shall have power to revive and live again; on account of which doctrines they are able greatly to persuade the body of the people; and whatsoever they do about Divine worship, prayers, and sacrifices, they perform them according to their direction ...
> 4. But the doctrine of the Sadducees is this: That souls die with the bodies; nor do they regard the observation of any thing besides what the law enjoins them ... but this doctrine is received but by a few, yet by those still of the greatest dignity. But they are able to do almost nothing of themselves; for when they become magistrates ... they addict themselves to the notions of the Pharisees, because the multitude would not otherwise bear them.

Read it for three things. **Belief:** the Pharisees expect the righteous to "revive and live again"; the Sadducees say souls die with bodies. **Authority:** the Sadducees accept nothing beyond "what the law enjoins", implying the Pharisees held more: ancestral traditions. **Power:** the Sadducees are few but of "the greatest dignity" (the priestly aristocracy), yet the Pharisees have the crowd.

## The argument

1. **Pharisees.** A lay movement of exact interpreters of the Law. They held to ancestral traditions beyond the written Torah, to the resurrection of the dead, and to a providence that does not cancel human freedom. Josephus counts some six thousand under Herod (*Ant.* 17.2.4). Paul was one (Philippians 3:5). *In words:* popular, precise about purity and Sabbath, hopeful of resurrection.
2. **Sadducees.** The priestly and aristocratic families around the high priesthood. They held to the written Law only, denied the resurrection, and had the Temple, but not the crowd. They vanish with the Temple in 70. *In words:* the establishment, conservative in doctrine.
3. **Essenes.** A disciplined brotherhood that held property in common and kept strict purity. Josephus counts some four thousand and says they kept apart from the Temple's common sacrifices (*Ant.* 18.1.5). Most scholars identify the community of the [Dead Sea Scrolls](../reference.md#qumran-and-the-essenes) (found at Qumran from 1947) as Essene or closely related. The case rests on Pliny the Elder's placing of Essenes on the Dead Sea's western shore, on shared rules for common property and initiation, and on the Scrolls' hostility to the Jerusalem priesthood. A majority is not a proof: Norman Golb argued that the Scrolls were Jerusalem libraries hidden during the revolt. *In words:* a priestly protest movement awaiting God's cleansing of a defiled Temple.
4. **The "fourth philosophy".** Josephus's name for the movement of Judas the Galilean, who resisted the Roman census of AD 6. It agrees with the Pharisees in all else but holds "that God is to be their only Ruler and Lord" (*Ant.* 18.1.6). Josephus blames it for the road to the revolt of 66. *In words:* theology as tax resistance; the tribute question put to Jesus (Mark 12:14) lives here.
5. **Messianic hopes, plural.** There was no single expected Messiah. The *Psalms of Solomon*, usually dated after Pompey took Jerusalem in 63 BC, pray in Psalm 17 for a **royal** son of David who will purge Jerusalem of the nations and rule in righteousness. Qumran's *Community Rule* expects messiahs of Aaron and of Israel: a **priestly** figure beside a royal one (1QS 9.11). A **prophetic** figure, the prophet like Moses (Deuteronomy 18:15), appears too. A Qumran fragment, 4Q521, speaks of heaven and earth obeying God's anointed one, and then of healing the wounded, making the dead live and bringing good news to the poor. Whether God or the anointed does these things is disputed. Many texts of the period mention no messiah at all (John J. Collins, *The Scepter and the Star*, maps the types). *In words:* "Are you the Messiah?" was ambiguous, which is why the gospels keep saying what kind.
6. **Apocalyptic and the kingdom.** Daniel 7 sees "one like the Son of man" come with the clouds of heaven and receive an everlasting kingdom; the book's genre is taught in [`old-testament`](../../old-testament/syllabus.md). [Apocalyptic](../reference.md#apocalyptic-and-the-kingdom-of-god) thought expected God to break into history, end the present evil age and vindicate his people. Jesus's first proclamation in Mark speaks its language: "The time is accomplished and the kingdom of God is at hand" (Mark 1:15, Douay–Rheims). *In words:* "kingdom of God" meant God acting as king, publicly and soon, not a place souls go.
7. **Synagogue, diaspora, Septuagint.** Probably more Jews lived outside the land than in it: Alexandria, Antioch, Rome and Asia Minor. They met in synagogues to read and expound the Law on the Sabbath (Luke 4:16; Paul in Acts). They read scripture in Greek, in the [Septuagint](../reference.md#the-septuagint), a translation begun with the Pentateuch in Alexandria in the third century BC. Most New Testament quotations of scripture follow the Greek where it differs from the Hebrew. *In words:* the first Christian mission ran along diaspora synagogue routes, with a Greek Bible in hand.
8. **Caution on sources.** The **Mishnah**, the first great rabbinic code, was compiled about AD 200, after the Temple was gone. Reading its rules back into Jesus's Galilee as "what the Pharisees taught" is anachronism unless the rule is shown early. Josephus, the Scrolls and the New Testament are nearer witnesses, each with its own slant. *In words:* date the evidence before you use it.

**Mark the levels.** Nothing in steps 1–8 is Church teaching: the groups' beliefs, the Essene identification of Qumran, the dating of the *Psalms of Solomon* and Sanders's "common Judaism" are **historical reconstructions**, with no magisterial standing either way (see [Church teaching vs hypothesis](../reference.md#church-teaching-vs-hypothesis)). Church teaching bears on *how* to use them: Dei Verbum 12 on studying the writer's world and Dei Verbum 19 on the gospels' historical character are conciliar teaching ([1.1](01-01-reading-the-new-testament-in-the-church.md)), weighed with [the ladder](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-04-the-ladder-of-doctrinal-authority.md). The 1985 *Notes* of the Vatican's Commission for Religious Relations with the Jews warn preachers that a wholly negative picture of the Pharisees is likely inaccurate and unjust. That is a pastoral directive about preaching, not a ruling on first-century history.

## Map of groups

The [three philosophies](../reference.md#the-three-philosophies) plus the fourth, and the majority who belonged to none, on the four questions that divide them:

| Group | Resurrection | Law and tradition | Temple | Rome |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Pharisees** | Yes: the righteous "revive and live again" (*Ant.* 18.1.3) | Written Law plus ancestral traditions | Took part; shaped popular worship | Accommodated, mostly |
| **Sadducees** | No: souls die with bodies (*Ant.* 18.1.4) | Written Law only | Ran it (high-priestly families) | Collaborated to keep order |
| **Essenes / Qumran** | Immortal soul in Josephus (*War* 2.8.11); the Scrolls' view debated | Strict, with their own rules and calendar | Withdrew from the common sacrifices; the priesthood judged defiled | Awaited God's war against the nations |
| **Fourth philosophy** | As the Pharisees | As the Pharisees | Zeal for its purity | Refused tribute: God alone is Lord |
| **Common Judaism** (most Jews) | Varied | Sabbath, circumcision, food laws | Pilgrimage, tithes, sacrifice | Endured it |

**Check against Acts 23:8** (Douay–Rheims): "For the Sadducees say that there is no resurrection, neither angel, nor spirit: but the Pharisees confess both." Luke and Josephus, writing independently, agree on the resurrection line; Acts adds angels and spirits, which Josephus never mentions. Two independent witnesses make this the table's strongest datum.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean): the Sadducees' question, Mark 12:18–27.** Mark introduces them as those who deny the resurrection (12:18). Run the map. *Who:* the Temple aristocracy, so the scene belongs in Jerusalem, in the Temple, in the last week, and it does. *Why this question:* a woman married to seven brothers in turn under the levirate law (Deuteronomy 25:5–6) is a reductio built from the Law they accept. *Why this answer:* Jesus replies from the Law too, from the burning bush (Exodus 3:6), not from Daniel 12:2, the clearest resurrection text in the Hebrew Bible. Some Fathers said the Sadducees accepted only the five books of Moses; Josephus says only that they rejected traditions beyond the Law, so treat that as possible, not established.

**Example 2 (hard): John's question and 4Q521.** John the Baptist sends to ask whether Jesus is the one who is to come (Luke 7:19). Jesus answers with a list: the blind see, the lame walk, the dead rise, the poor have the gospel preached to them (Luke 7:22; Matthew 11:5). 4Q521 pairs an anointed figure with healing, raising the dead and good news to the poor, joining the dead raised to Isaiah 61's good news, a pairing Isaiah itself does not make. So the gospel's reply is *messianic* in a recognised idiom, not a random list of miracles.

Here the method strains. One fragment shows that *some* Jews linked these works to an anointed figure, not that John, or most Jews, did. And if God, not the anointed, heals in 4Q521, the parallel says "the works of the messianic age are happening", not "the messiah is doing them". The fragment makes a messianic reading of Luke 7:22 plausible and early, not the only reading. Use evidence for the language it shows, not for the claim it cannot carry.

## Watch out

- **"The Pharisees were legalists who thought they earned salvation."** That picture comes from reading Paul through later controversies, not from the sources. Sanders argued in *Paul and Palestinian Judaism* (1977) that the period's Judaism was [covenantal nomism](../reference.md#covenantal-nomism): one is in the covenant by God's election and keeps the Law to stay in it, with repentance and atonement provided. How far that holds is [5.4](05-04-the-new-perspective-on-paul.md)'s disputed question. Undisputed: Josephus presents the Pharisees as the party the crowd trusted.
- **Level error, in both directions.** Sanders's thesis is a **scholarly hypothesis**; do not preach it as Church teaching. The 1985 *Notes* are a real Church directive about preaching, not one scholar's opinion. Neither decides a historical question.
- **"The Essenes wrote the Dead Sea Scrolls" as fact.** It is the well-evidenced majority view; say "most scholars identify".
- **"The Messiah" as one job description.** Royal, priestly and prophetic hopes coexisted; many Jews had none. "Christ" was contested ground (see [messianic expectations](../reference.md#messianic-expectations)).

## One-liner

> Most Jews shared one God, one Law, one Temple; the parties were loud minorities split over resurrection, tradition, Temple and Rome, and the gospels assume you know which is which.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢)** *(Exegetical.)* Josephus, *Jewish War* 2.8.14 (Whiston):

> the Pharisees are those who are esteemed most skillful in the exact explication of their laws, and introduce the first sect. These ascribe all to fate [or providence], and to God, and yet allow, that to act what is right, or the contrary, is principally in the power of men, although fate does co-operate in every action. They say that all souls are incorruptible, but that the souls of good men only are removed into other bodies,--but that the souls of bad men are subject to eternal punishment. But the Sadducees ... take away fate entirely ... They also take away the belief of the immortal duration of the soul, and the punishments and rewards in Hades.

(a) State the Pharisees' view on (i) providence and human freedom and (ii) the fate of the righteous, quoting the phrase that gives each. (b) "Removed into other bodies" can sound like reincarnation. Give one reason, from this lesson's Source or from Acts 23:8, for reading it as resurrection, and one reason Josephus might phrase it this way. **Two sentences per part.**

**P2 (🟡)** *(Exegetical.)* Assign each invented statement to the group most likely to say it (Pharisee, Sadducee, Essene/Qumran, fourth philosophy) and name the one feature that decides it. **One line each.**

1. "The oral traditions of the elders are no part of the Law; show me the verse."
2. "Paying the census tax confesses Caesar as lord; God alone is our master."
3. "The sons of Aaron who serve at the altar in Jerusalem have profaned it; we keep the true calendar and wait in the wilderness."
4. "The righteous dead will rise, and the washing of hands before meals is our fathers' tradition, to be kept."
5. "When the high priest's council sits, I must speak as the people expect, or they will not bear me."

**P3 (🔴)** *(Exegetical.)* An **invented** sermon excerpt:

> "The Pharisees were the religious establishment of Jesus's day. They ran the Temple, taught that heaven must be earned by keeping 613 rules, and plotted his death at the trial. The Church has always taught that they stand for dead legalism, and modern scholars agree."

(a) Name **three** factual errors about the Pharisees and correct each from this lesson. (b) The last sentence makes two claims about authority. Say what the Church's 1985 directive actually asks, and what status Sanders's view has. **150 words or fewer in total.**

<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical.)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** (i) providence works but does not remove freedom. The phrase is "to act what is right, or the contrary, is principally in the power of men, although fate does co-operate". (ii) The righteous live again in a body. The phrase is "the souls of good men only are removed into other bodies". The wicked face "eternal punishment".
**Must hit, strict (b):** a reason for reading resurrection: the *Antiquities* version says the righteous "shall have power to revive and live again", and Acts 23:8 has the Pharisees "confess" the resurrection, both independent of this passage. A reason for the phrasing: Josephus is presenting the groups to Greek readers as "philosophies", in terms (souls, fate, Hades) that sound like Greek schools.
**Wrong turns:** concluding the Pharisees taught reincarnation on this sentence alone, against two other witnesses. Saying the Pharisees were determinists: the sentence keeps freedom explicitly.
**Model answer:** (a) They held that God's providence co-operates in every act while right and wrong remain "principally in the power of men", and that the souls of the good "are removed into other bodies". (b) The *Antiquities* says the righteous "revive and live again" and Acts 23:8 has the Pharisees confess the resurrection, so a body given back is the likelier sense. Josephus is translating a Jewish hope into the vocabulary of Greek philosophy for Greek readers.

**P2** *(Exegetical.)*

**Must hit, strict:** (1) **Sadducee**: rejects traditions beyond the written Law (*Ant.* 18.1.4). (2) **Fourth philosophy**: refuses the census and tribute, God alone Lord (*Ant.* 18.1.6). (3) **Essene/Qumran**: judges the Jerusalem priesthood defiled, keeps its own calendar, withdrawn. (4) **Pharisee**: resurrection plus ancestral tradition (compare Mark 7:3's "tradition of the ancients"). (5) **Sadducee**: a magistrate of the council who, per *Ant.* 18.1.4, defers to Pharisaic notions "because the multitude would not otherwise bear them".
**Wrong turns:** giving (5) to the Pharisees because the *view* voiced is theirs; the speaker is the one deferring, which is Josephus's Sadducee. Giving (2) to the Pharisees; Josephus says the fourth philosophy agrees with them on everything *except* this. Giving (4) to the Essenes for its purity concern; the resurrection-plus-tradition pair is Pharisaic.
**Model answer:** 1 Sadducee (Law only). 2 Fourth philosophy (God alone Lord). 3 Essene/Qumran (defiled Temple, own calendar). 4 Pharisee (resurrection and tradition). 5 Sadducee (defers to the Pharisees for the crowd's sake).

**P3** *(Exegetical.)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** three of: (1) the **Sadducees**, the high-priestly families, ran the Temple, not the Pharisees, a lay and popular party; (2) "earning heaven by rules" is the legalist caricature. Josephus presents them as the people's trusted teachers, and on Sanders's reading Jewish obedience was a response to God's election, not a way to earn it. Credit a hedge that this is disputed; (3) **no gospel puts the Pharisees at the trial**: the hearing is led by the high priest with the chief priests, elders and scribes (Mark 14:53), and the sentence is Pilate's. The 1985 *Notes* say the Pharisees are not mentioned in the passion accounts; strictly, John 18:3 and Matthew 27:62 name them at the arrest and at the request for a guard, but not at the trial; (4) Jesus shared Pharisaic doctrines such as the resurrection. Credit: Pharisees warn Jesus (Luke 13:31) and Gamaliel defends the apostles (Acts 5:34–39).
**Must hit, strict (b):** the Church has **not** "always taught" this. Its 1985 directive asks preachers not to present the Pharisees in wholly negative terms, and it is a pastoral directive, not a definition. Sanders's covenantal nomism is a **scholarly hypothesis** with no magisterial standing, and it cuts *against* the preacher. "Modern scholars agree" misreports it in both content and status.
**Wrong turns:** claiming the Pharisees are wholly absent from the passion narratives (John 18:3 and Matthew 27:62 name them); the error is about the *trial*. Answering (b) by making Sanders Church teaching; that is the same level error the sermon makes, run the other way. Faulting "613 rules" as the error. The number is a later rabbinic count, so the real mistake is the "earned" claim.
**Model answer:** The Sadducean high-priestly families, not the Pharisees, ran the Temple. Josephus presents the Pharisees as the popular party, and on Sanders's reading their Law-keeping answered God's election rather than earning it, though that is disputed. No gospel places the Pharisees at the trial, which the high priest and Pilate conduct. As for authority, the Church's 1985 directive asks preachers *not* to paint the Pharisees wholly negatively. That is pastoral guidance, not dogma. Sanders's view is a scholarly hypothesis, not a consensus the preacher can cite, and it contradicts him.
