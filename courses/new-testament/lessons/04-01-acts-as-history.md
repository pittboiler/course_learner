# The New Testament · Lesson 4.1: Acts as history

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 4: Acts and Paul's churches · Builds on: [2.3 Luke](02-03-luke.md), [1.3 Under Rome](01-03-under-rome.md), [3.3 What historians broadly agree on](03-03-what-historians-broadly-agree-on.md) · Unlocks: [4.2 Paul's life and letters](04-02-pauls-life-and-letters.md)

## Why this matters

Without Acts, Christianity's first thirty years would be a handful of Paul's letters with no map: no Pentecost, no Stephen, no voyage to Rome. Almost every narrative of the first generation, including [`church-history` 1.1](../../church-history/lessons/01-01-the-first-generation-and-its-sources.md), leans on this one book. What did an ancient historian owe his readers, and what does a successful check actually prove?

## The idea

Read a modern biography of Lincoln and you assume two things. The speeches in quotation marks are his words, and the author footnotes where she got them. Ancient history kept neither rule. A historian was expected to research events carefully, and also to *write* the speeches, giving each speaker words that fit the occasion and the man. Readers knew this; it was the genre, not deception.

Acts is the second volume of Luke's work ([2.3](02-03-luke.md)), dedicated to the same Theophilus (Acts 1:1). Read by the rules of its own shelf, it raises three separate questions:

- **The setting.** Are the places, officials, titles and travel times right? This can often be checked against inscriptions and other writers.
- **The events.** Did this riot, trial or voyage happen? Sometimes this can be checked, usually not.
- **The speeches.** They are about a third of the book on common estimates. Are they transcripts, summaries, or the author's composition? The ancient convention decides what "accurate" could even mean here.

One strange feature cuts across all three. In three stretches the narrator stops saying "they" and starts saying "**we**".

## Source

Acts 16:8–12 (Douay–Rheims, Challoner). Paul, Silas and Timothy are crossing Asia Minor:

> And when they had passed through Mysia, they went down to Troas. And a vision was shewed to Paul in the night, which was a man of Macedonia standing and beseeching him and saying: Pass over into Macedonia and help us. And as soon as he had seen the vision, immediately we sought to go into Macedonia: being assured that God had called us to preach the gospel to them. And sailing from Troas, we came with a straight course to Samothracia, and the day following to Neapolis. And from thence to Philippi, which is the chief city of part of Macedonia, a colony. And we were in this city some days conferring together.

Watch the pronouns. Verse 8 has "they"; verse 10 switches to "we" unannounced, and the narrator never says who "we" are. The detail also thickens right where "we" begins: a straight course, the next day, a named port, and Philippi's legal status as a Roman "colony". Then, at 16:17, the "we" stops just as silently. From 16:19 it is "Paul and Silas" again, and the narrator is back outside the story.

## The argument

1. **[Genre](../reference.md#the-genre-of-acts): what shelf Acts sits on.** Most scholars place Acts among ancient historiographical writing. Colin Hemer (*The Book of Acts in the Setting of Hellenistic History*) read it as history checkable in detail. Gregory Sterling (*Historiography and Self-Definition*) called it "apologetic historiography": the story of a people told to define and defend it. Richard Pervo (*Profit with Delight*) dissented. He argued that Acts is closest to the ancient novel, built to edify and entertain with its shipwrecks, prison escapes and riots. *In words:* the majority says history with a purpose, a serious minority says edifying fiction with historical furniture, and a majority is not a proof.
2. **The speeches follow [a known convention](../reference.md#the-thucydidean-convention).** Thucydides stated the rule for his own work (1.22, Crawley's translation): "my habit has been to make the speakers say what was in my opinion demanded of them by the various occasions, of course adhering as closely as possible to the general sense of what they really said." Martin Dibelius's essays on the speeches argued that Luke writes in this tradition. The speeches share one pattern and style, whether Peter or Paul speaks. More conservative scholars (F. F. Bruce, Hemer) grant that the wording is Luke's but argue that the substance often preserves early preaching. *In words:* nobody serious thinks the speeches are transcripts. The dispute is how much remembered content sits under Luke's composition.
3. **Structure: Acts 1:8 is the table of contents.** "You shall be witnesses unto me in Jerusalem, and in all Judea, and Samaria, and even to the uttermost part of the earth" (Douay–Rheims). Chapters 1–7 are Jerusalem. After Stephen's death, believers are "dispersed through the countries of Judea, and Samaria" (8:1), and chapters 8–12 follow them. Chapters 13–28 follow Paul to Rome, and the book ends with Paul preaching there "without prohibition" (28:31), not with his trial or death. *In words:* the plot is geography, and the ending is the programme fulfilled, not a biography completed.
4. **Pentecost is the programme in miniature.** In Acts 2 the Spirit comes and Jews and proselytes "of every nation under heaven" (2:5), from Parthians to "strangers of Rome" (2:9–11), each hear the apostles "in our own tongues". Peter's speech reads the event through Joel. Where Douay's Joel 2:28 has "after this", Acts 2:17 has "in the last days". *In words:* Luke puts the whole mission of 1:8 into one scene. Whether it happened is one question; what it means in Luke's design is another, answerable from the text.
5. **The "we" passages.** First-person plural narration runs through [16:10–17, 20:5–21:18 and 27:1–28:16](../reference.md#the-we-passages). The "Western" text, represented by Codex Bezae, adds a stray "we" at 11:28. There are three explanations:
   - **(a) The author was there.** Irenaeus argued this around 180. He says the passages show that Luke "was inseparable from Paul, and his fellow-labourer in the Gospel" (*Against Heresies* III.14.1, ANF). Joseph Fitzmyer defended a modern version: Luke was a sometime companion of Paul. Raymond Brown judged a minor companion the option with fewest difficulties.
   - **(b) A source.** The author took over a travel diary kept by someone else, "we" and all.
   - **(c) A literary device.** Vernon Robbins argued that ancient sea-voyage narratives conventionally slip into "we". Pervo read the "we" as a claim to participation meant to lend authority.
   *In words:* (a) says the pronoun means what it seems to, (b) says it is someone's, and (c) says it is style.
6. **[External checks](../reference.md#external-checks-on-acts), and what they can prove.** Luke's titles for officials are repeatedly right, and they vary correctly from place to place. He calls the governor of Cyprus a *proconsul* (*anthypatos*, 13:7), which fits a senatorial province. He calls the magistrates of Thessalonica *politarchai* (17:6, 8), a title found in inscriptions from Macedonia. He mentions "rulers of Asia" at Ephesus (19:31; Greek *Asiarchai*). He puts Gallio in Achaia as proconsul (18:12), and [a letter of Claudius inscribed at Delphi](../reference.md#the-gallio-inscription) places Gallio there around 51–52 (the date is worked out in [4.2](04-02-pauls-life-and-letters.md)). But a correct title confirms the **setting**. It does not confirm the **event**, and still less the **speech**. A later writer who knew the eastern cities could get titles right too, and so could a good novelist; that is Pervo's reply to Hemer. *In words:* checks show that Luke knows his world; none reaches what Paul said on the Areopagus.

**Mark the levels.** That Acts is sacred and canonical, written under inspiration, is **defined dogma** (rung 1). Trent's list names it ([`fundamental-theology` 3.3](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/03-03-the-canon-as-defined.md)). Its inerrancy is Church teaching whose scope Catholics dispute ([3.2](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/03-02-inerrancy.md)). Dei Verbum 12 teaches that truth is expressed differently in different kinds of historical writing, and requires attention to the literary forms of the writer's time. So asking what an ancient speech asserts is obedience to the Church's method, not an evasion of it. Trent's list calls Acts "written by Luke the Evangelist" (Schaff), and [a Biblical Commission response](../reference.md#the-early-biblical-commission-responses) of 12 June 1913 affirmed Lucan authorship, a date before the end of Paul's Roman captivity, and the book's historical authority. That response's present weight is a question for [1.1](01-01-reading-the-new-testament-in-the-church.md) and [`fundamental-theology` 4.5](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-05-reading-a-documents-weight.md); how far the tradition's authorship attributions bind at all is **freely disputed** among Catholics. The genre of Acts, the origin of the speeches, the explanation of the "we", and a date (usually put at 80–90, with minorities earlier and later) are **scholarly hypotheses** with no magisterial standing. Keep them [apart](../reference.md#church-teaching-vs-hypothesis).

## Where Acts can be checked

| Claim in Acts | Outside evidence | What it confirms | What it cannot reach |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sergius Paulus, *proconsul* of Cyprus (13:7) | Cyprus was a senatorial province under a proconsul. A Lucius Sergius Paullus is attested at Rome under Claudius, but identifying him with Luke's proconsul is debated. | the title, and possibly the man | his conversion (13:12) |
| *Politarchs* at Thessalonica (17:6, 8) | inscriptions from Macedonia use the title | local terminology | the riot at Jason's house |
| The Areopagus at Athens (17:19, 22) | a council by that name still sat in Roman Athens | the institution | the speech's content, or whether Paul faced the council or only stood on the hill |
| Gallio, proconsul of Achaia (18:12) | the Delphi inscription, c. 51–52 | the office and an approximate date | the hearing itself |
| Theudas's rising, then Judas the Galilean "after this man" (5:36–37) | Josephus, *Antiquities* 20.5.1 | nothing: it is in **tension** (Example 2) | whether Gamaliel said it |

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean): the Philippi "we".** Apply the three explanations to the Source. **(a) Eyewitness** predicts that the vivid detail should cluster where "we" appears, and it does: a named day, a named port, and the colony status. It also predicts that the narrator should drop out when he is not there. At 16:19 only Paul and Silas are arrested, and the "we" has stopped. **(b) Source** predicts the same, since a diary is vivid where its keeper was. **(c) Sea-voyage convention** fits the sailing of 16:11. It fits less well at 16:13–15, where "we" sit down by a river on land and speak to women at prayer. *Result:* (a) and (b) fit this passage equally. The convention account strains, since "we" persists on dry land. What the pronoun cannot tell you is whether the "we" is the *author's* or his *source's*.

**Example 2 (hard): Theudas.** In Acts 5, Gamaliel counsels the council around the mid-30s. He cites "Theodas" (Douay's spelling), who drew "about four hundred" men and was slain, and then "after this man" Judas of Galilee "in the days of the enrolling". Josephus puts a Theudas, a would-be prophet promising to part the Jordan, under the procurator Fadus. That is roughly 44–46, a decade *after* Gamaliel's speech. Josephus's Judas led his revolt at the census of about 6. So the check runs against Acts twice: Gamaliel cites a future event, in the wrong order. Three readings: **Luke erred**, and since the speech is his composition by convention, the slip would be his, not Gamaliel's. **A different, earlier Theudas.** The name was not rare, and Whiston's note on *Antiquities* 17.10 suggests one of the leaders of the many uprisings Josephus does not name. **Josephus erred.** The second is possible but unevidenced; the first is the majority's. Here the method strains honestly: the check that confirms Luke's titles elsewhere indicts him here, and you cannot keep one result and refuse the other. What a Catholic makes of a residue like this belongs to [`fundamental-theology` 3.2](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/03-02-inerrancy.md), not to the historian.

## Watch out

- **"The Church teaches Acts is accurate history, so the speeches are transcripts."** A hypothesis presented as teaching, on a genre mistake. No magisterial act says the speeches are verbatim. Dei Verbum 12 points you to the writer's literary conventions, and the Thucydidean convention is one of them.
- **The reverse error.** "Genre scholarship shows Acts isn't inspired history." Canonicity and inspiration are rung 1. What genre study changes is *what is asserted*, not whether the book is Scripture.
- **Checked setting is not checked event.** "Archaeology proves Acts" overreaches in the same way that "the Theudas problem disproves Acts" does. Each check is local.
- **"We" is not a signature.** It never names the narrator. The identification with Luke comes from the tradition (Irenaeus), not from the text.

## One-liner

> Acts gets its world right more often than not, writes its speeches the way ancient historians did, and says "we" without saying who. Check the setting, judge the speech by its convention, and keep all three verdicts apart from the dogma that it is Scripture.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢)** *(Exegetical)* Acts 20:4–6, 13–14 (Douay–Rheims):

> And there accompanied him Sopater, the son of Pyrrhus, of Berea: and of the Thessalonians, Aristarchus and Secundus: and Gaius of Derbe and Timothy: and of Asia, Tychicus and Trophimus. These, going before, stayed for us at Troas. But we sailed from Philippi after the days of the azymes and came to them to Troas in five days, where we abode seven days. ... But we going aboard the ship, sailed to Assos, being there to take in Paul. For so he had appointed, himself purposing to travel by land. And when he had met with us at Assos, we took him in and came to Mitylene.

(a) The previous "we" section ended in chapter 16. Where was the narrative then, and where does this one resume? Say in one sentence why that fact is often cited in the "we" debate. (b) Which words in verses 13–14 show that "we" cannot simply mean "Paul and his party"? (c) Which of the three explanations of the "we" is *least* helped by verses 13–14, and why? Two sentences per part.

**P2 (🟡)** *(Exegetical)* Classify each claim from Acts as **A** (confirmed by outside evidence), **B** (consistent with outside evidence but the event itself unattested outside Acts), **C** (in tension with outside evidence), or **D** (not open to historical check at all). Give one line of reason each.

1. A man named Gallio was proconsul of Achaia in the early 50s.
2. The magistrates of Thessalonica were called politarchs.
3. The Jews of Corinth brought Paul before Gallio, who refused to hear the case (18:12–17).
4. Theudas's rising came before that of Judas the Galilean (5:36–37).
5. The Holy Spirit forbade Paul to preach the word in Asia (16:6).

**P3 (🔴)** *(Exegetical)* An **invented** study-bible note:

> "Archaeology has confirmed Luke's titles for officials, from the proconsul of Cyprus to the politarchs of Thessalonica. Since Luke's accuracy is now proven and the Church teaches that Acts is inspired, Peter's Pentecost sermon in Acts 2 is a word-for-word record of what Peter said, and a Catholic who calls the 'we' passages a literary device denies the faith."

(a) Name the inference that fails in the first two sentences, and the convention that undercuts it. (b) Mark the level of three claims: *Acts is inspired*; *the speeches are verbatim*; *the "we" passages are the author's own eyewitness narration*. (c) Say what is wrong with "denies the faith". Two sentences per part.

<details>
<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** the "we" stopped at **Philippi** (16:17, just before the arrest of Paul and Silas) and resumes at **Philippi** ("we sailed from Philippi", 20:6), years later in the story. The fact is cited because it fits a narrator, or a diarist, who stayed behind in Philippi and rejoined Paul there. Both the eyewitness and the source explanations predict exactly this.
**Must hit, strict (b):** "being there to take in **Paul**", "**he** had appointed, **himself** purposing to travel by land", "when **he** had met with **us**". Paul goes by land and "we" go by sea, so "we" is a group Paul can be apart from and must rejoin.
**Must hit, strict (c):** the **literary-device** explanation, especially Robbins's sea-voyage convention. A convention should put the voyaging hero inside the "we". Here the "we" sails *without* Paul, and he travels by land, which is the kind of odd, unmotivated detail an itinerary records and a stylist would not invent.
**Wrong turns:** saying the passage *proves* the author was an eyewitness. The source explanation fits it equally well. Reading "stayed for us at Troas" as the named companions being the "we"; they are the ones who went ahead.
**Model answer:** (a) The first "we" section ends at Philippi in 16:17, and this one picks up at Philippi in 20:6, so the "we" seems to have stayed there. That pattern suits a companion, or a diarist the author used, who stayed in Philippi and rejoined Paul. (b) "To take in Paul" and "he ... purposing to travel by land" set Paul apart from "us". The "we" is a group without Paul in it for this stage. (c) The literary-device reading is least helped. A convention would not put the hero on land while "we" sail, and that is the kind of detail a real itinerary leaves behind.

**P2** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict:**
1. **A.** The Delphi inscription of Claudius's letter puts Gallio in office around 51–52.
2. **A.** Inscriptions from Macedonia use the title.
3. **B.** It fits the office and the date, but no source outside Acts reports the hearing.
4. **C.** Josephus places Theudas under Fadus (c. 44–46) and Judas at the census (c. 6): the reverse order, and after Gamaliel's speech.
5. **D.** It is a claim about divine action. Historical method can report that Paul changed route, but it cannot confirm or refute the cause.
**Wrong turns:** marking 3 as A because 1 is A, which confuses confirming the setting with confirming the event. Marking 5 as C on the ground that miracles are improbable. That is a philosophical judgment, not outside evidence, and it belongs to [`philosophy-of-religion`](../../philosophy-of-religion/syllabus.md). Marking 4 as B on the "different Theudas" reading; that reading is a proposed resolution of the tension, not a lack of one.
**Model answer:** 1 A (Delphi). 2 A (Macedonian inscriptions). 3 B (right office, unattested hearing). 4 C (Josephus reverses the order and puts Theudas later). 5 D (divine action, outside historical check).

**P3** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** the note infers the accuracy of **speeches** from the accuracy of the **setting**. Correct titles confirm local knowledge, not events, and still less wording. The **Thucydidean convention** (1.22) is that the historian composes speeches fitting the occasion and keeps to the general sense. So "verbatim" is not what an ancient history claims even when it is accurate. Credit for noting "proven" overreaches, since the Theudas check runs the other way.
**Must hit, strict (b):** *Acts is inspired*: **defined dogma**, rung 1 (Trent's canon). *The speeches are verbatim*: **no level**. It is a hypothesis, and one that almost no scholar holds; no magisterial act teaches it, and Dei Verbum 12 directs attention to ancient literary forms. *The "we" is the author's eyewitness narration*: a **scholarly hypothesis** (Irenaeus's reading, defended by Fitzmyer; rivals are the source and device explanations). It is not defined. Tradition and the 1913 Biblical Commission response back Lucan authorship, but their present weight is a separate question, and how far authorship attributions bind is freely disputed.
**Must hit, strict (c):** "Denies the faith" is the charge for denying a **rung-1** truth (heresy). The eyewitness reading of "we" is not defined at any level, so rejecting it cannot deny the faith. The note has promoted a hypothesis to dogma.
**Wrong turns:** conceding that the speeches must be verbatim because inerrancy covers them. Inerrancy covers what is **asserted**, and an ancient speech asserts its sense, not a transcript ([`fundamental-theology` 3.2](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/03-02-inerrancy.md)). Marking Lucan authorship itself as defined dogma because Trent's list says "written by Luke".
**Model answer:** (a) It moves from correct titles, which show that Luke knew the world he described, to verbatim speeches, which no such check can reach. By the convention Thucydides states, ancient historians composed speeches that fit the occasion, so a verbatim record is not what Acts claims. (b) Inspiration is defined dogma (rung 1, Trent). The verbatim speeches are a hypothesis with no magisterial backing and little scholarly support. The eyewitness "we" is a scholarly hypothesis among three. (c) Only denying rung-1 teaching is denying the faith, and no definition covers the "we". The note promotes a hypothesis to dogma, which is the course's basic level error.

## Flashback

**From Lesson [3.3](03-03-what-historians-broadly-agree-on.md) (What historians broadly agree on):** *(Exegetical.)* Two passages, Douay–Rheims:

> But in those days, after that tribulation, the sun shall be darkened and the moon shall not give her light. And the stars of heaven shall be falling down and the powers that are in heaven shall be moved. And then shall they see the Son of man coming in the clouds, with great power and glory. (Mark 13:24–26)

> The burden of Babylon which Isaias the son of Amos saw. ... For the stars of heaven, and their brightness shall not display their light: the sun shall be darkened in his rising, and the moon shall not shine with her light. ... Behold I will stir up the Medes against them ... And that Babylon, glorious among kingdoms, the famous pride of the Chaldeans, shall be even as the Lord destroyed Sodom and Gomorrha. (Isaiah 13:1, 10, 17, 19)

(a) In Isaiah, what event does the darkened sky accompany, and what does that show about how such language could work? (b) A defender of Wright's reading cites this parallel for Mark 13:24–26. Name the premise the eschatological-prophet reading (Schweitzer, Allison, Ehrman) must deny, and one piece of evidence from its own case it could cite. (c) Put "Jesus preached a coming kingdom of God" and "Jesus expected the physical cosmos to end within his hearers' lifetimes" in their bins, and say whether *Lamentabili* settles the second. **Two sentences per part.**

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):** The fall of **Babylon to the Medes** (13:17, 19), a conquest inside history, announced as "the day of the Lord" in cosmic imagery. So darkened sun, moon and stars could express the theological weight of an earthly event, not forecast a change in the sky.
**Must hit, strict (b):** The premise is that **Jesus, or Mark, used the imagery the way Isaiah did**, for events inside history (on Wright's reading, the Temple's fall and Jesus's vindication). The eschatological side must deny that Isaiah's usage decides this text: a parallel shows what the language *can* mean, not what it means here. It can cite **Allison's sandwich**: John before Jesus preached imminent judgment, and the church after him expected the Lord's literal coming soon (1 Thessalonians 4:15–17).
**Must hit, strict (c):** The first is **near-consensus**. The second is a **live dispute**: the eschatological-prophet reading is probably the majority, but Crossan denies an apocalyptic Jesus, Wright redefines "the end", and Meier doubts the deadline sayings. *Lamentabili* does **not** settle it: it condemned the dilemma "either Jesus erred or the gospels are inauthentic", not any historical reconstruction.
**Wrong turns:** reading Isaiah 13 as proof that Mark 13 is metaphor, or dismissing it as irrelevant. Treating the majority view in (c) as near-consensus. Reading *Lamentabili* as a definition of Jesus's eschatology.
**Model answer:** (a) The darkened heavens accompany Babylon's fall to the Medes, a political conquest in history. So such language could mark an earthly event as God's judgment rather than predict the end of the cosmos. (b) The premise is that Jesus's cosmic imagery refers, as Isaiah's does, to events inside history; the other side denies that one parallel fixes the sense here. It can cite the sandwich: John's imminent judgment before Jesus, and Paul's expectation in 1 Thessalonians 4 of the Lord's coming soon after him. (c) The kingdom preaching is near-consensus, while an expected end of the cosmos within a generation is a live dispute, a majority reading with serious rivals. *Lamentabili* refused the either-he-erred-or-the-gospels-are-false dilemma and left the historical question open.

</details>
