# The New Testament · Lesson 3.2: Sources and criteria

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 3: The historical Jesus · Builds on: [3.1 The quests](03-01-the-quests.md), [1.4 The Synoptic problem](01-04-the-synoptic-problem.md), [1.5 From oral tradition to written gospel](01-05-from-oral-tradition-to-written-gospel.md) · Unlocks: [3.3 What historians broadly agree on](03-03-what-historians-broadly-agree-on.md)

## Why this matters

A historian who sets faith aside has two questions about Jesus. What do sources outside the New Testament say? And which gospel material can be traced back to Jesus himself? The first answer is short: Josephus, Tacitus, and a few lines in Suetonius and Pliny. The second answer is a toolkit, the **[criteria of authenticity](../reference.md#criteria-of-authenticity)**. Every "most scholars hold that Jesus said…" you will ever read rests on them. You need to know what each criterion can prove, and what it cannot.

## The idea

The gospels are testimony from people who believed. That does not make them false. It does mean the historian asks, for each item, whether it is better explained by Jesus or by the later Church.

The criteria are rules of thumb for that question. Four of them do most of the work:

- **Multiple attestation.** Several independent sources report it.
- **Embarrassment.** The Church would not have invented something that made trouble for it.
- **Dissimilarity.** Nothing in the Church's later interests explains it.
- **Rejection and execution.** It helps explain why Jesus was killed.

The key point is that **the criteria are one-way.** Passing a criterion counts in an item's favour. Failing one proves nothing, because the Church also passed on much that Jesus really said.

## Source

Josephus, *Antiquities* 20.9.1 (Whiston; his bracketed glosses omitted). The year is 62. Festus, the Roman governor, has died, and his successor Albinus has not yet arrived:

> But this younger Ananus, who, as we have told you already, took the high priesthood, was a bold man in his temper, and very insolent; he was also of the sect of the Sadducees, who are very rigid in judging offenders, above all the rest of the Jews ... Festus was now dead, and Albinus was but upon the road; so he assembled the sanhedrim of judges, and brought before them the brother of Jesus, who was called Christ, whose name was James, and some others; and when he had formed an accusation against them as breakers of the law, he delivered them to be stoned ... king Agrippa took the high priesthood from him, when he had ruled but three months, and made Jesus, the son of Damneus, high priest.

Read the key phrase in Greek. McGiffert's note to Eusebius, *Church History* 2.23 (NPNF) prints it as *ton adelphon Iēsou tou legomenou Christou, Iakōbos onoma autō*. In this lesson's translation: "the brother of Jesus, the one called Christ; James was his name." Three things follow from it:

1. **The label "called Christ" sounds like an outsider.** *Legomenou* means "called" or "so-called." A Christian interpolator would more likely have written "the Lord" or "the Christ."
2. **The label has a job to do.** The same paragraph has another Jesus, the son of Damneus. "Called Christ" tells the reader which Jesus is meant.
3. **The label seems to assume an earlier mention.** Josephus says only "called Christ" and explains no further. That reads as if he had already introduced this Jesus. His one earlier mention is the disputed *Testimonium Flavianum* (*Ant.* 18.3.3).

## The argument

1. **The James passage is widely accepted as genuine.** It is incidental to the story, which is about Ananus. Its label is neutral, and Origen read it in the third century. Origen's *Against Celsus* 1.47 (ANF) says that Josephus wrote "although not believing in Jesus as the Christ." The [minority case](../reference.md#josephus-on-james) is Richard Carrier's (*Journal of Early Christian Studies*, 2012). He argues that "who was called Christ" began as a scribe's marginal note. On his reading, this James was the brother of Jesus son of Damneus. *In words:* the phrase fits Josephus, and a Christian scribe had little reason to write it.
2. **The Testimonium has three schools.** Some hold it wholly authentic, some wholly interpolated, and most hold it [partly authentic](../reference.md#the-testimonium-flavianum): a Christian hand touched up a genuine note by Josephus. Meier (*A Marginal Jew*, vol. 1, 1991) reconstructs such a core. Which phrases are additions is a separate close-reading exercise, kept for the module's end. *In words:* most scholars think Josephus mentioned Jesus twice, and that one of the mentions was later touched up.
3. **Tacitus is late and hostile.** Writing around 115–120, he reports that "Christus, from whom the name had its origin, suffered the extreme penalty during the reign of Tiberius at the hands of one of our procurators, Pontius Pilatus" (*Annals* 15.44, Church and Brodribb). [`church-history` 1.1](../../church-history/lessons/01-01-the-first-generation-and-its-sources.md) weighs this passage as evidence for Nero. For Jesus it confirms the execution, the governor, and the reign. It does not say where Tacitus learned these facts. The one error is "procurator": in Pilate's day the title was prefect.
4. **Suetonius and Pliny add little.** Suetonius (*Claudius* 25.4) has Claudius expel Jews from Rome after disturbances stirred up by one "Chrestus." That is *probably* a garbled reference to Christ, and Acts 18:2 also mentions the expulsion. Pliny, governing Bithynia around 112, reports that Christians met before dawn and sang a hymn to Christ as to a god (*Letters* 10.96, paraphrased). That is evidence about Christians, not about Jesus.
5. **The criteria.** Meier's primary list, paraphrased:
   - [multiple attestation](../reference.md#multiple-attestation): the item appears in independent sources or in different literary forms;
   - [embarrassment](../reference.md#criterion-of-embarrassment): it caused trouble for the Church;
   - **discontinuity**: it fits neither the Judaism of the time nor the later Church. This is the [double dissimilarity](../reference.md#dissimilarity-and-double-dissimilarity) that Käsemann and Norman Perrin made central;
   - **coherence**: it fits material already established;
   - **rejection and execution**: it helps explain why Jesus ended on a cross.

   Meier treats Aramaic traces, Palestinian setting and vividness as secondary criteria, usable only to support the others. *In words:* each primary criterion names one reason the Church is *less* likely than Jesus to be the source.
6. **The critique.** Every criterion has a known weakness:
   - Dissimilarity, applied strictly, removes Jesus from Judaism and from his own followers. What survives is an oddity, not a first-century Jew. Morna Hooker pressed this point early. Theissen and Winter proposed a replacement, historical plausibility: Jesus should fit his Jewish context and also explain what came after him.
   - Multiple attestation is only as good as your [Synoptic hypothesis](../reference.md#two-source-hypothesis), because the hypothesis decides which sources count as independent.
   - Embarrassment assumes we know what embarrassed whom.

   The deepest objection is in the volume edited by Chris Keith and Anthony Le Donne, *Jesus, Criteria, and the Demise of Authenticity* (2012). The criteria try to peel away the Church's interpretation and reach a bare, uninterpreted fact, but every tradition reached us already interpreted by memory. **Memory approaches** ask a different question: what past best explains the way Jesus was remembered? Examples are Dunn's *Jesus Remembered* (2003) and Allison's *Constructing Jesus* (2010), which looks for patterns that recur across many traditions rather than testing single sayings. *In words:* the critics do not call the gospels unreliable; they call saying-by-saying testing the wrong method.

**Mark the levels.** That the gospels have a **[historical character](../reference.md#historical-character-of-the-gospels)** and faithfully hand on what Jesus did and taught is Church teaching (Dei Verbum 19). It is solemn conciliar teaching in a dogmatic constitution, not a new definition, and it carries the weight of what it restates ([`fundamental-theology` 4.5](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-05-reading-a-documents-weight.md)). The Church affirms it on the grounds of the Church's own tradition. It does **not** rest on Josephus or on the criteria. Everything else in this lesson is a **scholarly hypothesis** with no magisterial standing either way. That includes the partial authenticity of the Testimonium, Carrier's reading of *Ant.* 20.200, every criterion, every verdict a criterion yields, and the memory critique. No Church document endorses a criterion. None condemns one. An item that fails a criterion is not thereby unhistorical, and one that passes is not thereby defined.

## The criteria at a glance

| Criterion | Asks | Passing suggests | Failing does **not** show | Main weakness |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Multiple attestation | Is it in two or more independent sources or forms? | the tradition is early and widespread | that it is late | independence depends on the Synoptic hypothesis |
| Embarrassment | Did it cause the Church trouble? | the Church did not invent it | that it was invented | we guess at what embarrassed whom |
| Dissimilarity | Is it unlike both Judaism and the later Church? | it is distinctive of Jesus | that it is not his | a Jesus cut off from Judaism and from his followers |
| Coherence | Does it fit what is already established? | it belongs with that material | anything | it depends on the other criteria |
| Rejection and execution | Does it help explain the cross? | it is part of why he was killed | anything | the cross can be explained in more than one way |

Read the fourth column first: the criteria are not a machine for sorting sayings into "authentic" and "invented."

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean): Jesus was crucified under Pilate.**
- *Multiple attestation:* Mark, John, Paul (1 Cor 2:2; Gal 3:1), Tacitus, and very probably the core of Josephus's Testimonium. That is Christian and non-Christian witnesses, in narrative and in letters.
- *Embarrassment:* a crucified Messiah was, in Paul's words, "unto the Jews indeed a stumblingblock" (1 Cor 1:23). Deuteronomy's curse on the hanged man had to be answered (Gal 3:13). No one invents a founder who died a slave's death.
- *Rejection and execution:* the crucifixion is the very thing that criterion exists to explain.

**Verdict:** as secure as anything in ancient history. **What the verdict does not reach** is the crucifixion's meaning: that it was "for our sins" is faith's reading of the event, and no criterion can test it.

**Example 2 (hard): "the Son of Man."** The phrase occurs dozens of times in the gospels, nearly always on Jesus's own lips. Outside the gospels, the New Testament uses it as a title for him only once, on Stephen's lips (Acts 7:56).
- *Multiple attestation:* strong. The phrase is in Mark, the Q material, Matthew's and Luke's own material, and John.
- *Dissimilarity from the later Church:* passed. The early Church did not confess Jesus under this title, so it probably did not create the phrase.
- *Dissimilarity from Judaism:* this is where the criterion strains. Daniel 7:13 has "one like a son of man." Geza Vermes (*Jesus the Jew*, 1973) argued that the Aramaic idiom could simply mean "I" or "a man." So the phrase is *not* dissimilar from Judaism, and the double criterion would throw out something the other criteria say is solid.
- *What even the passing criteria leave open:* which sayings are authentic, and what Jesus meant. Scholars divide the sayings into three groups: Jesus's present ministry, his suffering, and a future coming. Bultmann accepted some of the future sayings, and held that in them Jesus meant someone other than himself.

**Verdict:** most scholars conclude that Jesus used the expression. The criteria cannot settle what he meant by it. Here the memory critique bites: a test for single sayings cannot reach a pattern of speech.

## Watch out

- **"Failed dissimilarity, so inauthentic."** The criteria are one-way. A saying Jesus shared with Judaism, or one his Church repeated, can still be his.
- **"The Church teaches the criteria," or "the criteria disprove Church teaching."** Both are level errors. The criteria are scholarly tools with no magisterial standing. Dei Verbum 19 affirms the gospels' historical character without endorsing any method's verdicts, and no verdict of the criteria can overturn it: the two claims are on different levels.
- **"Josephus proves Jesus existed, which is why the Church believes it."** This reverses the order. The external sources are evidence historians use. The Church's affirmation does not rest on them.
- **Counting sources without checking independence.** Matthew copying Mark gives one witness, not two, on Markan priority.

## One-liner

> The criteria are one-way tests for what the Church is unlikely to have invented. They count in an item's favour and never against it. Their results are hypotheses, and the gospels' historical character does not rest on them.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢)** *(Exegetical)* Each argument below uses one criterion of authenticity. Name the criterion, then name one thing the argument does **not** establish. **One line per item.**

1. Mark 3:21 has Jesus's own people go out to seize him because they think he is out of his mind. Matthew and Luke both leave the verse out. So the report is early and not invented.
2. Jesus's ban on divorce appears in Mark 10:11–12, in a Q saying (Matt 5:32 // Luke 16:18), and in 1 Cor 7:10–11, where Paul says the command is "the Lord's," not his own.
3. The parables of the lost sheep and the lost son (Luke 15) fit his well-established habit of eating with sinners, so they are probably his.
4. A Jesus who only taught gentle moral maxims would never have been crucified by Rome, so his sayings about a coming kingdom that would overturn present rulers probably go back to him.

**P2 (🟡)** *(Exegetical)* An **invented** parish-bulletin paragraph:

> "Catholics need not fear the historians. The Church teaches that the gospels are historical because Josephus and Tacitus prove Jesus lived and died under Pilate. And the Church's approved criteria of authenticity show which sayings are really his: whatever fails the test of dissimilarity was added later, and whatever passes is guaranteed by the Church."

Name **three** errors. For each, say what is wrong and give the correct level of the claim involved. **Two sentences per error.**

**P3 (🔴)** Luke 7:33–34 (a Q saying; Matthew 11:18–19 is its parallel) and Luke 15:1–2 (Luke's own material), Douay–Rheims:

> For John the Baptist came neither eating bread nor drinking wine. And you say: He hath a devil. The Son of man is come eating and drinking. And you say: Behold a man that is a glutton and a drinker of wine, a friend of publicans and sinners.
>
> Now the publicans and sinners drew near unto him to hear him. And the Pharisees and the scribes murmured, saying: This man receiveth sinners and eateth with them.

Mark 2:15–17 also has Jesus at table with publicans and sinners.

(a) *(Exegetical)* Which **two** criteria does the charge "friend of publicans and sinners" pass? Name the words each one turns on. Then say why the number of *independent* witnesses depends on the Synoptic hypothesis you hold, counting them on the Two-Source hypothesis. **80 words or fewer.**

(b) *(Evaluative)* Do the criteria establish that Jesus ate with tax collectors and sinners, or only that opponents said he did? Any verdict. **150 words or fewer.**

<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict:** (1) **Embarrassment.** Matthew's and Luke's omission shows the verse caused trouble. It does not establish what the family actually thought, nor that the Beelzebul setting Mark gives it is original. (2) **Multiple attestation**, in three sources, including the earliest (Paul, in the 50s) and two literary forms (letter and gospel saying). It does not establish the exact wording. Mark's and Luke's forms differ, and Matthew adds an exception clause (5:32). (3) **Coherence.** It establishes nothing independently: it only borrows the strength of the table-fellowship finding, and a parable could fit that finding and still be the Church's. (4) **Rejection and execution.** It does not establish *which* kingdom sayings are authentic, or that this was the charge Rome actually acted on.

**Wrong turns:** calling (1) dissimilarity. The argument turns on the later evangelists' discomfort, not on any contrast with Judaism. Calling (3) multiple attestation. Treating any "does not establish" answer as a claim that the item is false.

**Model answer:** (1) Embarrassment; it does not show what the family actually thought. (2) Multiple attestation; it does not show the original wording or whether the exception clause is original. (3) Coherence; it adds nothing of its own beyond the finding it leans on. (4) Rejection and execution; it does not pick out which kingdom sayings are Jesus's.

**P2** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict:** any three of the following.
- **The order of grounds is reversed.** The historical character of the gospels is Church teaching (Dei Verbum 19, solemn conciliar teaching). The Church affirms it from its own tradition, not because pagan historians confirm it. Josephus and Tacitus are evidence historians use, at the level of history, not doctrine.
- **"Approved criteria."** No Church document approves the criteria. They are scholarly tools, hypotheses with no magisterial standing.
- **"Fails dissimilarity, so added later."** The criteria are one-way. Failing shows nothing, since Jesus shared much with Judaism and the Church preserved his words.
- **"Guaranteed by the Church."** A criterion's result is a probable historical judgment, not a teaching. The Church defines no list of authentic sayings.
- *(Credit)* "Prove Jesus lived and died under Pilate": Tacitus confirms the execution, but "prove" overstates what any single ancient source does.

**Wrong turns:** "correcting" the bulletin by saying the historical character of the gospels is only a hypothesis. That understates Dei Verbum 19. Naming the errors without giving levels.

**Model answer:** (1) The bulletin derives Church teaching from historians. Dei Verbum 19's affirmation of the gospels' historical character is solemn conciliar teaching grounded in tradition, while Josephus and Tacitus are historical evidence and carry no doctrinal weight. (2) No Church document approves the criteria. They are scholarly hypotheses and bind no one. (3) Failing dissimilarity does not make a saying late. The criteria count only in favour, and a passing verdict is a historian's probable judgment, which the Church does not guarantee.

**P3** *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b))*

**Must hit, strict (a):**
- **Embarrassment.** "glutton and a drinker of wine, a friend of publicans and sinners" is an insult, and the Church had no motive to invent it.
- **Multiple attestation.** The same practice appears in Mark 2:16, in Q (Luke 7:34 // Matt 11:19), and in L (Luke 15:2), in two forms: a saying and a narrative scene.
- **The count depends on the hypothesis.** On the Two-Source hypothesis there are three independent witnesses: Mark, Q and L. On Farrer, Luke took the Q saying from Matthew, so the count falls. On Griesbach, Mark depends on the others.

**Must hit, any verdict (b):**
- **Separate the charge from the practice.** Embarrassment secures that the charge was made.
- **Say what bridges the gap, or fails to.** The practice needs further support. The strongest support is that the same practice appears across independent strands (Mark's narrative, Luke's scene) and that coherence ties it to the parables of Luke 15.
- **Name the limit.** Opponents' charges can distort, and "sinners" can mean different things.

**Wrong turns:** counting Matthew 11:19 and Luke 7:34 as two witnesses. Treating the embarrassment of the *charge* as proof of every meal scene.

**Model answer (a):** It passes embarrassment ("glutton," "wine drinker," "friend of publicans": no one invents that about his Lord) and multiple attestation, across Mark 2:16, Q, and Luke 15:2, in both saying and narrative. On the Two-Source hypothesis these are three independent witnesses. On Farrer, Luke's saying comes from Matthew, so there are fewer. Independence is set by the hypothesis, not by the count of verses.

**Model answer (b), one of several:** The criteria secure the charge directly. An insult that the evangelists preserve against their own interest was almost certainly made. The practice is one inference further. But the charge would have had no force unless Jesus did something that invited it. And the practice itself, not just the charge, appears in independent strands: Mark's meal at Levi's house and Luke's scene of murmuring Pharisees. It also coheres with parables whose whole point is welcoming the lost. So the practice is highly probable. What the criteria do not settle is who the "sinners" were, or whether any given meal scene happened as told.

## Flashback

**From Lesson [2.5](02-05-the-theology-of-john.md) (The theology of John):** *(Exegetical.)* John 6, Douay–Rheims. The verse numbers are modern; the Douay runs one ahead from 6:51b, and its numbers are in brackets.

> 6:47 Amen, amen, I say unto you: He that believeth in me hath everlasting life. ...
> 6:53 [54] ... except you eat the flesh of the Son of man and drink his blood, you shall not have life in you. 54 [55] He that eateth my flesh and drinketh my blood hath everlasting life ...
> 6:63 [64] It is the spirit that quickeneth: the flesh profiteth nothing.

At 6:54, "eateth" renders *trōgō*, a blunt verb for chewing, where the discourse had used the ordinary *phagein*. There are three readings, paraphrased here. **Bultmann:** 6:51c–58 was added by a later church editor with sacramental interests; the evangelist did not write it. **Brown:** the discourse moves from Jesus's teaching as bread, received by believing, to the eucharistic bread, received by eating, and both halves belong to the gospel as we have it. **Metaphorical:** "eating" is a figure for believing, as 6:63 shows. (a) What do Bultmann and Brown agree on that the metaphorical reading denies, and what is the crux between them? **One sentence each.** (b) How does a eucharistic reader answer the appeal to 6:63, using only the wording above? **Two sentences.**

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):** **Agreement:** 6:51c–58 speaks of the Eucharist. Bultmann gives it to a church editor *because* it is sacramental. The metaphorical reading denies that it does. **Crux:** the question is not whether the passage is eucharistic, nor whether it now stands in the gospel. It is whether its sacramental realism belongs to the gospel's own theology, as a second movement of one discourse (Brown), or is foreign to the evangelist and added by another hand (Bultmann). The evidence is whether the shift from believing to eating, and from *phagein* to *trōgō*, reads as a development or a break.

**Must hit, strict (b):** 6:63 says "*the* flesh", not "*my* flesh" as in 6:54, so a eucharistic reader takes it as spirit set against merely human understanding, not as a retraction of 6:54. And the language grows more physical just where a figure for believing would soften, with *trōgō* and "drink his blood". Level: Trent (Session XXI, ch. 1) left "the various interpretations" of this chapter open. All three readings are exegetical hypotheses.

**Wrong turns:** saying Bultmann denies the passage is eucharistic. Saying the Church has defined that John 6:51–58 is about the Eucharist, or that Trent condemned the metaphorical reading. Eucharistic doctrine does not settle this passage's exegesis. Hearing "the flesh profiteth nothing" as a verdict on Christ's flesh without noticing the missing "my".

**Model answer:** (a) Both hold that 6:51c–58 is eucharistic, which the metaphorical reading denies. They divide on whether that realism is John's own theology, a second movement of one discourse (Brown), or a foreign addition by a later editor (Bultmann). (b) Verse 63 contrasts spirit with "the flesh", meaning unaided human understanding, and not with "my flesh", so it does not take back 6:54. Meanwhile the verbs grow more physical, not less, which is the opposite of what a figure for believing would need.

</details>
