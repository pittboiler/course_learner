# The New Testament · Lesson 2.2: Matthew

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 2: The four gospels · Builds on: [1.4 The Synoptic problem](01-04-the-synoptic-problem.md), [2.1 Mark](02-01-mark.md) · Unlocks: [2.3 Luke](02-03-luke.md), [3.4 The passion and resurrection narratives](03-04-the-passion-and-resurrection-narratives.md)

## Why this matters

For most of Christian history Matthew was *the* gospel: first in the canon, most quoted by the Fathers, source of the Sermon on the Mount, the Lord's Prayer as the Church prays it, and the only gospel to put the word "church" in Jesus's mouth. Read as an author, Matthew is also the gospel most worried about a question Mark barely raises: if Jesus is Israel's Messiah, what becomes of Israel's Law and Israel's scriptures? His answer is one word, used over and over: **fulfil**.

## The idea

Matthew describes his ideal disciple in a line only he records: a scribe trained for the kingdom, "who bringeth forth out of his treasure new things and old" (13:52). On the majority view ([Markan priority](../reference.md#markan-priority)), he takes over the great majority of Mark's material. He adds sayings shared with Luke, often assigned to a lost source, Q, on the [two-source hypothesis](../reference.md#two-source-hypothesis), and material only he has. He then arranges all of it around three signatures:

1. **Scripture fulfilled.** The narrator keeps stopping the story to say: *this happened so that the prophet's word would be filled full.*
2. **The Law fulfilled.** Jesus does not cancel the Torah; he demands a righteousness that goes beyond the scribes'.
3. **A church.** Jesus founds an *ekklēsia*, gives it rules, and sends it, at the very end, to all nations.

The three meet in one picture: Jesus as the goal of Israel's story, written for a Jewish-Christian community arguing with the synagogue down the street about who reads Moses rightly.

## Source

Matthew 5:17–20, Douay–Rheims (Challoner), the programme statement of the Sermon on the Mount:

> Do not think that I am come to destroy the law, or the prophets. I am not come to destroy, but to fulfil. For amen I say unto you, till heaven and earth pass, one jot, or one tittle shall not pass of the law, till all be fulfilled. He therefore that shall break one of these least commandments, and shall so teach men shall be called the least in the kingdom of heaven. But he that shall do and teach, he shall be called great in the kingdom of heaven. For I tell you, that unless your justice abound more than that of the scribes and Pharisees, you shall not enter into the kingdom of heaven.

Two Greek words decide the reading. "Destroy" is *katalysai*, to dismantle or annul. "Fulfil" is *plērōsai*, to fill full, bring to completion. The lesson's translation of 5:17b: *I did not come to annul them but to bring them to their full measure.* And the Douay's "justice" in 5:20 is *dikaiosynē*, "righteousness": a word Matthew uses seven times, Mark never and Luke's gospel once (1:75).

## The argument

1. **The book is built around five discourses.** Five blocks of teaching (chapters 5–7, 10, 13, 18, 24–25) each close with the same Greek formula, *kai egeneto hote etelesen ho Iēsous*, "and it came to pass when Jesus had finished" (7:28; 11:1; 13:53; 19:1; 26:1). Only the object varies ("these words", "commanding his twelve disciples", "these parables"). B. W. Bacon proposed that the five mirror the five books of Moses. Jack Dean Kingsbury replied that the real hinges are the two "From that time Jesus began" verses (4:17, 16:21), which give a three-part story. *In words:* the [five discourses](../reference.md#the-five-discourses) are certain; the Pentateuch analogy is a hypothesis.

2. **The narrator's formula quotations.** Ten times the narrator himself (not a character) says an event happened "that it might be fulfilled which was spoken by the prophet": 1:22; 2:15; 2:17; 2:23; 4:14; 8:17; 12:17; 13:35; 21:4; 27:9. Counts of eleven or more add 2:5–6 or 13:14, where the wording differs. These [fulfilment quotations](../reference.md#fulfilment-quotations) are mostly **typological**, not predictive. The text quoted often did not forecast anything in its own setting. Matthew reads Jesus's life as the place where Israel's pattern repeats and reaches its goal. *In words:* "fulfil" means *fill up*, not only *come true*.

3. **The Law: the Source, then [the antitheses](../reference.md#the-antitheses).** 5:17–20 promises that not a stroke of the Law passes away. Six antitheses follow ("You have heard that it was said... But I say to you", 5:21–48): on anger, lust, divorce, oaths, retaliation and love of enemies. Some plainly intensify: whoever is never angry will never kill. Others are harder. If no one swears, the Law's rule about keeping oaths is never broken, but it no longer has anything to do. John P. Meier (*Law and History in Matthew's Gospel*) argued that some antitheses do revoke the letter of the Law. On his reading, "till all be fulfilled" (5:18) points to the death and resurrection, after which the Law is read through Jesus's authority. Others read every antithesis as radicalising the commandment, not annulling it. *In words:* everyone agrees Jesus claims the right to say what the Law finally means; they disagree whether that ever sets its letter aside.

4. **The *ekklēsia* and Peter.** [*Ekklēsia*](../reference.md#ekklesia-in-matthew), "assembly", is the Septuagint's word for Israel gathered (the *qahal*). No other gospel uses it; Matthew uses it in two places. In 16:18 ("I will build my church") it is Jesus's people as a whole. In 18:17 ("tell the church") it is the local congregation settling a dispute. Peter's role grows to match. Only Matthew has him walk on the water and sink ("O thou of little faith", 14:28–31). Only Matthew has the Temple-tax coin paid "for me and thee" (17:24–27; Douay 17:23–26). Only Matthew has the blessing of 16:17–19. Matthew's Peter is spokesman, first of the Twelve, and a man of little faith at once. Whether 16:17–19 goes back to Jesus is Boss 2's question; the doctrine of primacy belongs to [`ecclesiology-and-mariology`](../../ecclesiology-and-mariology/syllabus.md).

5. **[A community in conflict](../reference.md#matthews-community-and-the-synagogue).** Jesus teaches in "*their* synagogues" (4:23; 9:35; 13:54); disciples will be scourged "in their synagogues" (10:17). Chapter 23 is a sustained polemic against scribes and Pharisees, yet it opens by saying their teaching from "the chair of Moses" is to be kept (23:2–3). The mission runs from "go ye not into the way of the Gentiles" (10:5) to "teach ye all nations" (28:19). Graham Stanton (*A Gospel for a New People*) read the community as recently separated from the synagogue. Anthony Saldarini (*Matthew's Christian-Jewish Community*) read it as a dissenting group still inside Judaism. David Sim (*The Gospel of Matthew and Christian Judaism*) argued it remained Law-observant. Matthew 27:25 belongs to [3.4](03-04-the-passion-and-resurrection-narratives.md).

6. **Author, date, place.** The gospel is anonymous. Papias, as quoted by Eusebius, says: "Matthew wrote the oracles in the Hebrew language, and every one interpreted them as he was able" (*Church History* III.39.16, NPNF). Irenaeus repeats the Hebrew-gospel tradition (*Against Heresies* III.1.1). Most critical scholars, Raymond Brown among them, doubt that the apostle wrote our Greek gospel, which depends on Greek Mark; "oracles" may not even mean a gospel. They date it c. 80–90, reading 22:7 (the king who "burnt their city") as looking back on 70. A minority, John A. T. Robinson most famously, date it before 70 and read 22:7 as stock language for punishment. Antioch is a common guess for the place. A majority is not a proof.

**Mark the levels.** That Matthew is canonical and inspired is **defined dogma**, rung 1 of [the ladder](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-04-the-ladder-of-doctrinal-authority.md) ([Trent's canon](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/03-03-the-canon-as-defined.md)). Dei Verbum 18–19 teaches that the four gospels are of apostolic origin, handed on by "the Apostles... and apostolic men", and that they are [historical in character](../reference.md#historical-character-of-the-gospels). The same paragraph expects the evangelists to explain things with their churches' situation in view, which is exactly what points 3–5 describe. Its weight is [1.1](01-01-reading-the-new-testament-in-the-church.md)'s question. The apostle's personal authorship, the date, Antioch, Q, the Pentateuch structure, and whether the antitheses revoke or radicalise: all are **scholarly hypotheses** with no magisterial standing either way. The early Biblical Commission response on Matthew is weighed in 1.1, not here.

## The synopsis

The Lord's Prayer in parallel, Douay–Rheims, with the lesson's notes on the Greek:

| Matthew 6:9–13 | Luke 11:2–4 | Greek behind the difference |
|---|---|---|
| Our Father who art in heaven | Father | Luke has the bare address |
| hallowed be thy name. Thy kingdom come. | hallowed be thy name. Thy kingdom come. | identical |
| Thy will be done on earth as it is in heaven. | (absent) | Matthew only |
| Give us this day our supersubstantial bread. | Give us this day our daily bread. | the **same** word, *epiousion*, rendered two ways by the Vulgate |
| And forgive us our debts, | And forgive us our sins, | *opheilēmata*, "debts" vs *hamartias*, "sins" |
| as we also forgive our debtors. | for we also forgive every one that is indebted to us. | Matthew's verb is past, *aphēkamen*, "as we too have forgiven"; Luke keeps the debt image in *opheilonti* |
| And lead us not into temptation. | And lead us not into temptation. | identical |
| But deliver us from evil. | (absent) | Matthew only |

Luke's "sins... every one that is indebted to us" shows the seam. The debt language was there before Luke explained it. Many scholars note that the Aramaic word for "debt" was also a common word for sin, so Matthew may keep the older idiom. What "debts" means theologically belongs to [`theology-of-debt`](../../theology-of-debt/syllabus.md); the release laws behind it are [`history-of-debt` 1.3](../../history-of-debt/lessons/01-03-release-laws-in-ancient-israel.md). The closing "for thine is the kingdom" is absent from both the Douay and the best manuscripts. A form of it appears in the Didache ([`patristics` 1.2](../../patristics/lessons/01-02-first-clement-and-the-didache.md)).

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean): Matthew 2:15, "Out of Egypt have I called my son."** The source is Hosea 11:1: "Because Israel was a child, and I loved him: and I called my son out of Egypt." In Hosea this is a memory, not a prediction; the son is Israel, and the next verse complains that Israel went after idols. Matthew uses it after the flight into Egypt. The method: (i) find the source, (ii) read it in its own context, (iii) name the link. Here the link is the *pattern*: God's son goes down to Egypt and is called out, first Israel, now Jesus. Jesus relives Israel's story (forty days in the desert, 4:1–11, for Israel's forty years) and gets it right. That is typology, and most of the ten work this way.

**Example 2 (hard): Matthew 27:9, "spoken by Jeremias."** The words about "thirty pieces of silver, the price of him that was prized" are closest to **Zechariah** 11:12–13, where the silver is weighed and cast "to the statuary" (the Douay's own note: the Hebrew word "signifies also a potter"). Jeremiah supplies a potter (Jer 18–19) and a field bought with silver (Jer 32). The common explanation is a composite citation filed under the more famous prophet, much as Mark 1:2–3 blends Malachi with Isaiah under "Isaias". A scribal slip and a deliberate pointer to Jeremiah's field are also proposed. Here the method strains: it tells you *what* Matthew did, not whether that is an error. That question belongs to [inerrancy](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/03-02-inerrancy.md), not to this course.

## Watch out

- **"The Church teaches that the apostle Matthew wrote this gospel in Hebrew before 70."** No. Dei Verbum 18 teaches apostolic *origin* and names "apostolic men" among those who wrote. Personal authorship, language and date are [hypotheses](../reference.md#church-teaching-vs-hypothesis), open to evidence. The reverse error is as bad: "if the apostle didn't write it, it isn't really Scripture" makes canonicity hang on a hypothesis.
- **Fulfil does not mean predict.** Reading every formula quotation as a forecast makes 2:15 and 2:23 (no prophet says "Nazarene") look like blunders. Matthew is filling a pattern, not checking off predictions.
- **The Douay's 27:35 has an eleventh formula** ("that it might be fulfilled which was spoken by the prophet"). It is a Vulgate reading borrowed from John 19:24 and absent from the best Greek manuscripts. Don't count it.
- **"Their synagogues" is not "the Jews".** Matthew's polemic is a family quarrel over Moses; reading chapter 23 as a verdict on the Jewish people is an exegetical error (3.4).

## One-liner

> Matthew is Mark rewritten by a Jewish-Christian scribe: five discourses, ten "that it might be fulfilled"s, a Law not annulled but filled full, and the only gospel with a church in it.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢)** *(Exegetical, both parts)* Matthew 5:43–44 (Douay–Rheims):

> You have heard that it hath been said, Thou shalt love thy neighbour, and hate thy enemy. But I say to you, Love your enemies: do good to them that hate you: and pray for them that persecute and calumniate you.

And Leviticus 19:17–18 (Douay–Rheims; its "friend" renders the Hebrew word the Greek gives as "neighbour"):

> Thou shalt not hate thy brother in thy heart: But reprove him openly, lest thou incur sin through him. Seek not revenge, nor be mindful of the injury of thy citizens. Thou shalt love thy friend as thyself. I am the Lord.

(a) Which half of "what hath been said" is a commandment of the Law, and which is not? (b) Does a disciple who obeys 5:44 break Leviticus 19:18? Use the lesson's translation of *plērōsai* to say what kind of antithesis this is. **Two sentences per part.**

**P2 (🟡)** Matthew 5:3, 6 and Luke 6:20–21 (Douay–Rheims):

| Matthew | Luke |
|---|---|
| Blessed are the poor in spirit: for theirs is the kingdom of heaven. | Blessed are ye poor: for yours is the kingdom of God. |
| Blessed are they that hunger and thirst after justice: for they shall have their fill. | Blessed are ye that hunger now: for you shall be filled. |

(a) *(Exegetical)* List every difference between the columns: person, qualifiers, and the name of the kingdom. Say which difference uses a word the lesson showed is characteristic of Matthew. (b) *(Evaluative)* On the Two-Source hypothesis both evangelists edit Q; on the Farrer hypothesis Luke edits Matthew. In **100 words or fewer**, any verdict, say which direction explains these differences more economically. Name the difference that decides it for you and the best point against your view.

**P3 (🔴)** *(Exegetical)* An **invented** study-bible note on Matthew 18:17:

> "'Tell the church': Matthew alone records this word because he alone, as the tax-collector apostle, heard it. Since the Church teaches that the gospels are historical, Catholics must hold that he wrote this gospel himself, in Hebrew, before the Temple fell; scholars who date it to the 80s contradict Dei Verbum 19."

(a) List each claim in the note and mark it Church teaching (with the document) or scholarly hypothesis. (b) Say what Dei Verbum 18–19 actually commits a Catholic to about this gospel's author and history, and why a date in the 80s does not contradict it. **150 words or fewer in total.**

<details>
<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical (a) · Exegetical (b))*

**Must hit, strict (a):** "Love thy neighbour" is Leviticus 19:18. "Hate thy enemy" is in no commandment of the Law. The antithesis sets Jesus against a current inference or reading, not against a written precept; Leviticus 19:17 even forbids hating a brother.
**Must hit, strict (b):** No: whoever loves his enemy still loves his neighbour, so Leviticus 19:18 is kept, and more than kept. This is an antithesis that **fills the commandment to its full measure** (*plērōsai*) by removing the limit on who counts, not one that annuls (*katalysai*) it.
**Wrong turns:** saying Jesus contradicts a Torah command to hate enemies (there is none). Classing this antithesis with oaths as one that might leave a precept idle. Here the precept stays fully in force.
**Model answer:** (a) "Thou shalt love thy neighbour" is Leviticus 19:18, but "hate thy enemy" is written nowhere in the Law, which in 19:17 forbids hating a brother. The second half is a reading of the commandment, not the commandment. (b) No: loving enemies includes loving neighbours, so the commandment is kept and its reach extended. It is an antithesis that fills the Law full rather than annulling it.

**P2** *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b))*

**Must hit, strict (a):** third person in Matthew vs second person ("ye", "yours") in Luke; Matthew adds "in spirit"; Matthew adds "and thirst after justice"; Luke adds "now"; "kingdom of heaven" vs "kingdom of God"; "have their fill" vs "be filled" is wording only (credit either way). The characteristic word is **"justice"**, *dikaiosynē*: seven times in Matthew, once in Luke's gospel, never in Mark.
**Must hit, any verdict (b):** state the direction clearly. Name the decisive difference and explain it on the chosen hypothesis. Give one real point against. For **Two-Source / Matthew edits**: "in spirit" and "justice" are Matthean additions, since *dikaiosynē* is his redactional word; Luke's plain "poor" and "hunger" are the harder, earlier form. Against: Luke also has motives, since reversal of rich and poor is his theme and he adds woes (6:24–26). For **Farrer / Luke edits Matthew**: Luke drops "in spirit" and "justice" to fit his theme of the literal poor, and adds "now" and the woes. Against: it requires Luke to strip out wording he would have had no reason to dislike, and Matthew's vocabulary is otherwise the clearest mark of Matthean editing. The verdict does not matter; both hypotheses are hypotheses.
**Wrong turns:** treating "justice" as settling the direction on its own. It shows Matthew's hand but cannot tell whether Luke saw it. Calling either hypothesis Church teaching.
**Model answer (b), one of several:** Matthew edited the source. "Justice" is his word (seven uses to Luke's one), so "hunger and thirst after justice" looks like his addition, and "in spirit" is the same move applied to "poor". The strongest point against is that Luke has his own reason to prefer the literal poor, and his woes show it, so the plain form could be his editing too.

**P3** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** "The gospels are historical" is **Church teaching** (Dei Verbum 19). Apostolic personal authorship, the "tax-collector heard it" inference, a Hebrew original and a date before 70 are **hypotheses** (or tradition, Papias and Irenaeus, weighed as historical evidence). "Scholars who date it to the 80s contradict Dei Verbum 19" is **false**. Credit noting that Dei Verbum 18 (apostolic origin) is also teaching.
**Must hit, strict (b):** Dei Verbum 18 teaches **apostolic origin**, written by apostles "and apostolic men", not personal authorship by Matthew. Dei Verbum 19 teaches the gospels faithfully hand on what Jesus did and taught, while the authors selected, synthesised and explained with their churches' situation in view. A date is a historical question the documents do not settle, so a date in the 80s is compatible with both.
**Wrong turns:** overcorrecting to "the Church teaches nothing about Matthew", which forgets canonicity (dogma) and Dei Verbum 18–19. Arguing about whether *ekklēsia* in 18:17 is authentic, which the note does not raise and which is not asked.
**Model answer:** (a) Teaching: the gospels' historical character (DV 19). Hypotheses: that the apostle wrote it personally, that he heard 18:17, that it was in Hebrew, that it predates 70, and that an 80s date contradicts DV 19 (a false claim). (b) DV 18 affirms that the gospels are of apostolic origin, set down by apostles and "apostolic men", which does not require the apostle's own pen. DV 19 affirms their faithful witness to Jesus while allowing the evangelists to select and shape material for their churches. Neither document fixes a date, so a date in the 80s leaves both untouched.

## Flashback

**From Lesson [1.5](01-05-from-oral-tradition-to-written-gospel.md) (From oral tradition to written gospel):** *(Exegetical (a), (c) · Evaluative (b).)* Acts, from Paul's farewell to the Ephesian elders and from the book's last verses (Douay-Rheims):

> **20:25** And now behold, I know that all you, among whom I have gone preaching the kingdom of God, shall see my face no more.
>
> **28:30–31** And he remained two whole years in his own hired lodging: and he received all that came in to him, preaching the kingdom of God and teaching the things which concern the Lord Jesus Christ, with all confidence, without prohibition.

"Remained" is *enemeinen*, an aorist: a finished stay, "he stayed on a whole two years". Two positions, paraphrased:

- **Robinson** (*Redating the New Testament*): Acts stops with Paul alive in Rome, about 62, and never mentions his death or the Temple's fall. The simplest explanation is that neither had happened when it was written, and Luke's gospel, the "former treatise" of Acts 1:1, is earlier still.
- **The majority:** Luke-Acts dates from c. 80–90. The book stops where its story is complete, with the word preached in Rome, not where its author's knowledge ran out.

(a) Name the single premise about the ending that Robinson needs and the majority must deny. (b) Which side do "two whole years" and 20:25 each help, and does either decide the date? Any verdict, **80 words or fewer**. (c) What is the level of the date of Luke-Acts? **Two sentences each for (a) and (c).**

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):** Robinson needs the premise that **where Acts stops marks the limit of what its author knew**: an author who knew of Paul's death, or of the year 70, would have said so. The majority must deny it, holding that the ending is set by the book's **purpose** (the gospel reaching Rome), so silence about later events is not evidence of ignorance.
**Must hit, any verdict (b):** "Two whole years", told as a completed stay, shows the author writing **after** the two years ended, so he knows more than he narrates. That weakens Robinson's premise, though it only moves the date past c. 62. 20:25 reads like **foreshadowing** of an end the author knew, but it can equally be Paul's own foreboding, and it says nothing of how he died. A verdict on whether either decides the date.
**Must hit, strict (c):** a **scholarly hypothesis** on both sides, with no magisterial standing; a majority is not a proof. Dei Verbum 19 teaches the gospels' historical character and names no date, so neither dating touches it.
**Wrong turns:** making the crux "whether Luke used Mark", which neither position here turns on. Reading "without prohibition" as a report that Paul was released: the text does not say. Saying the early date is the more Catholic one because it protects historicity; that confuses date with reliability, as 1.5 warned.
**Model answer:** (a) Robinson needs the premise that Acts ends where its author's knowledge ended, so that he would have told of Paul's death or the Temple's fall had he known them. The majority denies this: the ending is chosen because the story's goal, the word preached in Rome, has been reached. (b) *One of several:* "Two whole years", told as a finished stay, means the author writes after it ended, so he knows more than he tells, which undercuts Robinson's premise. 20:25 may foreshadow a death Luke knew of, or may be Paul's own foreboding. Both are modest points for the majority; neither settles the date. (c) Both dates are scholarly hypotheses with no magisterial standing. Dei Verbum 19 names no date, so Luke-Acts can be early or late without touching its historical character.

</details>
