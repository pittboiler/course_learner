# The New Testament · Lesson 6.1: The disputed Pauline letters and the pastorals

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 6: The other letters, Revelation, and the canon · Builds on: [4.2 Paul's life and letters](04-02-pauls-life-and-letters.md), [`fundamental-theology` 3.3](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/03-03-the-canon-as-defined.md) · Unlocks: [6.2 Hebrews](06-02-hebrews.md), [6.4 Peter, Jude, and the letters of John](06-04-peter-jude-and-the-letters-of-john.md), [6.6 The formation of the canon](06-06-the-formation-of-the-canon.md)

## Why this matters

Six letters carry Paul's name and a question mark. They hold some of the New Testament's most-used texts: Christ as "firstborn of every creature" (Col 1:15), marriage as a "great sacrament" (Eph 5:32), the qualifications of a bishop (1 Tim 3). Trent settled that they are Scripture. The open questions are who held the pen, what evidence can say, and what "a disciple, after Paul's death" would mean.

## The idea

[4.2](04-02-pauls-life-and-letters.md) sorted the thirteen letters into seven [undisputed and six disputed](../reference.md#undisputed-and-disputed-letters). This lesson opens the six.

Think of a disputed painting attributed to a master. Experts look at three things: the **brushwork** (vocabulary and style), the **ideas** (does the theology match his known work?) and the **provenance** (does the setting fit his known life?). No single test settles it. What you get is a cumulative case, strong for some canvases and weak for others.

Two facts keep the debate honest. The six are not one problem: The case against the pastorals is far stronger than the case against 2 Thessalonians. And the ancient world knew both honest school-writing and outright forgery, and the early Church condemned forgery when it caught it. So "pseudonymous" is a hypothesis with a price, not a neutral label.

## Source

2 Thessalonians 2:1–3 and 3:17 (Douay–Rheims):

> And we beseech you, brethren, by the coming of our Lord Jesus Christ and of our gathering together unto him: That you be not easily moved from your sense nor be terrified, neither by spirit nor by word nor by epistle, as sent from us, as if the day of the Lord were at hand. Let no man deceive you by any means: for unless there come a revolt first, and the man of sin be revealed, the son of perdition ... The salutation of Paul with my own hand: which is the sign in every epistle. So I write.

"As sent from us" renders *hōs di' hēmōn*, "as if through us". The letter knows of a letter falsely circulating under Paul's name, then closes with a signature it calls the mark of genuineness. Whoever wrote it, forged apostolic letters were a live worry.

## The argument

1. **The three tests.** *Vocabulary and style*: the words a writer favours and the way he builds sentences. *Theology*: whether the ideas fit the undisputed seven. *Setting*: whether the situation fits Paul's known life and his churches. Each has a standard reply: Paul used secretaries (Rom 16:22 names Tertius); his thought developed; new occasions call for new words.
2. **2 Thessalonians.** In 1 Thessalonians 5:2 the day of the Lord comes "as a thief in the night". Here signs must come first: a revolt, then "the man of sin" (2:3). The letter also copies 1 Thessalonians' structure closely, down to a second thanksgiving (2 Thess 2:13, like 1 Thess 2:13). William Wrede (1903) made that literary dependence the core of the case. The reply: apocalyptic writing often holds suddenness and warning signs together (Mark 13 does both), and a letter written weeks after the first would naturally echo it. Abraham Malherbe's Anchor commentary is one defence of Pauline authorship. On Brown's survey, critical opinion is roughly divided.
3. **Colossians: the cosmic Christ.** The hymn of 1:15–20 makes Christ "the image of the invisible God, the firstborn of every creature", in whom "were all things created". Then comes 1:18: "he is the head of the body, the church". In 1 Corinthians 12 the body is the local congregation, and the head is one member among the rest (12:21). In Colossians the body is the whole Church, Christ its head. The resurrection has also moved. Romans 6:5 says "we shall be" in the likeness of his resurrection; Colossians 2:12 says "you are risen again". The reply rests on setting. The greetings in Colossians 4 name the same circle as Philemon (Onesimus, Archippus, Epaphras, Aristarchus, Mark, Luke, Demas), and Timothy is co-sender (1:1). Either the letter is Paul's, perhaps drafted by Timothy, or its writer copied Philemon's circle with great care. The hymn's doctrinal use (the Scotist primacy of Christ) belongs to [`christology` 1.3](../../christology/lessons/01-03-why-the-incarnation.md). Colossians 2:14's "handwriting of the decree that was against us" goes to [`theology-of-debt`](../../theology-of-debt/syllabus.md).
4. **Ephesians: the Church as body, universalized.** Ephesians reuses much of Colossians' wording and order (Example 1), develops the body into the one Church of Jew and Gentile (2:14–16), and seats believers already "in the heavenly places" (2:6). The oldest manuscripts (P46, Sinaiticus and Vaticanus in their original hands) lack "at Ephesus" in 1:1. And though Acts gives Paul years in Ephesus (19:10; 20:31), the letter sends no personal greetings. So many read it as a circular letter. Its [household code](../reference.md#household-codes) (5:21–6:9) takes the form of Colossians 3:18–4:1: wives and husbands, children and fathers, slaves and masters. The three pairs are Greek household ethics (Aristotle, *Politics* I). Ephesians opens it with mutual subjection (5:21) and grounds marriage in Christ and the Church. The "great sacrament" of 5:32 is *mega mystērion*, "great mystery". Its sacramental reading is ceded to [`sacraments-and-liturgy`](../../sacraments-and-liturgy/syllabus.md). Brown's survey leans clearly against Pauline authorship.
5. **The pastorals: church order.** 1 Timothy 3 sets qualifications for the *episkopos* ("overseer", Douay "bishop") and the *diakonoi* ("servants, ministers", "deacons"); 1 Timothy 5:17 honours *presbyteroi* ("elders", Douay "priests") who rule well. In Titus 1:5–7 the two titles seem to name [the same men](../reference.md#episkopos-and-presbyteros) (P2). So do Acts 20:17, 28 and 1 Clement ([`patristics` 1.2](../../patristics/lessons/01-02-first-clement-and-the-didache.md)). Jerome drew the conclusion: "the apostle clearly teaches that presbyters are the same as bishops" (Letter 146, Fremantle, NPNF). Against authenticity, this is settled office with fixed qualifications, far from the charismatic Corinth of 1 Corinthians 12–14. Against a very late date, it is not yet Ignatius's one bishop per city, around 110 ([`patristics` 1.3](../../patristics/lessons/01-03-ignatius-of-antioch.md)).
6. **The pastorals: vocabulary and setting.** P. N. Harrison's *The Problem of the Pastoral Epistles* (1921) counted words absent from the ten other letters and argued that they align with second-century Christian writers. Notable examples: *eusebeia* ("godliness"), never in the undisputed seven; "sound doctrine" (Titus 1:9); the formula "a faithful saying" (1 Tim 3:1); "faith" as a body of teaching held. Critics reply that the letters are too short for statistics and that subject drives vocabulary. The setting has Paul in Crete (Titus 1:5) and wintering at Nicopolis (Titus 3:12), movements Acts never mentions. Defenders place these in a release after Acts 28 and a second Roman imprisonment. Eusebius reports it only as what "is said" (*Church History* 2.22). Luke Timothy Johnson's Anchor commentary on 1–2 Timothy argues for authenticity, pointing out that the three letters differ from one another and should not be judged as a block. On Brown's survey a large majority of critical scholars judges the pastorals pseudonymous. A majority is not a proof.
7. **[Pseudonymity](../reference.md#pseudonymity) in antiquity.** Philosophical schools circulated works under a founder's name. Some scholars, such as David Meade (*Pseudonymity and Canon*, 1986), read New Testament pseudepigraphy as a school presenting its founder's authoritative teaching to a new day: a claim to tradition, not a lie. Bart Ehrman (*Forged*, 2011) answers that ancient writers condemned forgery as deception, and that the letters' personal touches (2 Tim 4:13, the cloak left at Troas) look designed to be believed. The "noble lie" debate is over which it was. The early Church's own record is severe. Tertullian tells of the Asian presbyter who wrote the *Acts of Paul*: "after being convicted, and confessing that he had done it from love of Paul, was removed from his office" (*On Baptism* 17, ANF). The Muratorian fragment rejects letters "to the Laodiceans, and another to the Alexandrians, forged under the name of Paul" (ANF). Yet the same fragment receives all four letters to individuals, the pastorals included. *In words:* the Church excluded letters it judged forged; it received these six as Paul's. Whether that reception settles authorship is the open question.

**Mark the levels.** That all thirteen letters are **inspired and canonical** is **defined dogma, rung 1** of [the ladder](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-04-the-ladder-of-doctrinal-authority.md): Trent's decree lists "fourteen epistles of Paul the apostle" ([`fundamental-theology` 3.3](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/03-03-the-canon-as-defined.md)). Who held the pen of any disputed letter is a **scholarly hypothesis**, with no magisterial standing either way. Whether Trent's titles bind on human authorship is **freely disputed** among Catholics. Brown, among others, reads the decree as defining books, not authors. The Biblical Commission's 1913 response affirming Paul's authorship of the pastorals is one of [the early responses](01-01-reading-the-new-testament-in-the-church.md) whose force the 1955 clarification put in question. It is not a definition, and it has not been formally revoked. Trent's later canons on orders (Session XXIII, canons 6–7: a hierarchy of bishops, priests and ministers, bishops superior to priests) are dogma about the Church's order, not a ruling on how Titus 1 uses its words. Pseudonymity and inspiration are taken up in [6.6](06-06-the-formation-of-the-canon.md). See [Church teaching vs hypothesis](../reference.md#church-teaching-vs-hypothesis).

## The case, letter by letter

| Letter | Strongest case against Paul | Strongest reply | Critical opinion (Brown's survey) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2 Thessalonians | signs before the day (2:3) vs "thief in the night"; copies 1 Thess | apocalyptic holds both; written soon after | roughly divided |
| Colossians | cosmic head of a universal body; "you are risen" (2:12) | Philemon's circle; Timothy as co-writer | roughly divided |
| Ephesians | reuses Colossians; no "at Ephesus", no greetings | circular letter by Paul or a secretary | leans clearly against |
| 1–2 Timothy, Titus | office, vocabulary, journeys unknown to Acts | secretary; second imprisonment; short texts | heavily against |

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean): the Tychicus notice.** Lay the two side by side (Douay–Rheims).

| Colossians 4:7–8 | Ephesians 6:21–22 |
|---|---|
| All the things that concern me, Tychicus, our dearest brother and faithful minister and fellow servant in the Lord, will make known to you. | But that you also may know the things that concern me and what I am doing, Tychicus, my dearest brother and faithful minister in the Lord, will make known to you all things: |
| Whom I have sent to you for this same purpose, that he may know the things that concern you and comfort your hearts | Whom I have sent to you for this same purpose: that you may know the things concerning us, and that he may comfort your hearts. |

The Greek is nearly identical, so something was copied. Three hypotheses fit: Paul wrote both at once and reused his own sentence; a later writer took the notice from Colossians to give Ephesians a Pauline frame; or Colossians copied Ephesians, which almost no one holds, since Ephesians is the fuller text throughout. The data establish dependence. Direction and authorship need the other tests.

**Example 2 (hard): the signature that cuts both ways.** 2 Thessalonians 3:17 calls the handwritten greeting "the sign in every epistle". Read as Paul's, it is a sensible precaution after 2:2's false letter. Read as pseudonymous, it is the forger's boldest stroke, and on this reading the "letter as sent from us" of 2:2 might even be 1 Thessalonians itself, which the new letter sets out to correct. The strain is that the same verse is evidence for both hypotheses. Evidence that fits every hypothesis equally cannot decide between them ([`philosophical-method` 2.4](../../philosophical-method/lessons/02-04-weighing-evidence-in-odds-form.md)). So the case must rest on the eschatology and the literary dependence, which is why honest opinion on this letter stays divided.

## Watch out

- **Level errors, both directions.** "The Church teaches Paul wrote the pastorals" promotes a traditional attribution and an early Commission response to dogma. "If Paul didn't write Titus, it isn't Scripture" hangs canonicity on the attribution. Trent defines the books; human authorship is a hypothesis.
- **"Disputed" is a modern critical label.** All six were received early; the Muratorian fragment lists the pastorals among Paul's letters. Do not confuse them with the ancient *antilegomena* of [6.6](06-06-the-formation-of-the-canon.md).
- **Reading later order back into the words.** Douay's "priests" and "bishop" in Titus 1 translate *presbyteroi* and *episkopos* through later usage. Trent's canons on the hierarchy do not decide what those words meant in the first century.

## One-liner

> Canon settled, pen disputed: weigh brushwork, ideas and provenance letter by letter, and remember that the early Church punished forgery and still received all six.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢)** *(Exegetical)* For each observation, give (i) the test it belongs to (vocabulary and style, theology, or setting), (ii) the letter it concerns, and (iii) whether it is usually offered for or against Pauline authorship. **One line each.**

1. *Eusebeia* ("godliness") recurs in 1 Timothy but never appears in the seven undisputed letters.
2. Believers "are risen again" with Christ (2:12), where Romans 6:5 says "we shall be".
3. Paul leaves his delegate in Crete (1:5), a mission Acts never mentions.
4. The final greetings name Onesimus, Archippus, Epaphras, Aristarchus, Mark, Luke and Demas, the same circle as Philemon.
5. A revolt and "the man of sin" must come before the day of the Lord.

**P2 (🟡)** *(Exegetical (a)–(b) · Evaluative (c))* Titus 1:5–7 (Douay–Rheims):

> For this cause I left thee in Crete: that thou shouldest set in order the things that are wanting and shouldest ordain priests in every city, as I also appointed thee: If any be without crime, the husband of one wife, having faithful children, not accused of riot or unruly. For a bishop must be without crime, as the steward of God: not proud, not subject to anger, not given to wine, no striker, not greedy of filthy lucre.

(a) Which Greek word lies behind "priests" in verse 5, and which behind "bishop" in verse 7? Translate each literally. (b) Which small word joins verse 7 to verses 5–6, and what does it imply about the relation of the two titles? Name one other New Testament passage that does the same. (c) A defender of Pauline authorship says this passage helps his case; an opponent says 1 Timothy 3 helps hers. State each side's move in **100 words or fewer**, any verdict.

**P3 (🔴)** *(Exegetical)* Give each claim its status: a rung of the ladder (and the act that fixes it), "hypothesis / exegesis, no doctrinal level", or "overstated" (and why). **One or two lines each.**

1. "Colossians is inspired, canonical Scripture."
2. "Ephesians was written after Paul's death by a disciple who used Colossians."
3. "Catholics must hold that Paul wrote 1 Timothy, because the Biblical Commission ruled so in 1913."
4. "Since Trent defined that bishops are superior to priests, Titus 1:5–7 must name two distinct offices."

<details>
<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict:**
1. Vocabulary and style; 1 Timothy (the pastorals); against.
2. Theology (realized resurrection); Colossians; against.
3. Setting; Titus; against. Defenders fit it into a release after Acts 28 and a second imprisonment.
4. Setting; Colossians; for. The alternative is careful imitation of Philemon.
5. Theology (eschatology); 2 Thessalonians; against, set beside 1 Thess 5:2's "thief in the night".

**Wrong turns:** classing (3) as theology because it concerns church mission; it is about Paul's movements. Treating (4) as decisive: it counts for authenticity, but a skilled imitator could reproduce it. Assigning (2) to Ephesians; Ephesians 2:6 has the parallel idea, but the verse cited is Colossians.

**Model answer:** (1) Vocabulary; 1 Timothy; against. (2) Theology; Colossians; against. (3) Setting; Titus; against, unless a second imprisonment is assumed. (4) Setting; Colossians; for. (5) Theology; 2 Thessalonians; against.

**P2** *(Exegetical (a)–(b) · Evaluative (c))*

**Must hit, strict (a):** "priests" = *presbyterous* (*presbyteroi*), literally "elders"; "bishop" = *episkopon* (*episkopos*), literally "overseer". The Douay follows the Vulgate's *presbyteros* and *episcopum*, rendered through later church usage.

**Must hit, strict (b):** **"For"** (*gar*). Verse 7 gives the reason for the elder's qualifications in verse 6 by stating what an overseer must be. So the two titles apply to the same men, or at least the same office seen from two sides. Parallel: **Acts 20:17, 28** (the "ancients" of Ephesus are told the Spirit made them "bishops"). Credit Philippians 1:1 only if explained as bishops-plus-deacons with no separate presbyters; credit 1 Clement or Jerome's Letter 146 as a patristic parallel.

**Must hit, any verdict (c):** the defender: an undifferentiated order, with elder and overseer one office, fits the first generation (Acts 20; 1 Clement) and predates Ignatius's single bishop, so the passage does not demand a second-century date. The opponent: 1 Timothy 3 has a fixed list of qualifications for a singular *episkopos* and for deacons. That is settled, institutional office, far from 1 Corinthians 12–14. Either side must show what its verse establishes, not just cite it.

**Wrong turns:** answering (a) with "priest" as the Greek meaning; *hiereus*, the sacrificial priest, is a different word and does not appear here. In (b), concluding that the New Testament knows no distinction of ministries at all; the passage speaks only to these two titles. In (c), deciding authorship from this passage alone.

**Model answer (c), one of several:** The defender notes that Titus still uses "elder" and "overseer" for one group, as Acts 20 and 1 Clement do. That is a pre-Ignatian order, so the passage fits Paul's lifetime better than a date around 110. The opponent replies that 1 Timothy 3 already treats the overseer as a recognized post with a fixed list of qualifications, distinct from deacons. She calls that a settled institution unlike the charismatic assemblies of 1 Corinthians 12–14. The dispute is over how fast church order could develop, and Titus alone cannot settle it.

**P3** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict:**
1. **Defined dogma, rung 1**: Trent's decree on the canon, which lists "to the Colossians" among Paul's fourteen epistles.
2. **Hypothesis, no doctrinal level.** It is a common critical reconstruction, contradicted by no definition and required by none.
3. **Overstated.** The 1913 response is one of the early Commission responses, owed submission when issued. It is not a definition, and the 1955 clarification put authorship questions among those scholars may pursue freely (itself contested). Do not call it dogma; do not call it formally revoked either.
4. **Overstated / a category error.** Trent Session XXIII, canons 6–7, defines a divinely instituted hierarchy and the superiority of bishops. It does not define how first-century texts used *episkopos* and *presbyteros*. That the two titles overlap in Titus 1 is an exegetical reading with no doctrinal level. Jerome himself held it.

**Wrong turns:** in (1), calling canonicity a hypothesis because authorship is one. In (2), calling it heresy. In (3), swinging to "the response never had any authority". In (4), concluding that the overlap of titles contradicts Trent; the dogma concerns the order the Church has, not the vocabulary of one letter.

**Model answer:** (1) Rung 1, Trent's canon. (2) Hypothesis, no level. (3) Overstated: an early Commission response, not a definition; its force on authorship was put in question by the 1955 clarification. (4) Overstated: Trent defines the hierarchy, not the vocabulary of Titus 1; the overlap of titles is exegesis, no level.

## Flashback

**From Lesson [5.3](05-03-romans-5-11-adam-spirit-israel.md) (Romans 5–11: Adam, Spirit, Israel):** *(Exegetical.)* Romans 9:11–13, with the two Old Testament texts Paul quotes (Douay–Rheims):

> Romans 9:11. For when the children were not yet born, nor had done any good or evil (that the purpose of God according to election might stand): 12. Not of works, but of him that calleth, it was said to her: The elder shall serve the younger. 13. As it is written: Jacob I have loved: but Esau I have hated.

> Genesis 25:23. Two nations are in thy womb, and two peoples shall be divided out of thy womb, and one people shall overcome the other, and the elder shall serve the younger.

> Malachias 1:2–3. ... I have loved Jacob, but have hated Esau? and I have made his mountains a wilderness, and given his inheritance to the dragons of the desert.

"The purpose of God according to election" renders *hē kat' eklogēn prothesis tou theou*; "I have hated" is *emisēsa*. Readers divide over whether this election concerns **individuals' eternal destiny** (Augustine's reading) or **peoples' roles in history**.

(a) Give one detail from these texts that each reading can appeal to, and say what it shows. **Two sentences.**
(b) An **invented** study-Bible note reads: "Here Paul teaches, as the Church has defined, that before birth God chose Jacob for heaven and Esau for damnation." Name two distinct errors. **Two sentences.**

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):** **Peoples:** both quoted texts are about nations. Genesis 25:23 speaks of "two nations" and "two peoples", and "the elder shall serve the younger" assigns a role in history; Malachi's "hated" is carried out on Edom's mountains and inheritance, which is a judgment on a land and people, not on a soul. **Individuals:** 9:11 fixes the choice on two particular children "not yet born, nor had done any good or evil", and 9:12 says it is "not of works, but of him that calleth". The point is that the choice is grounded in God's call and not in anything the person did. Either appeal earns credit if it names the words and what they show.
**Must hit, strict (b):** (1) **Level error.** Which of these readings of Romans 9 is right is **freely disputed** exegesis. No reading of 9:13 is defined, and what the Church teaches about predestination is a doctrinal question with its own levels, owned by [`creation-grace-last-things`](../../creation-grace-last-things/syllabus.md) 5.4. (2) **Reading beyond the text.** The note turns "hated" into damnation. In Malachi the word is worked out in history, on Edom's land, and in Genesis the election is to serve, not to a final destiny. Even the individual reading has to argue for an eternal sense; the words do not state it.
**Wrong turns:** answering the level error with its mirror image, "the Church has defined that Romans 9 is about nations only". Treating *emisēsa* as settling the question by itself, when the dispute is over what the choosing is *for*. Losing the frame of chapters 9–11: the question Paul is answering is whether God's word to Israel has failed (9:6), which is why the nations reading has a foothold.
**Model answer:** (a) The peoples reading points to Genesis 25:23's "two nations" and Malachi's "hated" worked out on Edom's mountains, so the choice concerns roles and fortunes in history. The individual reading points to 9:11, "not yet born, nor had done any good or evil ... not of works, but of him that calleth", which fixes the choice on two persons before any deed. (b) It presents a freely disputed reading as defined, when the doctrine of predestination has its own levels and its own course. And it reads "hated" as damnation, when the texts Paul quotes speak of service and of a land laid waste, so an eternal sense has to be argued for.

</details>
