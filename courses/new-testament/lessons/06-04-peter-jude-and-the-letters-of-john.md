# The New Testament · Lesson 6.4: Peter, Jude, and the letters of John

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 6: The other letters, Revelation, and the canon · Builds on: [6.1 The disputed Pauline letters](06-01-the-disputed-pauline-letters-and-the-pastorals.md), [6.3 James](06-03-james-faith-and-works.md), [2.4 John and the Synoptics](02-04-john-and-the-synoptics.md) · Unlocks: [6.5 Revelation](06-05-revelation.md), [6.6 The formation of the canon](06-06-the-formation-of-the-canon.md)

## Why this matters

Six short letters hold three of the course's hardest problems. One New Testament book copies another almost paragraph by paragraph. A canonical letter quotes a book outside the canon and calls it prophecy. And the Douay–Rheims prints a verse about the Trinity that no Greek manuscript had for over a thousand years. Each tests the course's discipline: keep Church teaching apart from scholarly reconstruction.

## The idea

With James ([6.3](06-03-james-faith-and-works.md)), these are the "catholic" letters, mostly addressed to the Church at large. Ask each one a single question.

- **1 Peter:** who wrote it, and from where?
- **Jude and 2 Peter:** who borrowed from whom, and what does the borrowing date?
- **1–3 John:** who is inside the community and who has left it?

1 Peter's authorship is disputed, with respectable cases on both sides. Jude's is debated, but his sources are not. **2 Peter is the strongest pseudonymity case in the New Testament**: most critical scholars, Catholic ones included, doubt that Peter wrote it. A majority is not a proof, and the full case is saved for the boss problem. Here you need the evidence and the right label for each claim.

## Source

2 Peter 3:15–16 and 1 John 4:2–3 (Douay–Rheims):

> And account the longsuffering of our Lord, salvation: as also our most dear brother Paul, according to the wisdom given him, hath written to you: As also in all his epistles, speaking in them of these things; in which are certain things hard to be understood, which the unlearned and unstable wrest, as they do also the other scriptures, to their own destruction.
>
> By this is the spirit of God known. Every spirit which confesseth that Jesus Christ is come in the flesh is of God: And every spirit that dissolveth Jesus is not of God. And this is Antichrist, of whom you have heard that he cometh: and he is now already in the world.

Two words decide the readings. In 2 Peter, "the other scriptures" is *tas loipas graphas*. The *loipas*, "the rest of", puts Paul's letters **in the class of** scripture. "All his epistles" implies a known set. In 1 John, "is come in the flesh" is *en sarki elēlythota*, a perfect participle; this lesson translates "has come in flesh", a coming whose result remains. At 4:3 the Douay's "dissolveth" follows the Vulgate's *solvit*. The critical Greek text has "does not confess Jesus". Socrates (*Church History* VII.32) says the "ancient copies" read "separates Jesus"; most textual critics judge that variant secondary.

## The argument

1. **1 Peter on suffering.** Peter writes to "strangers dispersed" through five provinces of Asia Minor (1:1). They are to expect a "burning heat" (4:12) and suffer "as a Christian" (4:16), which is one of only three uses of the name in the New Testament. Yet they are also told "Honour the king" (2:17). The suffering is chiefly abuse and suspicion from their neighbours, not an empire-wide persecution. Christ's suffering is the model (2:21). At 3:19 Christ "preached to those spirits that were in prison": the descent to the dead ([`christology` 6.1](../../christology/lessons/06-01-he-descended-into-hell.md)), Christ preaching through Noah (Augustine, to Evodius), or a proclamation to the fallen angels of Genesis 6 (W. J. Dalton, SJ, from the Enoch traditions).
2. **[Babylon in 1 Peter](../reference.md#babylon-in-1-peter).** "The church that is in Babylon ... saluteth you. And so doth my son, Mark" (5:13). Eusebius already reads it as a figurative name for Rome, citing Clement and Papias (*Church History* II.15.2). Revelation uses the same code (14:8; chapters 17–18; see [6.5](06-05-revelation.md)). The name fits best once Rome, like Babylon, had destroyed the Temple, so many scholars read it as a hint of a date after 70, after Peter's death under [Nero](../../church-history/lessons/01-01-the-first-generation-and-its-sources.md). Others reply that a capital of exile is enough.
3. **The Silvanus hypothesis.** "By Sylvanus ... I have written briefly" (5:12). 1 Peter's Greek is polished and its quotations follow the Septuagint: surprising from a Galilean fisherman. E. G. Selwyn's commentary (1946) made Silvanus the secretary who drafted it. The difficulty is the formula. "Writing through X" usually names the **carrier**. Acts 15:23 uses it for the letter carried by Judas and Silas, and Silas is the same man. Ignatius, writing to the Romans, uses it for the Ephesians who took his letter. On the majority view, 1 Peter comes from a Roman circle loyal to Peter, c. 70–90, and Brown's *Introduction* inclines this way. A respectable minority still holds for Peter himself, with help, before his death.
4. **[Jude and 1 Enoch](../reference.md#jude-and-1-enoch).** "Jude ... brother of James" (v. 1) presents himself as a brother of the Lord (Mark 6:3 names a Jude; [2.1](02-01-mark.md)). Richard Bauckham (*Jude and the Relatives of Jesus in the Early Church*, 1990) defends that attribution. Many date the letter later. Verses 14–15 quote 1 Enoch 1:9, a Jewish apocalypse of the last centuries BC ([1.2](01-02-second-temple-judaism.md)), and introduce it with "Enoch ... prophesied". Verse 9, Michael disputing with the devil over Moses's body, comes from a work Origen calls "The Ascension of Moses" (*De Principiis* III.2.1). Jerome records what this cost Jude:

   > "because in it he quotes from the apocryphal book of Enoch it is rejected by many. Nevertheless by age and use it has gained authority and is reckoned among the Holy Scriptures." (*Lives of Illustrious Men* 4, NPNF)

5. **[Jude and 2 Peter](../reference.md#jude-and-2-peter).** 2 Peter 2:1–3:3 runs parallel to Jude 4–18 in content and largely in order (see the synopsis). Most scholars hold that **2 Peter used Jude**: the changes run one way, removing both non-canonical citations, and a Jude who extracted 2 Peter and added apocrypha is hard to picture. The case against Petrine authorship adds more. The fathers have died (3:4). Paul's letters are a collection and called scripture (3:15–16). The Greek differs from 1 Peter's, which Jerome notes (*Lives* 1). Attestation is late: Eusebius says he has "learned that his extant second Epistle does not belong to the canon", though "profitable to many" (*Church History* III.3.1). On the other side: the eyewitness claim to the Transfiguration (1:16–18), a possible different secretary, and its eventual reception. Bauckham reads it as a "testament" (1:14's coming death), a genre whose readers knew the attribution was a convention. The majority date is early second century.
6. **1 John's tests.** Nameless and without a greeting, 1 John sets three tests: belief (4:2), love ("he that loveth not his brother, whom he seeth", 4:20), and obedience (2:4). The occasion is a split: "They went out from us but they were not of us" (2:19). These leavers are the "many Antichrists" (2:18, 22). The word is found only in 1 and 2 John, and here it names deniers already present, not a single figure at the end of time. Brown (*The Epistles of John*, Anchor Bible, 1982) reads them as readers of the Fourth Gospel's high Christology ([2.5](02-05-the-theology-of-john.md)) who emptied Jesus's flesh and death of saving weight. Compare Cerinthus, for whom "Christ departed from Jesus" before the passion (Irenaeus, *Against Heresies* I.26.1). This is why 5:6 insists: "not by water only but by water and blood".
7. **2 and 3 John**, in a paragraph. Both are from "the Ancient" (*ho presbyteros*, "the Elder"). 2 John forbids welcoming anyone who denies Jesus Christ "come in the flesh" (v. 7, 10). 3 John is the reverse case: Diotrephes, "who loveth to have the preeminence", refuses the Elder's own envoys (v. 9–10). Authority is contested, and no office settles it.
8. **[The Johannine comma](../reference.md#the-johannine-comma).** The Douay's 5:7 reads "there are Three who give testimony in heaven, the Father, the Word, and the Holy Ghost. And these three are one." The critical Greek lacks it. Only a handful of late minuscules have it, the earliest from the fourteenth century, and so do none of the oldest Vulgate manuscripts. The first certain quotation is Latin, about 380, from Priscillian's circle. Erasmus added it to his third edition (1522). On 13 January 1897 the Holy Office replied that its authenticity could not safely be denied or doubted. A Holy Office declaration of 1927 (reported in the *Enchiridion Biblicum*) explained that the reply was meant to curb private teachers. Catholic scholars might investigate and incline against authenticity, provided they submitted to the Church's judgement. The Church's own Nova Vulgata omits the words.

**Mark the levels.** All seven catholic letters are **canonical and inspired: defined dogma, rung 1** of [the ladder](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-04-the-ladder-of-doctrinal-authority.md) ([the canon as defined](../reference.md#the-canon-as-defined), Trent Session IV). Who wrote 1 and 2 Peter and Jude, where 1 Peter was written, which way 2 Peter and Jude depend, and who the secessionists were are all **scholarly hypotheses** with no magisterial standing. Whether pseudonymity is compatible with inspiration is **freely disputed**, and it is Boss 6's question ([6.6](06-06-the-formation-of-the-canon.md); on [pseudonymity](../reference.md#pseudonymity), see [6.1](06-01-the-disputed-pauline-letters-and-the-pastorals.md)). 1 Enoch is **not canonical** for the Catholic Church, and quoting it does not make it so. The comma is a **textual question**. The 1897 reply was never a definition, and the same office narrowed it in 1927. The Trinity is defined dogma on other grounds ([`trinity-and-god` 2.2](../../trinity-and-god/lessons/02-02-the-father-and-the-son-in-the-new-testament.md)).

## The synopsis

| Jude | 2 Peter | What changes |
|---|---|---|
| 4: men "secretly entered in", denying the Lord | 2:1–3: "lying teachers", denying the Lord | Jude's intruders become future false teachers |
| 6: angels in "everlasting chains" | 2:4: angels cast into Tartarus (*tartarōsas*) | kept |
| 7: Sodom and Gomorrha | 2:5–8: the flood, Sodom, **Noe and Lot saved** | 2 Peter adds rescue |
| 9: Michael, the devil, Moses's body | 2:11: "angels ... bring not ... a railing judgment" | **names removed** |
| 11: Cain, Balaam, Core | 2:15–16: Balaam only, with the ass | trimmed |
| 12–13: "clouds without water", "wandering stars" | 2:17: "fountains without water", "mist of darkness" | reworded |
| 14–15: Enoch's prophecy | nothing | **citation removed** |
| 17–18: the apostles foretold "mockers" | 3:2–3: "deceitful scoffers" in the last days | kept |

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean): which way does Jude 9 / 2 Peter 2:11 run?** Jude's verse is concrete: Michael, the devil, a dispute over Moses's body, and "The Lord command thee". 2 Peter's is an abstract principle: angels do not bring railing judgments. If Jude copied 2 Peter, he illustrated a general remark with an apocryphal scene; if 2 Peter copied Jude, he deleted the story and kept its moral. The second fits 2 Peter's other omission (Enoch) and its additions of canonical figures (Noah, Lot). Verdict: Jude first, the majority reading and still a hypothesis.

**Example 2 (hard): does "prophesied" canonize Enoch?** Tertullian drew that inference: Enoch "possesses a testimony in the Apostle Jude" (*On the Apparel of Women* I.3). The Ethiopian Orthodox canon includes 1 Enoch; the Catholic canon does not. Here the method strains, because the text does not decide among three readings. Jude believed the patriarch spoke these words; or he used a book his readers valued, with no verdict on its authorship; or he held this one saying true, as Paul quotes the poet Aratus (Acts 17:28). What is fixed is the level. Jude's inspiration guarantees what Jude asserts. It does not turn his sources into Scripture ([`fundamental-theology` 3.1](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/03-01-inspiration.md)).

## Watch out

- **"The Church teaches that Peter wrote 2 Peter."** Trent defines the letter as canonical. It does not define its human author. The reverse error is also a level error: "if Peter didn't write it, it isn't Scripture" ([church teaching vs hypothesis](../reference.md#church-teaching-vs-hypothesis)).
- **"Antichrist" in 1 John is not the end-time figure of popular eschatology**, but people who have already left.
- **Trent's "entire with all their parts" does not settle the comma.** Rome has not read it so since 1927, and the Nova Vulgata omits the verse.
- **"The other scriptures" in 2 Peter 3:16 is not a closed canon.** It shows only that some of Paul's letters were read *as* scripture when 2 Peter was written.

## One-liner

> 1 Peter is disputed, 2 Peter copies Jude and is the likeliest pseudonym in the canon, Jude quotes Enoch without canonizing it, and 1 John tests belief in the flesh; every book is defined Scripture, and none of their authors is defined.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢)** *(Exegetical)* Jude 14–15 (Douay–Rheims) and 1 Enoch 1:9 (R. H. Charles's translation, 1913, from the Ethiopic):

> Now of these Enoch also, the seventh from Adam, prophesied, saying: Behold, the Lord cometh with thousands of his saints: To execute judgment upon all and to reprove all the ungodly for all the works of their ungodliness, whereby they have done ungodly: and for all the hard things which ungodly sinners have spoken against God.

> And behold! He cometh with ten thousands of His holy ones / To execute judgement upon all, / And to destroy all the ungodly: / And to convict all flesh / Of all the works of their ungodliness which they have ungodly committed, / And of all the hard things which ungodly sinners have spoken against Him.

(a) Name two differences in wording between the two texts. The Douay's "thousands" does not count: Jude's Greek has *myriasin*, "tens of thousands". (b) Which word shows how Jude regards the saying? (c) Mark the level of each claim: "Jude is Scripture"; "1 Enoch is Scripture"; "Jude believed the patriarch Enoch spoke these words". **One line per part, and one line per claim in (c).**

**P2 (🟡)** *(Exegetical)* Assign each claim its level, or say it has none, and name the act or evidence that fixes it. **One line each.**

1. "1 John is inspired and canonical."
2. "The Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit are one God."
3. "The heavenly witnesses of 1 John 5:7 were written by the author of 1 John."
4. "Trent's decree that the books are received 'entire with all their parts' defines the comma as Scripture."
5. "Catholic exegetes are forbidden to doubt the comma."

**P3 (🔴)** *(Exegetical)* An **invented** study-Bible note on 2 Peter 3:15–16:

> "Here Peter, whom the Church teaches wrote this letter shortly before his martyrdom, calls all of Paul's letters Scripture. So the New Testament canon was already closed in the apostles' lifetime, and those who say 2 Peter used Jude are denying Catholic doctrine."

Name the **three** errors and correct each in one sentence. You are not asked for the case against Petrine authorship. **150 words or fewer.**

<details>
<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict:** (a) Any two of the following. Jude names "the Lord" (*Kyrios* in the Greek) where Charles has "He". Jude lacks Enoch's "to destroy all the ungodly". Jude's frame adds "the seventh from Adam ... prophesied, saying", which is not in 1:9, though 1 Enoch uses the same title elsewhere (60:8). Credit "reprove all the ungodly" against "convict all flesh", but note that Jude's Greek manuscripts divide here: some read "every soul". (b) "**prophesied**" (*proephēteusen*): Jude presents the saying as true prophecy. (c) "Jude is Scripture" is **defined dogma** (Trent Session IV). "1 Enoch is Scripture" is **false for the Catholic Church**, because it is not in the canon Trent defines, and Jude's citation does not change that. "Jude believed the patriarch spoke them" is **a scholarly question with no magisterial standing**.

**Wrong turns:** counting "thousands" against "ten thousands", which the problem excludes because the gap is in the Douay's translation, not in Jude's Greek. The same goes for the Douay's "against God": Jude's Greek has "against him", as Enoch does. Treating "prophesied" as proof that Jude held 1 Enoch canonical; it shows what he thought of this saying. Grading the third claim as heresy, or as defined.

**Model answer:** (a) Jude says "the Lord" where Enoch has "He", and drops "to destroy all the ungodly". (b) "Prophesied": Jude treats the words as prophecy. (c) Defined dogma; not Scripture in the Catholic canon, and a citation does not canonize; an open historical question.

**P2** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict:**
1. **Defined dogma, rung 1**: Trent Session IV's list.
2. **Defined dogma, rung 1**, by the Church's trinitarian definitions (e.g. the creed of Constantinople I, Lateran IV). It does not rest on 5:7.
3. **No magisterial standing; a textual question** settled by manuscript evidence. The evidence runs heavily against it: no early Greek manuscript, no oldest Vulgate manuscript, and a first certain citation in Latin around 380.
4. **Overstated; nothing defines it.** Rome's 1927 permission to incline against authenticity shows the decree was not read as settling a verse-level textual question.
5. **False as stated.** The 1897 Holy Office reply said authenticity could not safely be denied. It was a non-definitive, prudential curial act, never rung 1 or 2. The 1927 declaration allowed scholars to incline against it, with submission to the Church's judgement.

**Wrong turns:** calling (3) heresy because (2) is dogma, which confuses the doctrine with one proof text. Grading (5) as true because the 1897 reply was never repealed by name; its own authors glossed it in 1927. Promoting the 1897 reply to rung 2.

**Model answer:** (1) Defined, by Trent. (2) Defined, by the trinitarian creeds, not by this verse. (3) No magisterial level; a textual question, and the evidence is against it. (4) Overstatement; Trent did not settle it and Rome has since permitted doubt. (5) False: the 1897 reply was prudential and non-definitive, and 1927 allowed scholars to lean against it.

**P3** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict:** (1) **Authorship stated as doctrine.** The Church defines 2 Peter as canonical. Its human author is a hypothesis, and how much the traditional titles bind is freely disputed. (2) **Misreading the text.** "All his epistles" means the Pauline letters the writer knows. "The other scriptures" puts them in the class of scripture. Neither says the canon is closed, and the canon took centuries to settle ([6.6](06-06-the-formation-of-the-canon.md)). (3) **Hypothesis turned into heresy.** That 2 Peter used Jude is a literary judgement with no magisterial standing either way, so holding it denies nothing.

**Wrong turns:** answering by arguing that Peter did not write it, which the problem excludes and which is a separate, disputed question. Correcting (1) by saying the Church teaches Peter did *not* write it, which is the mirror error.

**Model answer:** The note makes three errors. First, it gives Petrine authorship as Church teaching; Trent defines 2 Peter's canonicity, not its author, and the authorship is a scholarly question. Second, the verse treats Paul's known letters as scripture ("the other scriptures"); it says nothing about a closed list, and the canon's edges were argued over for centuries. Third, dependence on Jude is a literary hypothesis with no doctrinal weight, so holding it denies no doctrine.

</details>

## Flashback

**From Lesson [6.2](06-02-hebrews.md) (Hebrews):** *(Exegetical.)* Close reading, Hebrews 10:11–14 (Douay–Rheims, Challoner):

> And every priest indeed standeth daily ministering and often offering the same sacrifices which can never take away sins. But this man, offering one sacrifice for sins, for ever sitteth on the right hand of God, From henceforth expecting until his enemies be made his footstool. For by one oblation he hath perfected for ever them that are sanctified.

The Greek verbs of posture are *hestēken*, "stands" (10:11), and *ekathisen*, "sat down" (10:12). "For ever" in 10:12 and 10:14 is *eis to diēnekes*, "in perpetuity". In 10:12 the Greek order is "one ... sacrifice *eis to diēnekes* sat down", so the phrase can go with either the sacrifice or the sitting.
(a) Name the two contrasts in 10:11–12, and say what the change of posture argues. (b) Which psalm verse do 10:12–13 echo, and how does it combine with the verse of that psalm Hebrews has already used for Christ's priesthood? (c) Give the two ways of attaching *eis to diēnekes* in 10:12, and say why the argument survives either choice. **Two sentences per part.**

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict:** (a) **Many priests against "this man"**, and **"daily ... often ... the same sacrifices" against "one sacrifice"**. The Levitical priest still **stands** because his work is never finished. Christ **sat down** because his offering is complete, which is the argument of *ephapax* told through posture. (b) **Psalm 110:1** (DR 109:1): "Sit thou at my right hand: Until I make thy enemies thy footstool". Hebrews has already read **110:4**, "a priest for ever according to the order of Melchisedech", as addressed to the same person. So the enthroned king of verse 1 *is* the priest of verse 4, and **the priest sits**. (c) With the **sacrifice**: "one sacrifice for sins, valid in perpetuity". With the **sitting**: "sat down for ever", the Douay's choice. Either way the point stands, because **10:14 says it without ambiguity**: "by one oblation he hath perfected *eis to diēnekes*".
**Wrong turns:** calling the Douay's word order a mistranslation. The Greek allows it, and translations divide. Reading "sitteth" as idleness that ends Christ's work for us. Hebrews has him "always living to make intercession" (7:25), so the seated posture means the *sacrifice* is finished, not his priesthood. Settling from this passage how the one offering is present in the Mass: that is doctrine, handed to [`christology` 5.2](../../christology/lessons/05-02-ransom-christus-victor-and-sacrifice.md) and [`sacraments-and-liturgy`](../../sacraments-and-liturgy/syllabus.md), not a point of grammar.
**Model answer:** (a) Many priests against one, and repeated sacrifices that "can never take away sins" against one sacrifice. The old priest stands because his work is never done, and Christ sits because his is. (b) The verses echo Psalm 110:1, "Sit thou at my right hand ... thy footstool". Hebrews has already read 110:4 as spoken to the same person, so the king invited to sit is also the priest for ever, and a seated priest is a priest whose offering is finished. (c) *Eis to diēnekes* can mean the one sacrifice counts for all time, or that he sat down for ever, as the Douay reads. The choice does not change the argument, because 10:14 attaches the same phrase unambiguously to the "one oblation" that has "perfected for ever".

</details>
