# The New Testament · Lesson 6.2: Hebrews

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 6: The other letters, Revelation, and the canon · Builds on: [4.2 Paul's life and letters](04-02-pauls-life-and-letters.md), [6.1 The disputed Pauline letters](06-01-the-disputed-pauline-letters-and-the-pastorals.md), [`christology` 5.2](../../christology/lessons/05-02-ransom-christus-victor-and-sacrifice.md) · Unlocks: [6.3 James](06-03-james-faith-and-works.md), [6.6 The formation of the canon](06-06-the-formation-of-the-canon.md)

## Why this matters

Hebrews is the only New Testament book that calls Jesus a priest, and it builds that claim out of one psalm verse and a king who appears in three verses of Genesis. Nobody knows who wrote it, yet the Church defined it as canonical in a decree that lists it among "fourteen epistles of Paul". It is the cleanest case for keeping three questions apart: what a text argues, who wrote it, and what the Church has settled.

## The idea

Think of a sermon written out and posted with a short covering note. It has no opening greeting and no named sender. It calls itself a "word of consolation" (13:22; Greek *logos tēs paraklēseōs*, a word of exhortation, the phrase Acts 13:15 uses for a synagogue sermon). Only the last few verses look like a letter, with news of "our brother Timothy" and greetings from "the brethren from Italy" (13:23–24).

The sermon's method is one move made over and over: **take a text from the Jewish scriptures, find a gap or a tension in it, and show that Christ fills the gap.** If Psalm 110 promises a priest "for ever", the Levitical priests who die cannot be what it means. If Jeremiah promises a "new" covenant, the old one was not final. Each argument runs from lesser to greater and ends in a warning not to drift back.

## Source

Hebrews 7:23–27 (Douay–Rheims, Challoner), the argument's turning point:

> And the others indeed were made many priests, because by reason of death they were not suffered to continue: But this, for that he continueth for ever, hath an everlasting priesthood: Whereby he is able also to save for ever them that come to God by him; always living to make intercession for us. For it was fitting that we should have such a high priest, holy, innocent, undefiled, separated from sinners, and made higher than the heavens: Who needeth not daily (as the other priests) to offer sacrifices, first for his own sins, and then for the people's: for this he did once, in offering himself.

"Once" in 7:27 is ***ephapax***, "once for all": done a single time and never needing to be done again. Two contrasts carry the passage: many priests against one, daily sacrifices against one.

## The argument

1. **The text: Psalm 110:4 (DR 109:4).** "The Lord hath sworn, and he will not repent: Thou art a priest for ever according to the order of Melchisedech." Hebrews quotes or echoes it at 5:6, 6:20 and 7:17, 21. The psalm's first verse ("Sit thou at my right hand") was already applied to Jesus in the gospels, and Hebrews quotes it too (1:13). Its own move is to read verse 4 as spoken to the same person. *In words:* one psalm makes the Messiah both king and priest.

2. **The gap: Melchizedek (7:1–10; [Melchizedek and Psalm 110](../reference.md#melchizedek-and-psalm-110)).** Genesis 14:18–20 gives him three verses: king of Salem, priest of God Most High, he blesses Abram and receives a tenth. Hebrews reads the silences. His name means "king of justice", Salem means "peace" (7:2), and he is "without father, without mother, without genealogy" (7:3). That argues from what Genesis does *not* record, as Challoner's note says. Then priority: Abraham paid him tithes, and "that which is less is blessed by the better" (7:7). Levi, still "in the loins" of Abraham, paid through him (7:9–10). The Greek of 7:3 matters: Melchizedek is *aphōmoiōmenos*, "made to resemble" the Son of God. The Son is not modelled on Melchizedek; it is the other way round. *In words:* an older, greater priesthood already stood outside Levi.

3. **The priest from the wrong tribe (7:11–25).** Jesus "sprung out of Juda", a tribe Moses never linked with priests (7:14). So his priesthood cannot rest on descent. It rests on "the power of an indissoluble life" (7:16) and God's oath (7:20–21). "For the priesthood being translated, it is necessary that a translation also be made of the law" (7:12). *In words:* change the priesthood and you have changed the Law it served.

4. **Once for all ([once for all ephapax](../reference.md#once-for-all-ephapax)).** Hebrews uses *ephapax* three times: he offered himself "once" (7:27), entered the sanctuary "once" by his own blood (9:12), and "we are sanctified by the oblation of the body of Jesus Christ once" (10:10). The plain form *hapax* ("once") backs it up at 9:26 and 9:28. Against it stand "daily" (7:27) and "often offering the same sacrifices" (10:11). The model is the Day of Atonement (9:7): the high priest entered once a year, with blood. What this means for sacrifice as *doctrine* is [`christology` 5.2](../../christology/lessons/05-02-ransom-christus-victor-and-sacrifice.md)'s. How the one offering is made present in the Mass belongs to [`sacraments-and-liturgy`](../../sacraments-and-liturgy/syllabus.md). *In words:* repetition proved the old sacrifices incomplete, and a single offering proves this one complete.

5. **The new covenant (8:6–13; [the new covenant in Hebrews 8](../reference.md#the-new-covenant-in-hebrews-8)).** Hebrews quotes Jeremiah 31:31–34 in full, usually counted the longest Old Testament quotation in the New Testament. It quotes the Greek. Where Jeremiah's Hebrew has God say "I had dominion over them" (DR Jer 31:32, following Jerome's Latin from the Hebrew), Hebrews 8:9 has "I regarded them not" (*ēmelēsa*, "I disregarded"). That is the Septuagint's wording, where the chapter is numbered 38. Then the inference, which is Hebrews' own and not Jeremiah's: "Now in saying a new, he hath made the former old. And that which decayeth and groweth old is near its end" (8:13). *In words:* the word "new" in the prophet already implies the old covenant's end.

6. **The warnings (6:4–6; 10:26; 12:17; [the warning passages](../reference.md#the-warning-passages)).** "It is impossible for those who were once illuminated ... and are fallen away: to be renewed again to penance, crucifying again to themselves the Son of God" (6:4–6). "Once" here is *hapax* again. The early Church fought over this text. Tertullian, by then a Montanist, cited it against readmitting adulterers after baptism (*On Modesty* 20). Ambrose, answering the Novatians, who denied that the Church could reconcile the lapsed, read it as forbidding a *second baptism*, not a second repentance (*On Repentance* II.2). Challoner's note takes Ambrose's line. Many modern exegetes stress that the verbs describe apostasy, a continuing public repudiation. *In words:* the "once" of Christ's offering has a mirror image, the "once" of the believer's enlightenment, and the warning trades on it.

7. **Who wrote it ([who wrote Hebrews](../reference.md#who-wrote-hebrews)).** The book names no author, and the oldest judgments admit it. Clement of Alexandria held that Paul wrote in Hebrew and Luke translated (Eusebius, *Church History* VI.14). Eusebius reports that some rejected it because the church of Rome disputed that Paul wrote it (III.3.5). Origen weighed style against thought and ended here:

   > But who wrote the epistle, in truth, God knows. (Eusebius, *Church History* VI.25.14, McGiffert, NPNF II/1)

   The internal case against Paul is strong. The Greek is the New Testament's most polished, as Origen noticed. The author puts himself among those to whom the message "was confirmed unto us by them that heard him" (2:3), where Paul insists he received his gospel from no man, "but by the revelation of Jesus Christ" (Gal 1:12). Paul's name, used in every one of the thirteen letters, is missing. The candidates are in the table below. *In words:* the ancient Church knew it did not know.

**Mark the levels.** That Hebrews is **canonical and inspired** is **defined dogma, rung 1** of [the ladder](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-04-the-ladder-of-doctrinal-authority.md): Trent's decree (Session IV, 1546) lists "fourteen epistles of Paul the apostle", ending "to the Hebrews" ([the canon as defined](../reference.md#the-canon-as-defined); [`fundamental-theology` 3.3](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/03-03-the-canon-as-defined.md)). Whether the decree's titles also define human authorship is **freely disputed**; Brown, among others, reads it as defining books, not authors ([4.2](04-02-pauls-life-and-letters.md)). The Biblical Commission's response of 24 June 1914 is a **weight case** of the kind read in [1.1](01-01-reading-the-new-testament-in-the-church.md) ([early responses](../reference.md#the-early-biblical-commission-responses)). In summary, it said Catholics should not hesitate to count Hebrews among Paul's genuine letters. It also said, first, that its canonicity is what "has been defined as a matter of faith", and second, that Paul need not have given the letter its present form. So the Commission itself kept canonicity and authorship apart. It was papally approved and owed submission when issued, but it is not a definition. That a Christian fallen after baptism can rise again by God's grace is **defined** (Trent, Session VI, canon 29), so no Catholic reading of 6:4–6 can make it deny that. Which reading of the passage is right is **exegesis, open**. Every authorship candidate and date is a **scholarly hypothesis** ([church teaching vs hypothesis](../reference.md#church-teaching-vs-hypothesis)).

## The case: candidates for author

| Candidate | Proposed by | Best evidence | Chief difficulty |
|---|---|---|---|
| Paul | Eastern tradition; the 1914 response | Timothy in 13:23; themes shared with Paul | style; 2:3 against Gal 1:12; no name |
| Paul, with Luke as translator | Clement of Alexandria | explains the Greek | no trace of a Hebrew original; Hebrews argues from the Septuagint's wording |
| Barnabas | Tertullian, *On Modesty* 20 ("under the name of Barnabas") | a Levite (Acts 4:36), fits the priestly theme | one Western witness |
| Apollos | usually credited to Luther | "eloquent ... mighty in the scriptures", from Alexandria (Acts 18:24) | no ancient witness at all |
| Priscilla | Harnack (c. 1900) | Pauline circle, Rome, anonymity explained | the author speaks of himself with a masculine participle (*diēgoumenon*, 11:32) |
| Unknown | Origen; most modern scholars (Brown) | the evidence stops here | none, which is its appeal |

The 8:9 wording tells against a Hebrew original: the argument depends on the Greek. A date before 70 is often argued from the present tenses about sacrifices (10:1–2), though Hebrews speaks of the tabernacle, never the Temple. The latest possible date is fixed by 1 Clement 36, which echoes Hebrews 1:3–4 ("the brightness of His majesty"), conventionally dated about 95–97 ([`patristics` 1.2](../../patristics/lessons/01-02-first-clement-and-the-didache.md)).

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean): 7:11–12.** Run the method: text, gap, fill. Text: Psalm 110:4 promises a priest "according to the order of Melchisedech". Gap: "If then perfection was by the Levitical priesthood ... what further need was there that another priest should rise ... and not be called according to the order of Aaron?" (7:11). A promise of a *different* priestly order would be pointless if the existing one sufficed. Fill: Christ, from Judah, priest by oath and by indestructible life. Consequence: a change of law (7:12). Every step is in the text, and nobody disputes that this is the argument.

**Example 2 (hard): 6:4–6.** Text: "impossible ... to be renewed again to penance". The method strains at *which* gap is meant. On the Tertullian reading, a gravely lapsed Christian cannot be restored at all. On the Ambrose–Challoner reading, baptism cannot be repeated, so the text is about a sacrament, not about forgiveness. On a modern reading, the verbs describe continuing apostasy: a restoration cannot happen *while* they keep "crucifying again" (the participle is present). Each reading can point to a word in the text. Only Tertullian's absolute reading is excluded, and by a later definition (Trent VI can. 29), not by the grammar. The text is hard; the doctrine is settled; the two are distinct.

## Watch out

- **"Trent defined that Paul wrote Hebrews."** It defined the book's canonicity by listing it under Paul's name. Whether that title binds on authorship is freely disputed, and the 1914 response itself called canonicity, not authorship, the defined point.
- **The reverse error:** "Hebrews' place in the canon is uncertain, since nobody knows who wrote it." Canonicity is rung 1. Authorship is a hypothesis. The first does not wait on the second.
- **"Melchizedek had no parents."** Hebrews argues from Genesis's silence, and 7:3 makes Melchizedek resemble the Son, not a heavenly being.
- **"Hebrews says the old covenant was always worthless."** It says the Levitical priesthood was a "shadow of heavenly things" (8:5) and could not "make perfect". A shadow is cast by something real. How Hebrews bears on Judaism today is not settled by 8:13.

## One-liner

> Hebrews builds a priesthood from one psalm verse and a three-verse king: many priests against one, daily sacrifices against *ephapax*. The Church defined the book and never defined its author, and Origen's "God knows" is still the best answer.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢)** *(Exegetical)* Close reading, Hebrews 8:8–10, 13 (Douay–Rheims), with Jeremiah 31:32 (DR) beside it:

> **Heb 8:8–10, 13:** For, finding fault with them, he saith: Behold the days shall come, saith the Lord: and I will perfect, unto the house of Israel and unto the house of Juda, a new testament: Not according to the testament which I made to their fathers, on the day when I took them by the hand to lead them out of the land of Egypt: because they continued not in my testament: and I regarded them not, saith the Lord. For this is the testament which I will make to the house of Israel after those days, saith the Lord: I will give my laws into their mind: and in their heart will I write them. ... Now in saying a new, he hath made the former old. And that which decayeth and groweth old is near its end.
>
> **Jer 31:32 (end):** ... the covenant which they made void, and I had dominion over them, saith the Lord.

(a) Explain the difference between "I regarded them not" and "I had dominion over them", and say what it shows about the text the author of Hebrews used. (b) Which sentence is Hebrews' own inference rather than Jeremiah's words, and what does the inference turn on? **Two sentences per part.**

**P2 (🟡)** *(Exegetical)* Assign each claim its standing: Church teaching (with its level and the act that carries it), a document of lesser or disputed weight (say what weight), or a scholarly hypothesis. **One or two sentences each.**

1. Hebrews is sacred and canonical Scripture.
2. Catholics should not hesitate to count Hebrews among Paul's genuine letters (Biblical Commission, 24 June 1914).
3. Paul need not have given Hebrews its present literary form (same response).
4. Hebrews was written by Apollos to a church in Rome before 70.

**P3 (🔴)** *(Exegetical)* An **invented** parish catechist's handout:

> "Hebrews, which the Council of Trent defined was written by St Paul, teaches in 6:4–6 that a baptized person who commits mortal sin can never be forgiven. This is also why Melchizedek is so mysterious: 7:3 tells us he literally had no father or mother and never died, which shows he was an angel whom Christ was modelled on."

Find the three errors and correct each in **one or two sentences**, marking the level of any claim about Church teaching.

<details>
<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** "I regarded them not" is the **Septuagint's** wording (*ēmelēsa*, "I disregarded"; LXX Jeremiah 38:32). "I had dominion over them" renders the **Hebrew**, which the Douay follows through Jerome's Latin. So the author of Hebrews quotes the **Greek Old Testament**.

**Must hit, strict (b):** 8:13, "in saying a new, he hath made the former old", is **Hebrews' inference**. It turns on the **single word "new"** (*kainēn*): calling one covenant new implies the other is old and passing.

**Wrong turns:** calling the difference a Douay mistranslation or a copying error in Hebrews. Both faithfully render different source texts. Attributing 8:13 to Jeremiah.

**Model answer:** (a) Hebrews' "I regarded them not" matches the Septuagint, while the Douay's Jeremiah, translated from the Hebrew through the Vulgate, has "I had dominion over them". So the author was reading Jeremiah in Greek. (b) Verse 13 is the author's own comment, not the prophet's. It argues from the one word "new": by calling a covenant new, God has declared the first one old and near its end.

**P2** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict:**
1. **Defined dogma, rung 1:** Trent, Session IV (1546), lists Hebrews among the canonical books under anathema.
2. **A papally approved Biblical Commission response**, owed submission when issued but **not a definition**. Its present force falls under the 1955 clarification (freedom on authorship questions not touching faith and morals), a reading itself contested. Not dogma, and not formally revoked.
3. **The same response, same weight.** The Commission's own concession: Paul need not have given the letter its final form. It shows the response did not tie canonicity to Pauline composition.
4. **Scholarly hypothesis** on every point (author, destination, date), with no magisterial standing either way.

**Wrong turns:** ranking (2) as rung 1 because it cites Trent. The response itself calls canonicity, not authorship, the defined point. Calling (4) "contrary to Church teaching": no act defines Hebrews' human author. Treating (3) as a modern scholar's view: it is in the 1914 response.

**Model answer:** (1) Rung 1, defined by Trent's canon of 1546. (2) An early Biblical Commission response, papally approved and owed submission when issued, but not a definition. Its present force is the 1955 question, and it is neither dogma nor formally revoked. (3) Same document and weight. The Commission itself allowed that Paul may not have shaped the text. (4) A hypothesis throughout; no act of the Church touches it.

**P3** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict:**
1. "**Trent defined** it was written by Paul": Trent defined Hebrews' **canonicity** (rung 1) by listing it under Paul's name. Whether the title binds on authorship is **freely disputed**. Even the 1914 response distinguished the defined canonicity from authorship.
2. "**Can never be forgiven**": that a Christian fallen after baptism can rise again by God's grace is **defined** (Trent VI, canon 29), and Trent XIV, ch. 1, recalls the Church's condemnation of the Novatians. The passage speaks of apostasy ("crucifying again"), and Ambrose and Challoner read it as excluding a second baptism. Its exact sense is open; the absolute reading is excluded.
3. "**Literally no parents ... an angel ... Christ modelled on him**": Hebrews argues from Genesis's **silence** about his genealogy (Challoner's note says so). In 7:3 he is "made like" (*aphōmoiōmenos*) the Son, so Melchizedek resembles Christ, not the reverse.

**Wrong turns:** "correcting" (2) by saying 6:4–6 is easy or means nothing. It is a hard text, and the doctrine, not the grammar, excludes the absolute reading. Correcting (1) by saying Paul certainly did not write it; that is a hypothesis too.

**Model answer:** (1) Trent defined that Hebrews is canonical (rung 1), not who wrote it; whether its "of Paul" title binds is disputed, and most scholars think the author unknown. (2) Trent defines that the baptized who fall can rise again by grace (VI can. 29), so 6:4–6 cannot mean that; Ambrose read it as ruling out a second baptism, and its exact sense is still debated. (3) Hebrews reasons from what Genesis leaves out, and 7:3 says Melchizedek was made like the Son of God, not that Christ was modelled on him.

## Flashback

**From Lesson [5.4](05-04-the-new-perspective-on-paul.md) (The New Perspective on Paul):** *(Exegetical.)* Close reading, Ephesians 2:8–9, 11–12, 14–15 (Douay–Rheims, Challoner):

> For by grace you are saved through faith: and that not of yourselves, for it is the gift of God. Not of works, that no man may glory. ... For which cause be mindful that you, being heretofore gentiles in the flesh, who are called uncircumcision by that which is called circumcision in the flesh, made by hands: That you were at that time without Christ, being aliens from the conversation of Israel and strangers to the testament ... For he is our peace, who hath made both one, and breaking down the middle wall of partition, the enmities in his flesh: Making void the law of commandments contained in decrees: that he might make the two in himself into one new man, making peace.

"Not of works" in 2:9 is *ouk ex ergōn*, plain "works", not Galatians' *erga nomou*. "The middle wall of partition" in 2:14 is *to mesotoichon tou phragmou*, "the dividing wall of the fence".
(a) Name the words an Old Perspective reader cites from 2:8–9, and the words a boundary-marker (Dunn) reader cites from 2:11–15. **One sentence each.** (b) What does the bare "works" of 2:9 do to the debate over Ephesians itself, and why does that not settle what Galatians and Romans meant? **Two sentences.** (c) In Barclay's scheme, which one perfection of grace lets you read both halves as a single thought? **One sentence.**

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):** Old Perspective: "**not of yourselves**", "**the gift of God**", "**not of works, that no man may glory**": grace against human doing and boasting. Boundary-marker: "**uncircumcision ... circumcision**", "**aliens from the conversation of Israel**", "**the middle wall of partition**", "**made both one**": the Law's decrees as the fence between Jew and Gentile.
**Must hit, strict (b):** With *erga nomou* reduced to plain "works" and set against gift and boasting, 2:9 is most naturally read as **human doing as such**, which favours the Old Perspective reading **of Ephesians**. But Ephesians' authorship is **disputed** ([6.1](06-01-the-disputed-pauline-letters-and-the-pastorals.md)): if a disciple wrote it, 2:9 shows how an **early reader generalized** Paul, not what Paul's own phrase meant at Antioch. Even if Paul wrote it, a later letter's wording does not fix the sense of an earlier one.
**Must hit, strict (c):** **Incongruity**: the gift goes to those with no prior worth, both the "dead in sins" of 2:5 who could not boast and the Gentiles "without God" of 2:12 who had no share in Israel, so the "not of works" and the broken wall are one thought.
**Wrong turns:** claiming Ephesians proves the New Perspective wrong, or right; it is one witness, of disputed authorship. Saying the Church, by defining Ephesians canonical, has fixed which contrast Paul meant: canonicity is **defined** (Trent, Session IV); the exegesis of "works" is a **scholarly question, freely disputed**, and Trent VI defines justification by grace, not a reading of *erga nomou*. Answering (c) with "priority" alone: God's acting first does not explain why Gentiles are included.
**Model answer:** (a) An Old Perspective reader cites "not of yourselves ... the gift of God. Not of works, that no man may glory", which sets grace against human doing and boasting. A boundary-marker reader cites the circumcision/uncircumcision labels, "aliens from the conversation of Israel" and "the middle wall of partition", where the Law's decrees are the fence between two peoples. (b) Plain "works", set against gift and boasting, reads most naturally as human doing, so on Ephesians itself the Old Perspective has the easier verse. Since Ephesians' authorship is disputed, that may show only how an early Pauline writer generalized Paul, not what *erga nomou* meant in Galatians and Romans. (c) Incongruity: God's gift ignores every prior worth, moral or ethnic, so saving the unworthy and admitting the Gentile are the same act.

</details>
