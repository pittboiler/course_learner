# The New Testament · Lesson 2.1: Mark

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 2: The four gospels · Builds on: [1.4 The Synoptic problem](01-04-the-synoptic-problem.md), [1.5 From oral tradition to written gospel](01-05-from-oral-tradition-to-written-gospel.md) · Unlocks: [2.2 Matthew](02-02-matthew.md), [2.4 John and the Synoptics](02-04-john-and-the-synoptics.md)

## Why this matters

If [Markan priority](../reference.md#markan-priority) is right ([1.4](01-04-the-synoptic-problem.md)), Mark is the gospel the other two rewrote, so you cannot see what Matthew and Luke did until you have seen what Mark did. Mark is also the strangest of the four: a Jesus who silences talk about himself, disciples who understand less as the story goes on, and oldest manuscripts that end with frightened women telling no one. This lesson reads Mark as a deliberate narrative and meets three questions that are not about narrative at all: who the "brothers" of Jesus were, which ending of Mark is Scripture, and which ending Mark wrote.

## The idea

Mark tells the story of a Messiah who is hidden in plain sight. The reader knows who Jesus is from the first line: "the beginning of the gospel of Jesus Christ, the Son of God" (1:1). The demons know too. The characters mostly do not, and Jesus keeps it that way. Only at the cross does a human being say it without being silenced: the centurion's "Indeed this man was the son of God" (15:39).

So the reader stands where no character stands, watching people miss what the reader has been told. The disciples are the sharpest case. They are called first, taught privately, and given "the mystery of the kingdom" (4:11), and they fail at every step to the last: they argue about rank, they flee at the arrest, and Peter denies Jesus. Mark's claim: you cannot understand Jesus as Messiah until you have seen him crucified.

## Source

Four secrecy texts, Douay-Rheims (Challoner). Modern verse numbers; the Douay numbers Mark 9 one lower, given in brackets.

> **1:34** And he cast out many devils: and he suffered them not to speak, because they knew him.
>
> **1:44** And he saith to him: See thou tell no one; but go, shew thyself to the high priest ...
>
> **9:9** [DR 9:8] And as they came down from the mountain, he charged them not to tell any man what things they had seen, till the Son of man shall be risen again from the dead.
>
> **9:31–32** [DR 9:30–31] The Son of man shall be betrayed into the hands of men, and they shall kill him; and after that he is killed, he shall rise again the third day. But they understood not the word: and they were afraid to ask him.

Two things to see. The silence in 9:9 has an **expiry date**: "till the Son of man shall be risen". And the silence of 9:32 is different in kind. Nobody is ordering the disciples to be quiet; they do not understand, and they are afraid to ask.

## The argument

1. **[The messianic secret](../reference.md#the-messianic-secret).** William Wrede, *Das Messiasgeheimnis in den Evangelien* (1901), gathered the silencing of demons (1:34, 3:12), the commands to the healed (1:44, 5:43, 7:36), private teaching (4:10–12) and 9:9. He argued that the secret is not a memory of Jesus's life but a theological construction. On his reading, the earliest tradition did not present Jesus's ministry as messianic, while the Church after Easter confessed him as Messiah. The secret reconciles the two: Jesus *was* Messiah but told no one, until the resurrection. 9:9's time limit is Wrede's key text. *In words:* the silence explains why no one said it before Easter.

2. **The critics.** Albert Schweitzer's *Quest* (1906) set Wrede's "thoroughgoing scepticism" against his own eschatological reading of Jesus ([3.1](03-01-the-quests.md)). Later critics took the motif apart. The commands are not all of one kind: some are obeyed, some are broken at once (1:45, 7:36), and some protect a miracle, not a title. And a Jesus who avoided "Messiah", a title with political and military freight under Rome, is historically plausible in its own right; James Dunn argued this in a much-cited article. Most scholars now keep Wrede's discovery (the secrecy is a Markan *theme*) and drop his explanation (that it covers an unmessianic history). *In words:* the secret is real as literature; whether it began as history or as theology is disputed.

3. **[Discipleship failure](../reference.md#discipleship-failure-in-mark).** "Are you ignorant of this, parable?" (4:13, Douay punctuation); "their heart was blinded" (6:52); "Do you not yet know nor understand?" (8:17). Then 9:32, the flight of "all" (14:50), and Peter's denial. Theodore Weeden (*Mark: Traditions in Conflict*, 1971) read this as polemic against a party the disciples stood for. Robert Tannehill replied that the reader is meant to *identify* with the disciples: their failure is a mirror, and Jesus's promise to go before them into Galilee (14:28, 16:7) means failure is not final.

4. **[The way section](../reference.md#the-way-section) (8:27–10:52).** Its frame word is *hodos*, "way" or "road": *en tē hodō*, "on the way" (8:27; again 9:33–34, 10:32, 10:52). Inside it, the same three steps come round three times: a passion prediction, a failure to understand, and teaching on discipleship as service (table below). The section is bracketed by two healings of blind men. The first is healed in two stages ("I see men, as it were trees, walking", 8:24); the second, Bartimaeus, "followed him in the way" (10:52). *In words:* the disciples see only half-clearly, like the first blind man, and the true disciple is the one who sees and follows Jesus to Jerusalem.

5. **[The brothers of Jesus](../reference.md#the-brothers-of-jesus).** "Is not this the carpenter, the son of Mary, the brother of James, and Joseph, and Jude, and Simon? are not also his sisters here with us?" (6:3). The Greek is *adelphos*; the Greek name the Douay renders "Joseph" is *Iōsēs* (Joses). Three readings:
   - **Helvidian:** children of Mary and Joseph. This is the plain sense of *adelphos* read alone. Helvidius argued for it about 383. John Meier (*A Marginal Jew*) judged it the most probable view for a historian bracketing faith.
   - **Epiphanian:** children of Joseph by an earlier marriage. It is in the second-century *Protoevangelium of James*, and Epiphanius defended it in the fourth century. Richard Bauckham has argued it is the strongest non-sibling reading.
   - **Hieronymian:** cousins. Jerome's reply to Helvidius argued that scriptural "brother" covers kin ([`patristics` 4.2](../../patristics/lessons/04-02-jerome.md)). It adds Mark 15:40: a *different* Mary is "the mother of James the Less and of Joseph" (Greek *Iōsēs* again). If these are the James and Joses of 6:3, their mother is not Jesus's mother.

   *In words:* the word *adelphos* can carry all three; the case for each turns on inference, not on the word alone.

6. **The endings and textual criticism.** Textual criticism compares manuscripts to recover the earliest text. Mark ends in four ways in the manuscripts:
   - at **16:8**, "for they were afraid", in the two great fourth-century codices, Sinaiticus and Vaticanus;
   - with the **longer ending** (16:9–20), in the great majority of later manuscripts and in the Douay;
   - with a two-sentence **shorter ending**, in a few manuscripts, most of them adding the longer ending after it;
   - with the longer ending expanded (the "Freer logion" in Codex W).

   The longer ending is old. Irenaeus, about 180, quotes 16:19 as what Mark says "towards the conclusion of his Gospel" (*Against Heresies* III.10, ANF). Most critical scholars still judge it not Mark's. Its vocabulary and style differ from his, 16:9 reintroduces Mary Magdalen as if she had not just been named, and it reads as a digest of appearances told in Luke and John. The fullest defence of its Markan authorship is J. W. Burgon's (1871); few now follow it. Among those who deny Mark wrote it, a further dispute: did Mark mean to stop at 16:8, or was his ending lost? The Greek ends *ephobounto gar*, literally "they were afraid, for". Ending a sentence on *gar* is rare, and ending a book on it rarer, but it is not ungrammatical.

**Mark the levels.**
- That the gospels are historical in character, handing on what Jesus really did and taught, is **conciliar teaching** in *Dei Verbum* 19 ([1.1](01-01-reading-the-new-testament-in-the-church.md) weighs it). It is compatible with an evangelist who arranges his material for a theme: the three stages of gospel formation expect exactly that.
- That Mark 16:9–20 is **canonical and inspired** falls under **defined dogma**. Trent's decree on the canon binds us to receive the books "entire with all their parts", as read in the Church and contained in the Vulgate ([`fundamental-theology` 3.3](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/03-03-the-canon-as-defined.md)). The Vulgate contains 16:9–20.
- **Who wrote** those verses, and whether Mark ended at 16:8, are **hypotheses** with no magisterial standing. Trent defined what the books contain, not who wrote each part.
- Wrede's theory, Weeden and Tannehill, the way-section analysis, and Markan priority itself are all **scholarly hypotheses**.
- That Mary remained ever-virgin is held by the tradition as **dogma**; its basis and level belong to [`ecclesiology-and-mariology`](../../ecclesiology-and-mariology/syllabus.md). The dogma excludes the Helvidian reading. It does **not** choose between the Epiphanian and Hieronymian readings; that choice is **freely disputed**.

Each prediction is more detailed than the last (the third adds the Gentiles, mocking, spitting, scourging), and each misunderstanding worse.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean): 4:10–12 read with the secret.** The twelve ask about the parable. They are told the mystery is "given" to them, while "to them that are without, all things are done in parables", so that seeing they may not perceive (quoting Isaiah 6:9–10). Method: ask who is *inside* and who is *without*, then watch what happens next. At 4:13 the insiders do not understand either. The division is real, but Mark immediately shows the insiders behaving like outsiders. So the parables text is part of the discipleship theme, not only a statement about the crowds. Wrede reads it as the secret extended to Jesus's teaching; Tannehill, as the reader let into a mystery the disciples miss. Neither reading is Church teaching.

**Example 2 (hard): does 16:8 fit Mark?** The strain. On literary grounds 16:8 fits almost too well. The women are told to speak and say nothing; the command to tell is disobeyed out of fear. That reverses the secrecy pattern, where people were told to be silent and spoke anyway (1:45, 7:36). And it ends on the failure theme, with the promise of 16:7 hanging open. On historical grounds the case is weaker than it looks. Mark's other predictions come true in the narrative, and a book promising "there you shall see him" without showing the meeting is unusual in ancient literature. Here the evidence is genuinely balanced. An inference to the best explanation ([`philosophical-method` 2.1](../../philosophical-method/lessons/02-01-inference-to-the-best-explanation.md)) does not close it. Whichever view you take, 16:9–20 remains canonical.

## Watch out

- **"The oldest manuscripts lack it, so it is not Scripture."** This treats a hypothesis about origin as if it settled canonicity. Trent defined the books "with all their parts"; the Church receives 16:9–20 whatever hand wrote it. The reverse error is to say that Catholics must hold that Mark wrote it. Authorship of a passage is not defined.
- **"The Church teaches they were cousins."** The dogma rules out the siblings reading. The Hieronymian and Epiphanian readings are both open to a Catholic. The West has favoured Jerome, the East Epiphanius.
- **"Messianic secret" does not mean Mark doubts that Jesus is Messiah.** Mark announces it in 1:1. Wrede's claim was about the pre-Markan *history*, not Mark's belief. Mixing up the theme and the theory muddles any answer.

## One-liner

> Mark hides the Messiah in plain sight until the cross, shows his disciples failing all the way there, and in the oldest copies ends with silence. Its canonical ending, however, is defined as Scripture whoever wrote it.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢)** *(Exegetical)* Mark 16:5–8 (Douay-Rheims):

> And entering into the sepulchre, they saw a young man sitting on the right side, clothed with a white robe: and they were astonished. Who saith to them: Be not affrighted; you seek Jesus of Nazareth, who was crucified. He is risen: he is not here. Behold the place where they laid him. But go, tell his disciples and Peter that he goeth before you into Galilee. There you shall see him, as he told you. But they going out, fled from the sepulchre: for a trembling and fear had seized them. And they said nothing to any man: for they were afraid.

(a) Which words in 16:7 tie this scene back to (i) Peter's denial and (ii) an earlier promise of Jesus? Give the earlier verse. (b) Name the two earlier Markan patterns that 16:8 reverses or continues, with one verse for each. **Two sentences per part.**

**P2 (🟡)** *(Exegetical)* Assign each claim its level: defined dogma, dogma held by the tradition without a defining act, freely disputed among Catholics, or scholarly hypothesis with no magisterial standing. **One line each**, naming the act where there is one.

1. Mark 16:9–20 is inspired Scripture.
2. Mark himself wrote Mark 16:9–20.
3. Mark's gospel originally ended at 16:8.
4. The "brothers" of Mark 6:3 were not children of Mary.
5. The "brothers" of Mark 6:3 were Jesus's cousins.

**P3 (🔴)** *(Exegetical)* An **invented** study-Bible note:

> "Since Sinaiticus and Vaticanus end at 16:8, modern Catholic scholarship has admitted that 16:9–20 is a later addition and not really part of the Bible. On the brothers of 6:3, the Church requires us to hold they were cousins, and Mark 15:40 proves it by naming their mother as another Mary."

Name the three errors and correct each. **Two sentences per error, 150 words or fewer in all.**

<details>
<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** (i) "**and Peter**": Peter is singled out by name after his denial (14:66–72), so the message includes the one who failed worst. (ii) "**as he told you**": it points back to 14:28, "after I shall be risen again, I will go before you into Galilee". Credit 16:7's "goeth before you into Galilee" as the phrase that matches.

**Must hit, strict (b):** it **reverses the secrecy pattern**. Before, people were told to be silent and spread the news anyway (1:44–45, or 7:36). Now the women are told to speak and say nothing. It **continues discipleship failure**: fear and flight, like "they were afraid to ask him" (9:32) or "all fled away" (14:50). Credit also the frightened, uncomprehending reaction to the second prediction (9:32).

**Wrong turns:** citing 8:31 for "as he told you". 8:31 predicts the resurrection, but not the going before them into Galilee. Treating 16:8 as the women disbelieving: the text gives fear ("trembling and fear"), not unbelief. Noticing the "young man" of 14:51 is a fair observation, but it answers neither part.

**Model answer:** (a) "And Peter" names the disciple who denied Jesus, so the risen Lord's message restores the one who failed worst. "As he told you" refers back to 14:28, where Jesus promised to go before them into Galilee after rising. (b) The secrecy pattern is inverted: in 1:44–45 a man told to say nothing broadcasts it, while here women told to speak say nothing. Discipleship failure continues: as the twelve "were afraid to ask him" (9:32) and fled (14:50), the women flee in fear.

**P2** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict:**
1. **Defined dogma**, by Trent Session IV, first decree on the canon: the books "entire with all their parts" as in the Vulgate, which contains 16:9–20.
2. **Scholarly hypothesis**, no magisterial standing; most critical scholars deny it.
3. **Scholarly hypothesis**, disputed even among those who deny 2, since the ending may have been lost.
4. **Dogma held by the tradition**: it follows from Mary's perpetual virginity, which the tradition holds as dogma without a single defining act (the Lateran Synod of 649 confesses her ever-virgin); its basis belongs to `ecclesiology-and-mariology`.
5. **Freely disputed** among Catholics: the dogma leaves the Hieronymian and the Epiphanian readings both open.

**Wrong turns:** grading 2 as dogma because 1 is. Trent defined canonicity, not the human author. Grading 5 at the level of 4, which confuses the conclusion the dogma requires with one of the two readings compatible with it. Calling 1 a hypothesis because the manuscript evidence is disputed. Answering 4 as "freely disputed" confuses the question whether they were Mary's children with the question whose children they were.

**Model answer:** 1 defined (Trent IV, "with all their parts"); 2 hypothesis, majority against; 3 hypothesis; 4 dogma, via perpetual virginity, not a defined proposition; 5 freely disputed: cousins or Joseph's sons.

**P3** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict:** (1) **Canonicity confused with authorship.** Scholars can judge the verses non-Markan, but the Church receives them as Scripture under Trent's "with all their parts"; "not really part of the Bible" is false for a Catholic. (2) **Overstated teaching.** The dogma excludes children of Mary. It does not require cousins, since the Epiphanian reading (sons of Joseph by an earlier marriage) is equally open. (3) **"Proves" overstates an inference.** 15:40 supports the Hieronymian reading only if its James and Joses are the men of 6:3, which is an identification, not something the text states. Even then, it tells against Mary being their mother without deciding between cousins and step-brothers.

**Wrong turns:** "correcting" (1) by asserting that Mark wrote 16:9–20. That trades one level error for its mirror image. Accepting (2) because Jerome's reading prevailed in the West.

**Model answer:** First, the note turns a question of authorship into one of canon: most scholars think Mark did not write 16:9–20, but Trent received the books "with all their parts", so the verses remain Scripture. Second, the Church's dogma rules out that the brothers were Mary's sons, but it leaves open whether they were cousins or Joseph's sons by an earlier marriage. Third, 15:40 names a different Mary as mother of a James and a Joses, which supports the non-sibling view only if these are the same men as in 6:3. Even then it cannot decide between cousins and step-brothers.

</details>

## Flashback

**From Lesson [1.4](01-04-the-synoptic-problem.md) (The Synoptic problem):** *(Exegetical.)* The woman with the flow of blood (Douay-Rheims, with the Greek where it decides):

| | Mark 5:25–27 | Matthew 9:20 | Luke 8:43–44 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Her past | "had suffered many things from many physicians; and had spent all that she had, and was nothing the better, but rather worse" | not present | "had bestowed all her substance on physicians and could not be healed by any" |
| Her touch | "touched his garment" (*tou himatiou autou*) | "touched the hem of his garment" (*tou kraspedou tou himatiou autou*) | "touched the hem of his garment" (*tou kraspedou tou himatiou autou*) |

(a) Explain the "past" row on Markan priority and then on Griesbach, and say which way the harder-reading argument points. (b) Name the minor agreement in the table. Which hypothesis must explain it away, and which two explain it easily? **Two sentences per part.**

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):** On **Markan priority**, Luke keeps the physicians but drops Mark's barb that she "was nothing the better, but rather worse", and Matthew cuts the whole line. On **Griesbach**, Mark, working from Luke, sharpened a neutral line into a jab at the doctors and lengthened it. The harder-reading argument favours **Mark as the source**: an unflattering line is easier to see softened than invented.
**Must hit, strict (b):** Matthew and Luke agree against Mark in adding **"hem"** (*kraspedon*, the fringe or tassel of the cloak). It costs the **Two-Source hypothesis**, since two independent editors of Mark supply the same word. **Farrer** explains it (Luke read it in Matthew), and so does **Griesbach** (Mark dropped it).
**Wrong turns:** calling "touched ... garment" itself the agreement; all three have it, and the agreement is the added word. Treating the agreement as a refutation of Markan priority: Farrer keeps Mark first. Credit, not required: Q's defenders can point to Mark 6:56, where Mark himself writes "the hem of his garment", as a phrase both editors could have picked up independently. Credit also noting that some early manuscripts of Luke lack the physicians clause. On that text, Matthew and Luke also agree in omitting it.
**Model answer:** (a) If Mark came first, Luke kept the physicians and dropped Mark's sardonic "rather worse", and Matthew abbreviated. If Mark came last, he turned Luke's plain line into mockery. The harder reading, Mark's, is the likelier source. (b) Both Matthew and Luke add "hem" (*kraspedon*) to Mark's "garment". That is awkward for the Two-Source view, and easy for Farrer (Luke copies Matthew) and Griesbach (Mark omits).

</details>
