# The New Testament · Lesson 2.5: The theology of John

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 2: The four gospels · Builds on: [2.4 John and the Synoptics](02-04-john-and-the-synoptics.md), [1.2 Second Temple Judaism](01-02-second-temple-judaism.md) · Unlocks: [3.4 The passion and resurrection narratives](03-04-the-passion-and-resurrection-narratives.md), [6.4 Peter, Jude, and the letters of John](06-04-peter-jude-and-the-letters-of-john.md)

## Why this matters

[2.4](02-04-john-and-the-synoptics.md) asked how John differs from the other three gospels. This lesson asks what John is *arguing*. It tells you in verse 1 who Jesus is and in 20:31 why it was written; everything between is a trial of who recognizes him. Four of its words carry the argument: *logos*, *egō eimi*, *hoi Ioudaioi*, and the tense of one verb in its last sentence. The last two still decide how the gospel is translated and preached.

## The idea

Read John as a play whose audience has been handed the cast list. The prologue tells the reader what no character knows: the man from Nazareth is the Word who was with God. So every scene is ironic. Nicodemus and the crowds misunderstand him; his opponents accuse him of making himself God (5:18, 10:33), and the reader knows the charge is true.

So Jesus names himself in "I am" sayings, because the book's question is identity; the hostile crowd gets one label, and the label matters; and the cross is told not as defeat reversed at Easter but as the hour of glory.

## Source

John 1:1–5, 14 and 20:30–31, Douay–Rheims (Challoner):

> 1:1 In the beginning was the Word: and the Word was with God: and the Word was God. 2 The same was in the beginning with God. 3 All things were made by him: and without him was made nothing that was made. 4 In him was life: and the life was the light of men. 5 And the light shineth in darkness: and the darkness did not comprehend it. ...
> 14 And the Word was made flesh and dwelt among us (and we saw his glory, the glory as it were of the only begotten of the Father), full of grace and truth. ...
> 20:30 Many other signs also did Jesus in the sight of his disciples, which are not written in this book. 31 But these are written, that you may believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God: and that believing, you may have life in his name.

The lesson's own translation of 1:1: *In the beginning the Word already was; and the Word was turned toward God; and what God was, the Word was.* The imperfect *ēn*, "was", never says the Word came to be. *Pros ton theon* is "toward" or "in the presence of" God. *Theos* has no article because it is the predicate: the Word has what God is, without being the whole of what *ho theos*, with the article, names in the clause before. The Trinitarian reading belongs to [`trinity-and-god` 2.2](../../trinity-and-god/lessons/02-02-the-father-and-the-son-in-the-new-testament.md). Verse 14: *And the Word became flesh and pitched his tent among us*; *eskēnōsen* is from *skēnē*, "tent", the tabernacle word.

## The argument

1. **[The Word in the prologue](../reference.md#the-word-in-the-prologue).** *Logos* means word, reason, account. Four backgrounds are proposed. For **Heraclitus and the Stoics** it was the rational order of the cosmos. For **Philo of Alexandria**, a Greek-speaking Jew of John's century, it was God's instrument in creation; C. H. Dodd (*The Interpretation of the Fourth Gospel*, 1953) weighted this background heavily. **Wisdom** is present at creation (Proverbs 8:22–23), God "made all things with thy word" (Wisdom 9:1), and Wisdom is told to make her dwelling in Jacob (Sirach 24:13), the image John's *eskēnōsen* echoes. **Genesis 1** opens "In the beginning" and creates by speaking, and the Aramaic Targums speak of God acting through his *memra*, "Word"; Daniel Boyarin has argued that John draws on a Jewish Logos theology of this kind. Brown's commentary put Wisdom first, and most commentators now treat the Jewish scriptures as primary, in a word Greek ears would also hear. *In words:* what no background supplies is verse 14. Wisdom tents in Israel, but nowhere does she *become flesh*.

2. **[The I am sayings](../reference.md#the-i-am-sayings).** Seven sayings have a predicate (tabled below), each taking an image Israel already applied to God or itself: manna, the shepherd of Ezekiel 34, the vine of Isaiah 5. The **absolute** sayings have none: 8:24, 8:28, 13:19, 18:5–8 and, sharpest, 8:58, where the aorist *genesthai*, "came to be", is set against the present *eimi*: "before Abraham came to be, I am." The hearers take up stones (8:59). Most commentators hear the divine self-naming of Exodus 3:14 and the Greek Isaiah (43:10). The Douay–Rheims prints "I am he" at 8:24, 8:28, 13:19 and 18:5, hiding the bare Greek; at 18:6 the arresting party "went backward and fell to the ground". *In words:* the predicate sayings say what he gives; the absolute ones say who he is.

3. **John 6 as text.** Down to 6:50 the bread of life is received by *believing* (6:35, 6:40, 6:47); from 6:51 the bread is "my flesh", received by *eating*, and at 6:54 the verb changes from the ordinary *phagein* to *trōgō*, a blunt verb for chewing. **Bultmann** assigned 6:51c–58 to a later "ecclesiastical redactor". **Brown** read a sapiential first half (Jesus's teaching as bread) and a eucharistic second half, both part of the finished gospel. A **metaphorical** reading appeals to 6:63 ("the flesh profiteth nothing"). Trent declined to rule on the exegesis: it denied that communion under both kinds is commanded there, "however according to the various interpretations of holy Fathers and Doctors it be understood" (Session XXI, ch. 1, 1562, Waterworth). Eucharistic doctrine is ceded to [`sacraments-and-liturgy`](../../sacraments-and-liturgy/syllabus.md). Numbering here is modern; the Douay–Rheims runs one ahead from 6:51b, so 6:51b–58 is its 6:52–59.

4. **[The Jews in John](../reference.md#the-jews-in-john).** *Hoi Ioudaioi* occurs some seventy times in John, a handful of times in each Synoptic, and its sense shifts. Neutral: "the pasch of the Jews" (2:13). **Judeans**: Jesus "would not walk in Judea, because the Jews sought to kill him" (7:1); "many of the Jews" came from Jerusalem to comfort Martha (11:18–19). **The authorities**: at 1:19 "the Jews" send priests and Levites. Often simply the hostile party. Three translation strategies follow. Urban von Wahlde argued that most hostile uses denote the authorities. Malcolm Lowe and later Steve Mason argued that *Ioudaios* in antiquity was an ethnic-geographic term, best rendered "Judean". Adele Reinhartz (*Cast Out of the Covenant*, 2018) keeps "the Jews": the rhetoric should be faced, not softened, and "Judeans" writes Jews out of the story. Brown and Martyn heard in the hostility a community put "out of the synagogue" (9:22), a hypothesis weighed in [2.4](02-04-john-and-the-synoptics.md). [*Nostra Aetate* 4](../reference.md#nostra-aetate-4-on-the-passion) (28 October 1965), paraphrased: the Jewish authorities and those who followed them pressed for Jesus's death, but the passion cannot be charged against all Jews then living or against Jews today, and catechesis and preaching must not present the Jews as rejected or accursed as though scripture taught it.

5. **[The hour of glorification](../reference.md#the-hour-of-glorification).** "My hour is not yet come" (2:4) until "The hour is come that the Son of man should be glorified" (12:23), as the passion begins; then "Father, the hour is come. Glorify thy Son" (17:1). *Hypsoō*, "lift up", means both crucify and exalt (3:14, 8:28, 12:32), and John glosses it: "signifying what death he should die" (12:33). There is no agony in the garden, only "Father, save me from this hour. But for this cause I came" (12:27). The last word is *tetelestai* (19:30), in the lesson's translation *it has been brought to its end*, and then *paredōken to pneuma*, "he handed over the spirit". *In words:* in John the cross is the exaltation.

6. **[The purpose statement of John](../reference.md#the-purpose-statement-of-john).** In 20:31 the manuscripts split. The present *pisteuēte*, "that you may *go on* believing", is read by Sinaiticus's first hand, Vaticanus, Θ, 0250 and probably P66. The aorist *pisteusēte*, "that you may *come to* believe", is read by a corrector of Sinaiticus, A, C, D, L, W, Ψ and most later manuscripts. The critical text brackets the sigma: *pisteu[s]ēte*. The present suggests a book for believers, the aorist one for converts. D. A. Carson (*JBL*, 1987) reported that most recent commentators preferred the present, then argued that the tense cannot settle the purpose, since John uses both tenses for coming to faith and for staying in it (1:7, 11:15, 6:29). *In words:* the verse states the goal and leaves open whose faith.

**Mark the levels.** That Jesus is true God is **defined dogma** (rung 1 on [the ladder](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-04-the-ladder-of-doctrinal-authority.md)), and the prologue is one of its witnesses; but no council defined the exegesis of John 1:1 or 8:58, and whether Jesus spoke 8:58 in those words is a historical question for Module 3. The gospels' **historical character** is Church teaching (Dei Verbum 19, at the level set in [1.1](01-01-reading-the-new-testament-in-the-church.md)), and the same paragraph says the evangelists shaped their material, so a crafted discourse does not contradict it. *Nostra Aetate* 4 is an ecumenical council's authentic teaching, not a definition (rung 3): it binds how John is preached, not how *Ioudaioi* is translated. Everything else is **scholarly hypothesis** with no magisterial standing either way: the backgrounds of *logos*, the redaction of John 6, "Judeans" or "the Jews", the original reading of 20:31 ([teaching vs hypothesis](../reference.md#church-teaching-vs-hypothesis)). Whether 6:51–58 is eucharistic is exegesis; Trent left the interpretations open.

## The I am sayings, sorted

| Kind | Verse | Douay–Rheims | Greek |
|---|---|---|---|
| Predicate | 6:35 | I am the bread of life | *egō eimi* + predicate |
| Predicate | 8:12 | I am the light of the world | *egō eimi* + predicate |
| Predicate | 10:7, 10:11 | I am the door / the good shepherd | *egō eimi* + predicate |
| Predicate | 11:25 | I am the resurrection and the life | *egō eimi* + predicate |
| Predicate | 14:6 | I am the way, and the truth, and the life | *egō eimi* + predicate |
| Predicate | 15:1 | I am the true vine | *egō eimi* + predicate |
| Absolute | 8:24, 8:28, 13:19 | I am he | bare *egō eimi* |
| Absolute | 18:5–8 | I am he (and they fall back) | bare *egō eimi* |
| Absolute | 8:58 | before Abraham was made, I AM | *prin Abraam genesthai egō eimi* |

Not every *egō eimi* is a divine claim: the healed blind man says the same two words (9:9), meaning "I'm the one". Context, not the words alone, decides.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean): *hoi Ioudaioi* in 9:22.** "These things his parents said, because they feared the Jews: for the Jews had already agreed among themselves that if any man should confess him to be Christ, he should be put out of the synagogue." The parents and their son are Jews, so "the Jews" who can expel people from the synagogue are those with authority over it. The verse itself justifies "the Jewish authorities"; read as an ethnic label, it has Jews fearing the Jews.

**Example 2 (hard): 8:31–59.** The addressees are "those Jews who believed him" (8:31); the same audience is told "You are of your father the devil" (8:44) and takes up stones (8:59). These are believers turned hostile, not authorities, so von Wahlde's rendering strains, and "Judeans" (the scene is in the Temple) softens nothing. This is Reinhartz's strongest case: some passages cannot be translated into harmlessness. What a Catholic reader does with them is governed not by a translation choice but by *Nostra Aetate*: 8:44 is polemic inside a first-century argument among Jews, and cannot become a charge against the Jewish people.

## Watch out

- **Hypothesis stated as teaching.** "The Church teaches John was written to convert unbelievers" turns a textual variant and a scholarly reading into doctrine.
- **Teaching dissolved into hypothesis.** "The discourses are John's composition, so Christ's divinity is only Johannine theology" confuses a literary question with a rung-1 doctrine.
- **Arguing from the English "I am he".** Check the Greek first, and do not overcorrect: 9:9 has the same two words with no divine claim.
- ***Nostra Aetate* is not a translation rule.** It governs catechesis and preaching; keeping "the Jews" does not defy it, and "Judeans" is not required by it.

## One-liner

> John tells you who Jesus is in verse 1, lets the characters find out, and calls the cross the hour of glory; read *egō eimi* and *hoi Ioudaioi* in Greek and in context before you preach either.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢)** *(Exegetical)* Sort each saying into (A) a predicate "I am" saying of Jesus, (B) an absolute *egō eimi* of Jesus, or (C) an *egō eimi* that is ordinary self-identification. For every item translated "I am he" in the Douay–Rheims, say whether the Greek has a word for "he". One line each.

1. 8:12 "I am the light of the world."
2. 9:9 (the healed man) "I am he."
3. 13:19 "that you may believe that I am he."
4. 15:1 "I am the true vine."
5. 18:5 "Jesus saith to them: I am he."
6. 8:58 "before Abraham was made, I AM."

**P2 (🟡)** *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b))* Douay–Rheims:

> 5:16 Therefore did the Jews persecute Jesus, because he did these things on the sabbath.
> 11:45 Many therefore of the Jews, who were come to Mary and Martha and had seen the things that Jesus did, believed in him. 46 But some of them went to the Pharisees and told them the things that Jesus had done.

(a) Both verses have *hoi Ioudaioi*. Translate it in each and justify each choice from the context (5:10–15; 11:18–19, 46). **Two sentences per verse.** (b) Should a translation render *hoi Ioudaioi* the same way throughout John, or vary it by context? Any verdict. **100 words or fewer.**

**P3 (🔴)** *(Exegetical)* An **invented** study-bible note on John 20:

> "John 20:31 tells us the Church's teaching on why this Gospel was written: to convert unbelievers, 'that you may come to believe.' And because the Church defines Christ's divinity, Catholics must hold that Jesus spoke 'before Abraham was made, I AM' in exactly those words. Throughout this Gospel 'the Jews' are those who rejected Christ, and they carry the guilt of his death."

Find the three errors. For each, name the error, correct it, and give the level of whatever Church teaching is involved. **Two sentences per error.**

<details>
<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict:** (1) **A**. (2) **C**. The healed man identifies himself ("I'm the one"). The Greek is bare *egō eimi* with no "he", but the context makes it ordinary. (3) **B**. No "he" in the Greek; the Douay–Rheims supplies it. (4) **A**. (5) **B**. No "he" in the Greek, and 18:6's falling back shows John means more than "that's me". (6) **B**, the sharpest: the aorist *genesthai* ("came to be") is set against the present *eimi*. The Douay–Rheims prints no "he" here.

**Wrong turns:** filing 9:9 under B because the words match. The sense comes from context, not from the two words. Filing 18:5 under C because the soldiers asked for "Jesus of Nazareth" and the answer is literally an identification. John's narration in 18:6 is there to stop that reading.

**Model answer:** 1 A. 2 C, bare *egō eimi* used as "I'm the one". 3 B, "he" supplied. 4 A. 5 B, "he" supplied, and 18:6 confirms it. 6 B, *genesthai* against *eimi*.

**P2** *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b))*

**Must hit, strict (a):** **5:16**: the hostile religious authorities in Jerusalem. The healed man, himself a Jew, reports to "the Jews" (5:15) who had already enforced the sabbath on him (5:10), so they are an authority, not the people at large. **11:45**: Judeans, the people from Jerusalem and its neighbourhood who came to mourn at Bethany (11:18–19). They are not the authorities: many believe, and some go and report *to* the Pharisees (11:46), which separates them from the authorities. Acceptable renderings: 5:16 "the Jewish authorities" (or "the Judean leaders"); 11:45 "the Judeans", "the people from Jerusalem", or "the Jews" if the mourners are explained. The key is that the two senses differ.

**Must hit, any verdict (b):** state the cost of each option. **Uniform** "the Jews" keeps the text's rhetoric and its one word visible, but risks readers applying the hostile uses to all Jews. **Varying** protects readers but makes the translator decide who is culpable, and it can hide the text's polemic (Reinhartz's objection). Say that *Nostra Aetate* 4 governs preaching and catechesis, not translation, whichever option you choose.

**Wrong turns:** rendering 11:45 as "the Jewish authorities", which 11:46 contradicts. Answering (b) as if *Nostra Aetate* required "Judeans". It does not.

**Model answer (b), one of several:** Vary it. A translation is read without footnotes, and "the Jews persecuted Jesus" (5:16) will be heard as an ethnic charge. The context shows it means the sabbath-enforcing authorities of 5:10. The cost is real: the translator now decides who is culpable, and 8:31–44 cannot be softened honestly. So I would vary it only where the context fixes the referent, keep "the Jews" where it does not, and footnote the choice. *Nostra Aetate* 4 does not settle this. It governs how the text is preached.

**P3** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict:** (1) **Hypothesis stated as teaching.** The purpose of the gospel is not Church teaching. "Come to believe" follows the aorist *pisteusēte*, while Sinaiticus's first hand and Vaticanus read the present *pisteuēte* ("go on believing"). Carson argues that neither tense settles the purpose anyway. No level: a textual and scholarly question. (2) **Overstatement of what a dogma requires.** Christ's divinity is **defined dogma (rung 1)**, but no act defines that 8:58 is a verbatim report. Dei Verbum 19 teaches the gospels' historical character while saying the evangelists selected and explained, so it does not require verbatim speeches (Church teaching, at the level set in 1.1). (3) **Collective guilt, and a misreading.** *Nostra Aetate* 4 (authentic conciliar teaching, **rung 3**, not a definition) says the passion cannot be charged against all Jews then or now. The note also misreads John: *hoi Ioudaioi* varies in sense, and at 11:45 "the Jews" believe in him.

**Wrong turns:** calling Christ's divinity only probable in John in order to fix error (2). That overcorrects into the opposite level error. Ranking *Nostra Aetate* 4 as a definition (rung 1): overstatement is also an error. Missing the exegetical half of (3), since the note is wrong about the text as well as about teaching.

**Model answer:** (1) The Church has no teaching on why John was written. "Come to believe" is one side of a textual split (aorist vs Vaticanus's present), and even the tense does not decide the purpose. (2) Christ's divinity is defined dogma, but no definition makes 8:58 a verbatim transcript. Dei Verbum 19 affirms the gospels' historical character while allowing the evangelists' shaping. (3) *Nostra Aetate* 4, the council's authentic but non-definitive teaching, rejects charging the passion to all Jews then or now. And "the Jews" in John are not uniformly hostile: at 11:45 many believe.

</details>

## Flashback

**From Lesson [2.3](02-03-luke.md) (Luke):** *(Exegetical.)* Here is a saying that Matthew and Luke share but Mark lacks, set in parallel (Douay–Rheims):

| Luke 11:20 | Matthew 12:28 |
|---|---|
| But if I by the **finger** of God cast out devils, doubtless the kingdom of God is come upon you. | But if I by the **Spirit** of God cast out devils, then is the kingdom of God come upon you. |

Three facts. (i) Elsewhere Luke *adds* the Spirit to a shared saying: in Matthew 7:11 the Father gives "good things", in Luke 11:13 "the good Spirit". (ii) Matthew 12 has just quoted Isaiah 42, "I will put my spirit upon him" (12:18), and soon warns against "the blasphemy of the Spirit" (12:31). (iii) "This is the finger of God" is what Pharaoh's magicians say at the third plague (Exodus 8:19). On the Two-Source hypothesis, which word stood in Q? Give two reasons from these facts, then the strongest reason for the other word. **80 words or fewer.**

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict:** **"Finger" stood in Q**, which is the majority view. Reason from (i): Luke's habit is to add the Spirit, so an editor who writes it into 11:13 is unlikely to have deleted it here. Reason from (ii): Matthew had a motive to change the word, because "Spirit" ties the saying to the Isaiah quotation before it and to the blasphemy warning after it. The other side, from (iii): Luke had a motive too. "Finger of God" makes the exorcisms echo Moses's signs in Egypt, so "Spirit" could be original and "finger" Luke's allusion. Mark the level: which word Q had is a scholarly hypothesis, the majority is not proof, and the Church teaches neither.

**Wrong turns:** choosing "Spirit" because it is the richer theological word; the question is which change an editor with known habits would make, not which reading is better. Giving no counter-argument, or treating the majority as settled. Claiming that either answer threatens the gospels' historical character: Dei Verbum 19 expects the evangelists to shape the sayings they hand on.

**Model answer:** "Finger" stood in Q. Luke adds the Spirit even where Q lacked it (11:13), so he would hardly delete it here. Matthew had reason to add it, linking the saying to "I will put my spirit upon him" (12:18) and the blasphemy against the Spirit (12:31). Against me: "finger of God" echoes the Exodus plagues, which would give Luke his own motive for changing "Spirit". Either way it is a hypothesis, and the Church teaches neither.

</details>
