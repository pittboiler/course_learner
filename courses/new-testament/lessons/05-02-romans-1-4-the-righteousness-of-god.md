# The New Testament · Lesson 5.2: Romans 1–4: the righteousness of God

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 5: Justification in Galatians and Romans · Builds on: [5.1 Galatians](05-01-galatians-faith-law-and-the-gentiles.md) · Unlocks: [5.3 Romans 5–11](05-03-romans-5-11-adam-spirit-israel.md), [5.4 The New Perspective](05-04-the-new-perspective-on-paul.md)

## Why this matters

Late in life Luther told the story of a phrase he had hated: "the righteousness of God" in Romans 1:17. He had heard it as the justice by which God punishes; on his own telling, everything changed when he read it as the righteousness God *gives*. (When that happened is itself disputed; dates from 1513 to 1519 have their defenders.) The two Greek words, *dikaiosynē theou*, still divide exegetes, and not along confessional lines. Romans is Galatians rewritten, calmer and in order, for a church Paul did not found, most likely from Corinth in the mid-50s. Chapters 1–4 argue that Jew and Gentile are alike under sin and made righteous the same way.

## The idea

Take the English phrase "the love of God". It can mean God's love for us, or our love for God. Greek genitives are just as open, and *dikaiosynē theou* ("righteousness of God"; the Douay says "justice of God") has three live readings. Picture a judge:

1. **The judge's own quality.** He is just; he keeps faith with the law and with the people he serves. *Righteousness* here is an **attribute** of God.
2. **The verdict he hands down.** The acquitted man walks out with a status that is not his own achievement. *Righteousness* is a **gift** that comes *from* God.
3. **The judge stepping in.** He does not just rule; he acts to set things right, rescuing the wronged. *Righteousness* is God's **saving action**, a power at work in the world.

Paul uses all three somewhere. Which one his thesis (1:17) and his hinge (3:21–26) mean changes what "justified" means everywhere else in the letter.

## Source

Romans 3:21–26 (Douay–Rheims, Challoner), the hinge of chapters 1–4:

> But now, without the law, the justice of God is made manifest, being witnessed by the law and the prophets. Even the justice of God, by faith of Jesus Christ, unto all, and upon all them that believe in him: for there is no distinction. For all have sinned and do need the glory of God. Being justified freely by his grace, through the redemption that is in Christ Jesus, Whom God hath proposed to be a propitiation, through faith in his blood, to the shewing of his justice, for the remission of former sins, Through the forbearance of God, for the shewing of his justice in this time: that he himself may be just and the justifier of him who is of the faith of Jesus Christ.

"Justice" is *dikaiosynē* throughout; "just" is *dikaios*, and "justifier" is *dikaiounta*, "the one making righteous". Paul uses one Greek word family where English splits into *justice*, *righteousness* and *justify*. "Propitiation" is *hilastērion*. "By faith of Jesus Christ" is the *pistis Christou* phrase of [5.1](05-01-galatians-faith-law-and-the-gentiles.md), whose objective and subjective readings were set out there ([pistis Christou](../reference.md#pistis-christou)).

## The argument

1. **The thesis (1:16–17).** The gospel is God's power for salvation "to the Jew first and to the Greek", because in it *dikaiosynē theou* "is revealed", "from faith unto faith". Habakkuk 2:4 backs it: "The just man liveth by faith." *In words:* Paul's gospel is the announcement that God's righteousness has appeared, and it is received by faith.
2. **All under sin (1:18–3:20).** The next verse uses the same verb: "the wrath of God is revealed" (1:18). The Gentiles suppressed what they knew of God (1:18–32). The Jew who judges them does the same things (chapter 2). So "both Jews and Greeks ... are all under sin" (3:9). The indictment closes with "by the works of the law no flesh shall be justified" (3:20), the *erga nomou* of [5.1](05-01-galatians-faith-law-and-the-gentiles.md) ([works of the law](../reference.md#works-of-the-law)). *In words:* before the good news, a level field of guilt.
3. **The three readings, with their champions.** The **attribute** reading is God's own uprightness, and in N. T. Wright's version, his faithfulness to his covenant promises. It leans on 3:3–5, where "the justice of God" stands beside "the faith of God" (*pistis tou theou*, his faithfulness) and God's truthfulness. The **gift** reading is Rudolf Bultmann's (*Theology of the New Testament*). The genitive names the source: a righteous standing that comes from God and is given to faith. It leans on Philippians 3:9, where Paul writes out "the justice which is of God" (*tēn ek theou dikaiosynēn*). Augustine had read Romans 3:21 this way, and Luther later said he found his own reading confirmed there: the phrase means

   > not that whereby He is Himself righteous, but that with which He endows man when He justifies the ungodly. (*On the Spirit and the Letter* 15, Holmes, NPNF I/5)

   The **saving-action** reading is Ernst Käsemann's, in his 1961 essay on the righteousness of God in Paul. It is the gift, but a gift that carries its Giver's power: God reclaiming his creation, not just a status handed to individuals. It leans on the parallel "revealed" of 1:17–18. *In words:* quality, status, or act. Each reading has a verse the others must explain away.
4. **The hinge (3:21–26).** "But now": God's righteousness is shown "without the law" and "unto all ... that believe". God "hath proposed" Christ as a [hilastērion](../reference.md#hilasterion). In the Greek Old Testament that word names the gold cover of the ark, which the Douay calls the "propitiatory", sprinkled with blood on the Day of Atonement (Leviticus 16:14). Its only other New Testament use, Hebrews 9:5, names that same object. Whether Paul means "mercy seat" or more generally "means of atonement" is disputed. Whether the right English is expiation or propitiation belongs to [`christology` 5.1](../../christology/lessons/05-01-the-paschal-mystery-and-the-threefold-office.md), which notes the point that matters here: God is the subject, the one who provides the atonement. The purpose clause joins two of the readings: God acts "that he himself may be just and the justifier" (3:26). His own righteousness (attribute) is shown in his making others righteous (action and gift). *In words:* 3:26 is why many exegetes refuse to pick only one reading.
5. **Boasting excluded (3:27–31).** "Where is then thy boasting? It is excluded", not by a "law of works" but "by the law of faith". The reason: "it is one God that justifieth circumcision by faith and uncircumcision through faith" (3:30), an echo of the Shema ([`trinity-and-god` 2.1](../../trinity-and-god/lessons/02-01-the-one-god-of-israel.md)). Paul ends by denying that faith destroys the Law (3:31). *Whose* boast is excluded, the Jew's boast in a privilege or anyone's boast in achievement, is the hinge of [5.4](05-04-the-new-perspective-on-paul.md). *In words:* one God, so one way into righteousness for everyone.
6. **Abraham reckoned (chapter 4).** Genesis 15:6: "Abraham believed God: and it was reputed to him unto justice" (4:3). "Reputed" is *elogisthē*, from *logizomai*, "reckon, credit to an account". It is a bookkeeper's verb, and 4:4 makes the ledger explicit: to the worker, pay is reckoned "not ... according to grace but according to debt" (*opheilēma*). Then chronology: the reckoning came *before* Abraham's circumcision, so he is father of believers on both sides of that line (4:9–12). The same verb opens 3:28, "we account" (*logizometha*). What God's reckoning *does* is the classic fork ([reckoning in Romans 4](../reference.md#reckoning-in-romans-4)). Does God credit a righteousness the believer does not have, or declare what he thereby makes true? The Douay's own note on 4:3 takes the second line: God "reputeth nothing otherwise than it is". *In words:* the verb is agreed; its effect is the dispute.
7. **Luther's "alone" (3:28).** The Greek has *pistei ... chōris ergōn nomou*, "by faith, apart from works of law". Luther's 1522 German New Testament added *allein*: "by faith alone". In his *Sendbrief vom Dolmetschen* (1530), written at Coburg, he admitted that the word is not in the Greek or the Latin. He defended it on two grounds. German idiom needs *allein* when one thing is affirmed and another denied, and Paul's meaning demands it anyway. He also claimed Ambrose and Augustine as precedents. Fitzmyer's Anchor Bible commentary, drawing on a list Bellarmine had compiled, names earlier Greek and Latin writers who glossed Paul with "alone", Ambrosiaster and Aquinas among them. Fitzmyer then asks whether Luther meant by "alone" what they meant. That question marks [Luther and alone in Romans 3:28](../reference.md#luther-and-alone-in-romans-328) as **history of interpretation**, not a textual variant.

**Mark the levels.** Trent's decree on justification (Session VI) is **defined dogma** (rung 1 of [the ladder](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-04-the-ladder-of-doctrinal-authority.md)) on what it defines. Justification is gratuitous and faith is its beginning (ch. 8). Its formal cause is "the justice of God, not that whereby He Himself is just, but that whereby He maketh us just" (ch. 7, Waterworth). That is Augustine's formula, and canon 11 rejects justification by the *sole* imputation of Christ's justice. But a council borrowing a phrase is not a council exegeting a verse. Trent does not define the grammar of Romans 1:17, what *hilastērion* denotes, or how to read *pistis Christou*. Canon 9 condemns a *sense* of "faith alone", the one where nothing else is required to co-operate. It does not condemn the word. The three readings of *dikaiosynē theou*, "mercy seat", and the Old and New Perspective answers to "whose boast?" are **scholarly hypotheses** with no magisterial standing either way ([church teaching vs hypothesis](../reference.md#church-teaching-vs-hypothesis)). The doctrine itself belongs to [`creation-grace-last-things`](../../creation-grace-last-things/syllabus.md).

## Three readings of the righteousness of God

| Reading | Genitive | Defender (paraphrased) | Verse it leans on | 3:26 read as |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Attribute | subjective / possessive: God's own | Wright: God's covenant faithfulness | 3:3–5 (justice beside God's faithfulness and truth) | God proves himself righteous |
| Gift | of source: from God | Bultmann; Augustine's gloss | Phil 3:9 (*ek theou*, "of God") | God makes the believer righteous |
| Saving action | subjective, but active | Käsemann: gift with the character of power | 1:17–18 (righteousness and wrath both "revealed") | God acts to set the world right |

Card: [righteousness of God](../reference.md#righteousness-of-god). Many readers, following 3:26, combine rows; what matters is *which verse* makes a reader choose one.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean): Romans 3:5.** "But if our injustice commend the justice of God, what shall we say? Is God unjust, who executeth wrath?" Run the method: find the phrase, then let its neighbours choose the row. Here the neighbours are "the faith of God" (3:3, God's faithfulness) and "the truth of God" (3:7). All three name qualities God has, set against human faithlessness. God does not give us his justice here; our injustice *shows off* his. **Row 1, attribute**, and nobody in the debate disputes it. So Paul *can* use the phrase for God's own quality inside this very argument.

**Example 2 (hard): Romans 1:17.** Now the neighbours pull apart. "Revealed" is matched by "the wrath of God is revealed" in 1:18. Wrath is God's own act, so the parallel favours **row 3** or row 1. But "from faith unto faith" and Habakkuk's "the just man liveth by faith" are about the *human* who ends up righteous, and that favours **row 2**. Philippians 3:9 spells out *ek theou*, "from God". Romans 1:17 does not, and whether that silence matters is exactly what the sides argue over. The method gives no clean answer here. The honest report is that 1:17 is the contested case and 3:5 is not.

## Watch out

- **"The Church teaches that *dikaiosynē theou* in 1:17 means infused righteousness."** No act defines that exegesis. Trent's chapter 7 uses Augustine's formula for the *formal cause of justification*; it does not rule on the grammar of 1:17. Promoting a reading to dogma is a level error.
- **The reverse error:** "that justification is a free gift is one scholarly reading of Romans." Its gratuity is **defined** (Trent VI, ch. 8). What remains open is how the phrases are parsed, not whether grace comes first.
- **"Luther invented 'faith alone'" and "Trent condemned the words 'faith alone'"** are both false. Earlier writers used *sola*, and canon 9 condemns a sense of the phrase. What Luther *did* do was add the word to a translation, and he said so himself.
- **Hilastērion does not mean God's mind was changed.** Paul makes God the one who "hath proposed" it. The vocabulary question belongs to [`christology` 5.1](../../christology/lessons/05-01-the-paschal-mystery-and-the-threefold-office.md).

## One-liner

> One genitive, three readings: God's own righteousness, the righteousness he gives, and the righteousness he enacts. Romans 3:26 shows all three in one act, and Trent defines the doctrine without defining the grammar.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢)** *(Exegetical)* Close reading, Romans 4:9–11 (Douay–Rheims):

> This blessedness then, doth it remain in the circumcision only or in the uncircumcision also? For we say that unto Abraham faith was reputed to justice. How then was it reputed? When he was in circumcision or in uncircumcision? Not in circumcision, but in uncircumcision. And he received the sign of circumcision, a seal of the justice of the faith which he had, being uncircumcised: that he might be the father of all them that believe, being uncircumcised: that unto them also it may be reputed to justice.

(a) Which two chapters of Genesis does Paul's argument set in order, and what does he conclude from their order? (b) Give the Greek verb behind "reputed" and the lesson's gloss of it, and say what 4:11 makes circumcision *not* be. **Two sentences per part.**

**P2 (🟡)** *(Exegetical)* An **invented** study-bible note on Romans 3:25–28:

> "Paul's *hilastērion* means the mercy seat, as the Church teaches. In verse 28 Luther invented the phrase 'faith alone', which no Christian writer had used before him, and the Council of Trent accordingly condemned the words 'faith alone' as heresy."

Find the three errors and correct each in **one sentence**, marking the level of any claim about Church teaching.

**P3 (🔴)** *(Exegetical)* Find the crux. Paraphrased positions:

- **Reader B (Bultmann's line):** in Romans 1:17 the righteousness of God is the righteous standing God gives to the believer; the genitive names its source.
- **Reader K (Käsemann's line):** it is God's own saving power at work, reclaiming the world, which also includes the gift to the believer.

(a) Name the single premise about 1:17 on which they divide. (b) Give the verse or parallel each side cites for it. (c) Say what kind of evidence would move the question. **150 words or fewer in all.**

<details>
<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** **Genesis 15** (Abraham believes, and it is reckoned to him, 15:6) comes before **Genesis 17** (circumcision commanded). The reckoning happened while Abraham was uncircumcised, so he is father of **uncircumcised believers** as well as of the circumcised who share his faith (4:11–12).

**Must hit, strict (b):** ***logizomai***, "to reckon, credit to an account" (*elogisthē* in 4:3; the same verb as "we account" in 3:28). Circumcision is a **sign and seal** of a righteousness already reckoned. It is **not the cause or condition** of that righteousness.

**Wrong turns:** saying Paul rejects circumcision as worthless. He calls it a seal, and 3:1–2 has already affirmed the Jews' advantage. Glossing the verb as "infuse" or "impute". Both are later doctrinal readings of a verb whose meaning is "reckon"; what the reckoning *effects* is the disputed point.

**Model answer:** (a) Paul sets Genesis 15:6, where faith is reckoned as righteousness, before Genesis 17, where circumcision is given. So Abraham was reckoned righteous while uncircumcised and is father of Gentile believers too. (b) The verb is *logizomai*, "reckon, credit to an account". Verse 11 makes circumcision the seal of a righteousness already reckoned, not the condition for receiving it.

**P2** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict:**
1. "Mercy seat, **as the Church teaches**": no magisterial act defines what *hilastērion* denotes. It is a **scholarly reading**, well grounded in Leviticus 16 and Hebrews 9:5, with no doctrinal standing either way.
2. "**No writer before him**": false. Luther himself cited Ambrose and Augustine, and Bellarmine and later Fitzmyer list earlier writers who glossed Paul with "alone" (Ambrosiaster, Aquinas among them). What Luther did was **add *allein* to his 1522 translation**, and in 1530 he admitted the word is not in the Greek or the Latin.
3. "Trent **condemned the words**": canon 9 anathematizes a **sense**: faith alone "in such wise as to mean, that nothing else is required to co-operate". That condemnation is **defined dogma** about the doctrine, not a ban on the word.

**Wrong turns:** "correcting" (2) by saying Luther added nothing. Treating canon 9 as a ruling on how to translate 3:28.

**Model answer:** (1) "Mercy seat" is a strong scholarly reading from Leviticus 16 and Hebrews 9:5, but no Church act defines it, so it is a hypothesis. (2) Earlier writers, from Ambrosiaster to Aquinas, used "alone" in glossing Paul; Luther's innovation was putting *allein* into the translated text, which he conceded is not in the Greek. (3) Trent's canon 9, defined dogma, condemns faith alone in the sense that nothing else need co-operate, not the words themselves.

**P3** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict:** (a) The crux: whether the genitive in 1:17 names **the source of a status given to the believer**, or **God's own activity**, of which the gift is one effect. Equivalently: is the righteousness revealed in 1:17 primarily *in the believer* or *in God's act*? (b) B cites **Philippians 3:9** (*ek theou*, righteousness "from God") and the faith language of 1:17 with Habakkuk. K cites the **parallel "revealed" of 1:18**, where wrath is God's own act, and can add 3:26 ("that he himself may be just"). (c) Evidence about **Paul's usage** would move it: how the phrase works in his other occurrences (3:5, 3:21–26, 10:3, 2 Cor 5:21), and its background in the Jewish scriptures (the Psalms and Isaiah, where God's righteousness is his saving act).

**Wrong turns:** making the crux Catholic against Protestant. Both readers are Lutheran, and Catholic exegetes stand on both sides. Citing Trent as a tiebreaker; Trent does not rule on this grammar.

**Model answer:** They split over what the genitive in 1:17 names. For B it names the source of a righteous standing given to faith, and he points to Philippians 3:9, where Paul writes "from God". For K it names God's own saving action, which includes the gift, and he points to the parallel "revealed" of 1:18, where wrath is plainly God's own act, and to 3:26. The question moves on Paul's usage across his other occurrences, especially 3:5 and 10:3, and on the Old Testament background, where the righteousness of God often means his act of deliverance.

</details>

## Flashback

**From Lesson [4.3](04-03-paul-at-work.md) (Paul at work: Thessalonica, Corinth, Philippi):** *(Exegetical.)* Close reading, with mirror-reading. 1 Corinthians 6:12–14 (Douay–Rheims):

> All things are lawful to me: but all things are not expedient. All things are lawful to me: but I will not be brought under the power of any. Meat for the belly and the belly for the meats: but God shall destroy both it and them. But the body is not for fornication, but for the Lord: and the Lord for the body. Now God hath both raised up the Lord, and will raise us up also by his power.

The Greek has no quotation marks. "All things are lawful to me" is *panta moi exestin*, "everything is permitted me"; "I will not be brought under the power of any" is *ouk egō exousiasthēsomai hypo tinos*, "I will not be mastered by anything" (the lesson's translations). (a) Which words are most likely the Corinthians' own slogans, and what signals it? (b) What attitude to the body do the slogans imply, and which verse of 1 Corinthians 15 shows the same attitude on another question? (c) What does verse 14 add to Paul's reply? **Two sentences per part.**

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):** "All things are lawful to me" and "Meat for the belly and the belly for the meats". The signal is the pattern of a short maxim followed by Paul's "but", repeated for the first one: he quotes the slogan, then qualifies it. Credit noting the wordplay (*exestin* / *exousiasthēsomai*: "permitted" / "mastered"), and credit either answer on whether "God shall destroy both it and them" belongs to the slogan or to Paul, since commentators divide. Say that the identification is itself a mirror-reading judgment, widely held but not given by the text.
**Must hit, strict (b):** the slogans treat what one does with the body as morally indifferent, because the body belongs to what passes away, as the belly does. The same low view of the body lies behind the Corinthian denial in 15:12, "there is no resurrection of the dead", or the objection in 15:35, "with what manner of body shall they come?"
**Must hit, strict (c):** verse 14 sets **resurrection** against **destruction**. The belly and its food will be destroyed, but the body will be raised as the Lord was, so it is "for the Lord" and what it does matters. This is the argument of chapter 15 in miniature: Christ's resurrection is the pattern for ours.
**Wrong turns:** taking "All things are lawful to me" as Paul's own unqualified principle. The Douay's note reads it that way, limiting it to "indifferent things", but it misses the "but" that follows. Mirror-reading every clause as a slogan, including "the body is not for fornication". Treating the slogan identification as certain.
**Model answer:** (a) "All things are lawful to me" and "Meat for the belly and the belly for the meats" are probably Corinthian slogans. Each is a short maxim that Paul immediately qualifies with "but", and he quotes the first one twice to bend it ("permitted" / "mastered"), though no quotation marks in the Greek make this certain. (b) They imply that bodily acts are indifferent because the body, like the stomach, is temporary. That same disregard for the body shows in 15:12's "there is no resurrection of the dead" and in 15:35's "with what manner of body?" (c) Verse 14 answers "God shall destroy" with "God will raise us up": the body is not destroyed, like food, but raised, like Christ. So the body belongs to the Lord, and Paul answers a moral question with the resurrection, as he does in chapter 15.

</details>
