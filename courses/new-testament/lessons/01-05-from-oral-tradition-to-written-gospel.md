# The New Testament · Lesson 1.5: From oral tradition to written gospel

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 1: The world of the New Testament and the making of the gospels · Builds on: [1.1 Reading the New Testament in the Church](01-01-reading-the-new-testament-in-the-church.md), [1.3 Under Rome](01-03-under-rome.md), [1.4 The Synoptic problem](01-04-the-synoptic-problem.md) · Unlocks: [2.1 Mark](02-01-mark.md), [3.2 Sources and criteria](03-02-sources-and-criteria.md)

## Why this matters

Jesus dies around the year 30. The first gospel is written, on most reckonings, around 70. [1.4](01-04-the-synoptic-problem.md) asked how the written gospels relate to *each other*. This lesson asks what happened in the forty years *before* any of them: who carried the stories, in what shapes, and how faithfully. It also asks what kind of book came out at the end, and how anyone dates it. Every later argument about a saying's authenticity ([3.2](03-02-sources-and-criteria.md)) is an argument about this gap.

## The idea

Think of a relay. Jesus speaks and acts (stage 1). His followers preach, teach, settle disputes and worship, retelling what he said and did as they go (stage 2). Then four men write (stage 3). The Church's own outline of [the three stages](../reference.md#the-three-stages-of-gospel-formation), in Dei Verbum 19, has exactly this shape ([1.1](01-01-reading-the-new-testament-in-the-church.md)).

Twentieth-century scholarship asked four questions of the relay:

- **What shapes did the stories take while oral?** Form criticism.
- **What did each writer do with what he received?** Redaction criticism.
- **How reliably was it carried?** Memory, oral tradition, eyewitnesses.
- **What kind of book resulted, and when?** Genre and dating.

The answers are hypotheses. The relay itself is Church teaching. Keep those apart and the rest follows.

## Source

Papias, bishop of Hierapolis in the early second century, quoted by Eusebius, *Church History* III.39.15 (NPNF, McGiffert; note numbers omitted):

> "This also the presbyter said: Mark, having become the interpreter of Peter, wrote down accurately, though not in order, whatsoever he remembered of the things said or done by Christ. For he neither heard the Lord nor followed him, but afterward, as I said, he followed Peter, who adapted his teaching to the needs of his hearers, but with no intention of giving a connected account of the Lord's discourses, so that Mark committed no error while he thus wrote some things as he remembered them."

Three Greek phrases decide how this is read. *Hermēneutēs Petrou* is "Peter's interpreter": a translator, or more loosely a spokesman who passed on Peter's preaching. *Ou mentoi taxei* is "not, however, in order". Papias is defending Mark against a complaint about his arrangement, which already concedes that stage 3 involved arranging. *Pros tas chreias*, rendered "to the needs", can also mean "in the form of *chreiai*", the short pointed anecdotes of Greek rhetorical schooling. On that reading Papias describes Peter teaching in small self-contained units, which is what form critics claim to find. The phrase supports both readings; neither is settled.

## The argument

1. **The gap was bridged by teaching, not archives.** Paul writes in the 50s with the technical vocabulary of handing on: *paredōka* ("I handed on") and *parelabon* ("I received") in 1 Corinthians 15:3 and 11:23. *In words:* the tradition was being passed, formally, within twenty-five years.

2. **[Form criticism](../reference.md#form-criticism).** K. L. Schmidt (1919) argued that Mark's connecting framework ("and immediately", "again he went") is editorial glue. The units it joins, called **pericopes**, circulated separately. Martin Dibelius (*Die Formgeschichte des Evangeliums*, 1919) and Rudolf Bultmann (*Die Geschichte der synoptischen Tradition*, 1921) sorted them by form: pronouncement stories built to deliver one climactic saying (Dibelius's "paradigms", Bultmann's "apophthegms"), miracle stories, parables, sayings, legends. Each form was tied to a ***Sitz im Leben***, a "setting in life": the preaching, catechesis, controversy or worship in which the community used it. *In words:* a story's shape tells you what job it did in the Church.

3. **Method and verdict came apart.** Bultmann added a historical verdict: the community created much of the material and read its own controversies back into Jesus's life. He also judged the gospels to be unlike any biography. The method (identifying forms and settings) outlived the verdict. Catholic exegetes adopted the first; the Biblical Commission's instruction *Sancta Mater Ecclesia* (21 April 1964) told exegetes they could use the method's sound elements. It rejected its rationalist presuppositions: denying the supernatural, ruling out miracle and prophecy, and inflating the community's creative power at the expense of the apostles' authority (paraphrased).

4. **Redaction criticism.** Günther Bornkamm on Matthew, Hans Conzelmann on Luke (*Die Mitte der Zeit*, 1954) and Willi Marxsen on Mark (*Der Evangelist Markus*, 1956, which coined *Redaktionsgeschichte*) turned from the units to the writers. Their changes to their sources are evidence of each evangelist's theology. *In words:* the evangelists are authors, not scissors.

5. **[Oral tradition and memory](../reference.md#oral-tradition-and-memory).** Kenneth Bailey, drawing on decades in Middle Eastern village life, proposed "informal controlled" tradition. The community retells its stories freely but corrects a teller who changes the core. Critics, notably Theodore Weeden, argued his anecdotes do not bear the weight put on them. James Dunn (*Jesus Remembered*, 2003) built on Bailey. Tradition begins as the disciples' *remembered* impression of Jesus, performed with a stable core and flexible detail. So the old wedge between "faith" and "history" is false at the start. Others, such as Bart Ehrman (*Jesus Before the Gospels*, 2016), draw on memory research to stress how memory distorts.

6. **Eyewitness testimony.** Richard Bauckham (*Jesus and the Eyewitnesses*, 2006) argues that named eyewitnesses stayed alive and active as authorities until the gospels were written. So the gospels are closer to testimony than to anonymous community tradition. His evidence includes Papias's preference for the "living and abiding voice" (III.39.4) and the Source above. He also points to incidental names such as Rufus and Alexander (Mark 15:21) as possible cited witnesses. His critics say Papias is late and partisan. They add that the memory research Bauckham invokes cuts both ways.

7. **[The gospels as bioi](../reference.md#the-gospels-as-bioi).** Against the form critics' claim that the gospels are unique folk literature, Richard Burridge (*What Are the Gospels?*, 1992) compared them with Greco-Roman lives (*bioi*) such as Plutarch's. He found the same generic markers: a single subject dominating the verbs, and a disproportionate focus on the death. Most scholars now accept some version of this. *In words:* ancient readers would have shelved Mark with the lives of famous men, which fixes what kind of truth-claim to expect ([`fundamental-theology` 3.2](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/03-02-inerrancy.md) on genre).

8. **[Dating the gospels](../reference.md#dating-the-gospels).** The fixed anchor is the Temple's fall in 70 ([1.3](01-03-under-rome.md)). The common ranges run roughly: Mark c. 65–73, Matthew and Luke c. 80–90, John c. 90–100. They rest on three things. Markan priority puts Matthew and Luke after Mark. Luke 21:20 and Matthew 22:7 are read as reflecting 70. Irenaeus reports that Mark wrote "after their departure", that is, after Peter and Paul had left the scene (Eusebius V.8.3). John A. T. Robinson (*Redating the New Testament*, 1976) put every book before 70. He argued that none mentions the fall as past, and that Acts ends with Paul still alive, c. 62. He is a minority, but a serious one. A majority is not a proof. Every range above is an inference to the best explanation ([`philosophical-method` 2.1](../../philosophical-method/lessons/02-01-inference-to-the-best-explanation.md)), not an observation.

**Mark the levels.** That the gospels [faithfully hand on](../reference.md#historical-character-of-the-gospels) what Jesus really did and taught, through the three stages, is **Church teaching**. It is taught in a dogmatic constitution with no new definition attached, and [1.1](01-01-reading-the-new-testament-in-the-church.md) weighs it on [the ladder](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-04-the-ladder-of-doctrinal-authority.md). Dei Verbum 19's footnote cites *Sancta Mater Ecclesia*. The permission to use form criticism's sound elements, with the listed presuppositions excluded, is **authentic guidance of a curial instruction**. It is not a definition, and it does not certify any form critic's results. Everything else is a **scholarly hypothesis** with no magisterial standing either way: Schmidt's framework thesis, the form categories, the *Sitz im Leben* of any pericope, Bailey's model, Dunn's, Bauckham's, the *bios* genre, and every date. Irenaeus and Papias are **historical witnesses** to be weighed, not doctrine. The three stages overlap form criticism's picture, but the Church adopted the outline, not a method. See [Church teaching vs hypothesis](../reference.md#church-teaching-vs-hypothesis).

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean): the grainfields, Mark 2:23–28.** *Form:* a controversy story. There is an action (the disciples pluck grain on the Sabbath), an objection (the Pharisees), and a reply that climaxes in a saying. In fact there are two sayings. Verse 27 reads, in my translation, "The Sabbath came to be for man's sake, not man for the Sabbath's". Verse 28 is the Son-of-man saying. *Sitz im Leben* on the form-critical account: a Church arguing about Sabbath practice would keep and use this story. *Redaction:* Matthew and Luke both drop verse 27 and Mark's "under Abiathar the high priest". In 1 Samuel 21 the priest is Achimelech (Douay "1 Kings 21"). Matthew adds that the disciples were "hungry", plus the priests' Temple service and Hosea 6:6. On Markan priority, Matthew is tightening the legal argument. *Three stages:* an incident and saying in Jesus's life, a controversy form in the Church's use, and two evangelists' edits. Note what the form label does **not** decide. That the Church *used* this story in Sabbath disputes says nothing about whether Jesus *said* verse 27. Setting of use is not setting of origin.

**Example 2 (hard): dating Mark by Mark 13:14.** Mark writes, "when you shall see the abomination of desolation, standing where it ought not (he that readeth let him understand)". The aside is addressed to the *reader*, *ho anaginōskōn*, so it is Mark speaking, not Jesus. The participle "standing" (*hestēkota*) is masculine, though "abomination" (*bdelygma*) is neuter. So a *person* is meant. Does this point to 70? It strains both ways. Read as after the event, it could be Titus in the Temple. Read as before the event, it resembles Caligula's attempt to set up his statue (c. 40), or a fear felt in the crisis of 66–70. Unlike Luke, Mark names no encirclement and no burning. That is why most scholars place Mark *around* 70, some just before and some just after, and why Mark 13 alone cannot settle the question. The parallel in Luke 21:20 is sharper: "when you shall see Jerusalem compassed about with an army". Yet C. H. Dodd argued that Luke's siege language echoes the Greek Old Testament's accounts of 587 BC. If so, it is not an eyewitness report of 70.

## Watch out

- **"Form criticism assumes the gospels are fiction."** The method classifies forms. Bultmann's scepticism was a separate verdict, and it is the presuppositions behind that verdict that *Sancta Mater Ecclesia* rejects. Fitzmyer and Brown used form criticism throughout.
- **"The Church teaches Mark was written before 70"**, or **"Catholics must accept the late dates."** Both turn a hypothesis into doctrine. Dei Verbum 19 affirms that the gospels are historical, not when any of them was written.
- **Treating Dei Verbum 19's three stages as an endorsement of Bultmann**, or treating Bauckham as the "Catholic" position. The outline is teaching. Every account of how stage 2 worked is a hypothesis, including the reassuring ones.
- **"Luke 21:20 is after the event, therefore Luke is untrustworthy."** Dating a text and judging its history are different questions. A gospel written in 85 can hand on stage-1 material faithfully.

## One-liner

> Between Jesus and the gospels stood forty years of preaching; the Church teaches that the relay was faithful, and scholars argue, as hypotheses, about how it ran and when it ended.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢)** *(Exegetical)* Mark 12:13–17 (Douay-Rheims):

> And they sent to him some of the Pharisees and of the Herodians: that they should catch him in his words. Who coming, say to him: Master, we know that thou art a true speaker and carest not for any man; for thou regardest not the person of men, but teachest the way of God in truth. Is it lawful to give tribute to Caesar? Or shall we not give it? Who knowing their wiliness, saith to them: Why tempt you me? Bring me a penny that I may see it. And they brought it him. And he saith to them: Whose is this image and inscription? They say to him, Caesar's. And Jesus answering, said to them: Render therefore to Caesar the things that are Caesar's and to God the things that are God's. And they marvelled at him.

(a) Name the form (either Dibelius's or Bultmann's term), and quote the words the story is built to deliver. (b) Propose a *Sitz im Leben* in which a first-century church would use it. Then say in one sentence what that setting does **not** establish. **Two sentences per part.**

**P2 (🟡)** *(Exegetical)* Two positions, paraphrased:

- **Bultmann:** the tradition passed through anonymous communities that shaped, and often created, material to meet their own needs, so the gospels tell us chiefly about those communities.
- **Bauckham:** named eyewitnesses stayed alive and active as recognized authorities through the oral period, so the gospels are close to their testimony.

(a) Name the single premise about stage 2 that one side must deny. (b) Name one piece of evidence that bears on it, and say which way it cuts and why it is contested. (c) State the level of the question. **150 words or fewer in total.**

**P3 (🔴)** *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b))* An **invented** op-ed:

> "Luke 21:20 has Jesus predict Jerusalem 'compassed about with an army.' Since nobody can foresee a siege, this proves Luke wrote after 70, and so the Church's claim that the gospels are historical collapses."

(a) Name the two steps doing the work, and correct each, marking the level of every claim involved. (b) Does Luke 21:20 support a post-70 date for Luke better than Mark 13:14 supports one for Mark? Any verdict, but weigh the verse's wording and one counter-reading. **Two sentences for each step in (a); 100 words for (b).**

<details>
<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** a **pronouncement story**: Dibelius's **paradigm**, or Bultmann's **apophthegm**, specifically a controversy dialogue (credit Taylor's "pronouncement story"). Everything builds to "Render therefore to Caesar the things that are Caesar's and to God the things that are God's."

**Must hit, strict (b):** a plausible setting of use: a church facing the question of paying Roman taxes or its stance toward Rome, or preaching and catechesis on it. Then the limit: the setting says where the story was **used**, not whether it **happened** or where it **originated**. Setting of use is not setting of origin.

**Wrong turns:** calling it a miracle story, or a parable because a coin appears. Inferring from (b) that the community invented it, which is Bultmann's verdict, not the form's.

**Model answer:** (a) A pronouncement story (Dibelius's paradigm), built to deliver "Render therefore to Caesar the things that are Caesar's and to God the things that are God's." (b) A church asked whether believers should pay Roman tribute would retell it in teaching. That shows why it was kept, not whether Jesus said it.

**P2** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** the crux is **whether stage-2 tradition was controlled by identifiable living eyewitnesses or was anonymous and communal**. Bultmann must deny the first; Bauckham must deny the second. Credit also: whether the community was **creative** or **preservative**.

**Must hit, strict (b):** one piece of evidence with direction and contest. Papias's "living and abiding voice" and his report on Mark favour Bauckham, but are contested as late (second century) and apologetic. Named figures like Rufus and Alexander (Mark 15:21) favour Bauckham, but naming need not mean citing a witness. Memory research can favour either side: salient, rehearsed memory is fairly stable, but memory reconstructs.

**Must hit, strict (c):** a **scholarly hypothesis** on both sides, with no magisterial standing either way. Dei Verbum 19 teaches that the transmission was faithful but not which model of it is right. *Sancta Mater Ecclesia* rejects inflating communal creativity **as a presupposition**; it does not settle the historical question.

**Wrong turns:** making the crux "whether miracles happen", which is a different disagreement. Calling Bauckham's view Church teaching.

**Model answer:** The crux is whether identifiable eyewitnesses stayed in control of the tradition. Bauckham affirms it; Bultmann must deny it. Papias's preference for the "living and abiding voice" of disciples' followers supports Bauckham, but it is second-century and defensive, so how much it tells about the 40s is contested. Both are hypotheses: the Church teaches that the tradition was faithful, not which model of stage 2 is correct.

**P3** *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b))*

**Must hit, strict (a):** **Step 1**, "nobody can foresee a siege, so post-70." This assumes no genuine prediction is possible, which is a philosophical premise, not an exegetical finding (and one *Sancta Mater Ecclesia* names as a rejected presupposition). Sieges were foreseeable, and the wording may echo Old Testament siege language, so the verse is at most *evidence* for a post-70 date, not proof. The date itself is a **hypothesis**. **Step 2**, "post-70 means the historical character collapses." This confuses date with reliability. Dei Verbum 19 (**Church teaching**) affirms faithful transmission and names no date, so a Luke written in 85 is untouched by it.

**Must hit, any verdict (b):** compare the wording. Luke specifies encirclement by armies and desolation, which is more concrete than Mark's cryptic "abomination ... standing". Then give one counter-reading: Dodd's Old Testament-echo argument; general prophetic idiom; or the view that Luke's reworking of Mark reflects the event whether or not the saying goes back to Jesus. Reach a verdict.

**Wrong turns:** answering (a) by asserting that Luke *was* written before 70, which only replaces one hypothesis with another. Saying the Church requires a pre-70 date.

**Model answer (b), one of several:** Yes, somewhat. Luke turns Mark's veiled "abomination ... standing where it ought not" into a concrete scene: Jerusalem encircled by armies, its desolation near. That concreteness is what we would expect from a writer who knew how the war ended, and it fits Luke's other siege detail (19:43). But the language is also stock Old Testament siege vocabulary, as Dodd argued, so it is consistent with a pre-70 writer. On balance it is modest evidence for a post-70 Luke, and stronger than anything Mark 13 offers.

## Flashback

**From Lesson [1.3](01-03-under-rome.md) (Under Rome):** *(Formal (a) · Exegetical (b).)* In John 2:20 (Douay-Rheims) the Jews say to Jesus at Passover: "Six and forty years was this temple in building." Josephus (*Antiquities* 15.11.1, Whiston) says Herod undertook the rebuilding "in the eighteenth year of his reign". (a) Count Herod's reign as Lesson 1.3 did, with 37 BC as year 1. In what year did the work begin? Taking "six and forty years" as forty-six full years elapsed, in what year are the Jews speaking? Show the year-zero step. (b) Does your year fall inside the window 1.3 built from Luke 3:1–2 (26 to 33/34)? What standing does the date have? **Two sentences for (b).**

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):** Year 1 is 37 BC, so year 18 is $37 - 17 =$ **20 BC**. There is no year zero, so write 20 BC as the astronomical year $-19$. Then $-19 + 46 = 27$, which is **AD 27**. Equivalently, $46 - 20 + 1 = 27$.
**Must hit, strict (b):** **Yes.** AD 27 lies inside 26 to 33/34 and inside Pilate's 26–36. It is a **historians' reconstruction**, built on Josephus's regnal count and on one reading of John's phrase. It has **no magisterial standing**: neither the Creed nor Dei Verbum 19 fixes any date.
**Wrong turns:** $37 - 18 = 19$ BC, which forgets that 37 BC is itself year 1. $46 - 20 = 26$, which forgets there is no year zero. Presenting AD 27 as exact: other counting conventions move it by a year, which is why it is a reconstruction. Treating it as the year of the crucifixion; John sets this scene at the first Passover of the ministry.
**Model answer:** (a) Year 18 of a reign whose year 1 is 37 BC is 20 BC. With no year zero, 20 BC is $-19$, and $-19 + 46 = 27$, so AD 27. (b) Yes: 27 falls inside both the Luke 3:1–2 window and Pilate's term. It is a reconstruction from Josephus and John on stated counting conventions, not Church teaching.

</details>
