# The New Testament · Lesson 2.4: John and the Synoptics

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 2: The four gospels · Builds on: [1.4 The Synoptic problem](01-04-the-synoptic-problem.md), [1.5 From oral tradition to written gospel](01-05-from-oral-tradition-to-written-gospel.md), [2.1 Mark](02-01-mark.md) · Unlocks: [2.5 The theology of John](02-05-the-theology-of-john.md), [3.4 The passion and resurrection narratives](03-04-the-passion-and-resurrection-narratives.md)

## Why this matters

Read Mark, Matthew and Luke side by side and you get one story told three ways. Read John and you get a different story. The ministry lasts longer, happens mostly in Judea, and opens with the Temple scene. Jesus speaks in long discourses about himself, not short parables about the kingdom. And he dies on a different day. Readers have noticed since the second century. The questions are what exactly differs, how scholars explain it, and which of those explanations the Church's teaching touches (none of them).

## The idea

The Synoptics share a skeleton because (on the majority view) two copied the third ([1.4](01-04-the-synoptic-problem.md)). John does not share that skeleton, and most of his material has no Synoptic parallel at all. So the Synoptic problem's question, *who copied whom?*, becomes a different question here: *did John know the others at all?*

Hold three sets of differences apart, each with its own evidence: **chronology** (when, and how long), **content and form** (what Jesus does and how he talks), and **author and setting** (who wrote it, for whom, from what sources).

## Source

John's calendar, from the Douay-Rheims (Challoner), John 2:13, 6:4, 11:55, 18:28, 19:14:

> And the pasch of the Jews was at hand: and Jesus went up to Jerusalem. ...
> Now the pasch, the festival day of the Jews, was near at hand. ...
> And the pasch of the Jews was at hand: and many from the country went up to Jerusalem, before the pasch, to purify themselves. ...
> Then they led Jesus from Caiphas to the governor's hall. And it was morning: and they went not into the hall, that they might not be defiled, but that they might eat the pasch. ...
> And it was the parasceve of the pasch, about the sixth hour: and he saith to the Jews: Behold your king.

"Pasch" is Passover; *paraskeuē* is literally "preparation", the day one gets ready for a feast or a sabbath. Two facts sit here. John counts **three Passovers**, so the ministry runs at least two years; the Synoptics narrate one. And on the trial morning the priests **have not yet eaten** the Passover, so in John Jesus dies before the meal, not after it.

## The argument

1. **The Temple incident.** John sets it at the first Passover (2:13–22), before any Galilean ministry. Mark sets it in the last week (11:15–18), where it provokes the plot to kill Jesus. In John the raising of Lazarus provokes the plot (11:47–53). Either there were two incidents, or someone placed the scene by theme, not date. Most commentators think there was one incident; many judge Mark's late placement the likelier historically and read John's early one as a theological preface, in which the Temple of Jesus's body replaces the Temple of stone (2:19–21). *In words:* one event, two placements, and the placement carries a meaning.

2. **The day of death** (see [the Johannine chronology](../reference.md#the-johannine-chronology)). All four gospels agree that Jesus died on the day before a sabbath: a Friday (Mark 15:42, John 19:31). In Mark the Last Supper **is** the Passover meal (14:12–16), so Jesus dies on 15 Nisan. In John he dies on 14 Nisan, the "parasceve of the pasch" (19:14), in the afternoon hours when the lambs were slaughtered in the Temple. John makes the match explicit: the Baptist calls Jesus "the Lamb of God" (1:29), and the unbroken bones of 19:36 fulfil a rule for the Passover lamb (Exod 12:46). The hour differs too: Mark crucifies at the third hour (15:25), while John has Pilate still judging at the sixth (19:14). The problems work out the calendar.

   *Which is historical?* Meier (*A Marginal Jew*, vol. 1) argues for John's date, partly because a capital trial on the feast itself is hard to imagine. Others defend Mark's date. Annie Jaubert proposed that Jesus followed the Qumran solar calendar, which would place his Passover meal earlier in the week; most scholars have not followed her. Benedict XVI (*Jesus of Nazareth*, vol. 2) inclined to John's dating, with the Supper as Jesus's own Passover without a lamb. He said the book was not a magisterial act.

3. **Signs and discourses.** John has no exorcisms, no parables of the kingdom, and no words of institution at the supper (the foot-washing stands where they would be). His word for the miracles is *sēmeia*, "signs": Cana's "beginning of miracles" (2:11) is literally "beginning of the signs". Several are followed by a discourse unpacking them, as the feeding is by the bread of life (John 6). Jesus speaks of himself in "I am" sayings. The kingdom of God, the Synoptic Jesus's constant theme, appears only in 3:3–5. Clement of Alexandria had already named the difference, as reported by Eusebius (*Church History* VI.14.7, NPNF): John, "perceiving that the external facts had been made plain in the Gospel ... composed a spiritual Gospel."

4. **[The beloved disciple](../reference.md#the-beloved-disciple) and authorship.** The gospel never names its author. It points instead to "one of his disciples, whom Jesus loved" (13:23; also 19:26, 20:2, 21:7, 21:20), and 21:24 says this disciple "hath written these things". Irenaeus (*Against Heresies* III.1.1, ANF) is the first clear attribution: "John, the disciple of the Lord, who also had leaned upon His breast, did himself publish a Gospel during his residence at Ephesus in Asia." Tradition took this John to be the son of Zebedee. But note Irenaeus's title: "disciple of the Lord", not "apostle". Papias (in Eusebius III.39.4) lists "the presbyter John", along with Aristion, as "disciples of the Lord", separately from the John he names earlier beside Peter, James and Matthew, whom Eusebius took to be the apostle. Eusebius himself concluded that there were two Johns in Asia (III.39.5–6). The positions:
   - **John son of Zebedee.** The traditional view, and Brown's in his Anchor Bible commentary (1966–70).
   - **An unnamed disciple, not one of the Twelve.** Brown's later view, in *The Community of the Beloved Disciple* (1979). The disciple was a follower whose testimony a later evangelist and redactor shaped.
   - **John the Elder of Papias, an eyewitness but not one of the Twelve.** Bauckham, *Jesus and the Eyewitnesses* (2006).
   - **An idealized figure**, a minority view.

   Most critical scholars date the finished gospel around 90–100. Most think it grew in stages, chapter 21 added after 20:30–31 had closed the book.

5. **[The Johannine community](../reference.md#the-johannine-community).** J. Louis Martyn (*History and Theology in the Fourth Gospel*, 1968) read John on two levels. The healed blind man put "out of the synagogue" (9:22; also 12:42, 16:2) tells Jesus's story and, at the same time, the story of John's own church, expelled from the synagogue late in the century. Brown's 1979 book built a whole community history on this method. Critics reply that Martyn's link to a synagogue curse on heretics (the *Birkat ha-Minim*) has uncertain date and target, and that the method reads a community's history out of a story about Jesus. Bauckham's edited volume *The Gospels for All Christians* (1998) argued more broadly that the gospels were written for circulation among all the churches, not for one sect.

6. **[Did John know the Synoptics?](../reference.md#did-john-know-the-synoptics)** P. Gardner-Smith (*Saint John and the Synoptic Gospels*, 1938) argued for independence. The overlaps look like parallel oral tradition, and John's wholesale departures are hard to explain in an author with Mark open. C. H. Dodd extended the case, and independence long held the majority. Since then a substantial group, with Frans Neirynck and the Leuven school most insistent, has argued that John knew Mark (and perhaps Luke). Their evidence is shared sequences and rare shared wording. The field is now divided; state it as divided.

**Mark the levels.** That the four gospels have a **historical character**, faithfully handing on what Jesus really did and taught, is Church teaching: Vatican II's dogmatic constitution *Dei Verbum* 19, on the level worked out in [1.1](01-01-reading-the-new-testament-in-the-church.md). Their **apostolic origin** is also taught (*Dei Verbum* 18). But that paragraph has the gospels written by the apostles themselves "and apostolic men"; it does not assign this gospel to the son of Zebedee's own hand. The same paragraph 19 says the evangelists selected, synthesized and explained with the churches' situation in view. That covers a Temple scene placed for theological reasons. Neither paragraph fixes a chronology or a day of death. The early Biblical Commission response on this gospel's authorship is a weight case for [1.1](01-01-reading-the-new-testament-in-the-church.md). Everything in steps 1–6 (the day of death, the beloved disciple, the stages of composition, Martyn's community, John's knowledge of the Synoptics) is **scholarly hypothesis** with no magisterial standing either way; a Catholic may hold any listed position.

## Timeline

The ministry in Mark and in John, in the order each tells it:

| Mark | John |
|---|---|
| Baptism; Jesus goes to Galilee only after John the Baptist's arrest (1:14) | Baptist's testimony; first disciples, one of them unnamed (1:35–40); Cana, "beginning of the signs" (2:11) |
| Galilean ministry: exorcisms, parables, healings (1–9) | **Passover 1** (2:13): **Temple incident** in Jerusalem; Jesus and the Baptist baptizing side by side (3:22–24) |
| One journey to Jerusalem (10) | Galilee, Samaria, Jerusalem back and forth; an unnamed feast (5:1) |
| — | **Passover 2** (6:4): feeding and walking on water (as Mark 6); bread of life discourse |
| Entry into Jerusalem; **Temple incident** (11:15–18) triggers the plot | Feasts of Tabernacles (7:2) and Dedication (10:22); raising of Lazarus triggers the plot (11:47–53) |
| **The one Passover**: Last Supper = Passover meal (14:12–16) | **Passover 3** (11:55): supper *before* the feast (13:1), foot-washing, farewell discourses (13–17) |
| Crucifixion on **15 Nisan**, third hour (15:25) | Crucifixion on **14 Nisan**, after the sixth hour (19:14) |
| **About one year** (one Passover named) | **At least two years** (three Passovers) |

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean): how long was the ministry?** Method: count datable festivals. John names three Passovers (2:13, 6:4, 11:55), the first near the start and the third at the end, so at least two full years. Mark narrates one Passover, at the end, but his silence does not establish one year; it only fails to require more. So the narrative frames differ while the evidence conflicts less than it seems: a silence against a positive count. Many historians of Jesus take John's longer frame as at least as plausible. Nothing doctrinal hangs on it.

**Example 2 (hard): the harmonizer's solution.** Eusebius (*Church History* III.24.7–13) reports an answer already old in his day. John knowingly supplemented the others by recording what Jesus did *before* the Baptist was imprisoned, a period the Synoptics skip (compare John 3:24, "John was not yet cast into prison", with Mark 1:14). It works for the early Judean ministry. It strains twice. Mark ties the Temple incident to the last week and the plot, so the scheme needs two incidents. And the day of death cannot be supplemented. 14 Nisan and 15 Nisan are not two parts of one timeline; they are rival dates for one event. Harmonizing needs a further hypothesis (Jaubert's calendar, or "pasch" in 18:28 as the festival week, not the meal). Faith does not require harmonizing: [*Dei Verbum* 19](../reference.md#historical-character-of-the-gospels) lets the evangelists arrange.

## Watch out

- **"Dei Verbum requires that John the Apostle wrote the fourth gospel."** No. *Dei Verbum* 18 teaches apostolic origin, written by apostles and "apostolic men". Which person held the pen is a scholarly question ([church teaching vs hypothesis](../reference.md#church-teaching-vs-hypothesis)).
- **The mirror error: "the gospels' historicity is just a hypothesis, like the beloved disciple's identity."** The historical character is taught (*Dei Verbum* 19); particular identifications and dates are not. Mixing the two levels either way is an exegetical error.
- **"John is theology, the Synoptics history."** Clement's "spiritual Gospel" is no verdict on accuracy; on the day of death and the ministry's length many historians prefer John.
- **Counting Irenaeus as saying "son of Zebedee".** III.1.1 says "disciple of the Lord"; the identification is the tradition's (plausible) reading of him.

## One-liner

> John runs three Passovers where Mark runs one, cleanses the Temple first and dies with the lambs; who the beloved disciple was, and whether John knew Mark, are open scholarly questions, while the gospels' historical character is not.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢)** *(Formal (a)–(b) · Exegetical (c))* Take as given: a Jewish day runs from sunset to sunset; Passover lambs are slaughtered on the afternoon of 14 Nisan; the Passover meal is eaten after sunset, which is already 15 Nisan; all four gospels put the crucifixion on a Friday, the day before a sabbath.
(a) Using Mark 14:12–17, give the Nisan date of the Last Supper and of the crucifixion, and say which Roman-reckoned evening the Supper falls on.
(b) Using John 18:28 and 19:14, do the same for John, and give the Nisan date of the sabbath in John 19:31.
(c) Explain in two sentences why John 19:31 calls that sabbath "a great sabbath day", and name one other verse in John that his dating serves.

**P2 (🟡)** *(Exegetical)* Close reading, John 21:20–24 (Douay-Rheims):

> Peter turning about, saw that disciple whom Jesus loved following, who also leaned on his breast at supper and said: Lord, who is he that shall betray thee? Him therefore when Peter had seen, he saith to Jesus: Lord, and what shall this man do? Jesus saith to him: So I will have him to remain till I come, what is it to thee? Follow thou me. This saying therefore went abroad among the brethren, that that disciple should not die. And Jesus did not say to him: He should not die; but: So I will have him to remain till I come, what is it to thee? This is that disciple who giveth testimony of these things and hath written these things: and we know that his testimony is true.

(a) How does the passage identify "that disciple" with an earlier scene, and what does verse 24 claim about his role? (b) Who could "we" be? Give two readings held by named scholars, and say which word each turns on. (c) What does verse 23's correction suggest about when chapter 21 was written? **Two sentences per part.**

**P3 (🔴)** *(Exegetical (a), (c) · Evaluative (b))* Two paraphrased positions:

- **Independence (Gardner-Smith's line):** where John and Mark overlap, the wording mostly differs, as in parallel oral retellings. And an author with Mark in hand would hardly have dropped the exorcisms, the parables and the words of institution, or moved the Temple incident.
- **Dependence (Neirynck's line):** some agreements are too specific for oral tradition. The rare adjective *pistikē* ("genuine"?) describing the nard appears in the New Testament only in Mark 14:3 and John 12:3. Both give the ointment's value as three hundred denarii (Mark 14:5, "more than"; John 12:5). Both put the feeding before the walking on water. Both interleave Peter's denial with the hearing before the high priest.

(a) Name the single premise the independence side must deny, and the one the dependence side must deny. (b) Say what evidence would most move the question, and how strongly the denial-interleaving would cut if it could be shown to be Mark's own editorial device (**100 words or fewer**; any verdict). (c) Does Church teaching settle the question? One sentence, with the level.

<details>
<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Formal (a)–(b) · Exegetical (c))*

**Must hit, strict (a):** In Mark the lambs are sacrificed on 14 Nisan (14:12), and the Supper that evening (14:17) is the Passover meal, so it is eaten at the start of **15 Nisan**. Arrest, trial and crucifixion follow before the next sunset, so the crucifixion is also on **15 Nisan**. Since that day is a Friday, the Supper falls on **Thursday evening** in Roman reckoning (Jewish Friday began at Thursday sunset).

**Must hit, strict (b):** In John the priests have not yet eaten the Passover on the trial morning (18:28), and the day is the "parasceve of the pasch" (19:14). So the crucifixion is on **14 Nisan** (Friday). The Supper, the evening before, falls on **Thursday evening** as well, but that evening is the start of 14 Nisan, and the meal is not the Passover meal (13:1, "before the festival day"). The Passover meal is eaten at Friday sunset, which begins **15 Nisan**, and that day is also the sabbath of 19:31.

**Must hit, strict (c):** The sabbath coincides with the first day of Passover (15 Nisan), so two holy days fall together. That is why it is "great". Credit either **1:29** ("Behold the Lamb of God") or **19:36** ("You shall not break a bone of him", the Passover-lamb rule of Exod 12:46): Jesus dies at the hour of the lambs.

**Wrong turns:** Putting Mark's crucifixion on 14 Nisan because the lambs are "sacrificed" in 14:12. The sacrifice is the day of preparation; the Supper and the death come after sunset, on the 15th. Forgetting that the sunset boundary makes "Thursday evening" part of the Jewish Friday. Concluding that the two gospels disagree on the weekday: they agree on Friday and differ on the Nisan date.

**Model answer:** (a) Mark: Supper on Thursday evening, which is already 15 Nisan, and crucifixion on Friday, still 15 Nisan. (b) John: Supper on Thursday evening, the start of 14 Nisan, and crucifixion on Friday, 14 Nisan; Passover and sabbath both begin at Friday sunset, 15 Nisan. (c) The sabbath is "great" because it is also Passover's first day. The dating makes Jesus die as the lambs die, which 19:36 draws out.

**P2** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** The passage identifies him by a back-reference to the supper: the one who "leaned on his breast ... and said: Lord, who is he that shall betray thee?" (13:23–25). Verse 24 makes him the **witness** ("giveth testimony") and the one who "hath written these things". So it claims that the gospel rests on his testimony and, in some sense, his writing.

**Must hit, strict (b):** Two readings. (1) **"We" is a group distinct from the disciple**, a community or its editors vouching for his testimony, with "we know" set against "his testimony". This is Brown's reading (the redactor and community); on this view "hath written" may mean "caused to be written", as some commentators take it. (2) **"We" is the author's own voice**, a "we" of authoritative testimony by the disciple himself or his close circle. This is Bauckham's reading. The words each turns on are "**we**" (*oidamen*, "we know") against "**his** testimony", and "**hath written**" (*ho grapsas*).

**Must hit, strict (c):** The rumour that the disciple would not die needed correcting, which most naturally suggests he **had died** (or was very old) when the chapter was written. Together with the closing of 20:30–31, this is a main reason scholars treat chapter 21 as a later addition by the gospel's final editors.

**Wrong turns:** Treating "we know" as proving that someone other than the disciple wrote *every* word, or proving that he wrote every word. Both over-read. Reading 21:23 as a prophecy that he would live until the Second Coming, which is exactly what the verse denies Jesus said.

**Model answer:** (a) He is identified as the disciple who leaned on Jesus's breast and asked about the betrayer at the supper (13:23–25), and verse 24 makes him both the witness and the writer behind "these things". (b) Brown reads "we" as the community or redactors attesting the disciple's testimony, perhaps with "hath written" meaning "caused to be written". Bauckham reads it as the witness's own authoritative "we", with the weight on "we know" and "his testimony". (c) Correcting a rumour that he would not die makes most sense after his death. So chapter 21 was likely written after it, as a supplement to a gospel already closed at 20:31.

**P3** *(Exegetical (a), (c) · Evaluative (b))*

**Must hit, strict (a):** The independence side must deny that **rare shared wording and shared sequence exceed what oral tradition preserves** (or it must explain them as oral after all). The dependence side must deny that **an author who knew Mark would have followed it closely**: John used his source freely, as he used everything, so omission and relocation prove nothing. The two sides really differ on one model: what literary use of a source looks like.

**Must hit, any verdict (b):** The decisive evidence is agreement in **Mark's editorial work**, as opposed to his tradition. A feature Mark himself created, found also in John, cannot come from shared oral tradition. The denial-interleaving is Mark's familiar "sandwich" technique, so if it is Mark's own it cuts **strongly** toward dependence. The student must also note the rejoinder: the interleaving may simply reflect how the events ran (Peter outside while Jesus was questioned inside), so its editorial origin is the thing in dispute.

**Must hit, strict (c):** **No.** It is a scholarly hypothesis with no magisterial standing either way, freely disputed among Catholics (rung 5); *Dei Verbum* 18–19 settle neither answer.

**Wrong turns:** Treating the amount of non-overlap as decisive on its own; that is the independence side's premise, not a neutral fact. Citing the 300 denarii as rare wording; a round sum is weaker evidence than a hapax pair like *pistikē*. For (c), claiming that inspiration requires independent witnesses, or that dependence would undermine historicity.

**Model answer (b), one of several:** What would move it most is an agreement in something Mark made, not something Mark received. The interleaved denial is Mark's signature technique, and if it is his invention here, its reappearance in John is hard to explain without literary contact. But the arrangement may simply follow the events, with Peter in the courtyard while Jesus was questioned. So the case turns on showing it editorial, and I rate it a real but not conclusive point for dependence.

</details>

Matthew's Greek in 21:5 is *epi onon kai epi pōlon*, "upon an ass and upon a colt".

Assume Markan priority (a hypothesis). (a) List three changes Matthew makes to Mark, and quote the words of Zechariah 9:9 that account for the largest. (b) Hosea 11:1, behind Matthew 2:15, looked back on Israel, and Matthew found a pattern in it. Is Zechariah 9:9 the same kind of text? Answer from its own wording, and say what Matthew's handling of the animals shows about what "fulfil" means for him. **Two sentences per part.**

<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):** any three: **two animals** (an ass and a colt) where Mark has one colt, with the plurals that follow ("them", "hath need of them"); the **formula quotation** added by the narrator (21:4–5, one of the ten); Mark's "**upon which no man yet hath sat**" dropped; Jesus set on "them" rather than "him". The words that account for the second animal: "**riding upon an ass, and upon a colt, the foal of an ass**". Matthew reads the two lines of the verse as naming two animals.
**Must hit, strict (b):** **No.** Zechariah 9:9 is already forward-looking ("will come to thee"): an oracle of a coming king, so it is closer to prediction than Hosea's memory of the Exodus. Yet on Markan priority Matthew fulfils it **to the letter**, matching the event to both lines of the verse, so "fulfil" means filling the text full, word for word, not merely noting that a forecast came true. Credit the counter-reading that an unbroken colt would naturally come with its mother, so Matthew may be specifying rather than adding; the direction of dependence stays a hypothesis.
**Wrong turns:** listing "no man yet hath sat" as a Matthean addition; it is Mark's, and Matthew drops it. Building on "made him sit thereon" as proof that Jesus rode both animals at once: the Greek says he sat "upon them", which may mean the garments. Calling the quotation purely typological because most of Matthew's are. (Credit noticing that "Tell ye the daughter of Sion" is not Zechariah's opening; it matches Isaiah 62:11, so the citation is composite, like 27:9.)
**Model answer:** (a) Matthew has an ass and a colt where Mark has one colt, he drops "upon which no man yet hath sat", and he adds the narrator's "that it might be fulfilled" quotation. The second animal answers Zechariah's "riding upon an ass, and upon a colt, the foal of an ass", read as two beasts. (b) No: Zechariah 9:9 announces a king who "will come", so it looks forward in its own setting, unlike Hosea 11:1. Even so, Matthew makes the scene match each line of the verse, which shows that for him fulfilment means the text filled to its full measure, not just a prediction checked off.
