# The New Testament · Lesson 5.3: Romans 5–11: Adam, Spirit, Israel

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 5: Justification in Galatians and Romans · Builds on: [5.1 Galatians](05-01-galatians-faith-law-and-the-gentiles.md), [5.2 Romans 1–4](05-02-romans-1-4-the-righteousness-of-god.md) · Unlocks: [5.4 The New Perspective on Paul](05-04-the-new-perspective-on-paul.md)

## Why this matters

Romans 1–4 argued that God's righteousness is revealed through faith, for Jew and Gentile alike ([5.2](05-02-romans-1-4-the-righteousness-of-god.md)). That leaves two problems. If the Law does not justify, what was it for, and why does sin still have power? And if most of Israel has not believed, has God's word to Israel failed? Chapters 5–8 answer the first, 9–11 the second. Three of the most argued-over readings in Western theology sit here: original sin (5:12), the divided self (ch. 7), and the destiny of the Jewish people (11:26). Each turns on one short word.

## The idea

Read the chapters as one argument with three hinges.

- **Adam (5:12–21).** Sin and death entered through one man; righteousness and life came through one man. The hinge is *eph' hō*, which the Latin rendered "in whom".
- **The "I" (7:7–25).** Someone cries "unhappy man that I am". The hinge is *egō*: whose voice is it?
- **Israel (9–11).** The answer ends in "all Israel". The hinge is *Israēl*: which Israel?

At each hinge, a good reading follows Paul's argument to where it is heading instead of stopping at the verse a later controversy made famous.

## Source

Romans 5:12 and 11:25–26, 29 (Douay–Rheims):

> 5:12. Wherefore as by one man sin entered into this world and by sin death: and so death passed upon all men, in whom all have sinned.

> 11:25. For I would not have you ignorant, brethren, of this mystery (lest you should be wise in your own conceits) that blindness in part has happened in Israel, until the fulness of the Gentiles should come in. 26. And so all Israel should be saved ... 29. For the gifts and the calling of God are without repentance.

The lesson's translation of the Greek, with the hinge words left in place:

- 5:12, last clause: *eph' hō pantes hēmarton*, "*eph' hō* all sinned" (aorist).
- 11:26: *kai houtōs pas Israēl sōthēsetai*, "and in this way all Israel **will be** saved" (future indicative; the Douay's "should" follows the Latin subjunctive). At 11:29 "without repentance" renders *ametamelēta*, "irrevocable".

## The argument

1. **The grammar of 5:12.** *Eph' hō* is the preposition *epi* with a relative pronoun. The Vulgate's *in quo*, "in whom", makes the antecedent Adam, the "one man". Most modern translators and commentators read it instead as a conjunction: **"because"** all sinned. Fitzmyer argued for a **consecutive** sense, "with the result that" all sinned (*New Testament Studies*, 1993). In his Anchor commentary he catalogues roughly a dozen proposed readings. Two things count against "in whom": the antecedent is a long way back, and Paul's usual way to say "in someone" is *en*, while *epi* with the dative normally gives a ground. *In words:* the Greek most naturally says that death spread to everyone because everyone sinned, and it does not say by itself that they sinned *in Adam*.
2. **Augustine weighed the options himself.** He asked whether the antecedent is the man, the sin or the death. He ruled out "sin" because it is feminine in Greek and "death" because it makes no sense ("all die in the sin; they do not sin in the death"). He settled on "that first man" (*Against Two Letters of the Pelagians* 4.4.7, NPNF). His use of it against Pelagius is [`patristics` 4.5](../../patristics/lessons/04-05-augustine-against-the-pelagians.md). *In words:* "in whom" was a defended choice, not a blunder.
3. **Where the weight actually sits.** Verse 12 begins a comparison ("as by one man ...") that breaks off and is only completed at 5:18–19: through one man's disobedience "many were made sinners", through one man's obedience many will be made righteous. Exegetes who read *eph' hō* causally, Fitzmyer among them, find the Adam–Christ solidarity in the paragraph as a whole. *In words:* the doctrine does not depend on how two words are construed.
4. **The "I" of Romans 7.** Verses 7–13 are past tense ("sin ... seduced me: and by it killed me"); 14–25 present ("I do not that good which I will"). Five readings have defenders:
   - (i) **Paul before his conversion**, speaking autobiographically.
   - (ii) **Paul the Christian**, and every believer, caught between the "already" and the "not yet". This was Augustine's later view (quoted below), and Luther's; in modern times Dunn and Cranfield defend it.
   - (iii) **Adam.** "I lived some time without the law" (7:9) fits only the first man. At 7:11 sin *exēpatēsen*, "deceived", me, echoing Eve's "the serpent deceived (*ēpatēsen*) me" in Genesis 3:13 LXX. The Jesuit Stanislas Lyonnet was among its defenders.
   - (iv) **Israel receiving the Law at Sinai**, held in different forms by Moo and Wright.
   - (v) **A rhetorical "I"**: humanity under the Law as seen by faith, and not autobiography at all. This is the reading of W. G. Kümmel's *Römer 7 und die Bekehrung des Paulus* (1929), which most critical commentators broadly follow, Fitzmyer included.
5. **Augustine changed sides.** He had first read chapter 7 as a man under the Law. Later he wrote that "afterwards I was constrained to give up the idea by those words where he says, Now, then, it is no more I that do it" (*Against Two Letters of the Pelagians* 1.10.22, NPNF). The delight in God's law at 7:22 could only come from grace, he argued. *In words:* the West's most influential reader held two readings in turn.
6. **Romans 8 answers chapter 7.** "There is now therefore no condemnation" (8:1). What the Law could not do, God did by sending his Son, so that its just requirement is fulfilled in those who walk by the Spirit (8:3–4). The Spirit makes believers adopted children who cry "Abba", while creation groans in waiting (8:15–23). *Pneuma* occurs more often in chapter 8 than in chapters 1–7 together; the Spirit's divinity read from it is [`trinity-and-god` 2.3](../../trinity-and-god/lessons/02-03-the-holy-spirit-in-the-new-testament.md). *In words:* chapter 7 is the problem without the Spirit; chapter 8 is life with him.
7. **Romans 9–11: has God's word failed?** Paul's thesis: "all are not Israelites that are of Israel" (9:6); God has always chosen by selection (Isaac, not Ishmael; Jacob, not Esau). Whether this election concerns individuals' eternal destiny, as Augustine read it, or peoples' roles in history is disputed. Christ is the *telos* of the Law (10:4), "end" or "goal". A remnant believes now (11:5); Israel has stumbled but not so as to fall (11:11); grafted Gentile branches must not boast (11:17–24). Then the mystery: a partial hardening "until the fulness of the Gentiles", and so "all Israel will be saved" (11:25–26), because God's call is irrevocable (11:29).
8. **The readings of 11:26.**
   - **(A) Ethnic Israel as a whole**, turned to God at the End by the Deliverer from Zion. This is the majority modern reading, Fitzmyer's and Dunn's among others. "All" means the people as a whole, and not necessarily every individual.
   - **(B) The Church of Jews and Gentiles together.** Calvin wrote "I extend the word Israel to all the people of God" (*Commentary on Romans*, Owen's translation), citing Galatians 6:16. Wright holds a version of this reading.
   - **(C) The elect Jewish remnant**, gathered across history, held by some Reformed exegetes.
   - **(D) Israel saved by God's covenant faithfulness without coming to faith in Christ**, the "two-covenant" or *Sonderweg* reading. Stendahl, Gaston and Gager hold it in varying forms.

   Two textual facts cut across these. The word "Israel" in verse 25 plainly means ethnic Israel, which counts against B. And 11:23 grafts the branches back "if they abide not still in unbelief", which counts against D.

**Mark the levels.** That original sin passes to all from Adam by propagation, and not by imitation, is **defined dogma** (Trent, Session V). Canon 4 says that Romans 5:12 is to be understood only as the Catholic Church has always understood it. That binds the *sense* of Paul's teaching, not the philology of *epi* with the dative, so "because" is compatible with the canon on the common reading; the canon's exact scope belongs to [`creation-grace-last-things`](../../creation-grace-last-things/syllabus.md) 3.3. The "I" of Romans 7 is **freely disputed**, and so are readings A to C of 11:26. [Nostra Aetate](../reference.md#nostra-aetate-4-on-israel) 4 is a conciliar declaration: **authentic magisterium**, not a definition. Citing Romans 11:28–29, it teaches that the Jews remain most dear to God for the fathers' sake, since he does not revoke his gifts and call; citing 11:11–32, that the Church awaits the day all peoples call on the Lord with one voice. The 2015 reflection of the Commission for Religious Relations with the Jews says of itself that it is not magisterial. A reading on which anyone is saved apart from Christ (D in its strong form) meets teaching well above free dispute; that assessment, and these documents' weight, belong to [`apologetics-foundations`](../../apologetics-foundations/syllabus.md) 6.4. The exegete's question is narrower: what did Paul say?

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean): [the olive tree](../reference.md#the-olive-tree), 11:17–24.** Identify each figure. The holy **root** (11:16) is the patriarchs and God's promise to them. The **natural branches** are Israel, some "broken" off "because of unbelief" (11:20). The **wild olive** is the Gentile believer, grafted in "contrary to nature" (11:24). Then ask whom the passage warns: the *Gentiles*, "Boast not against the branches ... the root [bears] thee" (11:18). The logic runs lesser to greater: if wild branches could be grafted in, "how much more" the natural ones (11:24). So the Gentiles are grafted into Israel's tree; they do not replace it. Nostra Aetate 4 cites this image for the Church's roots. The reading is clean because the passage explains its own figures.

**Example 2 (hard): the "I" at 7:14 and at 7:25.** Test the Christian reading (ii) against the verses. **For it:** the present tenses from 7:14. The inner delight in God's law (7:22), which Augustine thought only grace could give. And 7:25b: *after* the thanksgiving "through Jesus Christ our Lord", the speaker still says "with the mind [I] serve the law of God: but with the flesh, the law of sin". **Against it:** 7:14 says "sold under sin", yet 6:18 says believers have been "freed from sin", and 8:2 says the law of the Spirit "hath delivered me". Here the method strains: each reading must explain away one set of verses. Reading (v) fits 8:2 but must treat 7:25b as a summary of the condition before deliverance; reading (ii) fits 7:25b but must soften "sold under sin". No verse settles it; you weigh which difficulty is easier to live with.

## Watch out

- **"The Council of Trent defined that *eph' hō* means 'in whom'."** Trent defined a doctrine and bound the sense of the passage. It did not rule on how to construe a Greek preposition. Treating a translation as dogma is a level error, and so is the reverse: "the Greek disproves original sin."
- **Rom 7 as Paul's tormented pre-conversion conscience.** Philippians 3:6 has Paul "according to the justice that is in the law, conversing without blame". This is where [5.4](05-04-the-new-perspective-on-paul.md) begins (Stendahl).
- **"All Israel will be saved" is not a timetable.** Paul calls it a *mystery*, with no date. The Catechism's use of it (CCC 674, Israel's recognition of the Messiah before Christ's return) keeps its sources' weight and defines no reading ([`fundamental-theology` 4.5](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-05-reading-a-documents-weight.md)).

## One-liner

> Three small words carry Romans 5–11: *eph' hō* (how we share Adam's sin), *egō* (whose struggle chapter 7 describes), and *Israēl* (which Israel will be saved), and in each case the argument around the word matters more than the word itself.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢)** *(Exegetical (a) · Exegetical (b))* Romans 5:12 in Greek ends *kai houtōs eis pantas anthrōpous ho thanatos diēlthen, eph' hō pantes hēmarton*.

(a) Translate the whole clause twice, once taking *eph' hō* as "in whom" and once as causal, and give two grammatical reasons most exegetes prefer the causal reading. **Two sentences.**
(b) An **invented** study-Bible note reads: "Since Trent quoted the verse as 'in whom all have sinned,' Catholics are bound to that translation, and a 'because' rendering contradicts the Council." Diagnose the error and say what Trent does bind. **Two sentences.**

**P2 (🟡)** *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b))* Reading the "I" of Romans 7.

(a) For each item, name the reading of the "I" that it **most supports** and one reading it **troubles**. One line each.
1. "And I lived some time without the law. But when the commandment came, sin revived" (7:9).
2. "Sin, taking occasion by the commandment, seduced me" (7:11), with *exēpatēsen*, beside Genesis 3:13 LXX, "the serpent deceived (*ēpatēsen*) me".
3. From 7:14 the verbs change to the present tense.
4. "According to the justice that is in the law, conversing without blame" (Philippians 3:6).

(b) Which reading do you find strongest? Answer in **100 words or fewer**, meeting the one item from (a) or Example 2 that most threatens your reading.

**P3 (🔴)** *(Exegetical (a) · Exegetical (b))* Two positions on Romans 11:26, paraphrased:
- **A:** "All Israel" is the Jewish people as a whole, whom God will turn to himself at the End, once the fulness of the Gentiles has come in.
- **B:** "All Israel" is the whole people of God, Jews and Gentiles together. "In this way" means through the Gentiles' coming in and the Jews it provokes to faith along the way.

(a) Find the crux. Name the single premise about the word "Israel" that B must hold and A denies. Name one detail of 11:25–28 each side appeals to. **Three sentences.**
(b) An **invented** catechist's handout reads: "The Church has defined that before the end every Jew will convert (see CCC 674 and Nostra Aetate)." Name two distinct level errors. **Two sentences.**

<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical (a) · Exegetical (b))*

**Must hit, strict (a):** two translations: "and so death spread to all human beings, in whom [Adam] all sinned" and "... because all sinned". Two reasons from these: the supposed antecedent ("one man") is far back in the sentence; Paul expresses "in" with *en*, and *epi* with the dative normally gives a ground ("on the basis of"), so *eph' hō* works as a conjunction; Augustine himself had to rule out two other possible antecedents, so "in whom" was never the obvious reading. Credit the consecutive reading "with the result that" (Fitzmyer) as a third option, but it is not required.

**Must hit, strict (b):** the note treats a **translation** as though it were the **doctrine**. Trent Session V defines original sin, transmitted by propagation and not by imitation, and canon 4 binds the *sense* in which Romans 5:12 is to be understood. It does not rule on the construal of *eph' hō*, and a causal rendering is compatible with the canon on the common reading. The paragraph as a whole (5:18–19) carries the Adam–Christ teaching.

**Wrong turns:** saying Trent is thereby "wrong" or that the Greek refutes original sin, which is the mirror-image level error. Blaming Augustine for inventing *in quo*: he inherited the Latin rendering and defended it among options.

**Model answer:** (a) "Death spread to all, in whom all sinned" versus "death spread to all, because all sinned". The antecedent "one man" is remote, and Paul would say "in" with *en*, while *eph' hō* with the dative normally functions as a conjunction of cause. (b) The note confuses a rendering with a dogma. Trent binds the doctrine of original sin and the sense of the passage, not the construal of two Greek words, which the Adam–Christ parallel of 5:18–19 carries regardless.

**P2** *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b))*

**Must hit, strict (a):**
1. Supports **Adam** (only the first man lived before any commandment) or **Israel before Sinai**. Troubles **Christian Paul**, and troubles literal **pre-conversion autobiography**, since Paul was a Jew raised under the Law from birth.
2. Supports **Adam** (the echo of Eve's words). Troubles a purely **autobiographical** reading.
3. Supports **Christian Paul** (a present struggle). Troubles **pre-conversion** autobiography, which would expect past tenses.
4. Troubles **pre-conversion autobiography**, since Paul remembers no tormented conscience. It is consistent with the **rhetorical "I"** (Kümmel) and with the **Israel** reading.

**Must hit, any verdict (b):** name one reading. Meet the item from (a) or Example 2 that tells hardest against it: for the Christian reading, "sold under sin" against 6:18 and 8:2; for the rhetorical or Adamic readings, the present tenses and 7:25b. Say why that difficulty is survivable. One-line verdict.

**Wrong turns:** treating Augustine as a single witness when he held two readings in turn. Citing Kümmel as proving Paul never struggled; his claim is about the grammatical person of the passage, not about Paul's psychology.

**Model answer (b), one of several:** The rhetorical "I", humanity under the Law as seen by faith, is strongest. It explains the Adamic echoes of 7:9–11 without claiming Paul ever lived without the Law. It also fits Philippians 3:6 and "sold under sin", which clashes with 6:18. The hardest item against it is 7:25b, where the divided service follows the thanksgiving. But that sentence can be read as a summary of the condition just described, before 8:1–2 announces the deliverance. That is a smaller strain than making the baptized "sold under sin". Verdict: rhetorical "I".

**P3** *(Exegetical (a) · Exegetical (b))*

**Must hit, strict (a):** the crux is whether **"Israel" in 11:26 keeps the sense it has in 11:25**. B must hold that the word shifts within the sentence from ethnic Israel to the people of God. A denies it. A appeals to "blindness in part has happened in Israel" (11:25, plainly ethnic), the "until", and "most dear for the sake of the fathers" (11:28). B appeals to *houtōs* as "in this way" (manner, not sequence), to Galatians 6:16's "Israel of God", and to 9:6, "all are not Israelites that are of Israel".

**Must hit, strict (b):** (1) **Nothing is defined.** CCC 674 carries only the weight of its sources, and Nostra Aetate is a conciliar declaration of authentic, non-definitive teaching. Neither defines an end-time conversion. The readings of 11:26 are freely disputed. (2) **Misreporting content.** "Every Jew" is stronger than Paul's "all Israel", which is corporate on reading A. Nostra Aetate 4 speaks of awaiting the day all peoples call on the Lord together; it does not predict the conversion of every Jew.

**Wrong turns:** stating the crux as "whether Jews are saved", which is the conclusion, not the premise. In (b), calling the Catechism a definition.

**Model answer:** (a) B must hold that "Israel" changes meaning between 11:25 and 11:26, and A denies that it can. A points to the ethnic "Israel" of 11:25 and "most dear for the sake of the fathers" in 11:28. B points to *houtōs* ("in this way") and to 9:6's distinction within Israel. (b) Neither the Catechism nor Nostra Aetate defines anything here: one keeps its sources' weight, and the other is authentic but non-definitive teaching, which leaves the reading of 11:26 free. And "every Jew will convert" goes beyond what both Paul and the Council say.

## Flashback

**From Lesson [5.1](05-01-galatians-faith-law-and-the-gentiles.md) (Galatians: faith, Law, and the Gentiles):** *(Exegetical.)* Galatians 5:2–3, 6, Douay–Rheims:

> Behold, I Paul tell you, that if you be circumcised, Christ shall profit you nothing. And I testify again to every man circumcising himself that he is a debtor to do the whole law. ... For in Christ Jesus neither circumcision availeth any thing nor uncircumcision: but faith that worketh by Charity.

"Debtor" is *opheiletēs*, someone who owes. "Worketh" is *energoumenē*: the phrase *pistis di' agapēs energoumenē* means "faith at work through love".

(a) Two readings of *erga nomou*, "works of the law", both claim 5:3. The **traditional** reading takes the phrase as the deeds the Law commands, offered as grounds for standing with God. The **identity-marker** reading (Dunn's) takes it as the practices that mark out the Jewish people: circumcision, food laws, sabbath. Say how each reading explains why circumcision makes a man "a debtor to do the whole law". **One sentence each.**
(b) Verse 6 says that *neither* circumcision *nor* uncircumcision counts. Name one misreading of Galatians that the "nor uncircumcision" rules out, and say what the verse puts in the place of both. **Two sentences.**

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):** **Traditional:** a man who seeks justification through the Law takes on the obligation to keep *all* of it, and (joined with 3:10's curse on whoever does not keep "all things") no one does, so this route to justification fails. **Identity-marker:** circumcision is the rite of entry into the Jewish people, so a Gentile who accepts it is enrolling in the whole Jewish way of life, food laws and sabbath included, which is exactly what Antioch was about. Credit noting that 5:3 does not itself say the Law cannot be kept: the traditional reading brings that premise in from 3:10.
**Must hit, strict (b):** it rules out reading Paul as saying the Law or circumcision is **bad**, or that being uncircumcised is itself a **merit**. The Law was a real, temporary guardian, and 3:21 denies that it is against the promises. In place of both, 5:6 puts **faith at work through love**: "in Christ", the circumcision question simply does not decide who belongs.
**Wrong turns:** claiming the Church has settled which reading of *erga nomou* is right, when that is freely disputed among Catholic exegetes (rung 5; Trent's canon 1 defines only that no one is justified by works of the law without grace). Reading 5:2 as Paul condemning Jewish believers who were circumcised as children, when the verse is addressed to Gentiles considering circumcision *as a condition*. Using "faith that worketh by Charity" to settle the later Catholic–Protestant dispute over justification: that belongs to [`creation-grace-last-things`](../../creation-grace-last-things/syllabus.md), and the exegete's point is the contrast with circumcision.
**Model answer:** (a) On the traditional reading, taking circumcision commits a man to the Law as his route to righteousness, and that route requires keeping all of it, which 3:10 says no one does. On the identity-marker reading, circumcision is the entry rite of the Jewish people, so a Gentile who takes it signs up for the whole Jewish way of life, the food laws that divided the table at Antioch included. (b) "Nor uncircumcision" rules out reading Paul as saying the Law was bad or that staying uncircumcised is itself a merit: the badge counts for nothing either way. What counts instead is faith at work through love.
