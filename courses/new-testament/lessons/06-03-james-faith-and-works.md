# The New Testament · Lesson 6.3: James: faith and works

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 6: The other letters, Revelation, and the canon · Builds on: [5.2 Romans 1–4](05-02-romans-1-4-the-righteousness-of-god.md), [6.1 The disputed Pauline letters](06-01-the-disputed-pauline-letters-and-the-pastorals.md) · Unlocks: [6.4 Peter, Jude, and the letters of John](06-04-peter-jude-and-the-letters-of-john.md), [6.6 The formation of the canon](06-06-the-formation-of-the-canon.md)

## Why this matters

Romans 3:28: "we account a man to be justified by faith, without the works of the law." James 2:24: "by works a man is justified, and not by faith only." Side by side they look like a flat contradiction. Luther called James an epistle of straw; Trent cited 2:24 in its decree on justification. This lesson reads James 2:14–26 on its own terms, then sets it beside Paul word by word. Most of the "contradiction" sits in three Greek words the two writers use differently. Some of it survives, and that part is a real dispute.

## The idea

Two doctors write two sentences. One says "No pill will cure you; only the surgery can." The other says "You are not cured until you take the pills." They sound opposed until you learn that the first means the operation that saves a dying man, and the second the medicine he takes afterwards. Same words, different stage, different pill.

James is the second doctor. His letter is Jewish wisdom: short units on trials, the tongue, the rich and the poor, with one sustained argument, against a faith that says the right things and does nothing. His "faith" is a creed recited, his "works" are bread for a hungry neighbour, his "justified" is the verdict on a whole life. Does that dissolve the conflict with Paul, or only soften it?

## Source

James 2:18–24 (Douay–Rheims, Challoner):

> But some man will say: Thou hast faith, and I have works. Shew me thy faith without works; and I will shew thee, by works, my faith. Thou believest that there is one God. Thou dost well: the devils also believe and tremble. But wilt thou know, O vain man, that faith without works is dead? Was not Abraham our father justified by works, offering up Isaac his son upon the altar? Seest thou that faith did cooperate with his works and by works faith was made perfect? And the scripture was fulfilled, saying: Abraham believed God, and it was reputed to him to justice, and he was called the friend of God. Do you see that by works a man is justified, and not by faith only?

Three Greek words carry the passage: *pistis* ("faith"), *erga* ("works") and *dikaioō* ("justify"). In 2:24 the Greek reads *ex ergōn dikaioutai anthrōpos kai ouk ek pisteōs monon*: "from works a person is justified, and not from faith alone" (lesson's translation). This is the only verse in the New Testament where "faith" and "alone" stand together. "Cooperate" in 2:22 is *synērgei*, "was working together with". In 2:20 the Douay's "dead" follows the Vulgate and the Byzantine Greek (*nekra*); the critical text reads *argē*, "idle, useless", which puns on *erga*: faith without works is work-less.

## The argument

1. **The setting: wisdom and the poor.** James opens on reversal: "let the brother of low condition glory in his exaltation: And the rich, in his being low" (1:9–10). In 2:1–7 the man with a gold ring gets the seat while the poor man stands, in an "assembly" that is in Greek a *synagōgē*. In 5:1–6 the rich "weep and howl" because the reapers' withheld wages cry out to "the Lord of Sabaoth" (5:4). This is Luke's reversal ([2.3](02-03-luke.md)), and it sets up 2:14–26, whose test case is a hungry brother. *In words:* James's "works" are mercy to the poor before they are anything else.
2. **What faith means here.** "If a man *say* he hath faith" (2:14): the faith attacked is a claim. Its content is given in 2:19, "there is one God", the Shema ([`trinity-and-god` 2.1](../../trinity-and-god/lessons/02-01-the-one-god-of-israel.md)), and the demons hold it too. So *pistis* here is correct belief without trust or obedience. Paul's faith is trust in "him that justifieth the ungodly" (Rom 4:5), and it acts: "faith that worketh by charity" (Gal 5:6). *In words:* James attacks a faith Paul would not call faith either.
3. **What works mean here.** James's examples are clothing and feeding (2:15–16), Abraham's offering and Rahab's hiding of the spies (2:25); never circumcision, food laws or sabbath. Paul's thesis verse says "works *of the law*", *erga nomou* ([works of the law](../reference.md#works-of-the-law)), and on either reading of that phrase ([5.1](05-01-galatians-faith-law-and-the-gentiles.md)) a believer's deeds of mercy are not what he excludes. The Douay's own note on Romans 3:28 draws the same line:

   > the works which he here excludes, are only the works of the law: that is, such as are done by the law of nature, or that of Moses, antecedent to the faith of Christ: but by no means, such as follow faith, and proceed from it.

   *In words:* Paul excludes works as the way in; James requires works as the way faith lives.
4. **What "justified" means here.** Paul's *dikaioō* is God's verdict on the ungodly at the start, reckoned to faith ([James 2:24 and Romans 3:28](../reference.md#james-224-and-romans-328)). For James there are three readings. **Demonstrative**: works *show* a person righteous (the "shew me" of 2:18). **Eschatological**: the final verdict, since 2:12–14 speaks of being "judged by the law of liberty" and asks whether faith can "save him". **Progressive**: justice already received grows. Trent used the third, citing 2:24 in Session VI, chapter 10, for the increase of justification. *In words:* the verb is shared; the moment it names differs.
5. **Abraham read twice.** Paul's proof text is Genesis 15:6, and his argument is chronological: it comes before circumcision in Genesis 17 (Rom 4:10). James starts from Genesis 22, the binding of Isaac, and says that there Genesis 15:6 "was fulfilled" (2:23). Jewish tradition had already joined the verse to the test: "Was not Abraham found faithful in temptation, and it was reputed to him unto justice?" (1 Maccabees 2:52; compare Sirach 44:20, DR 44:21). "Friend of God" echoes Isaiah 41:8. ([Abraham read twice](../reference.md#abraham-read-twice).) *In words:* Paul reads the verse from before the test; James reads it from after, where faith has been "made perfect".
6. **Where the collision stays.** Romans 4:2, "if Abraham were justified by works", and James 2:21, "Was not Abraham ... justified by works", have the same three Greek words, *ex ergōn edikaiōthē*. Paul denies of Abraham exactly what James asserts. So did James know Paul? Martin Hengel (1987 essay) and Dale Allison (International Critical Commentary, 2013) read 2:14–26 as a response to Paul or Pauline teaching. Many, Brown among them, see a correction of Paulinism reduced to a slogan. Luke Timothy Johnson (Anchor Bible, 1995) holds that James is not answering Paul at all; the Abraham material is common Jewish property. *In words:* the three words dissolve most of the conflict; the identical phrase keeps a residue.
7. **Author and date.** The letter claims only "James, the servant of God" (1:1). Tradition identified him as James "the brother of the Lord" (Gal 1:19), head of the Jerusalem church, stoned under the high priest Ananus while Festus was dead and Albinus was still on the road, which puts it in 62 ([Josephus on James](../reference.md#josephus-on-james)). Who the Lord's "brothers" were is the ceded question of [2.1](02-01-mark.md). *For* authorship: Jewish-Christian texture, the *synagōgē*, sayings of Jesus in unquoted form (5:12, compare Matthew 5:37). *Against*: polished Greek, silence on the ritual questions Galatians 2:12 ties to this James, and slow reception. Eusebius wrote that it "is disputed; at least, not many of the ancients have mentioned it", though it was read publicly "in very many churches" (*Church History* II.23.25, NPNF). Authentic means before 62; if not, most place it late in the first century. Brown judged that side slightly stronger; Johnson defends the early date. ([Authorship of James](../reference.md#authorship-of-james).)
8. **Luther, as reception history.** Luther's 1522 preface to the New Testament ranked John, Paul and 1 Peter as the books that show Christ, calling James, in the lesson's rendering of his German, "truly an epistle of straw compared with them, for it has nothing of the gospel's character about it". The straw is Paul's: "wood, hay, stubble" built on the one foundation (1 Cor 3:12). Timothy Wengert (*Word & World*, 2015) reports that the remark was cut from every edition of the complete Bible and, after 1539, from the separate New Testaments. His preface to James itself stayed: it objected that James makes Genesis 15 a text about works and does not teach Christ, and the letter was printed among the last books, after Hebrews ([Luther and the epistle of straw](../reference.md#luther-and-the-epistle-of-straw)). *In words:* Luther demoted James; he did not delete it.
9. **Anointing (5:14–15).** "Let him bring in the priests of the church": Greek *presbyterous*, elders. Trent read it as promulgating the sacrament of anointing; the doctrine belongs to [`sacraments-and-liturgy`](../../sacraments-and-liturgy/syllabus.md).

**Mark the levels.** That James is **canonical and inspired** is **defined dogma, rung 1** of [the ladder](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-04-the-ladder-of-doctrinal-authority.md). Trent's list includes "one of the apostle James", and the anathema covers the books "entire with all their parts" ([the canon as defined](../reference.md#the-canon-as-defined); [`fundamental-theology` 3.3](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/03-03-the-canon-as-defined.md)). Also **defined** (Trent VI, canon 24): justice received is preserved and increased by good works, which are not "merely the fruits and signs" of justification. That bounds a Catholic's theology of works but does not settle the grammar of *dikaioutai*: chapter 10's use of 2:24 is not a defined exegesis. Who wrote James, when, and whether he answered Paul are **scholarly hypotheses** with no magisterial standing either way. Whether Trent's titles ("the apostle James"; in Session XIV "James the Apostle, and brother of the Lord") bind on human authorship is **freely disputed**, as for Paul's letters in [4.2](04-02-pauls-life-and-letters.md). See [Church teaching vs hypothesis](../reference.md#church-teaching-vs-hypothesis).

## Three words, two writers

| Word | Paul (Romans 3–4, Galatians) | James 2:14–26 |
|---|---|---|
| *pistis*, faith | trust in the God who justifies the ungodly; "worketh by charity" (Gal 5:6) | a claim to right belief ("if a man say"); the demons have it (2:19) |
| *erga*, works | "works of the law" done to gain standing; wages owed (Rom 4:4) | mercy to the needy; Abraham's and Rahab's obedience |
| *dikaioō*, justify | God's verdict on the ungodly, reckoned to faith | shown righteous, or judged righteous at the end, or growing in justice |
| Abraham | Genesis 15:6, *before* circumcision (Gen 17) | Genesis 22, where 15:6 "was fulfilled" |

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean): the demons' faith (2:19).** Method: find the word, let the sentence define it. The faith's content is "there is one God", and the demons "believe and tremble" without trusting, loving or obeying. So *pistis* here is assent to a true proposition. Paul's Abraham, by contrast, "against hope believed in hope" (Rom 4:18): trust. On this word the writers are not in contact; James refutes a faith Paul never taught.

**Example 2 (hard): *ex ergōn edikaiōthē*.** Now the method strains. Romans 4:2 and James 2:21 use the same three Greek words about the same man, and Paul's "works" in 4:2 are not qualified "of the law". Two moves remain. (i) Context: Paul's next verse defines his works as wage-earning, "according to debt" (4:4). James's work is faith acting: "faith did cooperate with his works" (2:22). (ii) Sequence: Paul's Abraham is the man of Genesis 15, James's the man of Genesis 22. Neither move shows James wrote without Paul in view; the identical phrase is Allison's best evidence that he had. Honest report: on most readings the conflict is verbal; whether James meant a reply is open.

## Watch out

- **"The Church teaches that James, the brother of the Lord, wrote this letter."** Canonicity is defined. Human authorship is a hypothesis, and whether Trent's title binds is freely disputed. Promoting the tradition to dogma is a level error.
- **The reverse error: "that good works increase justification is one Catholic reading of James."** That good works preserve and increase justice received is **defined** (Trent VI, canon 24). What is open is whether 2:24 *says* it.
- **"Luther removed James from his Bible."** He printed it among the last books and denied it was apostolic; the straw line, in the 1522 general preface, was later cut.

## One-liner

> Different faith, different works, different moment of justification, different chapter of Genesis: most of the James–Paul contradiction is verbal, whether James was answering Paul is disputed, and only the letter's canonicity is defined.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢)** *(Exegetical)* Close reading, James 2:14–17 (Douay–Rheims):

> What shall it profit, my brethren, if a man say he hath faith, but hath not works? Shall faith be able to save him? And if a brother or sister be naked and want daily food: And one of you say to them: Go in peace, be ye warmed and filled; yet give them not those things that are necessary for the body, what shall it profit? So faith also, if it have not works, is dead in itself.

(a) Which word in 2:14 marks the kind of faith James is attacking, and what is that faith? (b) What kind of "works" does the example in 2:15–16 supply, and why does that matter for setting James beside Romans 3:28? (c) What is the parallel between the speaker in 2:16 and the faith of 2:17? **Two sentences per part.**

**P2 (🟡)** *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b))* Synopsis (Douay–Rheims):

| Romans 4:2–3 | James 2:21–23 |
|---|---|
| For if Abraham were justified by works, he hath whereof to glory, but not before God. For what saith the scripture? Abraham believed God: and it was reputed to him unto justice. | Was not Abraham our father justified by works, offering up Isaac his son upon the altar? Seest thou that faith did cooperate with his works and by works faith was made perfect? And the scripture was fulfilled, saying: Abraham believed God, and it was reputed to him to justice, and he was called the friend of God. |

The Greek of "justified by works" is *ex ergōn edikaiōthē* in both. (a) List what the columns share, and say what each writer *does with* Genesis 15:6: which event in Abraham's life each reads it from, and for what conclusion. (b) Does this overlap show that James knew Paul's argument (or a slogan drawn from it), or can it be explained by shared Jewish tradition? Any verdict, naming the strongest piece of evidence against you. **100 words for (b).**

**P3 (🔴)** *(Exegetical)* An **invented** parish-bulletin column:

> "James 2:24 says we are justified by works and not by faith only; Romans 3:28 says we are justified by faith without works. They cannot both be right, so one of the two is wrong. That is why Luther threw James out of his Bible. As Catholics we know better: the Church has defined that James the brother of the Lord wrote it before 62, and Trent defined that 2:24 means the increase of justification."

Find **four** errors and correct each in **one sentence**, marking the level of any claim about Church teaching.

<details>
<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** "**say**" ("if a man *say* he hath faith"): the faith in view is **professed**, a claim without the life that should go with it. By 2:19 its content is right belief ("there is one God"), which the demons share.

**Must hit, strict (b):** **deeds of mercy**: clothing and feeding a needy brother or sister. Not circumcision, food laws or sabbath, so these are not the ***erga nomou*** Paul excludes in Romans 3:28, and the two verses are not talking about the same works.

**Must hit, strict (c):** the speaker's **words** ("be ye warmed and filled") are right and **do nothing**; a faith that only speaks is equally useless, "dead in itself". The illustration *is* the argument: words without deeds help no one.

**Wrong turns:** treating James's faith as Paul's saving trust, then declaring a contradiction. Reading "works" as ritual law because the word is the same.

**Model answer:** (a) "Say" marks a faith that is only professed, the right creed with nothing done, which 2:19 shows the demons share. (b) The example supplies works of mercy to the poor, not circumcision or food laws, so they are not the "works of the law" that Romans 3:28 excludes. (c) The speaker offers the needy good words and no bread; a faith of words alone is just as empty, and James calls it dead.

**P2** *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b))*

**Must hit, strict (a):** Shared: Abraham, **"justified by works"** (*ex ergōn edikaiōthē*), and the quotation of **Genesis 15:6** (believed / reputed to justice). **Paul** quotes 15:6 in its own place, the promise before circumcision (Gen 15, 17), to show that Abraham was **not** justified by works: he has nothing to glory in before God. **James** reads 15:6 **from Genesis 22**, the offering of Isaac, as "fulfilled" there, to show faith **working with** works and **made perfect** by them.

**Must hit, any verdict (b):** state a position; cite evidence for it (for contact: the identical phrase, the same proof text, "not by faith only" as an apparent echo of a Pauline formula; for independence: 1 Maccabees 2:52 already joining Genesis 15:6 to the testing, James's silence on circumcision and the Law, the different sense of "works"); name the strongest evidence against your side.

**Wrong turns:** listing only differences. Claiming that the Church has settled the direction. It has not: the relation is a hypothesis.

**Model answer (b), one of several:** Contact is likelier. Both writers put Abraham, "justified by works" and Genesis 15:6 together, and James's "not by faith only" reads like a reply to a slogan in circulation after Paul, whether or not James knew the letters. The strongest evidence against is 1 Maccabees 2:52: Jewish tradition already read Genesis 15:6 through Abraham's testing, so James could have reached his reading with no Paul at all.

**P3** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict:**
1. "**One of the two is wrong**": the verses use *pistis*, *erga* and *dikaioō* in **different senses** (professed belief vs trust; mercy vs works of the law; final or demonstrated righteousness vs the initial verdict), so the contradiction is largely verbal. How much residue remains is a scholarly dispute. And both books are **canonical and inspired: defined dogma** (Trent IV).
2. "**Luther threw James out**": he kept it, printing it among the last books with a preface denying it was apostolic; the "straw" remark was in his 1522 general preface and was later cut.
3. "**Defined that James the brother of the Lord wrote it before 62**": the Church defines the book, not its human author or date. These are **hypotheses**, and whether Trent's title binds is **freely disputed**.
4. "**Trent defined that 2:24 means the increase**": canon 24 **defines** that good works preserve and increase justice received; citing 2:24 in chapter 10 is a **use of the verse**, not a defined exegesis.

**Wrong turns:** "correcting" (1) by saying James and Paul mean exactly the same thing; Romans 4:2 keeps a real exegetical question open. Treating canon 24 as a matter of opinion.

**Model answer:** (1) James and Paul use faith, works and justify in different senses, so the verses need not contradict, and both books are canonical by defined dogma. (2) Luther did not remove James; he printed it among the last books and doubted it was apostolic. (3) Authorship by the brother of the Lord and a date before 62 are scholarly hypotheses, and whether Trent's title binds on authorship is freely disputed. (4) Trent defined that good works increase justification (canon 24) but did not define that James 2:24 means that.

</details>

## Flashback

**From Lesson [6.1](06-01-the-disputed-pauline-letters-and-the-pastorals.md) (The disputed Pauline letters and the pastorals):** *(Exegetical.)* Find the crux. 2 Timothy 4:9–13 (Douay–Rheims; the Douay numbers these verses one lower):

> Make haste to come to me quickly. For Demas hath left me, loving this world, and is gone to Thessalonica: Crescens into Galatia, Titus into Dalmatia. Only Luke is with me. Take Mark and bring him with thee: for he is profitable to me for the ministry. But Tychicus I have sent to Ephesus. The cloak that I left at Troas, with Carpus, when thou comest, bring with thee: and the books, especially the parchments.

"Cloak" is *phailonēs*, a heavy travelling cloak; "parchments" is *membranas*. For comparison, Philemon 24 sends greetings from "Mark, Aristarchus, Demas and Luke, my fellow labourers." (a) Which of 6.1's three tests does this passage bear on? Name two people it shares with Philemon 24, and one detail that puts it in a different scene from Philemon. **Two sentences.** (b) David Meade and Bart Ehrman both deny that Paul held the pen. In one sentence each, say how each would account for the cloak and the parchments. Then name the crux between them in one sentence.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):** **Setting**: Paul's movements and circle. Shared names: any two of **Mark, Demas, Luke** (also in Colossians 4:10, 14). The scene differs: **Demas has now deserted** ("hath left me, loving this world"), the circle has scattered, and "only Luke" remains. Defenders place this in a second Roman imprisonment after Acts 28; opponents say the writer borrowed Paul's known circle.

**Must hit, strict (b):** **Ehrman**: such incidental personal touches are verisimilitude, details *designed to be believed*, so they are evidence of an intent to deceive, which is forgery in the sense ancient writers condemned. **Meade**: the letter is a school presenting its founder's authoritative teaching to a new day, a claim to tradition rather than a lie, so the details belong to that portrait of Paul and deceive no one. **Crux:** whether the first readers would have recognized the name as a transparent convention or taken it at face value. That is, did early Christians accept school pseudonymity at all? Ehrman's side cites the Church's own record: Tertullian's deposed presbyter and the Muratorian fragment's rejection of letters "forged under the name of Paul".

**Wrong turns:** treating the cloak as proof of authenticity. A forger or an admirer could supply it, and on every hypothesis the detail is expected, so it cannot decide authorship on its own (6.1's Example 2 made the same point about 2 Thessalonians 3:17). Giving Meade the view that pseudonymity is deception; that is Ehrman's charge. Turning (b) into a question about canonicity: that 2 Timothy is canonical and inspired is **defined dogma, rung 1**, on every one of these hypotheses. Who wrote it is a **hypothesis**.

**Model answer:** (a) The passage bears on setting. Mark, Demas and Luke all greet in Philemon 24, but here Demas has deserted and only Luke remains, which is a later and lonelier scene. (b) Ehrman: the cloak and parchments are there to be believed, the forger's lifelike touch, so they show intent to deceive. Meade: they belong to a school's portrait of its founder's last testament, a convention that claims tradition and tells no lie. The crux is whether the first readers knew the convention and read the name as transparent, or took it at face value, as the early Church's condemnations of forgery suggest they did.

</details>
