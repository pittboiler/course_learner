# The New Testament · Lesson 5.4: The New Perspective on Paul

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 5: Justification in Galatians and Romans · Builds on: [5.1 Galatians](05-01-galatians-faith-law-and-the-gentiles.md), [5.2 Romans 1–4](05-02-romans-1-4-the-righteousness-of-god.md), [5.3 Romans 5–11](05-03-romans-5-11-adam-spirit-israel.md) · Unlocks: [6.3 James: faith and works](06-03-james-faith-and-works.md)

## Why this matters

For four centuries Protestants and Catholics fought over what Paul meant by "not by works of the law", and both assumed they knew what Paul was arguing *against*: Jews trying to earn salvation by keeping commandments. Since the 1970s a large body of scholarship has denied that assumption. If it is right, the passages of Galatians and Romans you have just read were answering a different question. This lesson states the Old and the New Perspectives as their best defenders would, and pins down the single disagreement that decides between them.

## The idea

Two stories about the same letters.

**The Old Perspective** (the name its critics gave it). Paul's opponents thought a person stands right with God by doing what the Law commands. Paul answers that no one does enough, so righteousness must be God's gift, received by faith. "Works of the law" stands for human achievement offered as grounds before God. Augustine read Paul against the Pelagians this way ([`patristics` 4.5](../../patristics/lessons/04-05-augustine-against-the-pelagians.md)), and Luther sharpened it.

**The New Perspective.** Judaism in Paul's day was not a religion of earning. Israel was chosen by grace, and the Law was how the chosen people lived. Paul's quarrel was about *who belongs*: must Gentiles become Jews, by circumcision, food laws and sabbath, to be God's people? "Works of the law" names the practices that marked Israel off from the nations. Paul's "not by works of the law" means "not by being Jewish".

Both fit much of the text. This lesson finds the verse where they cannot both be right.

## Source

Philippians 3:4–9, Douay–Rheims (Challoner). Paul lists what he gave up for Christ:

> Though I might also have confidence in the flesh. If any other thinketh he may have confidence in the flesh, I more: Being circumcised the eighth day, of the stock of Israel, of the tribe of Benjamin, an Hebrew of the Hebrews. According to the law, a Pharisee: According to zeal, persecuting the church of God: According to the justice that is in the law, conversing without blame. But the things that were gain to me, the same I have counted loss for Christ. Furthermore, I count all things to be but loss for the excellent knowledge of Jesus Christ, my Lord: for whom I have suffered the loss of all things and count them but as dung, that I may gain Christ. And may be found in him, not having my justice, which is of the law, but that which is of the faith of Christ Jesus, which is of God: justice in faith.

The phrase to watch is 3:9, *mē echōn emēn dikaiosynēn tēn ek nomou*: "not having a righteousness of my own, the one from the Law". Everything turns on what makes it "my own".

## The argument

1. **Stendahl: the conscience was the West's, not Paul's.** Krister Stendahl, in "The Apostle Paul and the Introspective Conscience of the West" (*Harvard Theological Review*, 1963), argued that Augustine and Luther read their own guilt into Paul. Paul himself had a "robust" conscience. Philippians 3:6 has him "without blame" under the Law, with no hint of a tormented search for a gracious God. Paul's real question, on Stendahl's reading, was what Christ's coming means for the Law and for the place of Gentiles. *In words:* Paul was not Luther, and his letters answer a missionary question, not an anguished one.
2. **Sanders: covenantal nomism.** E. P. Sanders, *Paul and Palestinian Judaism* (1977), surveyed the rabbinic literature, the Dead Sea Scrolls and the Apocrypha and found a common pattern he named [covenantal nomism](../reference.md#covenantal-nomism). "Getting in" is by God's gracious election of Israel. "Staying in" is by obedience, with sacrifice and repentance provided for failure. Obedience keeps one's place; it does not earn it. So what did Paul object to? Sanders's answer: he reasoned from solution to plight. Paul began from the conviction that God saves in Christ and concluded that the Law cannot be the way. On this reading his complaint against Judaism was, bluntly, that it was not faith in Christ. *In words:* Judaism already had grace; Paul's problem with it was Christological, not a problem with earning.
3. **Dunn: boundary markers.** Sanders left "works of the law" unexplained. James D. G. Dunn supplied a reading in his 1982 Manson Memorial Lecture, published in 1983 as "The New Perspective on Paul". (The phrase itself had appeared in a 1978 lecture by N. T. Wright; Dunn's title made it the movement's name.) For Dunn, [works of the law](../reference.md#works-of-the-law) are the Law's practices in their role as markers of Jewish identity, above all circumcision, food laws and sabbath. The Antioch incident ([5.1](05-01-galatians-faith-law-and-the-gentiles.md)) is his exhibit. In later work Dunn allowed that the phrase covers the whole Law, with the markers as the points where it bit. A Qumran text, 4QMMT, uses the Hebrew equivalent ("some of the works of the Law") for a set of sectarian legal rulings; both sides cite it. *In words:* Paul attacks the Law as a fence around Israel, not as a ladder to heaven.
4. **Wright: justification as membership.** N. T. Wright (*What Saint Paul Really Said*, 1997, and later work) argued that "justification" is God's declaration of who belongs to his covenant people. It is about the identity of the Church as much as about how an individual is saved, and faith in Christ is that people's one badge. *In words:* to be justified is to be declared a member of Abraham's one family.
5. **The replies.** Three lines of reply followed.
   - **The Old Perspective, restated.** Stephen Westerholm, *Perspectives Old and New on Paul* (2004), granted Sanders much about Judaism and still argued that Paul contrasts doing and believing as such. Romans 4:4–5 sets wages against gift, and Philippians 3:9 "my own" righteousness against the righteousness "from God". The two *Justification and Variegated Nomism* volumes (eds. Carson, O'Brien and Seifrid; 2001 on Second Temple Judaism, 2004 on Paul) argued that Sanders's pattern was real but not uniform: some texts put real weight on works in final judgment.
   - **Barclay: which kind of grace?** John Barclay, *Paul and the Gift* (2015), reframed the fight. "Grace" can be perfected in different ways: its size (superabundance), the giver's pure goodwill (singularity), its timing before any human move (priority), its disregard of the recipient's worth (incongruity), its power to transform (efficacy), and its freedom from any expected return (non-circularity). Judaism was full of grace, as Sanders said, but many Jewish texts expected God to give to the fitting. Paul's distinctive stress is incongruity: God's gift in Christ ignored every prior measure of worth, including being Jewish. That is why it could include Gentiles. [The perfections of grace](../reference.md#the-perfections-of-grace) let both sides be partly right.
   - **[The apocalyptic reading](../reference.md#the-apocalyptic-reading-of-paul).** J. Louis Martyn (*Galatians*, Anchor Bible, 1997) and Douglas Campbell (*The Deliverance of God*, 2009) cut across both perspectives. For them Paul's gospel is God's invasion of an enslaved world. It is neither a better route to acceptance nor a redrawn membership line.

**The crux.** Both perspectives agree that Paul rejects "works of the law" as the ground of righteousness. They disagree about *why*. Is it because they are human doing, contrasted with receiving? Or because they are Jewish, contrasted with open to all? Philippians 3 shows the crux. Paul's list mixes things given at birth (circumcised, Israel, Benjamin) with things achieved (Pharisee, zeal, blameless). Dunn's side reads "my own" righteousness as Israel's privileged status, owned by Paul as a Jew. Westerholm's side reads it as righteousness based on Paul's own doing, set against the one "from God".

**Mark the levels.** That the sinner is justified by grace, and not by works of the Law done without grace, is **defined dogma** (Trent, Session VI; the decree is owned by [`creation-grace-last-things`](../../creation-grace-last-things/syllabus.md)). Trent does not define what Paul meant by *erga nomou*, what Judaism was like, or whether "justification" in Paul means membership. The New Perspective, the Old, Barclay and the apocalyptic reading are **scholarly hypotheses with no magisterial standing either way**: freely disputed (rung 5 of [the ladder](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-04-the-ladder-of-doctrinal-authority.md)). The *Joint Declaration on the Doctrine of Justification* (Lutheran World Federation and Catholic Church, 1999) is an agreement on doctrine, not a ruling on Pauline exegesis ([church teaching vs hypothesis](../reference.md#church-teaching-vs-hypothesis)). Benedict XVI, in a general audience of 19 November 2008, described the works of the Law as circumcision, food and purity rules and sabbath, the observances that marked Israel's identity. That shows a boundary-marker reading is at home in papal catechesis. It does not make it Church teaching. A general-audience catechesis defines nothing.

## Map of positions

| | Old Perspective (Luther; Westerholm) | Sanders | Dunn | Wright | Barclay | Apocalyptic (Martyn; Campbell) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Judaism in Paul's day | tended to rely on doing | covenantal nomism: grace first | covenantal nomism | covenantal nomism | full of grace, perfected differently | not the main question |
| "Works of the law" | deeds the Law commands, as human achievement | the Law as not-Christ | identity markers | identity markers | the Law as a measure of worth | the Law as one of the enslaving powers |
| Paul's objection | no one earns righteousness | the Law is not the way God now saves | it excludes Gentiles | it redraws God's family too narrowly | the gift ignores worth | God acts first, from outside |
| Test verse | Rom 4:4–5 | Phil 3:7–9 ("loss", not "bad") | Gal 2:11–16 | Gal 3:29 | Rom 5:6 | Gal 1:4 |

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean): Antioch through each lens.** In Galatians 2:11–14 the dispute is literally a menu. On Dunn's reading, 2:16 follows naturally. Peter's withdrawal made Jewish food practice the price of full fellowship, and "not by works of the law" denies that Jewishness is a condition of belonging. The Old Perspective can absorb this. The food laws were the occasion, it says, and 2:16 states the general principle beneath them. The text fits both, so Antioch alone cannot decide.

**Example 2 (hard): Philippians 3:9, where they must choose.** Run the crux. For a boundary-marker reading, the list in 3:5 is Israel's identity, so "my own righteousness" is righteousness as a covenant privilege Paul held as a Jew. Its strength: four of the seven items are birth status, not effort. For a doing-versus-receiving reading, "my own" is set against "from God" (*ek theou*). Its strength: that contrast is the verse's own, and "blameless" in 3:6 is a claim about conduct. Sanders offers a third reading: the Law's righteousness was real, and counts as "loss" only by comparison with Christ. Dunn's side must say *emēn* ("my own") means "mine as an Israelite", not "achieved by me". Westerholm's must say the birth items are there as grounds of confidence, not markers. The words allow both. The decision comes from which contrast, Jew/Gentile or human/God, a reader takes Paul's letters as a whole to be driving.

## Watch out

- **A hypothesis promoted to teaching, either way.** "Catholics hold the New Perspective" and "the Church teaches that works of the law means legalism" are both false. Trent defines that justification is by grace; it defines no reading of *erga nomou*.
- **A catechesis treated as a definition.** Benedict XVI's 2008 audience shows a view is compatible with Catholic faith. It does not settle the exegesis.
- **Stendahl upside down.** He argued that Paul had a *robust*, untroubled conscience, and that the anguish belongs to Augustine and Luther.
- **"Sanders proved Judaism was about grace, so the debate is over."** Sanders's pattern is a strong, widely influential hypothesis; the *Variegated Nomism* volumes contest how uniform it was. A majority, if there is one, is not a proof.
- **Making the New Perspective one position.** Sanders, Dunn and Wright disagree with each other: Sanders thought Paul's thought about the Law was not fully consistent; Dunn and Wright find a coherent logic.

## One-liner

> The Old and New Perspectives agree that Paul says "not by works of the law" and disagree about why: because works are human doing, or because they are Jewish badges; Philippians 3:9's "my own righteousness" is where they must choose, and the Church defines neither.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢)** *(Exegetical)* Five **invented** statements about grace:

> (i) "God's choice of Israel came before Israel had done anything at all; his gift always comes first."
> (ii) "A wise giver chooses worthy recipients, so God's mercy goes to the righteous."
> (iii) "Grace so changes a person that it produces the obedience it asks for."
> (iv) "A true gift looks for nothing in return."
> (v) "Christ died for the ungodly" (Romans 5:6).

(a) For each, name the one perfection of grace in Barclay's scheme that it perfects, or, for the one that *refuses* a perfection, the perfection it refuses. (b) In **two sentences**, say which perfection Barclay takes to be Paul's distinctive emphasis, and how the scheme lets Sanders be right about Judaism while Paul still differs from it.

**P2 (🟡)** Romans 10:2–5, Douay–Rheims, with Challoner's note on 10:3:

> For I bear them witness that they have a zeal of God, but not according to knowledge. For they, not knowing the justice of God and seeking to establish their own, have not submitted themselves to the justice of God. For the end of the law is Christ: unto justice to everyone that believeth. For Moses wrote that the justice which is of the law: The man that shall do it shall live by it.
>
> *Note:* the Jews' own justice is, that which they pretended to by their own strength, or by the observance of the law, without faith in Christ.

The Greek of "their own" in 10:3 is *tēn idian* (*dikaiosynēn*), "their own righteousness".
(a) *(Exegetical)* Give a boundary-marker (Dunn) reading and a doing-versus-receiving (Westerholm) reading of "their own", and name the words **in 10:2–5** each appeals to. **Four sentences or fewer.**
(b) *(Exegetical)* Which reading does Challoner's note take, and what authority does the note carry? **Two sentences.**
(c) *(Exegetical)* Name the crux: the one claim about *idian* that each side must deny. **Two sentences.**

**P3 (🔴)** *(Exegetical)* An **invented** study-Bible note on Galatians 2:16:

> (1) Stendahl showed that Paul, before his conversion, was tormented by a guilty conscience, as Philippians 3:6 makes clear. (2) Since Sanders proved that Judaism was a religion of grace, Catholic teaching no longer holds that Paul opposed any reliance on human works. (3) The Joint Declaration of 1999 officially adopted Dunn's reading of "works of the law". (4) Benedict XVI then defined in 2008 that the phrase means circumcision, food laws and sabbath.

For each numbered sentence, name the error and correct it, stating the right level or kind of claim. **Two sentences per sentence.**

<details>
<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical (a) · Exegetical (b))*

**Must hit, strict (a):** (i) **priority**: the gift comes before any initiative by the recipient. (ii) refuses **incongruity**: it makes the gift fit the recipient's worth. (iii) **efficacy**: the gift transforms the recipient's agency. (iv) **non-circularity**: the gift escapes the cycle of return. (v) **incongruity**: the gift goes to the unworthy.
**Must hit, strict (b):** **incongruity** is Paul's distinctive stress: in Christ, God gives without regard to any prior worth, ethnic or moral. The perfections are independent, so Judaism can be full of grace (superabundance, priority), as Sanders said, while not perfecting incongruity the way Paul does.
**Wrong turns:** labelling (i) incongruity. "Before Israel had done anything" is about timing, and a gift can come first and still go to the fitting. Labelling (ii) as a denial of grace altogether: Barclay's point is that it is still grace, perfected differently. Reading (iv) as Barclay's view of Paul. He argues Paul's gift is unconditioned but still expects a return of obedience and thanks.
**Model answer:** (a) (i) priority; (ii) refuses incongruity; (iii) efficacy; (iv) non-circularity; (v) incongruity. (b) Barclay holds that Paul's distinctive emphasis is incongruity, God's gift in Christ given without regard to worth. Because the six perfections are independent, Second Temple Judaism can be saturated with grace, as Sanders showed, and still differ from Paul over whether grace must fit its recipient.

**P2** *(Exegetical (a)–(c))*

**Must hit, strict (a):** Dunn: "their own" = righteousness as **Israel's own**, belonging to them and not to the nations; he appeals to "**to everyone that believeth**" (10:4), the universal scope set against a national possession. Westerholm: "their own" = righteousness **based on their own doing**, set against God's righteousness received; he appeals to "**the man that shall do it shall live by it**" (10:5), doing contrasted with the faith-righteousness of 10:6. Credit citing "zeal" (10:2) for either side, if the use is explained.
**Must hit, strict (b):** the note takes the **doing** (Old Perspective) reading: "by their own strength, or by the observance of the law". It is a translator's explanatory note with **no magisterial standing**, so it reports a reading and defines nothing.
**Must hit, strict (c):** Dunn's side must deny that *idian* here means "achieved by their own effort"; Westerholm's side must deny that it means "theirs as opposed to the Gentiles'". The adjective allows both, so the context (10:4 against 10:5) decides.
**Wrong turns:** treating the Douay note as Church teaching because it is printed in a Catholic Bible. Claiming the Greek settles it. Reading 10:5 as Paul rejecting obedience itself rather than setting two kinds of righteousness side by side.
**Model answer:** (a) On Dunn's reading "their own" is Israel's own righteousness, a covenant status held against the nations, which is why 10:4 insists on "everyone that believeth". On Westerholm's reading it is righteousness established by their own doing, which is why 10:5 defines the Law's righteousness as "the man that shall do it". (b) Challoner takes the doing reading ("by their own strength, or by the observance of the law"). His note is a translator's gloss and carries no magisterial weight. (c) Dunn must deny that *idian* means "self-achieved", and Westerholm must deny that it means "Israel's as against the Gentiles'". Which contrast 10:4–5 foregrounds is the evidence that would move it.

**P3** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict:** (1) **Misreport of Stendahl.** He argued the reverse: Paul had a robust conscience ("without blame", Phil 3:6), and the introspective guilt belongs to Augustine and Luther. (2) **Hypothesis stated as proof, and as grounds for a change in teaching.** Covenantal nomism is a scholarly hypothesis, contested (the *Variegated Nomism* volumes). The Church's defined teaching is that justification is by grace (Trent VI); it never defined, and so never withdrew, an exegesis of Paul's opponents. (3) **Wrong kind of document.** The Joint Declaration is a Lutheran–Catholic agreement on the doctrine of justification; it adopts no reading of *erga nomou*, which stays freely disputed. (4) **Level overstated.** A general-audience catechesis defines nothing. It shows the boundary-marker reading is compatible with Catholic faith, not that it is taught.
**Wrong turns:** correcting (2) by saying the Church teaches the Old Perspective, which repeats the error in reverse. In (4), denying that Benedict said it: he did describe the works of the Law this way. The error is the verb "defined".
**Model answer:** (1) Stendahl argued the opposite: Paul's conscience was robust, "without blame" under the Law (Phil 3:6), and the tormented conscience is the West's. (2) Sanders's covenantal nomism is an influential but contested hypothesis, not a proof. Church teaching defines justification by grace, not any account of Paul's opponents, so there was nothing to abandon. (3) The Joint Declaration is an agreement on justification doctrine and makes no ruling on what "works of the law" means. That question remains freely disputed among Catholic exegetes. (4) Benedict XVI did describe the works of the Law as Israel's identity observances, but in a general-audience catechesis, which defines nothing. The reading is permitted, not taught.

## Flashback

**From Lesson [5.2](05-02-romans-1-4-the-righteousness-of-god.md) (Romans 1–4: the righteousness of God):** *(Exegetical.)* Close reading, 2 Corinthians 5:19–21 (Douay–Rheims, Challoner):

> For God indeed was in Christ, reconciling the world to himself, not imputing to them their sins. And he hath placed in us the word of reconciliation. For Christ therefore we are ambassadors, God as it were exhorting by us, for Christ, we beseech you, be reconciled to God. Him, who knew no sin, he hath made sin for us: that we might be made the justice of God in him.

In 5:21, "that we might be made the justice of God" is *hina hēmeis genōmetha dikaiosynē theou*, literally "that we might become the righteousness of God".
(a) Name the Greek verb behind "imputing" in 5:19 (Paul's verb for Abraham in Romans 4:3), gloss it, and say how 5:19 uses the same picture in the opposite direction from Romans 4:3. (b) Of the three readings of *dikaiosynē theou* (attribute, gift, saving action), which one does "we ... become" most naturally favour, and what in 5:18–20 could a reader of one of the other rows point to? **Two sentences per part.**

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):** ***logizomai***, "reckon, credit to an account" (here the participle *logizomenos*). In Romans 4:3 faith is **credited** to Abraham as righteousness; in 5:19 trespasses are **not debited** to the world. Same ledger, one entry made, the other withheld.
**Must hit, strict (b):** **Gift** is favoured: human beings are the ones who *become* the righteousness of God, so it ends up as the believers' standing, received from God "in him". But the verse is **contested**. The "we" of 5:18–20 is Paul's ministry ("the word of reconciliation", "ambassadors"), so an attribute reader can take "we" as the apostles who embody God's own faithfulness. A saving-action reader can point to "God ... reconciling the world", God's own act, into which the "we" are taken. Either counter-reading earns full credit.
**Wrong turns:** glossing *logizomai* as "infuse" or "impute" in the doctrinal sense. The verb means "reckon"; what the reckoning *effects* is the separate, disputed question. Claiming "become" settles whether righteousness is credited or made real: the words alone do not decide that fork. Saying the Church defines the reading of 5:21: no act rules on this genitive, so it is a scholarly question.
**Model answer:** (a) The verb is *logizomai*, "reckon, credit to an account". In Romans 4:3 God credits faith to Abraham as righteousness; in 5:19 he declines to debit the world's trespasses to it. (b) "We ... become the righteousness of God" makes it something believers come to have from God in Christ, which favours the gift reading. An attribute reader can reply that the "we" of 5:18–20 is the apostolic ministry of reconciliation, whose preaching shows God's own faithfulness, and a saving-action reader can reply that 5:19 makes God's reconciling of the world the controlling act.

</details>
