# The New Testament · Lesson 4.2: Paul's life and letters

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 4: Acts and Paul's churches · Builds on: [4.1 Acts as history](04-01-acts-as-history.md), [`church-history` 1.1](../../church-history/lessons/01-01-the-first-generation-and-its-sources.md) · Unlocks: [4.3 Paul at work](04-03-paul-at-work.md), [5.1 Galatians](05-01-galatians-faith-law-and-the-gentiles.md), [6.1 The disputed Pauline letters](06-01-the-disputed-pauline-letters-and-the-pastorals.md)

## Why this matters

Paul is the only first-generation Christian whose own words survive in quantity, and they are the oldest Christian documents we have. We also have a second account of his life, written by an admirer a generation later: Acts. The two do not always agree. Before you can read Galatians or Romans, you need to know which letters are certainly his, when he wrote them, and which source wins when the letters and Acts collide. This lesson gives you the method, the one fixed date, and the sorting of the thirteen letters that bear his name.

## The idea

Think of a biography built from two files: a man's own letters, and a friend's memoir written thirty years on. A historian trusts the letters first, even though they are partisan, because they are *his* and they are *from the time*. The memoir fills the gaps, and it is checked against the letters wherever the two overlap.

That is the working rule of modern Pauline chronology, usually credited to John Knox's *Chapters in a Life of Paul* (1950): **letters first, Acts second.** The letters give a *relative* sequence ("after three years", "after fourteen years"), but no calendar. One inscription at Delphi supplies the calendar. Everything else hangs from that peg.

The second job is sorting. Thirteen letters carry Paul's name. Scholars sort them by how confident they are that Paul himself wrote them. The Church, meanwhile, teaches something different about all thirteen: that they are inspired and canonical. Keep those two questions apart and most confusion disappears.

## Source

Galatians 1:15–19, 21; 2:1 (Douay–Rheims). Paul is defending his independence from Jerusalem, and swears to it (1:20).

> But when it pleased him who separated me from my mother's womb and called me by his grace, to reveal his Son in me, that I might preach him among the Gentiles: immediately I condescended not to flesh and blood. Neither went I to Jerusalem, to the apostles who were before me: but I went into Arabia, and again I returned to Damascus. Then, after three years, I went to Jerusalem to see Peter: and I tarried with him fifteen days. But other of the apostles I saw none, saving James the brother of the Lord. ... Afterwards, I came into the regions of Syria and Cilicia. ... Then, after fourteen years, I went up again to Jerusalem with Barnabas, taking Titus also with me.

Two things to notice. "To see Peter" renders *historēsai Kēphan*: the verb means to visit so as to get to know someone, to find things out from him (it is the root of our "history"). The Greek has the Aramaic name *Kēphas*, "Rock"; the Douay, following the Vulgate, has "Peter". And the whole passage is an **argument**, not a diary. Paul counts years and names the few people he met in order to prove he did not get his gospel from Jerusalem. The letters are first-hand, but they are not neutral.

## The argument

1. **The relative sequence from Galatians.** Call near Damascus; Arabia; back to Damascus; "after three years" a fifteen-day visit to Cephas, seeing only James besides; Syria and Cilicia; "after fourteen years" Jerusalem again with Barnabas and Titus. *In words:* a chain of intervals with no start date. What the "fourteen years" count from, and whether ancient inclusive counting shortens them, is the module's boss problem; it is not settled here.
2. **A floor from Aretas.** In 2 Corinthians 11:32–33 Paul escapes Damascus in a basket from "the governor of the nation under Aretas the king". Aretas IV of Nabataea died around 40 AD, so that escape, and hence his call some years before it, falls before about 40. How an Aretas official came to have power in Damascus is disputed, which is why this is a floor and not a date. Acts tells the same basket escape (9:23–25) but makes *the Jews* the ones watching the gates.
3. **The peg: [the Gallio inscription](../reference.md#the-gallio-inscription).** Fragments of a letter of the emperor Claudius, found at Delphi, call Gallio the emperor's friend and proconsul (the key words are partly restored). The letter gives Claudius's 26th acclamation as *imperator*, which places it in 52 AD. Proconsuls of Achaia normally served one year, taking office in early summer. So most scholars date Gallio's year to 51–52. Acts 18:12 has the Jews of Corinth bring Paul before Gallio. *In words:* Paul was in Corinth around 51, and this is the one date in his life fixed by outside evidence.
4. **Corroboration, softer.** Acts 18:2 says Aquila and Priscilla had just come from Italy "because that Claudius had commanded all Jews to depart from Rome". The fifth-century historian Orosius dates that expulsion to 49; his date is debated, but it fits a Corinthian arrival around 50.
5. **The letters placed.** 1 Thessalonians was written from Corinth, soon after Timothy's return (1 Thess 3:6; Acts 18:5), about 50–51. On the majority view it is the oldest surviving Christian writing. The others fall in the 50s. Romans comes last of the undisputed letters written in freedom, about 57–58, as Paul is about to take a collection to Jerusalem (Rom 15:25–26). Philippians and Philemon, written from prison, are dated either to an Ephesian imprisonment in the mid-50s or to Rome around 60; both options have serious defenders.
6. **The sort: [undisputed and disputed letters](../reference.md#undisputed-and-disputed-letters).** Seven are **undisputed**: Romans, 1–2 Corinthians, Galatians, Philippians, 1 Thessalonians, Philemon. Six are **disputed**: 2 Thessalonians, Colossians, Ephesians, 1–2 Timothy, Titus. The arguments against Pauline authorship are vocabulary and style, theology (believers already risen with Christ in Colossians and Ephesians; settled church offices in the pastorals), and setting. The replies are that Paul used secretaries (Rom 16:22 names one, Tertius), that his theology developed, and that the occasions differ. On Raymond Brown's survey in his *Introduction*, critical opinion is roughly divided on 2 Thessalonians and Colossians, leans clearly against Ephesians, and runs heavily against the pastorals. A majority is not a proof, and [6.1](06-01-the-disputed-pauline-letters-and-the-pastorals.md) weighs the cases. Hebrews never claims Paul at all ([6.2](06-02-hebrews.md)).
7. **[The Pauline letter form](../reference.md#the-pauline-letter-form).** Paul adapts the ordinary Greek letter. **Prescript**: sender, addressees, greeting. The stock greeting *chairein* ("greetings") becomes "grace and peace". **Thanksgiving**: "We give thanks" (*eucharistoumen*), which usually previews the letter's themes. **Body.** **Paraenesis**: moral exhortation. **Closing**: news, greetings, a blessing, sometimes a line in Paul's own hand. Deviations are signals. Galatians has *no* thanksgiving. Where it should stand, Paul writes "I wonder that you are so soon removed" (1:6). And the closing in Galatians 6:11 is not, as the Douay has it, "what a letter", but "with what large letters" (*pēlikois grammasin*): Paul taking the pen from his secretary.
8. **Acts' Paul and the letters' Paul.** In Galatians, Paul avoids Jerusalem for three years and meets only Cephas and James. In Acts 9:26–27 he goes up from Damascus and Barnabas presents him to the apostles. Acts names Gamaliel as his teacher (22:3); the letters, which call him a Pharisee (Phil 3:5), never do. Acts has him circumcise Timothy (16:3) and calls him an apostle only in passing (14:4, 14; Douay 14:13), while the letters make his apostleship the fight of his life. Acts never mentions that he wrote letters. Philipp Vielhauer's essay on the "Paulinism" of Acts (German original around 1950) argued that the theology of Acts' Paul differs so much from the letters that Luke could not have been his companion. Others, Fitzmyer among them, answer that a historian summarizing a man thirty years on will simplify him, and they point to the "we" passages ([4.1](04-01-acts-as-history.md)). The method that follows either way is Knox's: where they conflict on Paul's movements, the letters win.

**Mark the levels.** That all thirteen letters (with Hebrews, fourteen) are **canonical and inspired** is **defined dogma, rung 1** of [the ladder](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-04-the-ladder-of-doctrinal-authority.md): Trent's decree lists "fourteen epistles of Paul the apostle" ([`fundamental-theology` 3.3](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/03-03-the-canon-as-defined.md)). Who physically wrote a disputed letter is a **scholarly hypothesis**, with no magisterial standing either way. Whether the traditional attributions in Trent's titles bind at all is itself **freely disputed** among Catholics. Brown, among others, reads the decree as defining the books, not their human authors. The early Biblical Commission response on the pastorals is a weight case of the kind read in [1.1](01-01-reading-the-new-testament-in-the-church.md). The Gallio date, Knox's rule and the order of the letters are **history**, with no doctrinal level at all. See [Church teaching vs hypothesis](../reference.md#church-teaching-vs-hypothesis).

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean): sorting a letter opening.** Philemon 1–4. "Paul, a prisoner of Christ Jesus, and Timothy" (senders), "to Philemon ... and to the church which is in thy house" (addressees), "Grace to you and peace" (greeting): the prescript. Then "I give thanks to my God" (thanksgiving), whose themes, Philemon's love and the sharing of faith, are exactly what the body will ask him to act on in receiving Onesimus. The form is complete, and the thanksgiving doubles as a preview. Now run Galatians 1:1–6 the same way. Prescript present, with the self-description swollen into a defence ("an apostle, not of men, neither by man"), and then the thanksgiving slot is filled with a rebuke. The form tells you the tone before the argument starts.

**Example 2 (hard): when Acts and the letters conflict.** Where did Paul go right after Damascus? Acts 9:26 takes him to Jerusalem and the apostles; Galatians 1:17–19 says Arabia first, three years, and only two apostles seen. Knox's rule says follow Galatians: it is first-hand and sworn. But it strains, because Galatians is the *more* motivated source here. Paul needs a small, late Jerusalem contact to prove his independence, and 1:20's oath shows he expected to be doubted. So the honest statement is weaker than "the letters win". Paul's own account is primary evidence of what he was prepared to swear to his churches; Acts' compressed account is Luke's shaping (4.1). And the conflict is over sequence and emphasis, not over whether the visit to Cephas happened.

## Watch out

- **"The Church teaches that Paul wrote the pastorals."** Trent defines the pastorals as canonical Scripture. Their human authorship is a hypothesis, and how far the traditional titles bind is freely disputed. Stating authorship as dogma is a level error. So is the reverse: "if Paul didn't write 1 Timothy, it isn't Scripture". Canonicity does not hang on the attribution.
- **"Undisputed" means undisputed by critical scholars on authorship, not "more inspired".** The sort measures historical confidence, not doctrinal weight.
- **Gallio dates Paul's hearing, not his arrival.** The step from 51–52 to "Paul arrived in 50" needs Acts 18:11's eighteen months and an assumption about where they fall (P2).
- **Galatians is not a neutral diary.** First-hand is not the same as disinterested.

## One-liner

> Letters first, Acts second, Gallio for the calendar. Seven letters are certainly Paul's, all thirteen are Scripture, and those are two different claims.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢)** *(Exegetical)* 1 Thessalonians 1:1–3 (Douay–Rheims):

> Paul and Sylvanus and Timothy to the church of the Thessalonians: in God the Father and in the Lord Jesus Christ. Grace be to you and peace. We give thanks to God always for you all: making a remembrance of you in our prayers without ceasing, being mindful of the work of your faith and labour and charity: and of the enduring of the hope of our Lord Jesus Christ before God and our Father.

(a) Label the senders, the addressees, the greeting, and the first words of the thanksgiving. (b) Which three nouns in the thanksgiving announce themes, and in what order? (c) Name the one part of this sequence that Galatians 1 lacks, and quote what stands in its place. **One line per part.**

**P2 (🟡)** *(Formal (a) · Exegetical (b))* Take Gallio's year as July 51 to June 52, and suppose the hearing of Acts 18:12 fell in his first three months (July–September 51). (a) If the "year and six months" of Acts 18:11 all fall *before* the hearing, give the window for Paul's arrival in Corinth, showing the month arithmetic. (b) Name the verse in Acts 18 that makes that assumption uncertain. If instead the eighteen months are Paul's whole stay, with the hearing somewhere inside them, what is the latest possible arrival? **Two sentences for (b).**

**P3 (🔴)** *(Exegetical)* Give each claim its status: a rung of the ladder (and the act that fixes it), or "hypothesis / history, no doctrinal level". **One line each.**

1. "2 Timothy is inspired, canonical Scripture."
2. "Paul personally wrote 2 Timothy."
3. "1 Thessalonians is the oldest surviving Christian writing."
4. "Since Trent names 'fourteen epistles of Paul', a Catholic must hold that Paul wrote Hebrews."

<details>
<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict:** (a) Senders: Paul, Sylvanus (Silvanus/Silas), Timothy. Addressees: "the church of the Thessalonians: in God the Father and in the Lord Jesus Christ". Greeting: "Grace be to you and peace". Thanksgiving begins "We give thanks to God always". (b) **Faith, charity, hope**, in that order ("work of your faith", "labour ... of charity" / "labour and charity", "enduring of the hope"). (c) **The thanksgiving.** In its place: "I wonder that you are so soon removed from him that called you ..." (Gal 1:6).

**Wrong turns:** taking "in God the Father and in the Lord Jesus Christ" as part of the greeting; it qualifies "church". Being thrown by the Douay's versification: it prints "Grace be to you and peace" at the start of verse 2, while Greek editions end verse 1 with it. The division is modern and does not change the form. For (c), saying Galatians lacks a greeting (1:3 has one).

**Model answer:** (a) Senders Paul, Sylvanus and Timothy; addressees the Thessalonian church "in God the Father and in the Lord Jesus Christ"; greeting "Grace be to you and peace"; thanksgiving from "We give thanks to God always". (b) Faith, love (charity), hope. (c) Galatians has no thanksgiving; "I wonder that you are so soon removed" stands in its place.

**P2** *(Formal (a) · Exegetical (b))*

**Must hit, strict (a):** arrival = hearing − 18 months. July 51 − 12 months = July 50, − 6 more = **January 50**. September 51 − 18 months = **March 50**. Window: **January–March 50.**

**Must hit, strict (b):** **Acts 18:18**: after the hearing Paul "stayed yet many days" before sailing. So the eighteen months of 18:11 may cover the whole stay, not just the part before Gallio. If the hearing (July–September 51) fell anywhere inside an eighteen-month stay, arrival could be as early as January 50 or as late as just before the hearing, **September 51**.

**Wrong turns:** dating the arrival from the inscription's 52 instead of from Gallio's term. Subtracting a year and a half as 1.5 from 51 and calling it "49.5". Treating Gallio's July start as fixed fact; it is the normal practice for proconsuls, which is why the problem makes it an assumption.

**Model answer:** (a) July 51 − 18 months = January 50; September 51 − 18 months = March 50; so Paul arrived January–March 50. (b) Acts 18:18 says he stayed "yet many days" after the hearing, so the eighteen months may be the whole stay. Then arrival could fall as late as September 51, just before the hearing.

**P3** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict:**
1. **Defined dogma, rung 1**: Trent's decree on the canon, which lists "two to Timothy" among Paul's fourteen.
2. **Hypothesis, no doctrinal level.** It is the traditional attribution, which a large majority of critical scholars doubt. Whether the traditional titles bind is freely disputed (rung 5), so it is not a matter of faith either way.
3. **History / hypothesis, no doctrinal level**: the majority dating (about 50–51), inferred from Gallio and Acts 18.
4. **Overstated.** Trent defines Hebrews as canonical (rung 1). It does not define that Paul wrote it; whether the titles bind on authorship is freely disputed, and a common Catholic reading (Brown's, for one) is that the decree defines books, not human authors.

**Wrong turns:** calling (2) heresy to deny, or calling (1) a hypothesis because (2) is one. Giving (3) a doctrinal level because it is "about the Bible". In (4), swinging to "Trent says nothing about Paul"; it names him in the list, and that is why the question is disputed rather than empty.

**Model answer:** (1) Rung 1, by Trent's canon. (2) Hypothesis; the attribution's weight is freely disputed. (3) History, no level. (4) Overstates: Trent defines Hebrews' canonicity, not its human author; whether its titles bind on authorship is freely disputed.

## Flashback

**From Lesson [3.4](03-04-the-passion-and-resurrection-narratives.md) (The passion and resurrection narratives):** *(Exegetical.)* A synopsis of the centurion at the cross, Douay–Rheims:

> **Mark 15:39:** "And the centurion who stood over against him, seeing that crying out in this manner he had given up the ghost, said: Indeed this man was the son of God."
> **Matthew 27:54:** "Now the centurion and they that were with him watching Jesus, having seen the earthquake and the things that were done, were sore afraid, saying: Indeed this was the Son of God."
> **Luke 23:47:** "Now, the centurion, seeing what was done, glorified God, saying: Indeed this was a just man."

Luke's adjective is *dikaios*, "just", which in a courtroom setting also means "innocent". (a) List **three** changes Matthew or Luke make to Mark's scene: who speaks, what prompts it, what is said. (b) Which pattern that runs through Luke's trial scenes does his change continue? (c) On Markan priority, what is the usual explanation of that pattern, and what is its status? **Two sentences per part.**

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):** any three. **Who:** Matthew adds "they that were with him", so a group speaks, not the centurion alone. **What prompts it:** in Mark, the *manner* of Jesus's death ("crying out in this manner he had given up the ghost"); in Matthew, "the earthquake and the things that were done", with fear ("sore afraid"); in Luke, "what was done". **Response:** Luke adds "glorified God". **What is said:** Luke replaces "son of God" with "a just man" (*dikaios*).
**Must hit, strict (b):** Luke's pattern of declaring Jesus **innocent**. Pilate finds "no cause" in him three times (23:4, 14, 22). Now a Roman officer, at the execution itself, says the same thing: this man was *dikaios*.
**Must hit, strict (c):** the usual explanation is that a writer inside the Empire had reason to show that Rome's own officials found Jesus guiltless. That explanation is a **hypothesis about motive**, not a datum. The trend itself is an observation, and the order Mark → Luke rests on Markan priority, which is itself a hypothesis.
**Wrong turns:** treating the Douay's lower-case "son" in Mark against its capital in Matthew as one of the evangelists' changes. The capital is the printer's; in the Greek neither verse has the article. Reading Luke's "just man" as a denial that Jesus is Son of God, when Luke's own Gabriel says he "shall be called the Son of God" (1:35). Presenting the political motive as fact.
**Model answer:** (a) Matthew makes the speakers the centurion "and they that were with him", and has them moved by the earthquake and afraid, where Mark's centurion is moved by the way Jesus died. Luke adds that the centurion "glorified God", and changes the confession from "son of God" to "a just man". (b) It continues Luke's innocence theme: Pilate has three times found no cause in him, and now the officer in charge of the execution calls him *dikaios*, innocent. (c) The usual explanation is that Luke, writing inside the Empire, wanted Roman officials on record declaring Jesus guiltless. That is a hypothesis about motive, and it assumes Markan priority, itself a hypothesis; only the trend is observed.

</details>
