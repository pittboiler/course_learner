# The New Testament · Lesson 3.4: The passion and resurrection narratives

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 3: The historical Jesus · Builds on: [3.2 Sources and criteria](03-02-sources-and-criteria.md), [3.3 What historians broadly agree on](03-03-what-historians-broadly-agree-on.md) · Unlocks: [4.1 Acts as history](04-01-acts-as-history.md), [4.3 Paul at work](04-03-paul-at-work.md)

## Why this matters

The crucifixion under Pilate is the most secure fact about Jesus ([3.3](03-03-what-historians-broadly-agree-on.md)). Everything around it is disputed: who tried him and when, who was responsible, and what happened three days later. These chapters hold one line of Matthew that was read for centuries as a curse on the Jewish people, and the oldest datable piece of Christian tradition we have. This lesson lays the trial scenes side by side and maps what scholars make of the resurrection evidence. It does **not** argue the resurrection as history; that case belongs to [`apologetics-foundations`](../../apologetics-foundations/syllabus.md).

## The idea

Two kinds of text meet at the end of the gospels, and they behave differently.

In the **passion narrative** the four agree more than anywhere else: arrest, a Jewish hearing, Pilate, crucifixion, burial. Yet details move: Mark has a night trial before the whole council, Luke a session at daybreak, John only a questioning by Annas. Read them like any synoptic parallel: list the changes, then ask what each evangelist was doing.

The **resurrection texts** diverge widely: who went to the tomb, whom they met, where Jesus appeared. And the earliest witness is not a gospel but a creed-like list Paul quotes some 20 years after the event, which names witnesses and mentions no tomb.

Two tasks: compare accurately, and keep the historian's judgments apart from the Church's teaching.

## Source

1 Corinthians 15:3–8, Douay–Rheims (Challoner):

> For I delivered unto you first of all, which I also received: how that Christ died for our sins, according to the scriptures: And that he was buried: and that he rose again the third day, according to the scriptures: And that he was seen by Cephas, and after that by the eleven. Then was he seen by more than five hundred brethren at once: of whom many remain until this present, and some are fallen asleep. After that, he was seen by James: then by all the apostles. And last of all, he was seen also by me, as by one born out of due time.

Four Greek words carry the reading. *Paredōka* ("I handed on") and *parelabon* ("I received") are the technical pair for [handing on tradition](../reference.md#handing-on-tradition); Paul uses the same pair for the Supper words in 11:23. *Egēgertai* is a perfect passive, "he has been raised and remains raised" (lesson's translation), not the Douay's plain "rose". *Ōphthē*, "he was seen" or "he appeared", is repeated for every witness, **Paul included**. One more: the Douay's "the eleven" follows the Latin; the Greek reads *tois dōdeka*, "the Twelve", used as a title for the group.

## The argument

1. **The trials differ in time, place and form.** Mark 14:53–64 puts the whole council in session at night: false witnesses on the Temple saying, the high priest's question, a death verdict, then a morning consultation (15:1). Matthew follows, naming Caiaphas (26:57). Luke has the arrest and Peter's denial at night, then the council at daybreak (22:66). He has no witnesses, no Temple charge and no formal verdict. John sends Jesus first to Annas, Caiaphas's father-in-law, who questions him about his disciples and teaching (18:13, 19). Caiaphas gets a one-line transfer (18:24), and the council's decision had been taken chapters earlier (11:47–53). *In words:* one hearing told four ways; a formal trial in two gospels, none in two.
2. **What scholars make of the difference.** The later rabbinic rules against capital trials at night are often cited against Mark, but they were written down in the Mishnah (compiled around 200) and may not describe practice under Caiaphas. Many scholars (Raymond Brown's *The Death of the Messiah* is the standard study) judge that some Jewish proceeding lay behind the handing-over to Pilate, but doubt it was the formal night trial Mark tells. On that view Mark has dramatized a hearing, and Luke and John keep a less formal procedure. Others defend Mark's outline. A majority here is not a proof.
3. **The sentence was Roman.** Crucifixion was a Roman penalty and only the prefect could impose it. Tacitus and Josephus both name Pilate as executioner, Josephus adding that the leading men among the Jews accused him ([3.2](03-02-sources-and-criteria.md)). In John the council tells Pilate: "It is not lawful for us to put any man to death" (18:31). Scholars have long noted that Pilate becomes steadily less guilty from Mark to John. In Mark he asks what evil Jesus has done (15:14). In Matthew he washes his hands. In Luke he three times finds no cause (23:4, 14, 22). In John he says so three times as well (18:38; 19:4, 6). The usual explanation, that writers inside the Empire had reason to show Rome as not hostile, is a hypothesis about motive, not a datum.
4. **Matthew 27:25.** Only Matthew has it: "And the whole people answering, said: His blood be upon us and upon our children." For centuries it was read as a self-imposed curse on the Jewish people. [Nostra Aetate 4](../reference.md#nostra-aetate-4-on-the-passion) (Vatican II, 28 October 1965) closes that reading. Paraphrased: the Jewish authorities and those who followed them pressed for Christ's death, yet the guilt of the passion may not be laid on every Jew of that time indiscriminately, nor on Jews of today, and Scripture may not be used to portray the Jews as rejected or cursed by God. Catechism 597 repeats this, applies it to the cry, and reads the cry as the customary formula by which a sentence was accepted. Catechism 598 lays the gravest responsibility on sinners, Christians first.
5. **1 Corinthians 15:3–8 is older than the letter.** Paul says he *received* it. Its language is not Paul's usual idiom ("the Twelve" appears nowhere else in his letters), and it is built as a sequence of "that" clauses. Paul wrote in the mid-50s, and he had spent fifteen days with Cephas three years after his call (Gal 1:18). So most scholars date the formula, or at least its core in verses 3–5, to the 30s. That is the [early formula](../reference.md#the-1-corinthians-15-formula) consensus: a strong majority, not a certainty. Two features are debated: (a) **Does it imply an empty tomb?** "Buried" followed by "raised" suggests to many that the body left the grave. Others note that the tomb itself is never mentioned. (b) **No women.** The gospels make women the first witnesses (Mark 16:1–8; John 20:1–18). Paul's list leaves them out. Josephus suggests why testimony might: his restatement of Moses' law rules "let not the testimony of women be admitted" (*Antiquities* 4.8.15). The same fact is then used both ways. Some read the gospels' female witnesses as passing the [criterion of embarrassment](../reference.md#criterion-of-embarrassment). Others read the list's silence as a sign that the tomb stories came later.
6. **The empty-tomb accounts diverge in the ways that matter to historians.** The oldest manuscripts of Mark end at 16:8 with the women fleeing: "they said nothing to any man: for they were afraid." Matthew adds an earthquake, guards and an appearance to the women. Luke keeps every appearance around Jerusalem. John has Mary Magdalen come alone. Mark and Matthew point to Galilee (Mark 16:7).
7. **The readings mapped** (all paraphrased). **N. T. Wright** (*The Resurrection of the Son of God*) argues that for Second Temple Jews "resurrection" meant a body. On his case, only an empty tomb together with appearances explains why the first Christians used that word. **Dale Allison** (*Resurrecting Jesus* and later work) takes the appearances as real experiences and gives the tomb tradition serious weight. He holds that historical method cannot settle their cause. **Gerd Lüdemann** (*The Resurrection of Jesus*) reads Peter's experience as a vision born of grief and guilt, and Paul's as a conversion vision, with others following. He holds that the body decayed. **Bart Ehrman** (*How Jesus Became God*) accepts that some disciples had visions and sincerely believed. He doubts a crucified man got an honourable burial in a known tomb, so takes the tomb stories to be later. The crux is visible in the Source: Paul uses the same *ōphthē* for himself as for Cephas. Wright reads that as one kind of real seeing; Lüdemann, as visions all round.

**Mark the levels.** That Christ truly died, was buried and rose from the dead is **defined dogma** in the Creeds. What it requires of the risen body is [`christology` 6.2](../../christology/lessons/06-02-resurrection-and-ascension-as-doctrine.md); the Catechism itself calls the empty tomb no direct proof (640). That the passion may not be charged against all Jews then living or against Jews today is **authentic magisterium of an ecumenical council** (Nostra Aetate 4, restated in Catechism 597). Rung 3 at least; nothing is solemnly defined. That the gospels have [historical character](../reference.md#historical-character-of-the-gospels) is Church teaching (Dei Verbum 19), and [1.1](01-01-reading-the-new-testament-in-the-church.md) shows it does not require one trial chronology. Everything else here is **hypothesis**: Mark's night trial as dramatization, the motive behind Pilate's exoneration, the date of the formula, what the formula implies about the tomb, and the positions of Wright, Allison, Lüdemann and Ehrman. All of it is [freely disputed](../reference.md#church-teaching-vs-hypothesis).

## The trial scenes

| | Mark | Matthew | Luke | John |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Taken first to | the high priest (14:53) | Caiaphas (26:57) | the high priest's house (22:54) | **Annas** (18:13) |
| Jewish hearing | whole council, **night** (14:55) | council, night (26:59) | council **at daybreak** (22:66) | Annas questions him privately (18:19); no council trial |
| Temple charge | false witnesses (14:57–58) | yes (26:60–61) | none | none (saying placed at 2:19) |
| Verdict | all condemn him as deserving death (14:64) | deserving death (26:66) | no formal verdict (22:71) | decided earlier (11:53) |
| Before Pilate | "what evil hath he done?" (15:14) | wife's dream; handwashing; 27:25 | three times no cause (23:4, 14, 22); sent to **Herod** (23:7–11) | three times no cause (18:38; 19:4, 6) |

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean): Pilate across the four columns.** Method: take one variable and track it through the synopsis. Mark's Pilate half-protests, then gives in to please the crowd (15:15). Matthew adds two scenes that clear him, the dream and the washing, and has the crowd take the blood on itself. Luke adds Herod and has Pilate declare Jesus innocent three times. John does the same in dialogue. The direction is steady, and on Markan priority ([1.4](01-04-the-synoptic-problem.md)) it is a series of additions to Mark. Result: an observed trend (**exegetical**, strict) plus an explanation (writing under Rome), which is a **hypothesis**. Keep them in separate sentences.

**Example 2 (hard): what Matthew 27:25 says.** The strain: the Church's teaching cannot be defended by pretending the verse is mild. The word is *laos*, "people". In Matthew it often means Israel as God's people, and "the whole people" is Matthew's phrase, not a head-count of Jerusalem. The speakers are the crowd the chief priests and elders had talked round (27:20). "Blood upon us" is a formula for accepting responsibility for a death; the Catechism (597) reads it as the acceptance of a sentence. "Our children" is read by many commentators as pointing to the generation that saw Jerusalem fall in 70, in Matthew's own lifetime. That is a hypothesis about Matthew's intent. Nostra Aetate does not deny that Matthew wrote the verse or that a crowd shouted. It denies the **inference** from this crowd's cry to the guilt of a whole people across time. The exegete owes the text an accurate reading; the Church has ruled out one use of it.

## Watch out

- **"The Church teaches that Mark's night trial happened as described."** No. Dei Verbum 19 affirms that the gospels hand on what Jesus really did and taught; it canonizes neither Mark's chronology nor Luke's.
- **"Scholars have shown 1 Cor 15:3–5 dates to within five years of the crucifixion."** A strong majority dates it early; that is a scholarly judgment, neither Church teaching nor proof.
- **Overstating Nostra Aetate's level.** It is authoritative conciliar teaching, not a solemn definition; "defined dogma" overstates it.
- **Reading "the eleven" as Paul's word.** The Douay follows the Latin; the Greek has "the Twelve", a title.

## One-liner

> The trials differ in who, when and where; the sentence was Rome's; Matthew 27:25 is a crowd's cry, not a people's curse; and Paul's received list, the oldest resurrection text, names its witnesses but no tomb and no women.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢)** *(Exegetical)* Assign each claim its status: defined dogma, authentic magisterium (rung 3), other Church teaching (name the document), or scholarly hypothesis with no magisterial standing. **One line each.**

1. "Christ rose from the dead on the third day."
2. "1 Corinthians 15:3–5 is a formula Paul received in the 30s."
3. "Neither the Jews of Jesus's day as a whole nor Jews today bear the guilt of his death."
4. "Mark turned an informal hearing into a night trial."
5. "The empty tomb is by itself a direct proof of the resurrection."

**P2 (🟡)** *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b))* Douay–Rheims:

> Mark 14:53: "And they brought Jesus to the high priest. And all the priests and the scribes and the ancients assembled together." 14:64: "... Who all condemned him to be guilty of death." 15:1: "And straightway in the morning, the chief priests holding a consultation ... delivered him to Pilate."
> Luke 22:54: "And apprehending him, they led him to the high priest's house." 22:66: "And as soon as it was day, the ancients of the people and the chief priests and scribes came together. And they brought him into their council ..."
> John 18:13: "And they led him away to Annas first ..." 18:24: "And Annas sent him bound to Caiphas the high priest." 18:28: "Then they led Jesus from Caiphas to the governor's hall. And it was morning ..."

(a) List three differences of time, place or procedure among the three accounts. (b) In 120 words or fewer, any verdict: is a formal night trial (Mark) or an informal hearing (Luke/John) more likely to be historical? Name the evidence each side uses and one piece that cuts against your view.

**P3 (🔴)** *(Exegetical)* An **invented** study-Bible note on Matthew 27:25:

> "Here the Jewish nation formally accepts guilt for the death of Christ, binding all its future generations, as the Church has always taught. Modern scholarship confirms that Matthew preserves the exact words spoken by the population of Jerusalem."

(a) What does the text itself say about who speaks, and what Greek word is in play? (b) What Church teaching does the note contradict, and at what level? (c) What is wrong with the second sentence? **Two sentences per part.**

<details>
<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict:** (1) **Defined dogma**: the Creeds' "rose again on the third day". (2) **Hypothesis**, the scholarly majority, with no magisterial standing. (3) **Authentic magisterium (rung 3)**: Nostra Aetate 4, restated in Catechism 597; an ecumenical council teaching without a solemn definition. (4) **Hypothesis**, held by many scholars, with no magisterial standing. (5) **Not Church teaching, and contrary to what the Catechism says.** Catechism 640 says the empty tomb is not in itself a direct proof, because the body's absence could be explained otherwise.

**Wrong turns:** marking (2) as Church teaching because it supports the faith; its early date is a historian's judgment. Marking (3) as defined dogma. Marking (5) as dogma because the resurrection is dogma: the claim is about the *evidential force* of one sign, and that is not defined.

**Model answer:** (1) Defined dogma (the Creeds). (2) Hypothesis, majority view. (3) Rung 3, Nostra Aetate 4 and Catechism 597. (4) Hypothesis. (5) Not teaching; Catechism 640 says the tomb is not a direct proof.

**P2** *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b))*

**Must hit, strict (a):** any three of the following. **Time:** Mark's council meets at night and delivers its verdict then, with a further morning consultation; Luke's council meets only at daybreak; John has no council session, only a night questioning with the move to Pilate in the morning. **Place:** Luke puts the night at the high priest's house; John sends Jesus to Annas first, then to Caiaphas. **Procedure:** Mark has a formal death verdict by all; Luke's session reaches no formal verdict; John narrates no Caiaphas hearing at all.

**Must hit, any verdict (b):** name evidence on both sides. For Mark's night trial: it is the earliest account on Markan priority, and Matthew follows it. For an informal hearing: Luke and John agree against Mark, independently if John did not use the Synoptics ([2.4](02-04-john-and-the-synoptics.md)); Mark's scene has a dramatic structure, with Peter's denial set around it. Treat the Mishnah's rules as weak evidence because of their date (c. 200). Name one counter-point to your own view.

**Wrong turns:** using the Mishnah as a decisive proof that a night trial was illegal and so could not have happened. Treating (b) as settled by Dei Verbum 19, which does not choose a chronology.

**Model answer (b), one of several:** An informal hearing is somewhat more likely. Luke and John, probably independent of each other here, both lack a night verdict before the council, and Mark's trial is framed around Peter's denial in a way that looks composed. Against this: Mark is the earliest narrative, and a hurried proceeding before a feast is historically plausible. The Mishnah's rules against night trials were codified around 200 and prove little about Caiaphas's practice.

**P3** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** the speakers are the crowd before Pilate, whom the chief priests and ancients had persuaded (27:20). Matthew calls them "the whole people" (*pas ho laos*), his theological phrase, not a count of the nation or a legal act of the nation.

**Must hit, strict (b):** Nostra Aetate 4: the guilt of the passion may not be laid on every Jew of that time indiscriminately nor on Jews today, and Scripture may not be used to portray the Jews as cursed. Catechism 597 applies this to 27:25 explicitly. It is **authentic magisterium of an ecumenical council (rung 3)**, so "as the Church has always taught" states the reverse of current Church teaching.

**Must hit, strict (c):** there is no such consensus. The verse is unique to Matthew, and many scholars treat its wording as Matthew's. The gospels' historical character (Dei Verbum 19) does not require verbatim reports in any case ([1.1](01-01-reading-the-new-testament-in-the-church.md)).

**Wrong turns:** answering (a) by denying that Matthew wrote the verse or that it is harsh. Calling Nostra Aetate defined dogma. Correcting (c) by saying the Church teaches the words were never spoken; the Church teaches nothing either way on that.

**Model answer:** (a) The speakers are the crowd before Pilate, persuaded by the chief priests (27:20). Matthew calls it "the whole people", *laos*, a theological phrase, not a legal act of the nation. (b) It contradicts Nostra Aetate 4, which Catechism 597 applies to this very verse: the passion may not be charged to all Jews then or to Jews today. That is authentic conciliar magisterium (rung 3), so the note inverts the Church's teaching. (c) Scholarship confirms no such thing: the line is found only in Matthew, and many judge its wording to be his. Nor does Dei Verbum 19 require verbatim reports.

</details>

## Flashback

**From Lesson [3.2](03-02-sources-and-criteria.md) (Sources and criteria):** *(Exegetical.)* The last words from the cross, Douay–Rheims:

> **Mark 15:34:** And at the ninth hour, Jesus cried out with a loud voice, saying: Eloi, Eloi, lamma sabacthani? Which is, being interpreted: My God, My God, Why hast thou forsaken me?
>
> **Matthew 27:46:** And about the ninth hour, Jesus cried with a loud voice, saying: Eli, Eli, lamma sabacthani? That is, My God, My God, why hast thou forsaken me?
>
> **Luke 23:46:** And Jesus crying with a loud voice, said: Father, into thy hands I commend my spirit.
>
> **John 19:30:** ... he said: It is consummated.

Mark's cry is the opening of Psalm 22 (21:2 in the Douay). Luke's words are Psalm 31:5 (30:6 in the Douay).

(a) Which primary criterion does the cry pass, and what in the four columns is the evidence? Which of Meier's secondary criteria supports it? (b) On the Two-Source hypothesis, does it pass multiple attestation, and what follows if it does not? (c) A sceptic notes that the passion is told through Psalm 22 elsewhere too (compare Mark 15:24 with Ps 22:18, 21:19 in the Douay), so the cry may be Scripture put on Jesus's lips. What does that do to the argument in (a), and what in Luke's column cuts back? **Two sentences per part.**

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):** **Embarrassment.** A cry of abandonment by God sits awkwardly on the Son's lips, and Luke and John both replace it with words of trust or completion, while Matthew keeps it. The **Aramaic** form (*Eloi ... lamma sabacthani*, which Mark has to translate) is a secondary criterion, usable only in support.
**Must hit, strict (b):** **No.** On Markan priority Matthew copies Mark, so there is one witness, not two, and Luke and John do not have the saying. Failing proves nothing, because the criteria are one-way: a singly attested saying is not thereby inauthentic.
**Must hit, strict (c):** It **weakens** embarrassment. If the Church read the passion through Psalm 22, it had a positive motive to use the psalm's first line, and Mark may not have found it embarrassing at all (the weak point of the criterion: knowing what embarrassed whom). What **cuts back** is that Luke replaces one psalm verse with another, so what troubled a later evangelist was not quoting Scripture but the content of *this* verse. The discomfort was real by Luke's time, even if it does not prove Mark felt it.
**Wrong turns:** counting Matthew as a second independent witness. Concluding "fails attestation, so invented". Treating any verdict here as Church teaching or as a threat to it: the authenticity of the saying is a hypothesis, and *Dei Verbum* 19 does not turn on it.
**Model answer:** (a) It passes embarrassment: Luke and John drop a cry of abandonment that Matthew keeps, and the Aramaic words give secondary support. (b) It fails multiple attestation, since Matthew depends on Mark, but a one-way test failed shows nothing against the saying. (c) Psalm 22's role in the passion gives the Church a motive to use the verse, so the embarrassment may be Luke's rather than Mark's. But Luke's swap of one psalm for another shows that the trouble was this verse's content, not psalm-quotation as such.

</details>
