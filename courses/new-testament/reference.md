# The New Testament · Reference Card

> Open book. This card is meant to be open while you work — including during
> quizzes. Nothing here is something you're expected to recall cold.

Every claim in this course is one of two kinds, and every answer is graded strictly
on which kind it is. **Church teaching**, with its level: the gospels' historical
character, the canon as defined at Trent, inspiration and inerrancy. **Scholarly
hypotheses**, with no magisterial standing either way: Markan priority, Q, every
date, the authorship of the disputed letters, the New Perspective. Stating a
hypothesis as teaching, or teaching as a hypothesis, is an exegetical error. This card
is the lookup surface for both kinds: the Greek words that decide readings, the dates
and anchors, the positions as their defenders would sign them, and the level of every
claim that has one. Levels come from
[`fundamental-theology` 4.4](../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-04-the-ladder-of-doctrinal-authority.md),
which owns the ladder; where that course and this card differ, that course wins.

Scripture is quoted from the Douay-Rheims (Challoner), with modern verse numbers and
the Douay's in brackets where they differ ([the table](#douay-rheims-numbering-and-vulgate-readings)).
Translations of Greek marked *(lesson's translation)* are the lessons' own.

## Church teaching vs hypothesis

The spine of the course. Each row is a claim, its standing, the act or evidence behind
it, and the lesson that grades it. Rung 1 is **defined dogma**, rung 2 **definitive**,
rung 3 **authentic ordinary magisterium**, rung 4 **common teaching**, rung 5 **freely
disputed** (the ladder of [`fundamental-theology` 4.4](../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-04-the-ladder-of-doctrinal-authority.md)).
"Hypothesis" means a scholarly reconstruction that sits on **no rung at all**: its
weight is the weight of its evidence.

### Church teaching vs hypothesis

**Two kinds of claim, kept apart, graded strictly both ways.** A claim either carries a
level on the ladder, because some act of the Church teaches it, or it is a scholarly
reconstruction whose only weight is its evidence. The course's governing rule: every
lesson ends its argument with a **Mark the levels** paragraph that sorts its claims
into the two kinds.

- **Promoting** a hypothesis to teaching: "the Church teaches Matthew wrote first",
  "Catholic scholarship holds Markan priority", "the Church teaches Paul wrote the
  pastorals", "Trent defined *in quo*", "Catholics hold the New Perspective".
- **Demoting** teaching to hypothesis: "historical character is one scholarly view",
  "Hebrews' canonicity is uncertain since nobody knows who wrote it", "that good works
  increase justification is one Catholic reading of James".
- **Level-less claims are not low-level claims.** Dates, authorship, genre and the
  groups of Second Temple Judaism are history or philology: they sit on no rung, so
  "rung 5" is wrong for them too. Rung 5 names questions Catholics dispute *under* the
  magisterium, such as whether Trent's titles bind on authorship.
- **Three habits.** Find the act (a Catechism paragraph keeps its source's weight; a
  general audience, a Biblical Commission study, a pope's book defines nothing).
  Separate a doctrine from the text it was drawn from (Trent binds the sense of Rom 5:12,
  not the grammar of *eph' hō*). Keep canonicity apart from authorship (the canon
  defines books, not pens).
- **A majority is not a proof.** Give the scholarly majority where there is one, say so,
  and give the minority's best evidence.

### Historical character of the gospels

**The Church vouches for *what* the gospels hand on, not for how, when or by whom they
were assembled.** Dei Verbum 19 (1965), paraphrased: the Church has firmly and
constantly held, and unhesitatingly asserts, that the four gospels, whose historical
character she affirms, faithfully hand on what Jesus really did and taught for our
salvation until his Ascension. The same paragraph says the evangelists selected,
synthesized and explained with their churches' situation in view, keeping the form of
preaching, and always telling the truth about Jesus. Dei Verbum 18 adds apostolic
origin: the gospels come from the apostles "and apostolic men".

**Status:** conciliar teaching in a dogmatic constitution, presented as what the
Church has always held; no new definition. At least authentic magisterium at its
heaviest (rung 3), with a case for more ([1.1](lessons/01-01-reading-the-new-testament-in-the-church.md) weighs it;
[`fundamental-theology` 4.5](../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-05-reading-a-documents-weight.md)).

**What it does not require or fix:**

- verbatim reports (the two Our Fathers; the cup words of Mark 14:24 against 1 Cor 11:25 and Luke 22:20);
- one chronology (the Temple incident early in John, late in Mark; 14 or 15 Nisan; Mark's night trial against Luke's daybreak session);
- any date, author, order of the gospels, or model of transmission;
- any reconstruction of Jesus, criterion or historian's verdict;
- a harmonization of Luke's census, or a verdict on whether an incidental slip falls under inerrancy (freely disputed scope, [`fundamental-theology` 3.2](../fundamental-theology/lessons/03-02-inerrancy.md)).

It **does** rule out stage 2 as invention: the stages permit shaping and forbid
fabrication. And it does not rest on Josephus, Tacitus or the criteria: the Church
affirms it on its own grounds.

### The three stages of gospel formation

**Jesus acted and taught; the apostles preached it with post-Easter understanding; the
evangelists wrote it up for their churches.** Dei Verbum 19, labelled as in CCC 126:

| Stage | What the Church teaches (paraphrase) | Luke 1:1-4 | Who studies it |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. The life and teaching of Jesus | what he really did and taught | "the things that have been accomplished among us" (*pragmata*) | historical-Jesus research ([3.1](lessons/03-01-the-quests.md), [3.2](lessons/03-02-sources-and-criteria.md)) |
| 2. The oral tradition | the apostles handed it on with the fuller understanding given by Easter and the Spirit | "eyewitnesses and ministers of the word" *delivered* (*paredosan*) them | form criticism, memory ([1.5](lessons/01-05-from-oral-tradition-to-written-gospel.md)) |
| 3. The written gospels | the evangelists selected, synthesized, explained with an eye to their churches | "to write to thee in order" (*kathexēs*) | source and redaction criticism ([1.4](lessons/01-04-the-synoptic-problem.md)) |

**Rule:** the map is teaching; the mechanics inside each stage are hypotheses. The
outline overlaps form criticism's picture, but the Church adopted the outline, not the
method, and not Bultmann's verdict or Bauckham's reassurance either.

### Sancta Mater Ecclesia

**The Biblical Commission's instruction on the historical truth of the gospels**,
approved by Paul VI on **21 April 1964** and footnoted by Dei Verbum 19. It first set
out the three stages. Two further points the Council compressed (paraphrased):

1. The gospels' truth is not compromised when the evangelists report Jesus's words and deeds in different orders, or his words not literally but in varied forms that keep the sense.
2. Exegetes may use the sound elements of form criticism, but must reject the philosophical assumptions often bundled with it: denying the supernatural, ruling out miracle and prophecy, and inflating the community's creativity at the apostles' expense.

**Status:** a papally approved curial instruction whose core the Council received;
authentic guidance, not a definition, and no certificate for any form critic's results.

### The canon as defined

**Trent defined which books are Scripture; it did not define who wrote them.** Trent,
Session IV (**8 April 1546**), lists the books and anathematizes whoever does not
receive them "entire with all their parts, as they have been used to be read in the
Catholic Church, and as they are contained in the old Latin vulgate edition" (Schaff's
Waterworth). Its New Testament: four gospels, Acts "written by Luke the Evangelist",
"fourteen epistles of Paul the apostle" ending "to the Hebrews", two of Peter, three of
John, "one of the apostle James", one of Jude, and "the Apocalypse of John the apostle".
The Council of Florence (1442) had given the same list in its decree of union with
the Copts.

**Status:** canonicity of the 27 is **defined dogma, rung 1**. Vatican I teaches that the
Church recognizes inspired books rather than making them so. **Freely disputed:**
whether the titles bind on human authorship (Brown and others read the decree as
defining books, not authors).

**Consequences in this course:**

- Mark 16:9-20 is canonical whoever wrote it ([2.1](lessons/02-01-mark.md)).
- The disputed Paulines, Hebrews, James, 2 Peter and Revelation are defined Scripture; their authors are hypotheses ([4.2](lessons/04-02-pauls-life-and-letters.md), [6.1](lessons/06-01-the-disputed-pauline-letters-and-the-pastorals.md)-[6.5](lessons/06-05-revelation.md)).
- "Entire with all their parts" has **not** been read as settling the Johannine comma since 1927 ([6.4](lessons/06-04-peter-jude-and-the-letters-of-john.md)).
- The history of how the list formed has no magisterial level ([6.6](lessons/06-06-the-formation-of-the-canon.md)).

The act itself is owned by [`fundamental-theology` 3.3](../fundamental-theology/lessons/03-03-the-canon-as-defined.md).

### Nostra Aetate 4 on the passion

**The passion may not be charged against a whole people.** Vatican II's declaration
(**28 October 1965**), paraphrased: the Jewish authorities and those who followed them
pressed for Christ's death, yet what happened in his passion cannot be charged against
all Jews then living, without distinction, nor against Jews today; catechesis and
preaching must not present the Jews as rejected or accursed by God as if Scripture
taught it. CCC 597 applies this to Matthew 27:25 and reads the cry as the customary
formula accepting a sentence; CCC 598 lays the gravest responsibility on sinners,
Christians first.

**Status:** authentic conciliar magisterium, **at least rung 3**; nothing solemnly
defined, so "defined dogma" overstates it. **Scope:** it governs catechesis and
preaching, not translation: keeping "the Jews" in John does not defy it, and
"Judeans" is not required by it. It denies an **inference** from a crowd's cry to a
people's guilt across time; it does not deny that Matthew wrote the verse.

*Introduced:* [2.5](lessons/02-05-the-theology-of-john.md); owned by [3.4](lessons/03-04-the-passion-and-resurrection-narratives.md)

### Nostra Aetate 4 on Israel

**Companion to [the passion clause](#nostra-aetate-4-on-the-passion).** The same
paragraph, paraphrased: the Jews remain most dear to God for the sake of the fathers,
since God does not revoke his gifts and call (citing Rom 11:28-29); the Church draws
sustenance from the root of the good olive tree onto which the Gentiles are grafted
(Rom 11:17-24); and it awaits the day when all peoples will call on the Lord with one
voice (citing Rom 11:11-32).

**Status:** conciliar declaration, authentic magisterium, not a definition. The 2015
reflection of the Commission for Religious Relations with the Jews says of itself that
it is not magisterial. A reading on which anyone is saved apart from Christ meets
teaching well above free dispute; that assessment and these documents' weight belong
to [`apologetics-foundations`](../apologetics-foundations/syllabus.md) 6.4. The
exegete's question is narrower: what did Paul say?

*Introduced:* [5.3](lessons/05-03-romans-5-11-adam-spirit-israel.md)

### Lamentabili on the immediate coming

**A condemned dilemma, not a settled eschatology.** The Holy Office decree *Lamentabili*
(July 1907, approved by Pius X), proposition 33, condemned the claim that the
unprejudiced reader must choose: either Jesus erred about an immediate messianic
coming, or most of his gospel teaching is inauthentic (paraphrase).

**Status:** the propositions were condemned as a set, with no theological note assigned
to each. Read it as a **judgment against a dilemma**: a Catholic may not accept the
flat either-or. It does **not** settle which reconstruction of Jesus's eschatology is
right, or bar a Catholic from the history (Meier's judgment that the deadline sayings
are probably church formulations is one option; Wright's redefinition, or prophetic
foreshortening, are others).

*Introduced:* [3.3](lessons/03-03-what-historians-broadly-agree-on.md)

### Benedict XVI on historical criticism

**A pope's argument, explicitly not magisterium.** *Jesus of Nazareth* vol. 1 (2007),
foreword, paraphrased: the historical-critical method is **indispensable**, because
faith rests on real events; it has **limits**, since it leaves a text in its past, works
by hypothesis, and reads the words only as human words, so it needs a canonical,
faith-guided reading to complete it. His thesis: the Jesus of the gospels is
historically the most plausible and intelligible figure. He said the book is not an act
of the magisterium and that readers are free to contradict it. Vol. 2 inclines to
John's dating of the death, with the Supper as Jesus's own Passover without a lamb
([2.4](lessons/02-04-john-and-the-synoptics.md)).

**Status:** as argument, an inference to the best explanation historians may contest; as
teaching, nothing. The Church's own commitment is narrower: Dei Verbum 19.

*Introduced:* [3.1](lessons/03-01-the-quests.md)

## Second Temple Judaism

The world the gospels assume, from the Temple's rebuilding (515 BC) to its destruction
(AD 70). Nothing in this section is Church teaching: every item is a **historical
reconstruction** with no magisterial standing either way. Church teaching bears only on
*how* to use it (Dei Verbum 12: study the writer's customary ways of speaking).

### The three philosophies

**Josephus presented the Jewish parties to Greek readers as philosophical schools; hold
the label loosely.** *War* 2.8.2-14; *Antiquities* 18.1.2-6.

| Group | Resurrection | Law and tradition | Temple | Rome |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Pharisees** (some 6,000 under Herod, *Ant.* 17.2.4) | yes: the righteous "revive and live again" (*Ant.* 18.1.3) | written Law plus ancestral traditions | took part; shaped popular worship | accommodated, mostly |
| **Sadducees** (priestly aristocracy) | no: souls die with bodies (*Ant.* 18.1.4) | written Law only | ran it; vanish with it in 70 | collaborated to keep order |
| **Essenes** (some 4,000, *Ant.* 18.1.5) | immortal soul in Josephus (*War* 2.8.11); the Scrolls' view debated | strict, own rules and calendar | withdrew from the common sacrifices | awaited God's war on the nations |
| **Fourth philosophy** (Judas the Galilean, *Ant.* 18.1.6) | as the Pharisees | as the Pharisees | zeal for its purity | refused tribute: God alone is Lord |
| **Common Judaism** (most Jews) | varied | Sabbath, circumcision, food laws | pilgrimage, tithes, sacrifice | endured it |

**Independent check:** Acts 23:8, "the Sadducees say that there is no resurrection,
neither angel, nor spirit: but the Pharisees confess both." Luke and Josephus agree on
the resurrection line; the angels and spirits are Acts' alone. That the Sadducees
accepted only the five books of Moses is a patristic report, possible but not
established; Josephus says only that they rejected traditions beyond the Law.

*Introduced:* [1.2](lessons/01-02-second-temple-judaism.md)

### Common Judaism

**Most Jews belonged to no party.** E. P. Sanders, *Judaism: Practice and Belief, 63
BCE-66 CE* (1992): priests and people shared one God, one chosen people, one Law, one
Temple, and kept Sabbath, circumcision, food laws, pilgrimage and the Shema. The groups
were small, learned and loud, which is why they fill the sources. **Caution on
sources:** the Mishnah (compiled c. AD 200, after the Temple fell) cannot be read back
into Jesus's Galilee unless a rule is shown early; Josephus, the Scrolls and the New
Testament are nearer witnesses, each with its own slant. **Status:** hypothesis.

*Introduced:* [1.2](lessons/01-02-second-temple-judaism.md)

### Covenantal nomism

**In by grace, kept in by obedience.** E. P. Sanders, *Paul and Palestinian Judaism*
(1977), surveying the rabbinic literature, the Scrolls and the Apocrypha: "getting in"
to the covenant is by God's gracious election of Israel; "staying in" is by obedience,
with sacrifice and repentance provided for failure. Obedience keeps one's place; it does
not earn it. Sanders's Paul then reasons **from solution to plight**: starting from
salvation in Christ, he concludes the Law cannot be the way.

**Contested** as too uniform by the two *Justification and Variegated Nomism* volumes
(eds. Carson, O'Brien and Seifrid; vol. 1, 2001, on Second Temple Judaism; vol. 2, 2004,
on Paul): the pattern is real but some texts put real weight on works at the final
judgment. **Status:** a strong, widely influential hypothesis; not Church teaching, and
not to be preached as such. How far it holds is [5.4](lessons/05-04-the-new-perspective-on-paul.md)'s question.

*Introduced:* [1.2](lessons/01-02-second-temple-judaism.md); weighed at [5.4](lessons/05-04-the-new-perspective-on-paul.md)

### Qumran and the Essenes

**Most scholars identify the Dead Sea Scrolls community as Essene or closely related.**
Scrolls found at Qumran from 1947. The case: Pliny the Elder places Essenes on the Dead
Sea's western shore; shared rules for common property and initiation; the Scrolls'
hostility to the Jerusalem priesthood. The dissent: Norman Golb argued the Scrolls were
Jerusalem libraries hidden during the revolt. **Status:** well-evidenced majority, not
a proof. Say "most scholars identify", not "the Essenes wrote the Scrolls".

*Introduced:* [1.2](lessons/01-02-second-temple-judaism.md)

### Messianic expectations

**There was no single expected Messiah.**

- **Royal**: a son of David who purges Jerusalem of the nations (*Psalms of Solomon* 17, usually dated after Pompey took Jerusalem in 63 BC).
- **Priestly beside royal**: the messiahs of Aaron and of Israel (Qumran's *Community Rule*, 1QS 9.11).
- **Prophetic**: the prophet like Moses (Deuteronomy 18:15).
- **4Q521**: heaven and earth obey God's anointed; then healing the wounded, making the dead live, good news to the poor. Whether God or the anointed does these works is disputed. It joins raising the dead to Isaiah 61's good news, as Jesus's reply to John does (Luke 7:22; Matt 11:5).
- **None**: many texts of the period mention no messiah (John J. Collins, *The Scepter and the Star*, maps the types).

**Use evidence for the language it shows.** 4Q521 makes a messianic reading of Luke 7:22
plausible and early; it does not show that John, or most Jews, made the link.

*Introduced:* [1.2](lessons/01-02-second-temple-judaism.md)

### Apocalyptic and the kingdom of God

**God breaking into history to end the present age and vindicate his people.** Daniel 7:
"one like the Son of man" comes with the clouds and receives an everlasting kingdom.
Jesus's first proclamation speaks this language: "The time is accomplished and the
kingdom of God is at hand" (Mark 1:15). "Kingdom of God" (*basileia*) means God acting
as king, publicly and soon, not a place souls go.

Revelation is *apokalypsis* (1:1), prophecy (1:3) and letter (1:4) at once; its symbols
(beasts for empires, horns for kings, numbers for spans of time) were a code the first
readers could break. The genre is owned by [`old-testament`](../old-testament/syllabus.md)
(6.3).

*Introduced:* [1.2](lessons/01-02-second-temple-judaism.md); used at [6.5](lessons/06-05-revelation.md)

### The Septuagint

**The diaspora's Bible, and the New Testament's.** A Greek translation begun with the
Pentateuch in Alexandria in the third century BC. Probably more Jews lived outside the
land than in it, meeting in synagogues to read the Law on the Sabbath. Most New
Testament quotations follow the Greek where it differs from the Hebrew. Cases in this
course:

- Amos 9:11-12 in Acts 15:16-17: "the residue of men" where the Hebrew has "Edom".
- Jeremiah 31:32 in Hebrews 8:9: "I regarded them not" (LXX Jer 38), where the Hebrew has "I had dominion over them" ([6.2](lessons/06-02-hebrews.md)).
- Isaiah 45:23 in Rom 14:11 and Phil 2:10-11 ([4.3](lessons/04-03-paul-at-work.md)).
- Genesis 3:13 "deceived" echoed at Rom 7:11 ([5.3](lessons/05-03-romans-5-11-adam-spirit-israel.md)).
- Isaiah 43:10 behind the absolute *egō eimi* ([2.5](lessons/02-05-the-theology-of-john.md)).

*Introduced:* [1.2](lessons/01-02-second-temple-judaism.md)

**No year zero.** 1 BC is followed directly by AD 1, so a span that crosses the line is
one year shorter than plain subtraction suggests. For someone born in year $B$ BC, their
age at their birthday in AD $Y$ is $Y + B - 1$. The era was computed by Dionysius
Exiguus in the sixth century, for Easter tables, so "Jesus born before 4 BC" is no
paradox.

**Fencing a passage** (Luke 3:1-2): Tiberius 14-37, Pilate 26-36, Antipas 4 BC-AD 39,
Philip to 33/34, Caiaphas to about 36/37; the intersection is **26 to 33/34**, and
Luke's fifteenth year falls inside it on either count.

**Status:** all historians' reconstructions; the Creed's "under Pontius Pilate" is dogma
held on apostolic witness, not on Josephus. *Introduced:* [1.3](lessons/01-03-under-rome.md)

### Herod the Great and his sons

**A client king, and a kingdom split three ways.** Herod the Great (king 37-4 BC; Matt
2:1) kept order, paid Rome, and rebuilt the Temple on a vast scale. His will divided the
kingdom:

- **Archelaus**, ethnarch of Judaea, Samaria and Idumaea, deposed AD 6 (Matt 2:22).
- **Herod Antipas**, tetrarch of Galilee and Peraea, 4 BC-AD 39: beheaded the Baptist; the "fox" (Luke 13:32); rebuilt Sepphoris, four miles from Nazareth, and founded Tiberias (*Ant.* 18.2.1, 18.2.3). Mark 6:14 calls him "king"; Matt 14:1 and Luke 9:7 "tetrarch".
- **Philip**, tetrarch in the north-east until Tiberius's twentieth year (33/34).

Then **Agrippa I** (Acts 12, died 44) and **Agrippa II** (Acts 25-26). Jesus the Galilean
lived under a Jewish client ruler; Jerusalem, where he died, lay under a Roman governor.
No Roman legion was stationed in Galilee in his lifetime (Sanders). Whether Antipas's
building squeezed the peasantry (Horsley) or left the rural economy largely intact
(Jensen, from the archaeology) is a live dispute.

*Introduced:* [1.3](lessons/01-03-under-rome.md)

### Pontius Pilate, prefect of Judaea

**Governor of Judaea, with Samaria and Idumaea, 26-36; not of Galilee.** Josephus: Gratus
stayed eleven years, then Pilate; Vitellius sent Pilate to Rome after ten years, and he
arrived after Tiberius had died, in March 37 (*Ant.* 18.2.2, 18.4.2). He lived at
Caesarea and came up with troops. Two incidents (*Ant.* 18.3.1-2): the standards with
the emperor's image brought into Jerusalem and withdrawn before a crowd willing to die;
the aqueduct paid from the Temple treasury, and the protesters clubbed. Compare Luke
13:1. Because Galilee was Antipas's, Luke's Pilate can send the Galilean Jesus to Herod
(23:6-7).

**Status:** "crucified under Pontius Pilate" is **creedal dogma**; his dates are a
reconstruction.

*Introduced:* [1.3](lessons/01-03-under-rome.md)

### Prefect and procurator

**Pilate's title was prefect; "procurator" is the later title read back.** The Pilate
stone, found at Caesarea in 1961 reused in the theatre (now in the Israel Museum), is
the one inscription securely known to name him in his lifetime. Its damaged lines are
usually restored *[Ponti]us Pilatus [praef]ectus Iuda[ea]e*, dedicating a "Tiberieum";
only the ending *-ectus* survives, which is why most historians read prefect. On the
usual view Judaea's governors were prefects until 41, and "procurator", originally a
financial title, became theirs under Claudius. Tacitus (*Annals* 15.44) and Josephus in
Whiston both say "procurator": the title of their own day. Luke 3:1 uses the generic
*hēgemoneuontos*, "while governing".

*Introduced:* [1.3](lessons/01-03-under-rome.md); Tacitus's slip noted at [3.2](lessons/03-02-sources-and-criteria.md)

### The census of Quirinius

**Luke dates the birth by a census Josephus puts about a decade later.** Luke 2:1-2: a
decree of Augustus, "this enrolling was first made by Cyrinus, the governor of Syria",
with the birth "in the days of Herod" (Luke 1:5; Matt 2:1). Josephus (*Ant.* 17.13.5,
18.1.1, 18.2.1) puts Quirinius's census after Archelaus was deposed, when Judaea was
annexed to Syria, ending in the thirty-seventh year after Actium: **AD 6-7**. It
provoked Judas the Galilean's revolt, which Luke also knows (Acts 5:37). The Greek:
*hautē apographē prōtē egeneto hēgemoneuontos tēs Syrias Kyrēniou*.

Three kinds of solution, all **hypotheses**:

1. **Read *prōtē* as "before"** (as *prōtos mou* in John 1:15): the census preceded Quirinius's governorship. Some conservative commentators; N. T. Wright has floated it. Cost: an unusual construction here, and why date by a governor not yet arrived?
2. **An earlier census or command.** Tertullian (*Against Marcion* 4.19) has a census in Judaea under Sentius Saturninus, legate of Syria late in Herod's reign; others propose an earlier Quirinius command or an oath under Herod. Cost: no source attests a Roman census in Herod's client kingdom, and Tertullian's governor is not Luke's.
3. **Luke misdated it**, attaching the famous census of AD 6 to a birth the tradition placed under Herod: Brown (*The Birth of the Messiah*) and Fitzmyer (Anchor *Luke*); the critical majority, not a proof.

**Levels:** a dispute about a detail's history, not about the gospel's historical
character. Dei Verbum 19 does not decide which census stands behind 2:2; whether an
incidental dating error falls under inerrancy is the freely disputed scope question
([`fundamental-theology` 3.2](../fundamental-theology/lessons/03-02-inerrancy.md)).

*Introduced:* [1.3](lessons/01-03-under-rome.md) (the AD 6 census); the problem is [2.3](lessons/02-03-luke.md)'s

### Claudius and Chrestus

**An expulsion with no year in either source.** Acts 18:2: Aquila and Priscilla had lately
come to Corinth "because that Claudius had commanded all Jews to depart from Rome."
Suetonius, *Claudius* 25.4 (c. 120): *Iudaeos impulsore Chresto assidue tumultuantis
Roma expulit*, "He expelled from Rome the Jews, who were constantly rioting at the
prompting of Chrestus" *(lesson's translation)*.

- **Who is Chrestus?** Most take it as a common misspelling of *Christus*, making the riots synagogue disputes over Jesus. But *Chrestus* was also a real slave name, and Suetonius writes as if the man were in Rome. Probable, not proven.
- **What year?** 49 comes only from Orosius (fifth century), who puts it in Claudius's ninth year and cites a Josephus passage that does not survive. Cassius Dio 60.6.6, under **41**, says Claudius did not expel the Jews but banned their meetings. Lüdemann builds an early Pauline chronology on 41; Riesner defends 49.

**The crux:** do Dio and Suetonius describe one measure or two? The fit with Acts is
itself part of what is weighed. Say **c. 49, disputed**.

*Introduced:* [1.3](lessons/01-03-under-rome.md); used at [4.2](lessons/04-02-pauls-life-and-letters.md)

### The Gallio inscription

**The one date in Paul's life fixed by outside evidence.** Fragments of a letter of
Claudius found at Delphi call Gallio the emperor's friend and proconsul (the key words
partly restored). The letter gives Claudius's **26th acclamation** as *imperator*, which
places it in **52**. Proconsuls of Achaia normally served one year, taking office in early
summer, so most date Gallio's year **51-52**. Acts 18:12 brings Paul before him at
Corinth.

**What it dates:** the hearing, not Paul's arrival. The step to "Paul arrived c. 50"
needs Acts 18:11's eighteen months and an assumption about where they fall (early 50,
or on another reckoning later). It confirms Luke's **setting** (Gallio held that office
then), not the hearing itself.

*Introduced:* [4.1](lessons/04-01-acts-as-history.md) (as an external check on Acts); the date is worked at [4.2](lessons/04-02-pauls-life-and-letters.md)

### Dating the gospels

**Anchor: 70. Everything else is inference.**

| Book | Common range | Main grounds |
|---|---|---|
| Mark | c. 65-73 | Mark 13:14 read around 70; Irenaeus: Mark wrote "after their departure" (Eusebius V.8.3) |
| Matthew | c. 80-90 | uses Mark; 22:7 (the king who "burnt their city") read as looking back on 70 |
| Luke(-Acts) | c. 80-90 | uses Mark; Luke 21:20 "Jerusalem compassed about with an army" |
| John | c. 90-100 | grew in stages; ch. 21 added after 20:30-31 |

**Minority:** John A. T. Robinson, *Redating the New Testament* (1976), put every book
before 70: none mentions the fall as past, and Acts ends with Paul alive, c. 62. Serious,
and a minority. **Where it strains:** Mark 13:14's aside "he that readeth let him
understand" is Mark speaking; *hestēkota*, "standing", is masculine though *bdelygma* is
neuter, so a person is meant; it fits Titus in the Temple after the event, or Caligula
(c. 40) or the crisis of 66-70 before it. C. H. Dodd argued Luke's siege language echoes
the Greek Old Testament on 587 BC. **Status:** every range is an inference to the best
explanation and a hypothesis; the Church teaches no date. Dating a text and judging its
history are separate questions: a gospel of 85 can hand on stage-1 material faithfully.

*Introduced:* [1.5](lessons/01-05-from-oral-tradition-to-written-gospel.md)

## The Synoptic problem

Matthew, Mark and Luke share whole Greek sentences word for word; oral memory does not
do that, copying does. **Mark is the middle term**: Matthew and Luke often agree with
Mark and rarely agree with each other against him. Three explanations fit that fact,
and **none is Church teaching**. Weigh them as rival explanations
([`philosophical-method` 2.1](../philosophical-method/lessons/02-01-inference-to-the-best-explanation.md)).

| Hypothesis | Arrows | Needs a lost document? | Best datum | Weak point |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Two-Source | Mark to Matthew and to Luke; Q to both | yes, Q | harder readings, rougher Greek, omissions | the minor agreements |
| Farrer-Goulder-Goodacre | Mark to Matthew and Luke; Matthew to Luke | no | the minor agreements | Luke almost never has Matthew's additions to Mark |
| Griesbach | Matthew to Luke; Matthew and Luke to Mark | no | Mark's doubled phrases; Clement via Eusebius VI.14 | the omission problem |

**Papias is not a synopsis.** Eusebius III.39.15-16 has Papias report that Mark, Peter's
interpreter, wrote accurately "though not in order", and that Matthew wrote "the oracles
in the Hebrew language" (McGiffert). He says nothing about any evangelist using
another's book. Our Greek Matthew does not read like a translation (in the triple
tradition it reproduces Mark's Greek), so most scholars doubt Papias's "oracles" were
our Matthew.

### Markan priority

**Mark was written first and used by Matthew and Luke.** The data: B. H. Streeter (*The
Four Gospels*, 1924) found the substance of over 600 of Mark's 661 verses in Matthew and
about 350 in Luke. The arguments for direction:

1. **Order** shows Mark is the middle term (Lachmann, 1835), not that he is first: B. C. Butler (*The Originality of St Matthew*, 1951) named the inference the "Lachmann fallacy".
2. **Harder readings.** Mark 6:5, Jesus "could not" (*ouk edynato*) do mighty works at Nazareth; Matthew 13:58, "he did not many", "because of their unbelief". "Could not" to "did not" is easy to explain; the reverse is not.
3. **Rougher Greek.** Historic presents ("there *comes* to him a leper", 1:40), *euthys*, strings of "and"; Matthew and Luke smooth them, each his own way.
4. **The omission problem.** A Mark who used Matthew and Luke dropped the infancy narratives, the Sermon, the Lord's Prayer and every resurrection appearance, while making the stories he kept longer.

**Test case:** the storm at sea (Mark 4:38 "doth it not concern thee that we perish?";
Matt 8:25 "Lord, save us"; Luke 8:24 "Master, we perish"): each later writer softens the
reproach his own way.

**Status:** the majority hypothesis, and a majority is not a proof. **No magisterial
standing**; Dei Verbum 19 is silent on the order of the gospels. The 1911-12 responses'
contrary line is a weight case ([early responses](#the-early-biblical-commission-responses)).

*Introduced:* [1.4](lessons/01-04-the-synoptic-problem.md); the base of [2.1](lessons/02-01-mark.md), [2.2](lessons/02-02-matthew.md), [2.3](lessons/02-03-luke.md)

### Two-Source hypothesis

**Matthew and Luke each used Mark and, independently, a lost sayings source Q.** Q
explains the double tradition (about 200 verses, mostly sayings). H. J. Holtzmann (1863)
gave it its classic form; Streeter refined it. Raymond Brown and Joseph Fitzmyer adopted
it as the most satisfactory working solution while calling it a hypothesis. Q survives
in no manuscript, and no ancient writer mentions it.

**Its weak point** is the [minor agreements](#minor-agreements). **Its defence** (John
Kloppenborg and others): Luke almost never shows Matthew's additions to Mark's stories;
in the grainfields Luke 6:1-5 lacks Matthew 12:5-7.

**Why it matters downstream:** the Synoptic hypothesis decides which sources count as
independent for [multiple attestation](#multiple-attestation). On Two-Source, Mark 2:16,
the Q saying (Luke 7:34) and L (Luke 15:2) are three witnesses; Matthew copying Mark is
one. Example of a Q-level redaction: Luke 11:13 "the good Spirit" against Matt 7:11
"good things" (the frame's "good gifts" makes Matthew's the original; the change is
Luke's on either hypothesis).

**Status:** hypothesis stacked on Markan priority; no magisterial standing.

*Introduced:* [1.4](lessons/01-04-the-synoptic-problem.md); used at [2.2](lessons/02-02-matthew.md), [2.3](lessons/02-03-luke.md), [3.2](lessons/03-02-sources-and-criteria.md)

### Griesbach hypothesis

**Matthew first, Luke used Matthew, Mark combined both.** J. J. Griesbach (1789); revived
by William Farmer (*The Synoptic Problem*, 1964). Evidence:

- **Mark's double phrases**: Mark 1:32 "when it was evening, after sunset"; Matt 8:16 has only the first half, Luke 4:40 only the second. Reply: doubled phrases are Mark's habit everywhere.
- **Patristic support**: Clement of Alexandria, per Eusebius VI.14, says the gospels with the genealogies were written first.

**The other rival: Farrer-Goulder-Goodacre.** Austin Farrer ("On Dispensing with Q",
1955), Michael Goulder, Mark Goodacre (*The Case Against Q*, 2002): keep Markan priority,
drop Q; Luke used Matthew as well as Mark. Chief evidence: the minor agreements.

**Status:** hypotheses, freely disputed; their defenders are not cranks.

*Introduced:* [1.4](lessons/01-04-the-synoptic-problem.md)

### Minor agreements

**Places in the triple tradition where Matthew and Luke agree against Mark**: evidence
against their independence, so the Two-Source hypothesis's cost.

- **"Who is it that struck thee?"** Mark 14:65 has only "Prophesy"; Matt 26:68 "Prophesy unto us, O Christ. Who is he that struck thee?"; Luke 22:64 "Prophesy: Who is it that struck thee?" Five identical Greek words, *tis estin ho paisas se*, absent from Mark. Mark covers Jesus's face; Matthew has no blindfold, so his question hangs oddly. Farrer: Luke read it in Matthew. Griesbach: Mark dropped it. Two-Source: strained. Streeter proposed scribal assimilation from Luke into every surviving manuscript of Matthew, a conjecture, since none lacks the words.
- **The leper** (Mark 1:40-44 and parallels): shared *idou*, *kyrie*, and the wording of the touch, against Mark.

**Rule:** a minor agreement does not refute Q; it shows where Q's defenders have to pay.

*Introduced:* [1.4](lessons/01-04-the-synoptic-problem.md)

### Form criticism

**A story's shape tells you what job it did in the Church.** K. L. Schmidt (1919): Mark's
framework ("and immediately", "again he went") is editorial glue joining units
(pericopes) that circulated separately. Martin Dibelius (*Die Formgeschichte des
Evangeliums*, 1919) and Rudolf Bultmann (*Die Geschichte der synoptischen Tradition*,
1921) sorted them by form: pronouncement stories (Dibelius's paradigms, Bultmann's
apophthegms), miracle stories, parables, sayings, legends. Each form was tied to a *Sitz
im Leben*: the preaching, catechesis, controversy or worship in which it was **used**.

**Method and verdict came apart.** Bultmann added a verdict: the community created much
of the material, and the gospels are unlike any biography. The method outlived the
verdict; Brown and Fitzmyer used it throughout. **Redaction criticism** followed
(Bornkamm on Matthew; Conzelmann on Luke, 1954; Marxsen on Mark, 1956, who coined
*Redaktionsgeschichte*): the evangelists are authors, not scissors. Bultmann's inference
from kerygmatic units to "almost nothing knowable" drove the [no quest](#bultmann-and-kasemann).

**Rule: setting of use is not setting of origin.** That the Church used the grainfields
story (Mark 2:23-28) in Sabbath disputes says nothing about whether Jesus said 2:27.

**Status:** *Sancta Mater Ecclesia* permits the sound elements and rejects the
rationalist presuppositions (curial guidance, no certificate for any result); every form
category and setting is a hypothesis.

*Introduced:* [1.5](lessons/01-05-from-oral-tradition-to-written-gospel.md); used at [3.1](lessons/03-01-the-quests.md)

### Oral tradition and memory

**How reliably was the tradition carried?** All positions are hypotheses.

- **Informal controlled tradition** (Kenneth Bailey, from Middle Eastern village life): the community retells freely but corrects a teller who changes the core. Critic: Theodore Weeden, that the anecdotes do not bear the weight.
- **Remembered impact** (James Dunn, *Jesus Remembered*, 2003): tradition begins as the disciples' remembered impression of Jesus, performed with a stable core and flexible detail, so the wedge between faith and history is false at the start.
- **Distortion** (Bart Ehrman, *Jesus Before the Gospels*, 2016): memory research stresses how memory distorts.
- **Eyewitness testimony** (Richard Bauckham, *Jesus and the Eyewitnesses*, 2006): named eyewitnesses stayed active as authorities until the gospels were written; Papias's preference for the "living and abiding voice" (III.39.4); incidental names like Rufus and Alexander (Mark 15:21). Critics: Papias is late and partisan, and memory research cuts both ways.
- **Memory approaches to Jesus** (Dunn; Dale Allison, *Constructing Jesus*, 2010): ask what past best explains the way Jesus was remembered, looking for recurrent patterns ([criteria](#criteria-of-authenticity)).

**Papias's phrases** (Eusebius III.39.15): *hermēneutēs Petrou* (translator, or
spokesman); *ou mentoi taxei*, "not, however, in order" (a defence of Mark's
arrangement); *pros tas chreias*, "to the needs" or "in the form of *chreiai*" (both
readings open).

**Rule:** the outline of the three stages is teaching; every account of how stage 2
worked is a hypothesis, including the reassuring ones. Bauckham is not "the Catholic
position".

*Introduced:* [1.5](lessons/01-05-from-oral-tradition-to-written-gospel.md)

### The gospels as bioi

**Ancient readers would have shelved Mark with the lives of famous men.** Richard
Burridge (*What Are the Gospels?*, 1992) compared the gospels with Greco-Roman *bioi*
such as Plutarch's and found the same generic markers: one subject dominating the verbs,
and a disproportionate focus on the death. Against the form critics' view of the gospels
as unique folk literature. Most scholars now accept some version. The genre fixes what
kind of truth-claim to expect ([`fundamental-theology` 3.2](../fundamental-theology/lessons/03-02-inerrancy.md)
on genre). **Status:** hypothesis.

*Introduced:* [1.5](lessons/01-05-from-oral-tradition-to-written-gospel.md)

### The messianic secret

**Mark's Jesus silences talk about himself until the cross.** William Wrede, *Das
Messiasgeheimnis in den Evangelien* (1901), gathered the silencing of demons (1:34,
3:12), commands to the healed (1:44, 5:43, 7:36), private teaching (4:10-12) and 9:9 [DR
9:8], whose silence has an expiry date: "till the Son of man shall be risen again from
the dead". His theory: the earliest tradition did not present the ministry as
messianic; the secret reconciles that with the post-Easter confession.

**Critics** (Schweitzer's *Quest*, 1906, onward): the commands are not all one kind
(some are broken at once, 1:45, 7:36; some protect a miracle, not a title); and a Jesus
who avoided "Messiah", a title with political freight under Rome, is plausible in its
own right (Dunn). **Most keep the theme and drop the theory.** The centurion's "Indeed
this man was the son of God" (15:39) is the first human confession not silenced.

**Status:** hypothesis. It does not mean Mark doubts Jesus is Messiah: he says so in 1:1.

*Introduced:* [2.1](lessons/02-01-mark.md)

### Discipleship failure in Mark

**Insiders who behave like outsiders.** Given "the mystery of the kingdom" (4:11), the
disciples misunderstand (4:13; 6:52 "their heart was blinded"; 8:17; 9:32 [DR 9:31]),
argue about rank, all flee (14:50), and Peter denies. Theodore Weeden (*Mark: Traditions
in Conflict*, 1971): polemic against a party the disciples stood for. Robert Tannehill:
the reader is meant to identify with them; failure is not final (14:28, 16:7). **Status:**
both hypotheses.

*Introduced:* [2.1](lessons/02-01-mark.md)

### The way section

**Mark 8:27-10:52: three predictions, three misunderstandings, three lessons in
service**, framed by two blind men and the phrase *en tē hodō*, "on the way" (8:27; 9:33-34;
10:32; 10:52).

| | Prediction | Misunderstanding | Teaching |
|---|---|---|---|
| Frame | 8:22-26: the blind man healed in two stages ("men, as it were trees, walking") | | |
| 1 | 8:31 | Peter rebukes Jesus (8:32-33) | take up the cross (8:34-38) |
| 2 | 9:31 | they do not understand (9:32); argue who is greatest (9:33-34) | the first shall be last; receive a child (9:35-37) |
| 3 | 10:33-34 | James and John ask for the seats (10:35-41) | the Son of man came to serve and "to give his life a redemption for many" (10:45) |
| Frame | 10:46-52: Bartimaeus sees and "followed him in the way" | | |

Each prediction is more detailed and each misunderstanding worse. The secret is real as
literature; its origin is disputed. Peter's confession, which opens the section, is
boss material and is not set out here.

*Introduced:* [2.1](lessons/02-01-mark.md)

### The brothers of Jesus

**Mark 6:3 names James, Joseph (Greek *Iōsēs*), Jude and Simon, and sisters.** *Adelphos*
can carry all three readings; each turns on inference, not the word alone.

- **Helvidian**: children of Mary and Joseph (Helvidius, c. 383; Meier, as a historian bracketing faith, judged it most probable).
- **Epiphanian**: Joseph's children by an earlier marriage (*Protoevangelium of James*; Epiphanius; Bauckham calls it the strongest non-sibling reading). Favoured in the East.
- **Hieronymian**: cousins (Jerome against Helvidius, [`patristics` 4.2](../patristics/lessons/04-02-jerome.md)); adds Mark 15:40, where a different Mary is mother of "James the Less and of Joseph" (*Iōsēs* again). Favoured in the West.

**Levels:** Mary's perpetual virginity is held as dogma (basis and level owned by
[`ecclesiology-and-mariology`](../ecclesiology-and-mariology/syllabus.md)); it
**excludes** the Helvidian reading and does **not** choose between the other two, which
are **freely disputed**. "The Church teaches they were cousins" is a level error.

*Introduced:* [2.1](lessons/02-01-mark.md); James at [6.3](lessons/06-03-james-faith-and-works.md), Jude at [6.4](lessons/06-04-peter-jude-and-the-letters-of-john.md)

### The endings of Mark

**Four endings in the manuscripts.** At **16:8**, "for they were afraid" (*ephobounto
gar*), in Sinaiticus and Vaticanus; the **longer ending** 16:9-20 in the great majority
of later manuscripts and the Douay; a two-sentence **shorter ending**; and the longer
expanded (the Freer logion, Codex W). The longer ending is old: Irenaeus (*Against
Heresies* III.10, c. 180) quotes 16:19 as what Mark says toward the end of his gospel.
Most critical scholars judge it not Mark's: vocabulary and style, 16:9 reintroducing
Mary Magdalen, a digest of appearances in Luke and John. J. W. Burgon (1871) is the
fullest defence; few follow. Among deniers: did Mark mean to stop at 16:8 (rare, not
ungrammatical), or was the ending lost?

**Levels:** 16:9-20 is **canonical** (Trent: "with all their parts", as in the Vulgate,
which contains it); **who wrote it** and whether Mark ended at 16:8 are hypotheses.

*Introduced:* [2.1](lessons/02-01-mark.md)

### The five discourses

**Five blocks of teaching, each closed by the same formula.** Chapters 5-7, 10, 13, 18,
24-25, each ending *kai egeneto hote etelesen ho Iēsous*, "and it came to pass when Jesus
had finished" (7:28; 11:1; 13:53; 19:1; 26:1). Only three have "these words" (7:28, 19:1,
26:1); 11:1 has "commanding his twelve disciples", 13:53 "these parables". **Outlines:**
B. W. Bacon read the five as mirroring the five books of Moses; Jack Dean Kingsbury made
the hinges the two "From that time Jesus began" verses (4:17, 16:21), a three-part
story. **Status:** the discourses are on the page; the Pentateuch analogy is a
hypothesis.

*Introduced:* [2.2](lessons/02-02-matthew.md)

### Fulfilment quotations

**Ten times the narrator stops to say an event happened "that it might be fulfilled
which was spoken by the prophet".** Matthew 1:22; 2:15; 2:17; 2:23; 4:14; 8:17; 12:17;
13:35; 21:4; 27:9. Counts of eleven or more add 2:5-6 or 13:14, where the wording
differs. The Douay's 27:35 has an extra one, a Vulgate reading borrowed from John 19:24
and absent from the best Greek manuscripts: do not count it.

**Mostly typological, not predictive**: "fulfil" means *fill up*, not only *come true*.
Method: find the source, read it in its own context, name the link. Hosea 11:1 ("I
called my son out of Egypt") is a memory about Israel; Matthew 2:15 reads Jesus reliving
Israel's pattern. **Hard case:** 27:9, "spoken by Jeremias", is closest to Zechariah
11:12-13 (the Douay's "statuary" is a potter in Hebrew); usually read as a composite
citation filed under the more famous prophet, as Mark 1:2-3 files Malachi under Isaiah.
Whether that is an error is [inerrancy](../fundamental-theology/lessons/03-02-inerrancy.md)'s
question, not this course's.

*Introduced:* [2.2](lessons/02-02-matthew.md)

### The antitheses

**Six "You have heard that it was said ... But I say to you" sayings** (Matthew
5:21-48): anger, lust, divorce, oaths, retaliation, love of enemies. They follow the
programme of 5:17: not *katalysai*, "annul", but *plērōsai*, "fill full" ("I did not
come to annul them but to bring them to their full measure", *lesson's translation*);
and 5:20's *dikaiosynē* that must exceed the scribes'. Some plainly intensify; others
(oaths) leave a commandment nothing to do. John P. Meier (*Law and History in Matthew's
Gospel*): some revoke the letter, with "till all be fulfilled" (5:18) pointing to the
death and resurrection. Others read every antithesis as radicalizing. **Agreed:** Jesus
claims the right to say what the Law finally means. **Disputed:** whether that ever sets
its letter aside.

*Introduced:* [2.2](lessons/02-02-matthew.md)

### Ekklesia in Matthew

**The only gospel with the word "church".** *Ekklēsia*, "assembly", is the Septuagint's
word for Israel gathered (the *qahal*). Matthew uses it twice: 16:18, "I will build my
church" (Jesus's people as a whole), and 18:17, "tell the church" (the local
congregation settling a dispute). Peter's role grows to match: only Matthew has him walk
on the water and sink (14:28-31), pay the Temple tax "for me and thee" (17:24-27 [DR
17:23-26]), and receive 16:17-19. Whether 16:17-19 goes back to Jesus is reserved boss
material; the doctrine of primacy belongs to
[`ecclesiology-and-mariology`](../ecclesiology-and-mariology/syllabus.md).

*Introduced:* [2.2](lessons/02-02-matthew.md)

### Matthew's community and the synagogue

**A family quarrel over Moses.** Jesus teaches in "*their* synagogues" (4:23; 9:35;
13:54); disciples will be scourged in "their synagogues" (10:17); chapter 23 is a
sustained polemic, yet opens by telling hearers to keep what is taught from "the chair
of Moses" (23:2-3). The mission runs from "go ye not into the way of the Gentiles" (10:5)
to "teach ye all nations" (28:19). Readings: Graham Stanton (*A Gospel for a New
People*), recently separated from the synagogue; Anthony Saldarini (*Matthew's
Christian-Jewish Community*), a dissenting group still inside Judaism; David Sim (*The
Gospel of Matthew and Christian Judaism*), still Law-observant. **Status:** hypotheses.
"Their synagogues" is not "the Jews": reading chapter 23 as a verdict on the Jewish
people is an exegetical error.

*Introduced:* [2.2](lessons/02-02-matthew.md)

### The Lord's Prayer in two forms

**Same prayer, two settings, varied wording.** Matthew 6:9-13 (seven petitions, in the
Sermon) against Luke 11:2-4 (in the Douay, following the Vulgate's shorter text, five
petitions, in answer to a disciple's request). The same Greek *epiousion* is
"supersubstantial" in the Douay's Matthew and "daily" in its Luke. Matthew asks
forgiveness of "debts" (*opheilēmata*), Luke of "sins" (*hamartias*), yet Luke keeps the
debt image in "every one that is indebted to us" (*opheilonti*); Matthew's verb is past,
*aphēkamen*, "as we too have forgiven". "For thine is the kingdom" is absent from the
Douay and the best manuscripts; a form of it is in the Didache
([`patristics` 1.2](../patristics/lessons/01-02-first-clement-and-the-didache.md)).
Joachim Jeremias argued Luke keeps the original length and Matthew often the older
wording. **Levels:** Dei Verbum 19 commits you to the gospels faithfully handing on what
Jesus taught; *Sancta Mater Ecclesia* says varied, non-literal wording costs them
nothing. Which wording is closer to Jesus is open. The debt theology belongs to
[`theology-of-debt`](../theology-of-debt/syllabus.md); the release laws behind it are
[`history-of-debt` 1.3](../history-of-debt/lessons/01-03-release-laws-in-ancient-israel.md).

*Introduced:* [1.1](lessons/01-01-reading-the-new-testament-in-the-church.md); synopsis at [2.2](lessons/02-02-matthew.md)

### Luke-Acts

**One author, two volumes, to Theophilus.** Acts opens by addressing Theophilus again
and summing up its "former treatise" (Acts 1:1). Henry Cadbury's *The Making of
Luke-Acts* (1927) made the hyphenated name standard. One author for both is close to
unanimous; whether they form one narrative and one genre was questioned by Mikeal
Parsons and Richard Pervo (*Rethinking the Unity of Luke and Acts*, 1993). Hans
Conzelmann (*Die Mitte der Zeit*, 1954) read three epochs: Israel to John the Baptist,
Jesus as "the middle of time", the Church; many now find it too neat. The frame: from
the Temple (Zechariah, 1:5-23) to the Temple (24:53); then Jerusalem to Rome, with the
Spirit as hinge.

**Luke's prologue** (1:1-4) is the New Testament's nearest thing to a methods section:
Luke is not an eyewitness and says so; he promises care (*akribōs*) and an ordered
account (*kathexēs*), not strict chronology, since he moves the Nazareth rejection to
the start (4:16-30). The majority reads it as a historian's preface; Loveday Alexander
(*The Preface to Luke's Gospel*, 1993) found its closest analogues in technical and
scientific treatises. **Status:** all hypothesis-level; tradition names the author Luke,
Paul's companion (Irenaeus III.1.1).

*Introduced:* [2.3](lessons/02-03-luke.md); Acts at [4.1](lessons/04-01-acts-as-history.md)

### Reversal in Luke

**God lifts the lowly and sends the rich away empty**, announced before it is enacted.
The Magnificat (1:52-53); the Nazareth programme from Isaiah 61 (4:18-19); four blessings
and four woes on the Plain (6:20-26); the rich man and Lazarus (16:19-31, only in Luke).
**Reach** runs alongside: shepherds, Samaritans, tax collectors, prodigals, the nations.
The L parables (the Good Samaritan, 10:29-37; the Prodigal Son, 15:11-32) make reach into
story. James carries the same reversal ([6.3](lessons/06-03-james-faith-and-works.md)).

*Introduced:* [2.3](lessons/02-03-luke.md)

### The two infancy narratives

**Matthew and Luke share no scene but agree on a core**: Mary betrothed to Joseph, of
David's house; conception by the Holy Spirit, not by Joseph; the angel gives the name
Jesus; born at Bethlehem in Herod's days; raised at Nazareth. Matthew tells it from
Joseph's side (dreams, magi, the flight, avoiding Archelaus); Luke from Mary's (the
annunciation, the census, shepherds, the Temple framing all). Brown's *The Birth of the
Messiah* (1977; updated 1993) builds on this pattern. The Marian dogmas belong to
[`ecclesiology-and-mariology`](../ecclesiology-and-mariology/syllabus.md).

*Introduced:* [2.3](lessons/02-03-luke.md)

### The Johannine chronology

**Three Passovers, an early Temple scene, and death with the lambs.**

| | Mark (followed by Matthew, Luke) | John |
|---|---|---|
| Passovers named | one, at the end | three (2:13, 6:4, 11:55): at least two years |
| Temple incident | last week (11:15-18), triggers the plot | first Passover (2:13-22); Lazarus triggers the plot (11:47-53) |
| Last Supper | the Passover meal (14:12-16) | before the feast (13:1); no institution words; foot-washing |
| Day of death | **15 Nisan** | **14 Nisan**, the "parasceve of the pasch" (19:14): the priests have not yet eaten (18:28) |
| Hour | third hour (15:25) | Pilate still judging at the sixth (19:14) |
| Weekday | Friday (Mark 15:42) | Friday (19:31) |

John ties the death to the Passover lamb: "the Lamb of God" (1:29), the unbroken bones
(19:36; Exod 12:46). **Which date is historical?** Meier (*A Marginal Jew* vol. 1) argues
for John's, partly because a capital trial on the feast is hard to imagine; others
defend Mark's; Annie Jaubert's Qumran-solar-calendar solution has few followers;
Benedict XVI inclined to John's (not magisterial). A silence (Mark's one Passover) is not
a count: many historians take John's longer ministry as at least as plausible.

**Harmonizing** (Eusebius III.24.7-13: John recorded what Jesus did before the Baptist's
arrest) works for the early Judean ministry but needs two Temple incidents, and 14 and
15 Nisan are rival dates for one event, not two parts of one timeline. **Levels:** Dei
Verbum 19 fixes no chronology or day of death and lets the evangelists arrange; faith
does not require harmonizing.

*Introduced:* [2.4](lessons/02-04-john-and-the-synoptics.md); used at [1.1](lessons/01-01-reading-the-new-testament-in-the-church.md)

### The beloved disciple

**The gospel names no author; it points to "the disciple whom Jesus loved"** (13:23;
19:26; 20:2; 21:7; 21:20), who "hath written these things" (21:24). Irenaeus (*Against
Heresies* III.1.1, ANF) is the first clear attribution: "John, the disciple of the Lord,
who also had leaned upon His breast, did himself publish a Gospel during his residence
at Ephesus in Asia." Note the title: **"disciple of the Lord", not "apostle" or "son of
Zebedee"**. Papias (Eusebius III.39.4) lists "the presbyter John" among the "disciples
of the Lord" separately from the John beside Peter, James and Matthew; Eusebius
concluded there were two Johns in Asia (III.39.5-6).

**Positions:** John son of Zebedee (tradition; Brown's Anchor commentary, 1966-70); an
unnamed disciple outside the Twelve (Brown, *The Community of the Beloved Disciple*,
1979); John the Elder of Papias, an eyewitness outside the Twelve (Bauckham, 2006); an
idealized figure (minority). Most date the finished gospel c. 90-100, grown in stages,
ch. 21 added after 20:30-31.

**Levels:** Dei Verbum 18 teaches apostolic origin through apostles "and apostolic
men"; it does not assign the pen. The 1907 response is a weight case. The identity is a
hypothesis; a Catholic may hold any listed position.

*Introduced:* [2.4](lessons/02-04-john-and-the-synoptics.md)

### The Johannine community

**Reading John on two levels.** J. Louis Martyn (*History and Theology in the Fourth
Gospel*, 1968): the healed blind man put "out of the synagogue" (9:22; also 12:42, 16:2)
tells Jesus's story and the story of John's own church, expelled late in the century.
Brown's 1979 book built a community history on the method, and heard the same history
in John's hostility to *hoi Ioudaioi*. **Critics:** the link to the *Birkat ha-Minim* has
uncertain date and target; the method reads a community's history out of a story about
Jesus; Bauckham (ed.), *The Gospels for All Christians* (1998), argued the gospels were
written for circulation among all the churches. **Status:** hypothesis.

*Introduced:* [2.4](lessons/02-04-john-and-the-synoptics.md); used at [2.5](lessons/02-05-the-theology-of-john.md)

### Did John know the Synoptics

**Independence, or dependence on Mark?** P. Gardner-Smith (*Saint John and the Synoptic
Gospels*, 1938) and C. H. Dodd: independent; overlaps are parallel oral tradition, and
wholesale departures are hard to explain in an author with Mark open. Independence long
held the majority. Frans Neirynck and the Leuven school: John knew Mark (and perhaps
Luke), from shared sequences and rare shared wording: the "pistic" nard (*nardou
pistikēs*, Mark 14:3 / John 12:3), the three hundred denarii, the feeding followed by the
walking on water, the interleaved denial. **The crux:** what literary use of a source
looks like. **Status:** the field is divided; state it as divided.

*Introduced:* [2.4](lessons/02-04-john-and-the-synoptics.md)

### The Word in the prologue

**What no background supplies is verse 14.** John 1:1, *(lesson's translation)*: "In the
beginning the Word already was; and the Word was turned toward God; and what God was,
the Word was." The imperfect *ēn* never says the Word came to be; *pros ton theon* is
"toward" or "in the presence of" God; *theos* without the article is the predicate. 1:14:
"the Word became flesh and pitched his tent among us" (*eskēnōsen*).

**Backgrounds (hypotheses):** Heraclitus and the Stoics (the cosmos's rational order);
Philo (God's instrument in creation; Dodd, *The Interpretation of the Fourth Gospel*,
1953); Wisdom, present at creation (Prov 8:22-23), "made all things with thy word" (Wis
9:1), told to dwell in Jacob (Sir 24:13; Brown put Wisdom first); Genesis 1 and the
Targums' *memra* (Daniel Boyarin). Most now treat the Jewish scriptures as primary.
Wisdom tents in Israel; nowhere does she **become flesh**.

**Levels:** Christ's divinity is rung 1, but no council defined the exegesis of 1:1.
The Trinitarian reading belongs to
[`trinity-and-god` 2.2](../trinity-and-god/lessons/02-02-the-father-and-the-son-in-the-new-testament.md).

*Introduced:* [2.5](lessons/02-05-the-theology-of-john.md)

### The I am sayings

**The predicate sayings say what he gives; the absolute ones say who he is.**

| Kind | Verse | Douay-Rheims |
|---|---|---|
| Predicate | 6:35 | I am the bread of life |
| Predicate | 8:12 | I am the light of the world |
| Predicate | 10:7, 10:11 | I am the door / the good shepherd |
| Predicate | 11:25 | I am the resurrection and the life |
| Predicate | 14:6 | I am the way, and the truth, and the life |
| Predicate | 15:1 | I am the true vine |
| Absolute | 8:24, 8:28, 13:19 | "I am he": no "he" in the Greek |
| Absolute | 18:5-8 | "I am he", and the party falls back (18:6) |
| Absolute | 8:58 | "before Abraham was made, I am": aorist *genesthai* against present *eimi* |

The predicate images are ones Israel applied to God or itself (manna, Ezekiel 34's
shepherd, Isaiah 5's vine). Most commentators hear Exodus 3:14 and Greek Isaiah (43:10)
behind the absolute sayings; the hearers take up stones (8:59). **Context decides:** the
healed blind man says the same two words, meaning "I'm the one" (9:9). Check the Greek
before arguing from the Douay's "he".

*Introduced:* [2.5](lessons/02-05-the-theology-of-john.md)

### The Jews in John

***Hoi Ioudaioi* occurs some seventy times in John, and its sense shifts.** Neutral ("the
pasch of the Jews", 2:13); Judeans (7:1; 11:18-19, 11:45); the authorities (1:19 sends
priests and Levites; 9:22, where Jews fear "the Jews" who can expel from the synagogue;
5:16); the hostile party (8:31-59, addressed to "those Jews who believed him" and then
told "You are of your father the devil", 8:44).

**Translation strategies (hypotheses):** Urban von Wahlde, most hostile uses mean the
authorities; Malcolm Lowe, later Steve Mason, *Ioudaios* was ethnic-geographic, so
"Judean"; Adele Reinhartz (*Cast Out of the Covenant*, 2018), keep "the Jews", face the
rhetoric, since "Judeans" writes Jews out of the story. 8:31-59 is Reinhartz's strongest
case.

**Levels:** [*Nostra Aetate* 4](#nostra-aetate-4-on-the-passion) (rung 3) governs how
John is preached, not how *Ioudaioi* is translated: 8:44 is polemic inside a
first-century argument among Jews and cannot become a charge against the Jewish people.

*Introduced:* [2.5](lessons/02-05-the-theology-of-john.md)

### The hour of glorification

**In John the cross is the exaltation.** "My hour is not yet come" (2:4) to "The hour is
come that the Son of man should be glorified" (12:23) to "Father, the hour is come.
Glorify thy Son" (17:1). *Hypsoō*, "lift up", means crucify and exalt (3:14, 8:28,
12:32-33, glossed "signifying what death he should die"). No agony in the garden, only
"Father, save me from this hour. But for this cause I came" (12:27). The last word is
*tetelestai* (19:30), then *paredōken to pneuma*, "he handed over the spirit".

*Introduced:* [2.5](lessons/02-05-the-theology-of-john.md)

### The purpose statement of John

**John 20:31 states the goal and leaves open whose faith.** "These are written, that you
may believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God: and that believing, you may have
life in his name." The manuscripts split on the tense:

- present *pisteuēte*, "that you may *go on* believing" (a book for believers): Sinaiticus's first hand, Vaticanus, Theta, 0250, probably P66;
- aorist *pisteusēte*, "that you may *come to* believe" (a book for converts): a corrector of Sinaiticus, A, C, D, L, W, Psi and most later manuscripts.

The critical text brackets the sigma, *pisteu[s]ēte*. D. A. Carson (*JBL*, 1987) reported
that most recent commentators preferred the present, then argued the tense cannot settle
the purpose, since John uses both tenses for coming to faith and staying in it (1:7,
11:15, 6:29). **Status:** textual and exegetical question; no Church teaching on it.

*Introduced:* [2.5](lessons/02-05-the-theology-of-john.md)

### John 6 as text

**Down to 6:50 the bread is received by believing; from 6:51 by eating.** At 6:54 the
verb shifts from *phagein* to *trōgō*, a blunt verb for chewing. Readings: Bultmann gave
6:51c-58 to a later "ecclesiastical redactor"; Brown read a sapiential first half and a
eucharistic second half, both in the finished gospel; a metaphorical reading appeals to
6:63. Trent declined to rule on the exegesis (Session XXI, ch. 1), whatever the
various interpretations of the Fathers. Eucharistic doctrine is ceded to
[`sacraments-and-liturgy`](../sacraments-and-liturgy/syllabus.md). The Douay runs one
ahead from 6:51b (6:51b-58 = DR 6:52-59).

*Introduced:* [2.5](lessons/02-05-the-theology-of-john.md)

## The historical Jesus

Three bins keep this module straight. **Near-consensus**: claims that survive every
serious method. **Live dispute**: the same evidence read differently by competent
historians. **Beyond the method**: claims history cannot reach at all ("Jesus had a
reputation as an exorcist" is bin 1; "he cast out demons by the Spirit of God" is bin 3).
Bin 3 is not failed bin 1. Every reconstruction is a **hypothesis**; the Church
**endorses none** and refuses only a split between the Jesus who lived and the Christ of
faith.

### The quests for the historical Jesus

**Each quest found a Jesus shaped by its assumption about the sources.**

| Phase | Figures | Assumption |
|---|---|---|
| Old quest | Reimarus (Wolfenbüttel Fragments, 1774-78): a failed political messiah, body stolen | the gospels are cover stories |
| | Strauss, *Das Leben Jesu* (1835-36): the miracles are myth | the gospel form itself is faith, not report |
| | Renan (1863); Harnack, *Das Wesen des Christentums* (1900): a teacher of God's fatherhood | a modern-congenial Jesus lies beneath the dogma |
| Objection | Kähler (1892): the Christ who shaped history is the preached Christ | the sources are proclamation all the way down |
| Verdict | Schweitzer, *Von Reimarus zu Wrede* (1906; Eng. *The Quest of the Historical Jesus*, 1910): each built Jesus in his own image; the real one is a strange apocalyptic figure | he belongs to his time, not ours |
| "No quest" | Bultmann, *Jesus* (1926): almost nothing knowable, and faith needs only the "that" | the gap is uncrossable and does not matter |
| New quest | Käsemann (Marburg lecture, 20 Oct 1953, pub. 1954); named by J. M. Robinson (1959) | same kerygmatic sources, but continuity is required |
| Third quest (from c. 1980) | Sanders, *Jesus and Judaism* (1985); Meier, *A Marginal Jew* (vol. 1, 1991); Wright, *Jesus and the Victory of God* (1996) | sources are faith documents *and* evidence; Jesus inside Second Temple Judaism; start from deeds |

**The deep-well image is Tyrrell's, not Schweitzer's.** George Tyrrell, *Christianity at
the Cross-Roads* (1909), on Harnack: the Christ Harnack sees is only the reflection of a
Liberal Protestant face at the bottom of a deep well (paraphrase). Tyrrell was a Catholic
modernist ([`church-history` 8.3](../church-history/lessons/08-03-leo-xiii-and-the-modernist-crisis.md));
quoting him is not quoting the magisterium.

**Caveat:** the three-quest scheme is itself a Wright-era convention that undercounts
English work between 1906 and 1953 (Dodd, T. W. Manson). **Test case:** run Mark 1:15 through
the phases (inner moral reign; imminent end, Matt 10:23; call to decision; Israel's
restoration); each reading is fixed by the phase's assumption before the verse is read.

*Introduced:* [3.1](lessons/03-01-the-quests.md)

### Real, historical and gospel Jesus

**Meier's three Jesuses** (*A Marginal Jew* vol. 1, 1991). The **real Jesus**: all he
was, said and did, most of it never written down. The **historical Jesus**: the modern
construct a historian can recover from public evidence by public methods. The **Jesus
of the gospels**: the Jesus the evangelists present, written in faith and for faith. A
historian's minimum does not claim that nothing more is true.

*Introduced:* [3.1](lessons/03-01-the-quests.md)

### Bultmann and Kasemann

**Teacher and pupil on whether faith needs the historical Jesus.** **Bultmann**: the
sources are the kerygma of communities (form criticism); we can know almost nothing of
Jesus's life and personality; faith needs only the bare *that* he came and was
crucified, and propping faith up with history is a search for security. **Käsemann**
(lecture to Bultmann's former students at Marburg, 20 October 1953; published 1954):
the gospels themselves insist that the exalted Lord *is* the earthly Jesus; cut the link
and the kerygma becomes a myth about a heavenly redeemer, a new docetism. Some sayings
are recoverable, chiefly by [dissimilarity](#dissimilarity-and-double-dissimilarity).
**The crux is theological**, not only historical: does faith require continuity?

*Introduced:* [3.1](lessons/03-01-the-quests.md)

### The Jesus Seminar

**Voting with beads.** Founded 1985 by Robert Funk. Red: Jesus said it; pink: probably
something like it; grey: not his words but close to his ideas; black: later tradition.
*The Five Gospels* (1993) printed the results: by its own count under a fifth of the
sayings red or pink. Its Jesus was mainly a non-apocalyptic sage. Wright classed it as a
revived new quest, not third quest. Critics: a vote tallies opinions rather than
weighing evidence. **Status:** hypothesis.

*Introduced:* [3.1](lessons/03-01-the-quests.md)

### Criteria of authenticity

**One-way tests for what the Church is unlikely to have invented.** Meier's primary list
(*A Marginal Jew* vol. 1, paraphrased):

| Criterion | Asks | Passing suggests | Failing does **not** show | Main weakness |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| [Multiple attestation](#multiple-attestation) | in two or more independent sources or forms? | early and widespread | that it is late | independence depends on the Synoptic hypothesis |
| [Embarrassment](#criterion-of-embarrassment) | did it trouble the Church? | not invented by the Church | that it was invented | we guess what embarrassed whom |
| Discontinuity ([dissimilarity](#dissimilarity-and-double-dissimilarity)) | unlike both Judaism and the later Church? | distinctive of Jesus | that it is not his | a Jesus cut off from Judaism and his followers |
| Coherence | fits what is established? | belongs with that material | anything | depends on the others |
| Rejection and execution | helps explain the cross? | part of why he was killed | anything | the cross has more than one explanation |

Secondary (support only): Aramaic traces, Palestinian setting, vividness. **Read the
fourth column first**: passing counts in an item's favour; failing proves nothing.

**The critique.** Chris Keith and Anthony Le Donne (eds.), *Jesus, Criteria, and the
Demise of Authenticity* (2012): the criteria try to peel away interpretation to reach a
bare fact, but every tradition reached us already interpreted by memory. Memory
approaches (Dunn, *Jesus Remembered*, 2003; Allison, *Constructing Jesus*, 2010) ask
what past best explains the way Jesus was remembered, looking for recurrent patterns.
The critics call saying-by-saying testing the wrong method, not the gospels unreliable.

**Worked cases:** *crucified under Pilate* passes multiple attestation (Mark, John, Paul,
Tacitus, probably Josephus), embarrassment (1 Cor 1:23; Gal 3:13) and rejection: as
secure as anything in ancient history, but the verdict does not reach "for our sins".
*Son of Man*: strong attestation, passes dissimilarity from the Church (only Acts 7:56
outside the gospels), fails dissimilarity from Judaism (Daniel 7:13; Vermes's Aramaic
idiom for "I"); most conclude Jesus used the expression; the criteria cannot settle what
he meant.

**Status:** scholarly tools with no magisterial standing; no Church document endorses or
condemns one. Dei Verbum 19 endorses no criterion's verdicts, and no verdict overturns it.

*Introduced:* [3.2](lessons/03-02-sources-and-criteria.md); used at [3.3](lessons/03-03-what-historians-broadly-agree-on.md)

### Multiple attestation

**Several independent sources or literary forms report it.** Sources: Mark, Q, M, L,
John, Paul, and outsiders (Josephus, Tacitus). **Independence depends on your Synoptic
hypothesis**: on Markan priority, Matthew copying Mark is one witness, not two. Example
(Two-Source): Jesus eating with sinners in Mark 2:16, Q (Luke 7:34) and L (Luke 15:2) is
three independent witnesses. **The Twelve**: Mark, John, the saying behind Matt 19:28,
and 1 Cor 15:5.

*Introduced:* [3.2](lessons/03-02-sources-and-criteria.md)

### Criterion of embarrassment

**The Church would not invent what made trouble for it.** Cases: the crucifixion ("unto
the Jews indeed a stumblingblock", 1 Cor 1:23; the curse of Deut 21:23 answered in Gal
3:13); Mark 3:21, where his own people say "He is become mad", which Matthew and Luke omit; "a
friend of publicans and sinners" (Luke 7:34); the betrayer "one of the twelve" (Mark
14:10); the Greek "the twelve" kept after Judas's defection (1 Cor 15:5). Hostile
concession works similarly: Mark 3:22's opponents explain the exorcisms, not deny them.

**Weakness:** we guess what embarrassed whom. **Double-edged case:** women as first tomb
witnesses (Mark 16; John 20) are read as passing embarrassment, given the low standing
of women's testimony (Josephus, *Ant.* 4.8.15); others read their absence from 1 Cor 15's
list as a sign that the tomb stories came later. The same fact is argued both ways.

*Introduced:* [3.2](lessons/03-02-sources-and-criteria.md); used at [3.4](lessons/03-04-the-passion-and-resurrection-narratives.md)

### Dissimilarity and double dissimilarity

**Unlike both contemporary Judaism and the later Church.** Made central by Käsemann
(1953) and Norman Perrin (1967). Applied strictly it removes Jesus from Judaism and from
his own followers, leaving an oddity, not a first-century Jew (Morna Hooker's early
critique). Theissen and Winter's replacement, **historical plausibility**: Jesus should
fit his Jewish context and explain what came after him. The Son of Man is the case where
it strains: it passes against the Church and fails against Judaism.

*Introduced:* [3.2](lessons/03-02-sources-and-criteria.md)

### The Testimonium Flavianum

**Josephus's disputed mention of Jesus**, *Antiquities* 18.3.3. **Three schools:** wholly
authentic; wholly interpolated; **partly authentic** (the majority: a Christian hand
touched up a genuine note, and Meier reconstructs such a core). Origen (*Against Celsus*
1.47) says Josephus did not believe Jesus was the Christ, which tells against a wholly
authentic text as we have it. Many who cut the Christian additions keep the phrase "a
doer of wonderful works" as Josephus's own. The phrase-by-phrase reading is reserved
boss work and is not set out here. **Status:** hypothesis.

*Introduced:* [3.2](lessons/03-02-sources-and-criteria.md)

### Josephus on James

**Josephus's second mention of Jesus, widely accepted as genuine.** *Antiquities* 20.9.1
(20.200): in 62, with Festus dead and Albinus "but upon the road", the high priest
Ananus, a Sadducee, had "the brother of Jesus, who was called Christ, whose name was
James" stoned (Whiston); Agrippa deposed Ananus after three months and made Jesus son
of Damneus high priest.

The Greek, *ton adelphon Iēsou tou legomenou Christou, Iakōbos onoma autō*: "called
Christ" is an outsider's label (a Christian would write "the Lord"); it distinguishes
this Jesus from the son of Damneus; and it seems to presuppose an earlier mention (the
Testimonium). Origen read it. **Minority:** Richard Carrier (*Journal of Early
Christian Studies*, 2012) argues "who was called Christ" began as a scribe's marginal
note, making James the brother of Jesus son of Damneus. It also dates the death of James
the Lord's brother, traditional author of the letter ([6.3](lessons/06-03-james-faith-and-works.md)).

*Introduced:* [3.2](lessons/03-02-sources-and-criteria.md); used at [6.3](lessons/06-03-james-faith-and-works.md)

### Outside witnesses to Jesus

**Short list, all weighed by historians, none the ground of faith.** Tacitus (*Annals*
15.44, c. 115-120): "Christus, from whom the name had its origin, suffered the extreme
penalty during the reign of Tiberius at the hands of one of our procurators, Pontius
Pilatus" (Church and Brodribb) — confirms execution, governor, reign; the source of his
information is unknown; "procurator" is anachronistic. Suetonius (*Claudius* 25.4):
"Chrestus", probably Christ ([Claudius and Chrestus](#claudius-and-chrestus)). Pliny
(*Letters* 10.96, c. 112): Christians sing a hymn to Christ as to a god (evidence about
Christians). Josephus: [the Testimonium](#the-testimonium-flavianum) and
[James](#josephus-on-james). Tacitus 15.44 as evidence for Nero is weighed in
[`church-history` 1.1](../church-history/lessons/01-01-the-first-generation-and-its-sources.md).

*Introduced:* [3.2](lessons/03-02-sources-and-criteria.md)

### Sanders list of secure facts

**Start from what is hardest to deny.** E. P. Sanders, *Jesus and Judaism* (1985), began
from deeds and listed eight facts he judged almost beyond dispute (paraphrased). Jesus:

1. was baptized by John;
2. was a Galilean who preached and healed;
3. called disciples and spoke of there being twelve;
4. confined his work to Israel;
5. was involved in a controversy about the Temple;
6. was crucified outside Jerusalem by the Roman authorities;

and after his death

7. his followers continued as an identifiable movement;
8. at least some Jews persecuted at least parts of it (Gal 1:13).

**Commonly added:** a reputation as healer and exorcist, with hostile concession (Mark
3:22; Josephus's "doer of wonderful works"), and Meier's conclusion that Jesus did deeds
he and others took as miracles, whether they *were* being beyond history; eating with
"publicans and sinners" (the fact agreed, the scandal disputed: Sanders's offer without
prior repentance vs Dunn and others).

**Rule:** read across, never up. Each fact has a live dispute beside it (what the Twelve
meant; what the Temple act meant; why he was crucified) and some items have a bin-3
counterpart (that the kingdom is God's reign in Christ; that the death was for sins; that
he rose). **Status:** historians' consensus, no magisterial standing either way.
"Crucified under Pilate" is also creedal dogma, held on apostolic witness, not because
Tacitus confirms it.

*Introduced:* [3.3](lessons/03-03-what-historians-broadly-agree-on.md)

### The eschatological prophet reading

**The biggest live dispute: what did Jesus expect God to do next?** The two verbs:
*ēngiken*, "has come near" (Mark 1:15) and *ephthasen*, "has arrived upon you" (Luke
11:20). Dodd (1935) made the kingdom already arrived; most exegetes now keep both,
future and breaking in.

- **Eschatological prophet** (the majority, not a proof): Schweitzer (1906); Sanders; Dale Allison (*Jesus of Nazareth: Millenarian Prophet*, 1998); Bart Ehrman (*Jesus: Apocalyptic Prophet of the New Millennium*, 1999). Allison's **sandwich**: John before Jesus preached imminent judgment; the movement after him, Paul included (1 Thess 4:15-17), expected the Lord soon; the simplest bridge is an eschatological Jesus. Sayings: Mark 9:1 [DR 8:39], 14:25.
- **Non-eschatological sage**: John Dominic Crossan (*The Historical Jesus*, 1991), Marcus Borg, most of the Jesus Seminar; a kingdom present in how people live; Jesus broke with John. Showpiece: Luke 17:21, *entos hymōn*.
- **Restoration prophet**: N. T. Wright (*Jesus and the Victory of God*, 1996): imminent crisis, but the cosmic language is Jewish metaphor for events inside history (exile's end, God's return to Zion, the Temple's fall, Jesus's vindication).
- **Meier**: a future kingdom central; the deadline sayings (Mark 9:1, 13:30, Matt 10:23) more probably church formulations.

**"Jesus was mistaken"** needs two further disputed premises: the deadline sayings
authentic *and* read literally. [*Lamentabili*](#lamentabili-on-the-immediate-coming)
bars the flat dilemma, not the history. How the Church reads Jesus's words on "the day"
(Mark 13:32) is owned by [`christology` 4.2](../christology/lessons/04-02-christs-human-knowledge-the-classical-account.md).

*Introduced:* [3.3](lessons/03-03-what-historians-broadly-agree-on.md)

### The passion narratives

**The four agree more here than anywhere, and details still move.** Mark 14:53-64: the
whole council at night, false witnesses on the Temple saying, the high priest's
question, a death verdict, then a morning consultation (15:1); Matthew follows, naming
Caiaphas. Luke: the council at daybreak (22:66), no witnesses, no Temple charge, no
formal verdict; Jesus sent to Herod (23:7-11). John: questioned by Annas (18:13, 19),
a one-line transfer to Caiaphas (18:24), the decision taken back at 11:47-53.

- **Night trial.** The rabbinic rules against night capital trials are in the Mishnah (c. 200) and may not describe practice under Caiaphas. Many (Raymond Brown's *The Death of the Messiah* is the standard study) judge some Jewish proceeding lay behind the handing-over, but doubt Mark's formal night trial; others defend Mark's outline.
- **The sentence was Roman.** Only the prefect could crucify; John 18:31: "It is not lawful for us to put any man to death."
- **Pilate grows less guilty** from Mark (15:14) through Matthew (the dream, the handwashing) and Luke (three times no cause, 23:4, 14, 22) to John (three times, 18:38; 19:4, 6). The trend is exegetical; the motive (writing inside the Empire) is a hypothesis.
- **Matthew 27:25**, "His blood be upon us and upon our children": *laos*, the crowd the chief priests had talked round (27:20), a formula accepting responsibility; "our children" often read as the generation that saw 70. [*Nostra Aetate* 4](#nostra-aetate-4-on-the-passion) rules out the inference to a people's guilt across time.

**Levels:** Dei Verbum 19 canonizes neither Mark's chronology nor Luke's.

*Introduced:* [3.4](lessons/03-04-the-passion-and-resurrection-narratives.md)

### The 1 Corinthians 15 formula

**The oldest datable piece of Christian tradition.** 1 Cor 15:3-8: received and handed
on (*parelabon* / *paredōka*): that Christ died for our sins according to the
scriptures; that he was buried; that he was raised (*egēgertai*, perfect passive) the
third day according to the scriptures; that he appeared (*ōphthē*) to Cephas, then the
Twelve (the Greek *tois dōdeka*; the Douay's "the eleven" follows the Latin), then more
than five hundred at once, then James, then all the apostles, and last to Paul.

**Why early:** Paul says he received it; its language is not his usual idiom ("the
Twelve" nowhere else in his letters); built of "that" clauses; Paul wrote in the
mid-50s and spent fifteen days with Cephas three years after his call (Gal 1:18). Most
date the core (vv. 3-5) to the **30s**: a strong majority, not a certainty.

**Debated:** (a) does "buried ... raised" imply an empty tomb (the tomb is never
mentioned)? (b) no women, though the gospels make them first witnesses. **The crux in
the text:** the same *ōphthē* for Paul as for Cephas: Wright reads one kind of real
seeing; Lüdemann visions all round.

**Readings (paraphrased):** N. T. Wright (*The Resurrection of the Son of God*): for Second
Temple Jews "resurrection" meant a body; only an empty tomb plus appearances explains
the word. Dale Allison (*Resurrecting Jesus*): the appearances were real experiences, the
tomb tradition weighty, the cause beyond historical method. Gerd Lüdemann (*The
Resurrection of Jesus*): Peter's vision from grief and guilt, Paul's a conversion vision;
the body decayed. Bart Ehrman (*How Jesus Became God*): some disciples had visions and
sincerely believed; doubts an honourable burial in a known tomb. The historical case
itself is ceded to [`apologetics-foundations`](../apologetics-foundations/syllabus.md);
the four-gospel tomb comparison is reserved boss work.

**Levels:** that Christ died, was buried and rose is **defined dogma** (the Creeds; what it
requires of the risen body is [`christology` 6.2](../christology/lessons/06-02-resurrection-and-ascension-as-doctrine.md));
the Catechism calls the empty tomb no direct proof (CCC 640). The formula's date, what it
implies, and every reading above are hypotheses.

*Introduced:* [3.4](lessons/03-04-the-passion-and-resurrection-narratives.md)

### Handing on tradition

**"I received ... I handed on": Paul's formula for fixed tradition.** *Parelabon* /
*paredōka* at 1 Cor 11:23 (the Supper, a formula closer to Luke 22 than to Mark 14, so
already fixed before the mid-50s) and 15:3 (the creed). At 11:23 Paul "handed on"
(*paredōka*) the account of the night Jesus "was being handed over" (*paredideto*). Luke
1:2's *paredosan* is the same verb. The pair is how scholars identify pre-Pauline
tradition and date it early.

*Introduced:* [3.4](lessons/03-04-the-passion-and-resurrection-narratives.md); used at [4.3](lessons/04-03-paul-at-work.md), [1.5](lessons/01-05-from-oral-tradition-to-written-gospel.md)

## Acts and Paul

**Letters first, Acts second, Gallio for the calendar** (the working rule usually credited
to John Knox, *Chapters in a Life of Paul*, 1950). Acts' canonicity and inspiration are
**defined**; its genre, speeches, "we", date and author are **hypotheses**; the 1913
response on Acts is a weight case.

### The genre of Acts

**What shelf Acts sits on fixes what kind of accuracy to expect.** Majority: ancient
historiography (Colin Hemer, *The Book of Acts in the Setting of Hellenistic History*:
checkable in detail; Gregory Sterling, *Historiography and Self-Definition*: "apologetic
historiography", a people's story told to define and defend it). Minority: Richard Pervo
(*Profit with Delight*): closest to the ancient novel, built to edify and entertain with
shipwrecks, escapes and riots. **Structure:** Acts 1:8 is the table of contents
(Jerusalem, 1-7; Judea and Samaria, 8-12; to the ends of the earth, 13-28), and the book
ends with Paul preaching in Rome "without prohibition" (28:31), not with his death.
Pentecost (Acts 2) is the programme in miniature (Joel's "after this" becomes "in the
last days", 2:17). Date usually 80-90, minorities earlier and later. **Status:**
hypothesis.

*Introduced:* [4.1](lessons/04-01-acts-as-history.md)

### The Thucydidean convention

**Ancient historians wrote the speeches.** Thucydides 1.22 (Crawley): "my habit has been
to make the speakers say what was in my opinion demanded of them by the various
occasions, of course adhering as closely as possible to the general sense of what they
really said." Martin Dibelius read Acts' speeches (about a third of the book) this way:
one pattern and style whether Peter or Paul speaks. F. F. Bruce and Hemer: Luke's
wording, often early substance. **Agreed:** not transcripts. **Disputed:** how much
remembered content lies under Luke's composition. Dei Verbum 12's attention to literary
forms points here; asking what an ancient speech asserts is the Church's method, not an
evasion.

*Introduced:* [4.1](lessons/04-01-acts-as-history.md)

### The we passages

**Three stretches where the narrator says "we" without saying who.** Acts 16:10-17,
20:5-21:18, 27:1-28:16 (the Western text adds 11:28). Detail thickens where "we" begins
(Troas to Samothrace to Neapolis to Philippi, "a colony") and the narrator drops out
when only Paul and Silas are arrested (16:19).

- **(a) The author was there.** Irenaeus (*Against Heresies* III.14.1): Luke "was inseparable from Paul, and his fellow-labourer in the Gospel"; Fitzmyer (a sometime companion); Brown (a minor companion, fewest difficulties).
- **(b) A source**: a travel diary, "we" and all.
- **(c) A literary device**: Vernon Robbins's sea-voyage convention; Pervo's claim to participation. Strains when "we" persists on dry land (16:13-15).

"We" is not a signature; the identification with Luke is Irenaeus's, not the text's.
**Status:** hypotheses.

*Introduced:* [4.1](lessons/04-01-acts-as-history.md)

### External checks on Acts

**A checked setting is not a checked event, still less a checked speech.**

| Claim in Acts | Outside evidence | Confirms | Cannot reach |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sergius Paulus, proconsul (*anthypatos*) of Cyprus (13:7) | a senatorial province; a Lucius Sergius Paullus at Rome (identification debated) | the title, possibly the man | his conversion |
| *Politarchai* at Thessalonica (17:6, 8) | Macedonian inscriptions | local terminology | the riot at Jason's house |
| *Asiarchai* at Ephesus (19:31) | the title | the setting | the riot |
| The Areopagus (17:19, 22) | the council still sat in Roman Athens | the institution | the speech |
| Gallio, proconsul of Achaia (18:12) | the Delphi inscription, c. 51-52 | office and approximate date | the hearing |
| Theudas, then Judas "after this man" (5:36-37) | Josephus, *Ant.* 20.5.1: Theudas c. 44-46 under Fadus, a decade *after* Gamaliel's speech; Judas c. 6 | **tension** | |

**Theudas:** readings are Luke erred (majority; by convention the slip is Luke's, not
Gamaliel's), a different earlier Theudas (Whiston's note suggests one of the unnamed
rebels), or Josephus erred (unevidenced). The check that confirms Luke's titles indicts
him here; you cannot keep one result and refuse the other. "Archaeology proves Acts"
and "Theudas disproves Acts" overreach alike. A good novelist could get titles right
too (Pervo against Hemer).

### Paul's chronology anchors

**A chain of intervals with one peg.** From Galatians: call near Damascus; Arabia; back
to Damascus; "after three years" fifteen days with Cephas (*historēsai Kēphan*), seeing
only James besides; Syria and Cilicia; "after fourteen years" Jerusalem again with
Barnabas and Titus (Gal 1:15-2:1). The text is an argument sworn to (1:20), not a
diary: first-hand is not disinterested.

- **Floor:** the Damascus escape under Aretas IV's governor (2 Cor 11:32-33), before c. 40.
- **Peg:** [Gallio](#the-gallio-inscription), 51-52.
- **Soft corroboration:** Claudius's expulsion (Acts 18:2), c. 49 disputed.
- **Letters placed:** 1 Thessalonians from Corinth c. 50-51, probably the oldest Christian writing; Galatians, 1-2 Corinthians mid-50s; Romans c. 57-58 before the collection trip (Rom 15:25-26); Philippians and Philemon from an Ephesian imprisonment (mid-50s) or Rome (c. 60).

What the "fourteen years" count from, inclusive counting, and the identification of
Gal 2 with an Acts visit are reserved boss work. **Acts' Paul and the letters' Paul**
diverge: Acts 9:26-27 has him presented to the apostles straight from Damascus; Acts
names Gamaliel (22:3), has him circumcise Timothy (16:3), calls him apostle only in
passing (14:4, 14 [DR 14:13]), never mentions letters. Philipp Vielhauer (essay on the
"Paulinism" of Acts, c. 1950): too different for Luke to have been a companion; Fitzmyer
and others: a historian simplifies. Knox's rule: on Paul's movements, the letters win.
**Status:** history, no doctrinal level.

*Introduced:* [4.2](lessons/04-02-pauls-life-and-letters.md)

### Undisputed and disputed letters

**Thirteen letters carry Paul's name; scholars sort them by confidence of authorship.**

| Group | Letters | Critical opinion (Brown's survey) |
|---|---|---|
| **Undisputed (7)** | Romans, 1 Corinthians, 2 Corinthians, Galatians, Philippians, 1 Thessalonians, Philemon | nearly all accept |
| Disputed | 2 Thessalonians | roughly divided |
| Disputed | Colossians | roughly divided |
| Disputed | Ephesians | leans clearly against |
| Disputed (the pastorals) | 1 Timothy, 2 Timothy, Titus | heavily against |
| Not claimed | Hebrews | never names Paul ([6.2](lessons/06-02-hebrews.md)) |

**Tests:** vocabulary and style; theology (believers already risen in Colossians and
Ephesians; settled offices in the pastorals); setting. **Replies:** secretaries (Rom
16:22, Tertius), development, new occasions.

**Levels:** the canonicity of all thirteen (with Hebrews, Trent's "fourteen epistles of
Paul the apostle") is **defined, rung 1**; human authorship of any disputed letter is a
**hypothesis**; whether Trent's titles bind on authorship is **freely disputed** (Brown:
books, not authors). "Undisputed" measures historical confidence, not doctrinal weight;
it is a modern critical label, not the ancient *antilegomena*.

*Introduced:* [4.2](lessons/04-02-pauls-life-and-letters.md); used at [4.3](lessons/04-03-paul-at-work.md), [5.1](lessons/05-01-galatians-faith-law-and-the-gentiles.md), [6.1](lessons/06-01-the-disputed-pauline-letters-and-the-pastorals.md)

### The Pauline letter form

**Paul adapts the ordinary Greek letter; deviations are signals.**

1. **Prescript**: sender, addressees, greeting; the stock *chairein* becomes "grace and peace".
2. **Thanksgiving** (*eucharistoumen*, "we give thanks"): usually previews the themes.
3. **Body.**
4. **Paraenesis**: moral exhortation.
5. **Closing**: news, greetings, blessing, sometimes Paul's own hand.

**Galatians has no thanksgiving**: "I wonder that you are so soon removed" (1:6) stands
in the slot, and the self-description swells into a defence (1:1). Gal 6:11 is "with
what large letters" (*pēlikois grammasin*), not the Douay's "what a letter": Paul taking
the pen. Philemon 1-4 is the complete form, the thanksgiving previewing the request.
Revelation opens with the same "grace ... and peace" (1:4).

*Introduced:* [4.2](lessons/04-02-pauls-life-and-letters.md)

### Mirror-reading

**Rebuilding the community's question from Paul's answer.** Indispensable and risky.
Safest where Paul says what he was told ("by them that are of the house of Chloe", 1 Cor
1:11) or quotes the question back ("How do the dead rise again?", 15:35). John Barclay's
essay on Galatians warns that not every sentence implies an opponent.

| Church | Problem (mirror-read) | Paul's answer | Word that carries it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Thessalonica | the dead will miss the Lord's arrival | the dead rise first; nobody gets ahead (1 Thess 4:13-18 [DR 4:12-17]) | *phthasōmen*, *apantēsis* |
| Corinth (1) | a status contest among teachers (1:12) | the cross reverses status (1:18, 26) | *logos tou staurou* |
| Corinth (2) | the rich eat, the poor go hungry (11:21) | recite the Supper tradition | *parelabon / paredōka* |
| Corinth (3) | "no resurrection of the dead"; "what body?" | seed and plant; a body animated by the Spirit | *sōma pneumatikon* |
| Philippi | rivalry (2:3-5) | Christ emptied, God exalted | *Kyrios*, Isaiah 45:23 |

**Did Paul expect to survive to the parousia?** Most critical scholars say yes ("we who
are alive"); the best case against reads the "we" as a category (5:2, "as a thief in the
night"). The Douay's 1 Cor 15:51, following the Vulgate, reads differently from the
Greek. The 1915 response ([early responses](#the-early-biblical-commission-responses))
is a weight case; "Paul taught an error" collides with inerrancy as taught. Erik
Peterson's argument that *apantēsis* implies a return to earth with the Lord is
contested.

*Introduced:* [4.3](lessons/04-03-paul-at-work.md)

### Spiritual body

**All agree Paul says "body"; they dispute what "spiritual" says about it.** 1 Cor 15:44,
"It is sown a natural body: it shall rise a spiritual body": *sōma psychikon* / *sōma
pneumatikon*, a "soul-type" and a "spirit-type" body; *psychikon* echoes Adam made "a
living soul" (15:45, Gen 2:7). N. T. Wright: *-ikos* adjectives name what **animates** a
thing, so a body driven by the Spirit. Dale Martin, Troels Engberg-Pedersen: in ancient
physics *pneuma* was a fine material stuff, so a body **made of** spirit-matter. The
Corinthian deniers accepted Christ's resurrection and balked at a revived corpse (15:12,
35).

**Levels:** bodily resurrection of the dead is **defined dogma** (owner
[`creation-grace-last-things`](../creation-grace-last-things/syllabus.md)); what a
*sōma pneumatikon* is made of is **freely disputed**. "Spiritual body" does not mean no
body.

*Introduced:* [4.3](lessons/04-03-paul-at-work.md)

### The Philippians hymn

**Philippians 2:6-11: Christ the subject of the descent, God the subject of the
exaltation.** Since Ernst Lohmeyer (1928) many take it as a pre-Pauline hymn, with "even
the death of the cross" (2:8) as Paul's addition breaking the rhythm; Gordon Fee
answered that it is elevated Pauline prose. Structure either way: 2:6-8 Christ empties
himself; 2:9-11 God exalts him. *Harpagmos* (2:6): a prize not yet held, to be seized; a
possession clung to; or (after Roy Hoover's idiom study) something to exploit for
advantage. **Isaiah 45:23**: in Greek Isaiah God says that to him "every knee shall bend";
Paul quotes it of God at Rom 14:11 with "every tongue shall confess" (the Hebrew, and the
Greek in Brenton's edition, have "swear"); the verse before is "I am God, and there is no
other". Phil 2:10-11 gives the bending and confessing to "the name of Jesus" and
*Kyrios Iēsous Christos*.

**Levels:** Christ's divinity is defined; the doctrine of the emptying belongs to
[`christology` 3.3](../christology/lessons/03-03-kenosis.md); whether the text is
pre-Pauline is a hypothesis. An early date does not prove the dogma, and a late one would
not refute it.

*Introduced:* [4.3](lessons/04-03-paul-at-work.md)

### Early high Christology

**How soon, and in what sense, was Jesus worshipped?** Nobody disputes the texts are
early; the dispute is what they show (all paraphrased):

- **Larry Hurtado** (*Lord Jesus Christ*): within two decades, a pattern of devotion without parallel for any other figure (hymns, invocation *Marana tha* 1 Cor 16:22, baptism in his name, his Supper, "Lord"): a mutation inside Jewish monotheism.
- **Richard Bauckham** (*Jesus and the God of Israel*): monotheism defined God by unique identity as sole Creator and Ruler; Phil 2 puts Jesus inside it with Isaiah's most monotheistic text; the earliest Christology was the highest. He reads Revelation 5 the same way (the Lamb worshipped with the one on the throne, while the angel refuses worship, 19:10, 22:9).
- **James Dunn**: an Adam Christology ("form of God" echoing "image of God") need not imply pre-existence (*Christology in the Making*); worship went to God *through* Jesus (*Did the First Christians Worship Jesus?*).
- **Bart Ehrman** (*How Jesus Became God*): a pre-existent but lesser heavenly being, exalted higher.

**Status:** hypotheses, no magisterial standing either way.

*Introduced:* [4.3](lessons/04-03-paul-at-work.md); Revelation at [6.5](lessons/06-05-revelation.md)

## Justification vocabulary

Galatians and Romans in their own Greek. **What is defined** (Trent, Session VI; the
decree is owned by [`creation-grace-last-things`](../creation-grace-last-things/syllabus.md)):
no one is justified by works done through the Law without grace (canon 1); justification
is gratuitous and faith its beginning (ch. 8); its formal cause is the justice of God by
which he makes us just (ch. 7); not by the *sole* imputation of Christ's justice (canon
11); "faith alone" condemned in the sense that nothing else co-operates (canon 9); good
works preserve and increase justice received (canon 24). **What is not defined:** the
grammar of any phrase below. Trent borrowing a phrase is not Trent exegeting a verse.
Every reading of *erga nomou*, *pistis Christou*, *dikaiosynē theou* and *hilastērion*,
and every Perspective, is **freely disputed** (rung 5), with Catholic scholars on each
side. The doctrinal dispute over justification and imputation is ceded to
[`apologetics-catholic-claims`](../apologetics-catholic-claims/syllabus.md) and
[`creation-grace-last-things`](../creation-grace-last-things/syllabus.md).

### The Antioch incident

**The first dispute was over a menu, and Paul saw a gospel in it.** Galatians 2:11-14:
at Antioch Jewish and Gentile believers ate together, Cephas too; when "some came from
James" he withdrew, "fearing them who were of the circumcision", and the other Jewish
believers and Barnabas followed. Paul calls it *hypokrisis*, play-acting, and charges
that Peter "compels" (*anankazeis*) the Gentiles to live as Jews: full table fellowship
now costs Jewish food practice.

**Two loose ends:** where Paul's speech to Peter ends (2:14, or 2:21 with 2:15-21 as its
core), and who won; Paul never says. Many, Dunn among them, infer Paul lost: an argument
from silence. **Patristic reading:** Jerome read the clash as staged; Augustine refused,
because a staged rebuke puts a lie in Scripture ([`patristics` 4.2](../patristics/lessons/04-02-jerome.md)).
The Jerusalem agreement just before (2:1-10) and its relation to Acts 15 are
[`church-history` 1.1](../church-history/lessons/01-01-the-first-generation-and-its-sources.md)'s
event and reserved boss work.

*Introduced:* [5.1](lessons/05-01-galatians-faith-law-and-the-gentiles.md)

### Works of the law

***Erga nomou*: three times in Galatians 2:16; again at Romans 3:20 and 3:28.** Two main
readings:

- **Traditional**: the deeds the Law commands, and behind them any human doing offered as grounds for standing with God. Its best evidence: Gal 3:10, the curse on whoever does not keep "all things which are written in the book of the law"; Rom 4:4-5, wages against gift; Phil 3:9.
- **Boundary markers** (James D. G. Dunn, 1982 Manson lecture, published 1983): the Law's practices in their role as markers of Jewish identity: circumcision, food laws, sabbath. Its best evidence: Antioch, literally a dispute about food and circumcision. Dunn later let the phrase cover the whole Law, with the markers as the points where it bit.

**Background texts:** 4QMMT uses the Hebrew equivalent ("some of the works of the Law")
for sectarian rulings; both sides cite it. The positions of Sanders, Wright, Barclay and
the apocalyptic readers are under [New Perspective on Paul](#new-perspective-on-paul).

**James's "works" are not these.** James's examples are clothing and feeding, Abraham's
offering, Rahab's hiding of the spies; never circumcision, food or sabbath
([6.3](lessons/06-03-james-faith-and-works.md)). The Douay's note on Rom 3:28 limits Paul's excluded works to those
"antecedent to the faith of Christ".

**Weight-free witnesses:** Benedict XVI's general audience of 19 November 2008 described
the works of the Law as circumcision, food and purity rules and sabbath (a catechesis;
it shows the view is at home in papal teaching and defines nothing). Challoner's note on
Rom 10:3 takes the doing reading (no magisterial weight: a Catholic Bible's note is not
Church teaching).

*Introduced:* [5.1](lessons/05-01-galatians-faith-law-and-the-gentiles.md); used at [5.2](lessons/05-02-romans-1-4-the-righteousness-of-god.md), [5.4](lessons/05-04-the-new-perspective-on-paul.md), [6.3](lessons/06-03-james-faith-and-works.md)

### Pistis Christou

**"The faith of Jesus Christ"** (Gal 2:16 twice; Rom 3:22, 3:26; Phil 3:9). The Douay
keeps the Vulgate's literal *fidem Iesu Christi*, preserving the ambiguity most modern
translations resolve.

- **Objective genitive**: Christ is the object of faith, "faith *in* Christ". Defended by Dunn; among Catholics, Fitzmyer. Appeals to Gal 2:16's middle clause, "we also believe in Christ Jesus", where the believing is plainly ours.
- **Subjective genitive**: Christ is the subject, "the faith*fulness* of Christ", his obedience unto death. Richard Hays (*The Faith of Jesus Christ*, 1983), from the story Galatians 3-4 tells; among Catholics, Luke Timothy Johnson. Replies that on the objective reading 2:16 says the same thing three times.

On the subjective reading human faith stays, but moves: we are justified by Christ's
fidelity, received by trusting him. **Status:** freely disputed (rung 5); Trent defines
that justification is by grace, not the genitive.

*Introduced:* [5.1](lessons/05-01-galatians-faith-law-and-the-gentiles.md); used at [5.2](lessons/05-02-romans-1-4-the-righteousness-of-god.md)

### Abraham and the promise

**Abraham was reckoned righteous by believing, before the Law.** Galatians 3:6-18: "Abraham
believed God: and it was reputed to him unto justice" (Gen 15:6); those "of faith" are his
children; "In thee shall all nations be blessed" (Gen 12:3) was about Gentiles from the
start; the Law, 430 years later, cannot annul a ratified promise (3:15-17), exploiting
*diathēkē* as both covenant and last will. **The singular "seed"** (3:16): "not ... seeds as
of many ... but as of one ... which is Christ." Hebrew *zera'* and Greek *sperma* are
collective, and Paul knows it (3:29 makes all the baptized the seed), so it is a
recognized Jewish exegetical move, not a grammatical proof in the modern sense. Before
this, 3:1-5: the Galatians received the Spirit by "the hearing of faith", before any
circumcision. The rival teachers' case was not crazy: circumcision was the covenant's
sign (Gen 17). [`old-testament`](../old-testament/syllabus.md) reads Abraham from the
other side.

*Introduced:* [5.1](lessons/05-01-galatians-faith-law-and-the-gentiles.md)

### The Law as paidagogos

**A guardian for a minor, and minority ends.** Galatians 3:24, "the law was our pedagogue
in Christ". The *paidagōgos* was not a teacher but the household slave who walked a boy
to school and back, watched his conduct, and was dismissed when the boy came of age.
*Eis Christon* (Douay "in Christ") is temporal ("until Christ") or purposive ("to lead us
to Christ"); the before/after frame of 3:23-25 ("kept under the law shut up",
*ephrouroumetha*; "no longer under a pedagogue") makes the time limit the point either
way. The Law was "set because of transgressions, until the seed should come" (3:19);
"Was the law then against the promises of God: God forbid!" (3:21): temporary, not
wrong. The Douay's note glosses "schoolmaster", which misleads. **Status:** philology,
settled by evidence, not by any level.

*Introduced:* [5.1](lessons/05-01-galatians-faith-law-and-the-gentiles.md)

### Righteousness of God

***Dikaiosynē theou*, "the justice of God" in the Douay** (Rom 1:17; 3:5; 3:21-26). Greek
uses one word family where English splits into justice, righteousness and justify
(*dikaiosynē*, *dikaios*, *dikaioō*). Three readings, each with a verse the others must
explain away:

- **Attribute**: God's own uprightness; in N. T. Wright's version, his covenant faithfulness. Leans on 3:3-5, where God's justice stands beside his faithfulness (*pistis tou theou*) and truth. **Romans 3:5 is clearly this reading**, and nobody disputes it.
- **Gift**: a righteous standing from God, given to faith (Bultmann, *Theology of the New Testament*). Leans on Phil 3:9, "the justice which is of God" (*tēn ek theou dikaiosynēn*). Augustine: "not that whereby He is Himself righteous, but that with which He endows man when He justifies the ungodly" (*On the Spirit and the Letter* 15, NPNF); Luther said he found his reading confirmed there.
- **Saving action**: the gift that carries its Giver's power, God reclaiming creation (Ernst Käsemann, 1961 essay). Leans on the parallel "revealed" of 1:17 and 1:18 (wrath is God's own act).

**3:26 joins them**: God acts "that he himself may be just and the justifier", his own
righteousness shown in making others righteous; many refuse to pick one. **Romans 1:17 is
the contested case**: "revealed" pulls toward act or attribute; "from faith unto faith"
and Habakkuk 2:4 toward the gift. **Luther's story:** late in life he told of hating the
phrase as punishing justice until he read it as the righteousness God gives; the date of
that breakthrough is disputed (1513 to 1519 have defenders).

**Levels:** Trent VI ch. 7 uses Augustine's formula for the *formal cause of
justification*; no act defines the grammar of 1:17. "The Church teaches *dikaiosynē
theou* in 1:17 means infused righteousness" is a level error.

*Introduced:* [5.2](lessons/05-02-romans-1-4-the-righteousness-of-god.md); used at [5.3](lessons/05-03-romans-5-11-adam-spirit-israel.md)

### Hilasterion

**Romans 3:25: God "hath proposed" Christ as a *hilastērion*** (Douay "propitiation").
In the Greek Old Testament the word names the gold cover of the ark, the Douay's
"propitiatory", sprinkled with blood on the Day of Atonement (Lev 16:14); its only other
New Testament use, Hebrews 9:5, names that object. "Mercy seat" or more generally "means
of atonement" is disputed. **God is the subject** who provides it: it does not describe
God's mind being changed. Expiation or propitiation is
[`christology` 5.1](../christology/lessons/05-01-the-paschal-mystery-and-the-threefold-office.md)'s
question. **Status:** freely disputed; no ruling on what it denotes.

*Introduced:* [5.2](lessons/05-02-romans-1-4-the-righteousness-of-god.md)

### Reckoning in Romans 4

**The verb is agreed; its effect is the dispute.** *Logizomai*, "reckon, credit to an
account", a bookkeeper's verb. Gen 15:6 in Rom 4:3: "Abraham believed God: and it was
reputed to him unto justice" (*elogisthē*). 4:4 makes the ledger explicit: to the worker
pay is reckoned "not ... according to grace but according to debt" (*opheilēma*). 3:28
opens with the same verb, "we account" (*logizometha*). **Chronology** (4:9-12): the
reckoning came before circumcision (Gen 15 before Gen 17), so Abraham is father of
believers on both sides of that line.

**The fork:** does God credit a righteousness the believer does not have, or declare what
he thereby makes true? The Douay's note on 4:3: God "reputeth nothing otherwise than it
is". Trent VI ch. 7: we are not only reputed but truly called and are just (paraphrase).
The debt image connects to [`theology-of-debt`](../theology-of-debt/syllabus.md).

*Introduced:* [5.2](lessons/05-02-romans-1-4-the-righteousness-of-god.md)

### Luther and alone in Romans 3:28

**History of interpretation, not a textual variant.** The Greek has *pistei ... chōris
ergōn nomou*, "by faith, apart from works of law"; no "alone". Luther's 1522 German New
Testament added *allein*. In his *Sendbrief vom Dolmetschen* (1530, written at Coburg) he
admitted the word is in neither the Greek nor the Latin and defended it from German
idiom (*allein* when one thing is affirmed and another denied) and from Paul's sense,
claiming Ambrose and Augustine as precedent. Fitzmyer's Anchor commentary, drawing on a
list Bellarmine compiled, names earlier writers who glossed Paul with "alone",
Ambrosiaster and Aquinas among them, and asks whether Luther meant by "alone" what they
meant. James 2:24 is the only New Testament verse where "faith" and "alone" stand
together, denied ([6.3](lessons/06-03-james-faith-and-works.md)).

**Levels:** Trent VI canon 9 condemns a *sense* of "faith alone" (nothing else required to
co-operate), not the word. "Luther invented *sola*" and "Trent condemned the words" are
both false.

*Introduced:* [5.2](lessons/05-02-romans-1-4-the-righteousness-of-god.md)

### Romans 5:12 eph ho and in quo

**One preposition, read two ways.** "Wherefore as by one man sin entered into this world
and by sin death: and so death passed upon all men, in whom all have sinned" (Douay,
following the Vulgate's *in quo*). The Greek: *eph' hō pantes hēmarton*.

- **"In whom"** (Adam): the Vulgate. Augustine weighed the antecedents himself (the man, the sin, or the death), ruled out "sin" (feminine in Greek) and "death", and settled on "that first man" (*Against Two Letters of the Pelagians* 4.4.7). A defended choice, not a blunder.
- **"Because"** all sinned: most modern translators.
- **"With the result that"** (consecutive): Fitzmyer (*New Testament Studies*, 1993); his Anchor commentary catalogues about a dozen proposals.
- **"On the basis of which"** (death).

Against "in whom": the antecedent is far back, and Paul's usual "in someone" is *en*,
while *epi* with the dative normally gives a ground. **Where the weight sits:** 5:12
begins a comparison that breaks off and is completed at 5:18-19 ("by the disobedience of
one man, many were made sinners"); the Adam-Christ solidarity is in the paragraph.

**Levels:** original sin, passed by propagation not imitation, is **defined** (Trent,
Session V); canon 4 binds the **sense** of Rom 5:12, not the philology, so "because" is
compatible with it on the common reading. "Trent defined *in quo*" and "the Greek
disproves original sin" are both level errors. The doctrine is ceded to
[`creation-grace-last-things`](../creation-grace-last-things/syllabus.md) 3.3; Augustine's
use of the verse is [`patristics` 4.5](../patristics/lessons/04-05-augustine-against-the-pelagians.md).

*Introduced:* [5.3](lessons/05-03-romans-5-11-adam-spirit-israel.md)

### The I of Romans 7

**Whose voice cries "unhappy man that I am"?** 7:7-13 is past tense ("sin ... seduced me:
and by it killed me"); 7:14-25 present ("I do not that good which I will"). Five readings:

1. **Paul before conversion**, autobiographically.
2. **Paul the Christian**, and every believer between "already" and "not yet": Augustine's later view (he changed sides, *Against Two Letters of the Pelagians* 1.10.22, because the delight in God's law at 7:22 needs grace), Luther, Dunn, Cranfield.
3. **Adam**: "I lived some time without the law" (7:9); sin *exēpatēsen*, "deceived", me (7:11), echoing Eve's "the serpent deceived me" (Gen 3:13 LXX). Stanislas Lyonnet, SJ.
4. **Israel at Sinai**: Moo, Wright, in different forms.
5. **A rhetorical "I"**: humanity under the Law as seen by faith (W. G. Kümmel, *Römer 7 und die Bekehrung des Paulus*, 1929); most critical commentators broadly, Fitzmyer included.

**Pressure points:** "sold under sin" (7:14) against "freed from sin" (6:18) and 8:2;
7:25b, after the thanksgiving, still divided. Each reading explains away one set of
verses. Philippians 3:6 ("conversing without blame") counts against a tormented
pre-conversion conscience: Stendahl's starting point. **Status:** freely disputed.

*Introduced:* [5.3](lessons/05-03-romans-5-11-adam-spirit-israel.md)

### Life in the Spirit in Romans 8

**Chapter 7 is the problem without the Spirit; chapter 8 is life with him.** "There is
now therefore no condemnation" (8:1). What the Law could not do, God did by sending his
Son, so that the Law's just requirement is fulfilled in those who walk by the Spirit
(8:3-4). The Spirit makes believers adopted children who cry "Abba" (8:15), while
creation groans in waiting (8:22-23). *Pneuma* occurs more often in chapter 8 than in
chapters 1-7 together. The Spirit's divinity read from it is
[`trinity-and-god` 2.3](../trinity-and-god/lessons/02-03-the-holy-spirit-in-the-new-testament.md).

*Introduced:* [5.3](lessons/05-03-romans-5-11-adam-spirit-israel.md)

### The olive tree

**Gentiles grafted into Israel's tree, not replacing it.** Romans 11:17-24: the holy root
(11:16) is the patriarchs and God's promise; the natural branches are Israel, some
broken off "because of unbelief" (11:20); the wild olive is the Gentile believer, grafted
in "contrary to nature" (11:24). The warning is to the Gentiles: "Boast not against the
branches" (11:18). Lesser to greater: "how much more" the natural branches (11:24), if
they "abide not still in unbelief" (11:23). *Nostra Aetate* 4 cites the image for the
Church's roots ([Nostra Aetate 4 on Israel](#nostra-aetate-4-on-israel)).

*Introduced:* [5.3](lessons/05-03-romans-5-11-adam-spirit-israel.md)

### All Israel in Romans 11:26

**"And so all Israel will be saved"**: *kai houtōs pas Israēl sōthēsetai*, future
indicative (the Douay's "should be saved" follows the Latin subjunctive). Context: a
partial hardening "until the fulness of the Gentiles should come in" (11:25); "the gifts
and the calling of God are without repentance" (*ametamelēta*, "irrevocable", 11:29).
Paul calls it a *mystery*, with no date.

- **(A) Ethnic Israel as a whole**, turned to God at the End by the Deliverer from Zion ("all" = the people as a whole): the majority, Fitzmyer and Dunn among them.
- **(B) The whole Church of Jews and Gentiles**: Calvin extended "Israel" to all God's people (citing Gal 6:16); Wright in a version.
- **(C) The elect Jewish remnant** across history: some Reformed exegetes.
- **(D) Israel saved without faith in Christ** (two-covenant, *Sonderweg*): Stendahl, Gaston, Gager.

**Textual facts:** "Israel" in 11:25 is plainly ethnic (against B); 11:23 grafts back "if
they abide not still in unbelief" (against D). **The crux:** does "Israel" keep 11:25's
sense? **Levels:** A-C freely disputed; D in its strong form meets teaching well above free
dispute (owned by [`apologetics-foundations`](../apologetics-foundations/syllabus.md) 6.4).
CCC 674 uses the verse for Israel's recognition of the Messiah before Christ's return; it
keeps its sources' weight and defines no reading. Romans 9: whether election concerns
individuals' destiny (Augustine) or peoples' roles is disputed; Christ is the *telos* of
the Law (10:4), "end" or "goal".

*Introduced:* [5.3](lessons/05-03-romans-5-11-adam-spirit-israel.md)

### New Perspective on Paul

**Did Paul reject works of the law because they are human doing, or because they are
Jewish?** The movement, in order:

1. **Krister Stendahl**, "The Apostle Paul and the Introspective Conscience of the West" (*Harvard Theological Review*, 1963): Augustine and Luther read their own guilt into Paul; Paul had a **robust** conscience ("without blame", Phil 3:6); his question was what Christ means for the Law and the Gentiles.
2. **E. P. Sanders**, *Paul and Palestinian Judaism* (1977): Judaism was [covenantal nomism](#covenantal-nomism), not a religion of earning; Paul reasoned from solution to plight; Sanders thought Paul's thought on the Law not fully consistent.
3. **James D. G. Dunn**: the 1982 Manson Memorial Lecture, published 1983 as "The New Perspective on Paul", named the movement (the phrase had appeared in N. T. Wright's 1978 Tyndale lecture) and supplied the boundary-marker reading of [works of the law](#works-of-the-law).
4. **N. T. Wright** (*What Saint Paul Really Said*, 1997, and later): justification is God's declaration of who belongs to his covenant people, about the Church's identity as much as individual salvation.

**Replies.** The "Old Perspective" (a name its critics gave it) restated by Stephen
Westerholm (*Perspectives Old and New on Paul*, 2004): granting Sanders much about
Judaism, Paul still contrasts doing and believing as such. The *Variegated Nomism*
volumes (2001, 2004): the pattern was not uniform. John Barclay's
[perfections of grace](#the-perfections-of-grace) and the
[apocalyptic reading](#the-apocalyptic-reading-of-paul) cut across the dispute.

**The crux:** both sides agree Paul rejects works of the law as the ground of
righteousness and disagree **why**. Philippians 3:9, "not having my justice, which is of
the law" (*mē echōn emēn dikaiosynēn tēn ek nomou*), is where they must choose: Paul's list
in 3:5-6 mixes birth status (circumcised, Israel, Benjamin) with achievement (Pharisee,
zeal, blameless). Is "my own" righteousness Israel's covenant privilege, or righteousness
by Paul's own doing, set against the one "from God" (*ek theou*)? The New Perspective is
not one position: Sanders, Dunn and Wright disagree with each other.

**Levels:** justification by grace, not by works of the Law without grace, is **defined**
(Trent VI). What Paul meant by *erga nomou*, what Judaism was like, and whether Pauline
justification means membership are **freely disputed** (rung 5). The *Joint Declaration*
(1999) is doctrinal agreement, not a ruling on exegesis.

*Introduced:* [5.1](lessons/05-01-galatians-faith-law-and-the-gentiles.md) (Dunn's reading met at Antioch); owned by [5.4](lessons/05-04-the-new-perspective-on-paul.md)

### The perfections of grace

**Everyone says "grace"; not everyone perfects it the same way.** John Barclay, *Paul and
the Gift* (2015), distinguishes six ways a gift can be taken to an extreme:

| Perfection | Means |
|---|---|
| superabundance | the size and lavishness of the gift |
| singularity | the giver's pure benevolence |
| priority | given before any move by the recipient |
| incongruity | given without regard to the recipient's worth |
| efficacy | it transforms the recipient |
| non-circularity | no return expected |

The perfections are **independent**: a writer can maximize one and not another. Much
Second Temple Judaism was full of grace, as Sanders said, while expecting God to give to
the fitting; Paul's distinctive stress is **incongruity**: God's gift in Christ ignored
every prior measure of worth, including being Jewish, which is why it could reach
Gentiles. Barclay lets both Perspectives be partly right. **Status:** hypothesis.

*Introduced:* [5.4](lessons/05-04-the-new-perspective-on-paul.md)

### The apocalyptic reading of Paul

**Neither a better route to acceptance nor a redrawn membership line, but God's invasion
of an enslaved world.** J. Louis Martyn (*Galatians*, Anchor Bible 33A, 1997) and Douglas
Campbell (*The Deliverance of God*, 2009): the gospel as God's invasive, liberating act
from outside ("who gave himself ... that he might deliver us from this present wicked
world", Gal 1:4), with the Law among the powers of the old age. It cuts across Old and New
Perspectives. **Status:** hypothesis.

*Introduced:* [5.4](lessons/05-04-the-new-perspective-on-paul.md)

## Other letters and Revelation

Every book below is **defined Scripture** (Trent, Session IV). None of their human
authors is defined. That is the whole of this module's level-marking in one line.

### Pseudonymity

**Writing under another's name: a hypothesis with a price, not a neutral label.** Two
ancient models. A **school** presenting its founder's authoritative teaching to a new
day, a claim to tradition rather than a lie (David Meade, *Pseudonymity and Canon*,
1986). Or **forgery**, which ancient writers condemned as deception, and whose personal
touches (2 Tim 4:13, the cloak left at Troas) look designed to be believed (Bart Ehrman,
*Forged*, 2011). The "noble lie" debate is over which it was.

**The early Church's record is severe.** Tertullian (*On Baptism* 17) tells of the Asian
presbyter who wrote the *Acts of Paul* and, convicted, confessing he did it from love of
Paul, was removed from office. The Muratorian fragment rejects letters "to the
Laodiceans, and another to the Alexandrians, forged under the name of Paul" (ANF), yet
receives all four letters to individuals. 2 Thess 2:2 itself worries about a letter "as
sent from us" (*hōs di' hēmōn*).

**Status:** a hypothesis per book (2 Peter the strongest New Testament case; the pastorals
next; 1 Peter and Jude disputed). **Whether pseudonymity is compatible with inspiration
and canonicity is freely disputed**, taken up with
[`fundamental-theology` 3.1](../fundamental-theology/lessons/03-01-inspiration.md)-[3.3](../fundamental-theology/lessons/03-03-the-canon-as-defined.md);
the full 2 Peter case is reserved boss work.

*Introduced:* [6.1](lessons/06-01-the-disputed-pauline-letters-and-the-pastorals.md); used at [6.4](lessons/06-04-peter-jude-and-the-letters-of-john.md), [6.6](lessons/06-06-the-formation-of-the-canon.md)

### The disputed Paulines letter by letter

**Brushwork, ideas, provenance: a cumulative case, strong for some canvases and weak
for others.**

- **2 Thessalonians.** Against: signs must come first (a revolt, "the man of sin", 2:3) where 1 Thess 5:2 has the day come "as a thief in the night"; it copies 1 Thessalonians' structure, down to a second thanksgiving (Wrede, 1903). Reply: apocalyptic holds both (Mark 13); a letter weeks later would echo (Malherbe). The signature (3:17, "the sign in every epistle") fits both hypotheses equally, so it cannot decide ([`philosophical-method` 2.4](../philosophical-method/lessons/02-04-weighing-evidence-in-odds-form.md)). Roughly divided.
- **Colossians.** Against: Christ "the image of the invisible God, the firstborn of every creature" (1:15) and "head of the body, the church" (1:18), where 1 Cor 12 makes the body a congregation and the head one member; "you are risen again" (2:12) against Rom 6:5's future. Reply: the greetings name Philemon's circle (Onesimus, Archippus, Epaphras, Aristarchus, Mark, Luke, Demas); Timothy co-sender. Roughly divided. The hymn's doctrinal use is [`christology` 1.3](../christology/lessons/01-03-why-the-incarnation.md).
- **Ephesians.** Against: reuses Colossians' wording and order (the Tychicus notice, Col 4:7-8 / Eph 6:21-22, nearly identical Greek); "at Ephesus" missing in P46 and the original hands of Sinaiticus and Vaticanus; no greetings despite years in Ephesus. Many read a circular letter. Leans clearly against.
- **The pastorals.** Against: settled office; vocabulary (P. N. Harrison, *The Problem of the Pastoral Epistles*, 1921: *eusebeia*, "sound doctrine", "a faithful saying", faith as a body of teaching); journeys Acts never mentions (Crete, Nicopolis). Reply: short texts; secretaries; a release after Acts 28 and second imprisonment (Eusebius 2.22 reports it only as what "is said"); Luke Timothy Johnson: the three differ and should not be judged as a block. Heavily against.

**Levels:** canonicity defined; authorship a hypothesis; the 1913 response on the
pastorals a weight case, not a definition, not formally revoked.

*Introduced:* [6.1](lessons/06-01-the-disputed-pauline-letters-and-the-pastorals.md)

### Household codes

**Greek household ethics, rewritten in Christ.** Colossians 3:18-4:1 and Ephesians
5:21-6:9 (also 1 Peter 2:18-3:7): wives and husbands, children and fathers, slaves and
masters, the three pairs of Greek household ethics (Aristotle, *Politics* I). Ephesians
opens with mutual subjection (5:21) and grounds marriage in Christ and the Church. The
"great sacrament" of 5:32 is *mega mystērion*, "great mystery"; the sacramental reading
is ceded to [`sacraments-and-liturgy`](../sacraments-and-liturgy/syllabus.md).

*Introduced:* [6.1](lessons/06-01-the-disputed-pauline-letters-and-the-pastorals.md)

### Episkopos and presbyteros

**Two titles, apparently one group, in the first century.** *Episkopos*, "overseer"
(Douay "bishop"); *presbyteros*, "elder" (Douay "priest"); *diakonos*, "servant" (Douay
"deacon"). 1 Tim 3 sets qualifications for the overseer and the deacons; 1 Tim 5:17
honours elders who rule well. Titus 1:5-7 joins the two titles with "for" (*gar*), as if
naming the same men; so do Acts 20:17, 28 and 1 Clement ([`patristics` 1.2](../patristics/lessons/01-02-first-clement-and-the-didache.md)).
Jerome: "the apostle clearly teaches that presbyters are the same as bishops" (Letter
146, NPNF). The single bishop per city appears in Ignatius c. 110
([`patristics` 1.3](../patristics/lessons/01-03-ignatius-of-antioch.md)): the pastorals
are settled office but not yet that. James 5:14's "priests of the church" is also
*presbyterous*.

**Levels:** Trent XXIII canons 6-7 define a hierarchy of bishops, priests and ministers,
bishops superior to priests: dogma about the Church's order, **not** a ruling on
first-century vocabulary. Reading the Douay's "priests" and "bishop" back into Titus 1 is
a translation-through-later-usage error.

*Introduced:* [6.1](lessons/06-01-the-disputed-pauline-letters-and-the-pastorals.md)

### Melchizedek and Psalm 110

**One psalm verse and a three-verse king build a priesthood.** Psalm 110:4 (DR 109:4):
"The Lord hath sworn, and he will not repent: Thou art a priest for ever according to the
order of Melchisedech", quoted or echoed at Heb 5:6, 6:20, 7:17, 21; the psalm's first
verse ("Sit thou at my right hand") at 1:13. Genesis 14:18-20: king of Salem, priest of
God Most High, blesses Abram, receives a tenth.

- **Reading the silences** (7:1-3): "king of justice", "king of peace"; "without father, without mother, without genealogy": an argument from what Genesis does not record (so Challoner's note). Melchizedek is *aphōmoiōmenos*, "made to resemble" the Son, not the model for him.
- **Priority** (7:4-10): Abraham paid tithes, "that which is less is blessed by the better"; Levi paid "in the loins" of Abraham.
- **The wrong tribe** (7:11-16): Jesus "sprung out of Juda"; his priesthood rests on "the power of an indissoluble life" and God's oath, and a change of priesthood changes the Law (7:12).

The sermon's method throughout: take a text, find its gap, show Christ fills it.

*Introduced:* [6.2](lessons/06-02-hebrews.md)

### Once for all ephapax

**Repetition proved the old sacrifices incomplete; one offering proves this one
complete.** *Ephapax*, "once for all": he offered himself "once" (7:27), entered the
sanctuary "once" by his own blood (9:12), "we are sanctified by the oblation of the body
of Jesus Christ once" (10:10); plain *hapax* at 9:26, 28. Against "daily" (7:27) and
"often offering the same sacrifices" (10:11); the model is the Day of Atonement (9:7).
The warning of 6:4 trades on the believer's own *hapax* enlightenment. **Ceded:** sacrifice
as doctrine to [`christology` 5.2](../christology/lessons/05-02-ransom-christus-victor-and-sacrifice.md);
how the one offering is present in the Mass to
[`sacraments-and-liturgy`](../sacraments-and-liturgy/syllabus.md).

*Introduced:* [6.2](lessons/06-02-hebrews.md)

### The new covenant in Hebrews 8

**The word "new" already implies the old covenant's end.** Hebrews 8:8-12 quotes Jeremiah
31:31-34 in full, usually counted the New Testament's longest Old Testament quotation,
from the **Septuagint** (Jer 38 there). Hebrews 8:9 "I regarded them not" (*ēmelēsa*)
against the Douay's Jeremiah 31:32 "I had dominion over them", which follows the Hebrew
through Jerome's Latin: neither is a mistranslation. Then Hebrews' own inference, not
Jeremiah's: "Now in saying a new, he hath made the former old. And that which decayeth
and groweth old is near its end" (8:13). The Levitical order is a "shadow of heavenly
things" (8:5), and a shadow is cast by something real; how Hebrews bears on Judaism
today is not settled by 8:13.

*Introduced:* [6.2](lessons/06-02-hebrews.md)

### The warning passages

**"It is impossible ... to be renewed again to penance"** (Heb 6:4-6; also 10:26, 12:17).
Readings: **Tertullian**, by then a Montanist (*On Modesty* 20), against readmitting
adulterers after baptism; **Ambrose** against the Novatians (*On Repentance* II.2),
followed by Challoner's note: it forbids a second **baptism**, not a second repentance;
**modern**: the present participle "crucifying again" describes continuing apostasy,
during which restoration cannot happen.

**Levels:** that the fallen after baptism can rise again by grace is **defined** (Trent
VI, canon 29), which excludes only Tertullian's absolute reading, by a later definition
rather than by the grammar. Which of the others is right is open exegesis.

*Introduced:* [6.2](lessons/06-02-hebrews.md)

### Who wrote Hebrews

**The ancient Church knew it did not know.** No greeting, no named sender; a "word of
exhortation" (13:22) with a covering note (Timothy, "the brethren from Italy", 13:23-24).
Origen weighed style against thought: "But who wrote the epistle, in truth, God knows"
(Eusebius VI.25.14, McGiffert).

| Candidate | Proposed by | Best evidence | Chief difficulty |
|---|---|---|---|
| Paul | Eastern tradition; the 1914 response | Timothy in 13:23; shared themes | style; 2:3 ("confirmed unto us by them that heard him") against Gal 1:12; no name |
| Paul, Luke translating | Clement of Alexandria (Eusebius VI.14) | explains the Greek | no trace of a Hebrew original; argues from the Septuagint's wording (8:9) |
| Barnabas | Tertullian, *On Modesty* 20 | a Levite (Acts 4:36) | one Western witness |
| Apollos | usually credited to Luther | eloquent, from Alexandria (Acts 18:24) | no ancient witness |
| Priscilla | Harnack (c. 1900) | Pauline circle, anonymity explained | a masculine participle of the author (*diēgoumenon*, 11:32) |
| Unknown | Origen; most modern scholars (Brown) | the evidence stops here | none |

Roman doubts about Paul are reported by Eusebius (III.3.5). **Date:** before 70 is argued
from present tenses about sacrifice (10:1-2), though Hebrews speaks of the tabernacle,
never the Temple; the latest date is fixed by 1 Clement 36, which echoes Heb 1:3-4.

**Levels:** canonicity **rung 1** (Trent lists Hebrews among "fourteen epistles of Paul the
apostle"); whether the title binds on authorship **freely disputed**; the 1914 response a
weight case that itself called canonicity, not authorship, the defined point; every
candidate a hypothesis.

*Introduced:* [6.2](lessons/06-02-hebrews.md)

### James 2:24 and Romans 3:28

**Same words, different stage, different pill.** Rom 3:28: "we account a man to be
justified by faith, without the works of the law". Jas 2:24: "by works a man is justified,
and not by faith only" (*ex ergōn dikaioutai anthrōpos kai ouk ek pisteōs monon*). Three
words carry the apparent contradiction:

| Word | Paul (Romans 3-4, Galatians) | James 2:14-26 |
|---|---|---|
| *pistis*, faith | trust in the God who justifies the ungodly; "worketh by charity" (Gal 5:6) | a claim to right belief ("if a man say"); the demons have it (2:19) |
| *erga*, works | "works of the law"; wages owed (Rom 4:4) | mercy to the needy; Abraham's and Rahab's obedience |
| *dikaioō*, justify | God's verdict on the ungodly, reckoned to faith | shown righteous (demonstrative), judged righteous at the end (eschatological), or growing in justice (progressive) |

**The residue:** Rom 4:2 and Jas 2:21 share the same three Greek words, *ex ergōn
edikaiōthē*: Paul denies of Abraham exactly what James asserts. Did James know Paul?
Martin Hengel (1987 essay) and Dale Allison (ICC, 2013): a response to Paul or Pauline
teaching; Brown and many: a correction of Paulinism reduced to a slogan; Luke Timothy
Johnson (Anchor, 1995): not answering Paul at all, the Abraham material is common Jewish
property. Other readings in the text: 2:20's critical *argē*, "idle", puns on *erga*
(the Douay's "dead" follows the Vulgate and Byzantine Greek); 2:22 *synērgei*, faith "was
working together with" his works.

**Levels:** Trent VI ch. 10 cites 2:24 for the increase of justification (the progressive
reading); that use is **not a defined exegesis**. Canon 24 **defines** that good works
preserve and increase justice received, not merely as fruits and signs; what is open is
whether 2:24 *says* it.

*Introduced:* [6.3](lessons/06-03-james-faith-and-works.md)

### Abraham read twice

**Paul reads Genesis 15:6 before the test; James reads it after.** Paul: the reckoning of
Gen 15 comes before circumcision in Gen 17 (Rom 4:10). James: Gen 15:6 "was fulfilled"
in Gen 22, the binding of Isaac (Jas 2:21-23), where faith was "made perfect". Jewish
tradition had already joined the verse to the test: "Was not Abraham found faithful in
temptation, and it was reputed to him unto justice?" (1 Macc 2:52; compare Sir 44:20 [DR
44:21]). "Friend of God" echoes Isaiah 41:8.

*Introduced:* [6.3](lessons/06-03-james-faith-and-works.md)

### Authorship of James

**"James, the servant of God" (1:1), traditionally the brother of the Lord.** That James
(Gal 1:19), head of the Jerusalem church, was stoned in 62 ([Josephus on
James](#josephus-on-james)). *For*: Jewish-Christian texture, the assembly called
*synagōgē* (2:2), unquoted sayings of Jesus (5:12; Matt 5:37). *Against*: polished Greek;
silence on the ritual questions Gal 2:12 ties to this James; slow reception (Eusebius
II.23.25: "disputed", though read publicly in very many churches). Authentic means
before 62; otherwise most place it late in the first century (Brown judged that side
slightly stronger; Johnson defends the early date). Which "brother" is ceded to
[the brothers of Jesus](#the-brothers-of-jesus).

**Levels:** canonicity **defined**; Trent's titles ("one of the apostle James"; in Session
XIV, "James the Apostle, and brother of the Lord") bind on authorship only if the titles
bind at all, which is **freely disputed**; author and date are hypotheses.

*Introduced:* [6.3](lessons/06-03-james-faith-and-works.md)

### Luther and the epistle of straw

**Luther demoted James; he did not delete it.** His 1522 general preface to the New
Testament ranked John, Paul and 1 Peter as the books that show Christ and called James,
compared with them, an epistle of straw, lacking the gospel's character (paraphrase;
"right strawy epistle" in one rendering). The straw is Paul's: "wood, hay, stubble" on
the one foundation (1 Cor 3:12). Per Timothy Wengert (*Word & World*, 2015) the remark was
cut from the complete Bibles and, after 1539, from separate New Testaments. His preface
to James itself stayed: not apostolic, makes Genesis 15 a text about works, does not
teach Christ; the letter was printed among the last books, after Hebrews. **Status:**
reception history. "Luther removed James from his Bible" is false.

*Introduced:* [6.3](lessons/06-03-james-faith-and-works.md)

### Babylon in 1 Peter

**"The church that is in Babylon ... saluteth you. And so doth my son, Mark"** (5:13).
Eusebius reads it as a figurative name for Rome, citing Clement and Papias (*Church
History* II.15.2); Revelation uses the same code (14:8; 17-18). The name fits best once
Rome, like Babylon, had destroyed the Temple, so many read it as a hint of a date after
70, after Peter's death under Nero; others reply that a capital of exile suffices.
**Silvanus** (5:12, "By Sylvanus ... I have written briefly"): E. G. Selwyn (1946), the
secretary who drafted the polished Greek; but "writing through X" usually names the
**carrier** (Acts 15:23, the same Silas; Ignatius, *Romans* 10). Majority: a Roman
circle loyal to Peter, c. 70-90 (Brown inclines this way); a respectable minority: Peter
himself, with help, before his death. The letter's setting: abuse and suspicion from
neighbours, suffering "as a Christian" (4:16), yet "Honour the king" (2:17). 3:19's
preaching to "spirits that were in prison" is read as the descent
([`christology` 6.1](../christology/lessons/06-01-he-descended-into-hell.md)), as Christ
preaching through Noah (Augustine), or as proclamation to fallen angels (W. J. Dalton,
SJ). **Status:** hypotheses.

*Introduced:* [6.4](lessons/06-04-peter-jude-and-the-letters-of-john.md)

### Jude and 1 Enoch

**Citing a book does not canonize it.** Jude 14-15 quotes 1 Enoch 1:9, introducing it
with "Enoch ... prophesied" (the Douay's "thousands" is *myriasin*, "tens of thousands").
Jude 9, Michael disputing with the devil over Moses's body, comes from a work Origen
calls "The Ascension of Moses" (*De Principiis* III.2.1). Jerome (*Lives of Illustrious
Men* 4): rejected by many for quoting Enoch, yet by age and use received among the
Scriptures. Tertullian drew the opposite inference, that Enoch "possesses a testimony in
the Apostle Jude" (*On the Apparel of Women* I.3). Three readings of "prophesied": Jude
thought the patriarch spoke it; he used a book his readers valued without a verdict on
its authorship; he held this one saying true, as Paul quotes Aratus (Acts 17:28).

**Levels:** 1 Enoch is **not canonical** for the Catholic Church (the Ethiopian Orthodox
canon includes it). Jude's inspiration guarantees what Jude asserts; it does not turn
his sources into Scripture ([`fundamental-theology` 3.1](../fundamental-theology/lessons/03-01-inspiration.md)).
Jude's authorship ("brother of James"; Bauckham defends it) is disputed.

*Introduced:* [6.4](lessons/06-04-peter-jude-and-the-letters-of-john.md)

### Jude and 2 Peter

**One New Testament book copies another almost paragraph by paragraph.** 2 Peter 2:1-3:3
runs parallel to Jude 4-18 in content and largely in order. **Majority: 2 Peter used
Jude.** The changes run one way: both non-canonical citations removed (Enoch dropped;
the Michael scene reduced to a principle, 2:11), canonical rescues added (Noah and Lot),
Jude's intruders turned into future false teachers. A Jude who extracted 2 Peter and
added apocrypha is hard to picture.

**2 Peter** is the strongest pseudonymity case in the New Testament; most critical
scholars, Catholic ones included, doubt Peter wrote it, and the majority date is early
second century. The kinds of evidence in play: internal (3:4, the fathers have died;
3:15-16, Paul's letters as a collection read among "the other scriptures", *tas loipas
graphas*), stylistic (Jerome, *Lives* 1, on the difference from 1 Peter), reception
(Eusebius III.3.1), genre (Bauckham's "testament"), and the eyewitness claim (1:16-18).
The case and reply are reserved boss work and are not set out here. "The other
scriptures" shows some of Paul's letters read as scripture, not a closed canon.

**Levels:** canonicity **defined**; authorship and direction of dependence hypotheses;
compatibility of pseudonymity with inspiration **freely disputed**.

*Introduced:* [6.4](lessons/06-04-peter-jude-and-the-letters-of-john.md)

### The letters of John

**Tests for a community after a split.** 1 John sets three tests: belief (4:2, "Jesus
Christ is come in the flesh", *en sarki elēlythota*, a perfect participle), love (4:20),
obedience (2:4). "They went out from us but they were not of us" (2:19): the leavers are
the "many Antichrists" (2:18, 22), deniers already present, not a single end-time figure.
Brown (*The Epistles of John*, 1982): readers of the Gospel's high Christology who
emptied Jesus's flesh and death of saving weight; compare Cerinthus, for whom Christ
departed from Jesus before the passion (Irenaeus I.26.1); hence 5:6, "not by water only
but by water and blood". 4:3: the Douay's "dissolveth" follows the Vulgate *solvit*;
the critical Greek has "does not confess Jesus"; Socrates (*Church History* VII.32)
reports "separates Jesus" in ancient copies, generally judged secondary. 2 and 3 John
come from "the Elder" (*ho presbyteros*); 3 John's Diotrephes refuses the Elder's envoys:
contested authority, no office to settle it.

*Introduced:* [6.4](lessons/06-04-peter-jude-and-the-letters-of-john.md)

### The Johannine comma

**A verse about the Trinity no Greek manuscript had for over a thousand years.** 1 John
5:7 in the Douay (Clementine Vulgate): "there are Three who give testimony in heaven, the
Father, the Word, and the Holy Ghost. And these three are one." Absent from the critical
Greek, from all early Greek manuscripts (a handful of late minuscules have it, the
earliest fourteenth-century) and from the oldest Vulgate manuscripts. First certain
quotation: Latin, about 380, from Priscillian's circle. Erasmus added it in his third
edition (1522).

**Church acts:** Holy Office, **13 January 1897**: its authenticity cannot safely be
denied or doubted (a non-definitive reply). A Holy Office declaration of **1927**
(reported in the *Enchiridion Biblicum*): the reply was meant to curb private teachers;
Catholic scholars may investigate and incline against authenticity, provided they submit
to the Church's judgement. The **Nova Vulgata omits** the words. **Levels:** a textual
question; Trent's "entire with all their parts" has not been read as settling it since
1927; the Trinity is defined dogma on other grounds
([`trinity-and-god` 2.2](../trinity-and-god/lessons/02-02-the-father-and-the-son-in-the-new-testament.md)).

*Introduced:* [6.4](lessons/06-04-peter-jude-and-the-letters-of-john.md)

### The letters to the seven churches

**One form, seven local problems** (Revelation 2-3). Address to the church's "angel"; a
title of Christ from chapter 1; "I know thy works"; praise and blame; a call to "do
penance"; "He that hath an ear, let him hear"; a promise "to him that overcometh". The
problems: conflict with local synagogues (2:9; 3:9); tolerance of eating "things
sacrificed to idols" (2:14, 20); Laodicea's "I am rich" (3:17). One named martyr,
Antipas (2:13). **The main danger is accommodation**: dinners, guilds, civic loyalty;
violence is real but local. Chapters 4-5 put the throne before the beast: the Lion of
Judah seen as "a Lamb standing, as it were slain" (5:5-6), worshipped with the one on
the throne (5:13).

*Introduced:* [6.5](lessons/06-05-revelation.md)

### Gematria and the number of the beast

**Count the name and you find an emperor.** Rev 13:18: "He that hath understanding, let
him count the number of the beast. For it is the number of a man: and the number of him
is six hundred sixty-six." *Arithmos anthrōpou* is best "a human number", a count anyone
can do. Greek and Hebrew letters were numerals, so every name had a sum: *gematria* in
Hebrew, *isopsephy* in Greek.

**The Nero solution.** *Nero Caesar* in Hebrew letters, *nrwn qsr* (attested in an Aramaic
document from Wadi Murabba'at, per the authorities Bauckham cites); nun $= 50$, resh
$= 200$, waw $= 6$, qoph $= 100$, samekh $= 60$:

$$\underbrace{50 + 200 + 6 + 50}_{n\,r\,w\,n} + \underbrace{100 + 60 + 200}_{q\,s\,r} = 306 + 360 = 666.$$

Spell it as Latin *Nero* rather than Greek *Nerōn* (drop the final nun):

$$50 + 200 + 6 + 100 + 60 + 200 = 616.$$

**616** is the reading of Codex Ephraemi (fifth century) and P115 (P.Oxy. 4499, third
century). Irenaeus (*Against Heresies* V.30.1) never writes the figure: he complains of
copies that deduct fifty from the middle number, "so that instead of six decads they will
have it that there is but one", and blames copyists. He is our earliest witness that the
variant existed. **One hypothesis explains both readings**: the strongest evidence for Nero.

**Beyond the sum.** Suetonius (*Nero* 39) reports a Greek lampoon equating "Nero" with
"killed his own mother": $Ner\bar{o}n = 50+5+100+800+50 = 1005$, and *idian mētera
apekteine* $= 75 + 454 + 476 = 1005$. Some expected Nero to return (*Nero* 57); the beast
has a head "slain to death" and healed (13:3), "was, and is not, and shall come up"
(17:8), "the eighth, and is of the seven" (17:11).

**Limits.** Greek *Nerōn Kaisar* $= 50+5+100+800+50+20+1+10+200+1+100 = 1337$, so the
solution needs Hebrew letters in a Greek book. A sum does not pick out a name: Irenaeus's
own candidates (V.30.3) all make 666:
*Euanthas* $= 5+400+1+50+9+1+200$,
*Lateinos* $= 30+1+300+5+10+50+70+200$,
*Teitan* $= 300+5+10+300+1+50$. *Thērion*, "beast", in Hebrew
letters *trywn*: $400+200+10+6+50 = 666$ (Bauckham). And 666 is doubly triangular:
$1+2+\dots+36 = \tfrac{36\cdot 37}{2} = 666$ with $36 = \tfrac{8\cdot 9}{2}$.

**Status:** the best explanation on offer, a majority hypothesis, not teaching. The
arithmetic anyone can check.

*Introduced:* [6.5](lessons/06-05-revelation.md)

### The date of Revelation

**The 90s are the majority date; a majority is not a proof.** Irenaeus: the vision was
seen "towards the end of Domitian's reign" (*Against Heresies* V.30.3), about 95
(Domitian reigned 81-96); Eusebius repeats it (III.18). Brown and Adela Yarbro Collins
among the majority. "Babylon" for Rome (17:5) points after 70. **Minority:** late 60s (J.
A. T. Robinson, 1976): the Temple still there to be measured (11:1-2); the count of kings
in 17:10, which depends on where the count starts and whether the brief reigns of 68-69
count. Leonard Thompson (1990) and others doubt an empire-wide Domitianic persecution,
which fits the letters' local troubles and
[`church-history` 1.3](../church-history/lessons/01-03-persecution-and-martyrdom.md).
**Author:** "John", on Patmos (1:9), never claiming to be an apostle; Dionysius of
Alexandria (Eusebius VII.25) found the Greek full of "barbarous idioms" and judged
another John wrote it, while keeping the book. Most modern scholars agree; Trent titles
it "the Apocalypse of John the apostle" (whether that binds: freely disputed).

*Introduced:* [6.5](lessons/06-05-revelation.md)

### The four reading schools

**They differ on *when* the visions refer.**

| School | Visions refer to | The beast | The thousand years (20:1-6) | Classic exponents |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Preterist** | the author's own time | Nero, or Rome's imperial cult | usually symbolic | Luis de Alcázar, SJ (early 1600s); most critical scholarship |
| **Historicist** | church history in sequence | a later power, often the papacy in Reformation polemic | an era of church history | Joachim of Fiore; many Reformers; Isaac Newton |
| **Futurist** | mostly events still to come | a future Antichrist | a future reign on earth | Irenaeus on the Antichrist; Francisco Ribera, SJ (late 1500s); dispensationalism |
| **Idealist** | no one period; the permanent struggle | every persecuting power | the Church's age now, Satan bound | Tyconius; Augustine (*City of God* XX.7); the Douay notes |

**Catholic practice now:** preterist in method (addressed first to first-century Asia),
idealist in application. Augustine ties the binding of Satan to binding "the strong man"
(Mark 3:27) and reads the thousand years as the world's whole duration, $1000 = 10^3$ a
number of fullness. Revelation 12's woman: Israel, the Church, Mary; the Douay note puts
the Church first and Mary "by allusion"; the Marian doctrine is
[`ecclesiology-and-mariology`](../ecclesiology-and-mariology/syllabus.md)'s.

**Levels:** Christ's return in glory is **creedal dogma**; a visible earthly millennial
reign before the last judgment is **rejected**, even in modified forms (CCC 676, citing a
Holy Office decree of 1944 that mitigated millenarianism cannot safely be taught; at least
rung 3; doctrine owned by [`creation-grace-last-things`](../creation-grace-last-things/syllabus.md)
7.2). The choice among schools is free. Full preterism, denying a future return, is
excluded; Catholic preterist readings keep the parousia.

*Introduced:* [6.5](lessons/06-05-revelation.md)

## The canon

**Use first, list second.** The core (four gospels and Paul) settled early and was argued
*from*, not *about*; the edges (Hebrews, James, 2 Peter, 2-3 John, Jude, Revelation) took
three centuries; some books were near misses (the Shepherd, the Didache, the Apocalypse of
Peter); some were never close (gospels under apostles' names such as Thomas). *Kanōn*:
measuring rod, then rule. **The result is defined** (Trent, [the canon as
defined](#the-canon-as-defined)); **the history of how it formed has no magisterial
level**.

| Date | Witness | What it shows | Lesson |
|---|---|---|---|
| c. 144 | Marcion breaks with Rome: an edited Luke and ten letters of Paul | catalyst (Harnack, von Campenhausen) or accelerant (Barton) of a closed scripture | [6.6](lessons/06-06-the-formation-of-the-canon.md) |
| c. 180 | Irenaeus, *Against Heresies* III.11.7-9 | four gospels a settled number | [6.6](lessons/06-06-the-formation-of-the-canon.md) |
| late 2nd c. or 4th c. | the Muratorian fragment | a list, date disputed | [6.6](lessons/06-06-the-formation-of-the-canon.md) |
| 320s | Eusebius, *Church History* III.25 | accepted, disputed, rejected, heretical | [6.6](lessons/06-06-the-formation-of-the-canon.md) |
| 367 | Athanasius, Festal Letter 39 | earliest surviving list of exactly the 27 | [6.6](lessons/06-06-the-formation-of-the-canon.md) |
| 393, 397 | Hippo, Carthage | regional synods list the 27 | [6.6](lessons/06-06-the-formation-of-the-canon.md) |
| 1442 | Florence, decree of union with the Copts | the list | [6.6](lessons/06-06-the-formation-of-the-canon.md) |
| 1546 | Trent, Session IV | **defined** | [6.6](lessons/06-06-the-formation-of-the-canon.md) |

Marcion as event is [`church-history` 1.2](../church-history/lessons/01-02-bishops-canon-and-the-parting-of-the-ways.md).

### The fourfold gospel

**By 180, four gospels were a settled number.** Irenaeus, *Against Heresies* III.11.7:
the very heretics bear witness to the gospels, each starting from one of them: the
Ebionites use Matthew only; Marcion mutilates Luke; those who separate Jesus from Christ
prefer Mark; the Valentinians make copious use of John. The argument works only if both
sides already treat the four as common ground. III.11.8 argues neither more nor fewer
from the four zones of the world, the four winds and the four faces of the cherubim
(his pairing: John the lion, Luke the calf, Matthew the man, Mark the eagle, which
differs from Jerome's later one); III.11.9 attacks a fifth, the Valentinians' *Gospel of
Truth*. Weak as proof, strong as evidence that the number was fixed: nobody defends an
optional number with cosmology ([`patristics` 2.1](../patristics/lessons/02-01-irenaeus-against-the-gnostics.md)).

*Introduced:* [6.6](lessons/06-06-the-formation-of-the-canon.md)

### The Muratorian fragment

**Either our earliest list or one list among many from the 300s.** A Latin list, its
beginning lost, in a seventh- or eighth-century manuscript Muratori found at Milan and
published in 1740. It accepts four gospels (Luke "third", John "fourth"), Acts, thirteen
letters of Paul, Jude, two letters of John, Wisdom, Revelation and the Apocalypse of
Peter (which some will not have read in church); rejects forged letters to the
Laodiceans and Alexandrians; says nothing of Hebrews, James or 1-2 Peter. The Shepherd
was written by Hermas "very recently in our times in the city of Rome" while Pius was
bishop (ANF), so it may be read, but not publicly in church.

**Date disputed.** Majority (Everett Ferguson, Charles Hill): "in our times" at face value,
Pius c. 140-155, so late second century at Rome. Minority (Albert Sundberg, 1973; Geoffrey
Hahneman, *The Muratorian Fragment and the Development of the Canon*, 1992): fourth
century, probably Eastern; "our times" means recent compared with the apostles, and such
lists are a fourth-century genre. On the late date the second century has no list at
all. **Levels:** no magisterial level; a Catholic may side with Sundberg.

*Introduced:* [6.6](lessons/06-06-the-formation-of-the-canon.md)

### Eusebius on the accepted and disputed books

**The churches' usage sorted into classes, in the 320s.** *Church History* III.25
(McGiffert's renderings; "acknowledged" and "spurious" are common alternatives):

- **Accepted** (*homologoumena*): the core, including the four gospels, Acts and Paul's letters. Hebrews is counted among Paul's silently: he names it nowhere else in the chapter.
- **Disputed** (*antilegomena*): recognized by many, but spoken against.
- **Rejected** (*notha*, "illegitimate"): orthodox works not in the canon, some quoted by Fathers; the Shepherd and the Didache among them.
- **A fourth group**, below the rejected: heretics' fictions such as the gospels of Peter and Thomas, not even to be ranked.

He **wavers openly on Revelation**, placing it among the accepted "if it seem proper"
and again among the rejected. Elsewhere he reports doubts about individual books (James,
II.23.25; 2 Peter, III.3.1; Roman doubts on Hebrews, III.3.5). "Rejected" does not mean
"heretical". The book-by-book sort into these classes is reserved boss work and is not
set out here.

*Introduced:* [6.6](lessons/06-06-the-formation-of-the-canon.md)

### Antilegomena

**"The spoken-against": the long-disputed edges of the canon.** Eusebius's middle class;
more broadly the books accepted in some regions and doubted in others into the fourth
century: Hebrews (in the West), James, 2 Peter, 2-3 John, Jude, and Revelation (in the
East, where some churches stayed slow; the Syriac churches were slow with the shorter
Catholic letters). **Not the same as** the modern "disputed Paulines", which were all
received early ([6.1](lessons/06-01-the-disputed-pauline-letters-and-the-pastorals.md)). Their reception history tells you a book was doubted
early; it does not tell you who wrote it, and the definition does not either. Canonicity
of every one is **defined**.

*Introduced:* [6.6](lessons/06-06-the-formation-of-the-canon.md)

### Festal Letter 39

**The first closed list of the full set: a bishop fencing his flock off from apocrypha.**
Athanasius's Easter letter of **367** lists exactly the 27 New Testament books, in a
different order: gospels, Acts, the seven Catholic letters, fourteen of Paul (Hebrews
after 2 Thessalonians), Revelation. "Let no man add to these, neither let him take ought
from these" (NPNF). He then names books outside the canon appointed to be read by new
converts, the Didache and the Shepherd among them (with Wisdom, Sirach, Esther, Judith and
Tobit on the Old Testament side). The earliest surviving list of exactly the 27, on present
evidence (Bruce Metzger, *The Canon of the New Testament*, 1987). **Not** the man who
decided the canon: his letter reminds readers of what they already know. Codex Sinaiticus
copies the Shepherd and Barnabas after Revelation: a reading shelf beside the list.

*Introduced:* [6.6](lessons/06-06-the-formation-of-the-canon.md)

### Hippo and Carthage

**Regional witnesses, not the defining act.** The synod of Hippo (393) and the Council of
Carthage (397) listed the same 27 for their churches. Hippo's list survives as canon 24
of the African code of 419, which counts fourteen letters of Paul, asks that the list be
sent to Boniface of Rome for confirmation, and calls these the things received from the
fathers to be read in church (NPNF). **Levels:** regional synods; the definition is
Trent's.

*Introduced:* [6.6](lessons/06-06-the-formation-of-the-canon.md)

## Pitfalls

### Level errors: promoting a hypothesis

- "The Church teaches Matthew wrote first" / "Catholics must hold Matthew first": a 1911-12 response of disputed present force made into doctrine; the order is freely disputed. *([1.1](lessons/01-01-reading-the-new-testament-in-the-church.md), [1.4](lessons/01-04-the-synoptic-problem.md))*
- "Catholic scholarship holds Markan priority" / "Markan priority is established fact": a majority is evidence, not proof, and Q is a hypothesis stacked on it. *([1.1](lessons/01-01-reading-the-new-testament-in-the-church.md), [1.4](lessons/01-04-the-synoptic-problem.md))*
- "The Church teaches Mark was written before 70", or "Catholics must accept the late dates": no date is taught. *([1.5](lessons/01-05-from-oral-tradition-to-written-gospel.md))*
- "The Church teaches Jesus died in AD 33": no date is Church teaching; the Creed fixes "under Pontius Pilate", not a year. *([1.3](lessons/01-03-under-rome.md))*
- "The Church teaches the apostle Matthew wrote in Hebrew before 70" / "Dei Verbum requires John the Apostle wrote the fourth gospel" / "the Church teaches Paul wrote the pastorals" / "James the brother of the Lord wrote James" / "Peter wrote 2 Peter" / "Trent defined that Paul wrote Hebrews": Trent and Dei Verbum define books and apostolic origin, not pens; whether titles bind is freely disputed. *([2.2](lessons/02-02-matthew.md), [2.4](lessons/02-04-john-and-the-synoptics.md), [4.2](lessons/04-02-pauls-life-and-letters.md), [6.1](lessons/06-01-the-disputed-pauline-letters-and-the-pastorals.md), [6.2](lessons/06-02-hebrews.md), [6.3](lessons/06-03-james-faith-and-works.md), [6.4](lessons/06-04-peter-jude-and-the-letters-of-john.md))*
- "The Church teaches Luke used Mark" / "the census happened as Luke describes": a hypothesis and a harmonization made into doctrine. *([2.3](lessons/02-03-luke.md))*
- "The Church teaches John was written to convert unbelievers": a textual variant and a reading made into doctrine. *([2.5](lessons/02-05-the-theology-of-john.md))*
- "The Church teaches the criteria" / "Catholics must accept Sanders's facts" / "Catholics must hold Jesus expected no imminent end": reconstructions have no standing. *([3.1](lessons/03-01-the-quests.md), [3.2](lessons/03-02-sources-and-criteria.md), [3.3](lessons/03-03-what-historians-broadly-agree-on.md))*
- "The Church teaches Mark's night trial happened as described": Dei Verbum 19 canonizes no chronology. *([3.4](lessons/03-04-the-passion-and-resurrection-narratives.md))*
- "The Church teaches Acts is accurate history, so the speeches are transcripts": a genre mistake dressed as teaching. *([4.1](lessons/04-01-acts-as-history.md))*
- "The Church teaches Phil 2:6-11 is an early hymn, so Christ's divinity is historically proven": two level errors at once. *([4.3](lessons/04-03-paul-at-work.md))*
- "The Church teaches *pistis Christou* means faith in Christ" / "that works of the law are identity markers" / "that works of the law means legalism" / "Catholics hold the New Perspective" / "*dikaiosynē theou* in 1:17 means infused righteousness" / "Trent defined *eph' hō* means in whom": Trent defines doctrine, not grammar. *([5.1](lessons/05-01-galatians-faith-law-and-the-gentiles.md), [5.2](lessons/05-02-romans-1-4-the-righteousness-of-god.md), [5.3](lessons/05-03-romans-5-11-adam-spirit-israel.md), [5.4](lessons/05-04-the-new-perspective-on-paul.md))*
- "The Church teaches 666 is Nero" / "Revelation dates from 95" / "a futurist chart is the Catholic reading". *([6.5](lessons/06-05-revelation.md))*
- "The Church teaches the Muratorian canon is second-century". *([6.6](lessons/06-06-the-formation-of-the-canon.md))*
- "The Church teaches they were cousins": the dogma excludes siblings only. *([2.1](lessons/02-01-mark.md))*

### Level errors: demoting teaching

- "Historical character is one scholarly view among others" / "the census problem shows the gospels' historicity is just opinion" / "the gospels' historicity is just a hypothesis, like the beloved disciple's identity": it is conciliar teaching; hypotheses operate inside it. *([1.1](lessons/01-01-reading-the-new-testament-in-the-church.md), [2.3](lessons/02-03-luke.md), [2.4](lessons/02-04-john-and-the-synoptics.md), [3.1](lessons/03-01-the-quests.md))*
- "If Herod died in 4 BC the gospels are unhistorical" / "if Matthew rewrote Mark the gospels aren't historical": Dei Verbum 19 commits to historical character, not to the Dionysian calendar, and already describes redaction. *([1.3](lessons/01-03-under-rome.md), [1.4](lessons/01-04-the-synoptic-problem.md))*
- "The oldest manuscripts lack Mark 16:9-20, so it is not Scripture": canonicity defined; authorship not. The reverse ("Catholics must hold Mark wrote it") is also an error. *([2.1](lessons/02-01-mark.md))*
- "If the apostle didn't write it, it isn't Scripture" (Matthew, Titus, 1 Timothy, 2 Peter): canonicity does not hang on an attribution. *([2.2](lessons/02-02-matthew.md), [4.2](lessons/04-02-pauls-life-and-letters.md), [6.1](lessons/06-01-the-disputed-pauline-letters-and-the-pastorals.md), [6.4](lessons/06-04-peter-jude-and-the-letters-of-john.md))*
- "Hebrews' place in the canon is uncertain since nobody knows who wrote it" / "since scholars dispute 2 Peter, its canonicity is open" / Revelation's slow Eastern reception reopens it: rung 1 does not wait on authorship or reception. *([6.2](lessons/06-02-hebrews.md), [6.5](lessons/06-05-revelation.md), [6.6](lessons/06-06-the-formation-of-the-canon.md))*
- "Genre scholarship shows Acts isn't inspired history": genre changes what is asserted, not whether it is Scripture. *([4.1](lessons/04-01-acts-as-history.md))*
- "The discourses are John's composition, so Christ's divinity is only Johannine theology". *([2.5](lessons/02-05-the-theology-of-john.md))*
- "The crucifixion under Pilate is only a scholarly result the Church shares": it is also creedal dogma, held on apostolic witness. *([3.3](lessons/03-03-what-historians-broadly-agree-on.md))*
- "That justification is a free gift is one scholarly reading" / "that good works increase justification is one Catholic reading of James" / "Galatians' canonicity is a scholarly consensus": each is defined. *([5.1](lessons/05-01-galatians-faith-law-and-the-gentiles.md), [5.2](lessons/05-02-romans-1-4-the-righteousness-of-god.md), [6.3](lessons/06-03-james-faith-and-works.md))*
- "The Greek disproves original sin". *([5.3](lessons/05-03-romans-5-11-adam-spirit-israel.md))*

### Weighing documents wrongly

- Quoting *The Interpretation of the Bible in the Church* (1993) as magisterium: its preface denies it. *([1.1](lessons/01-01-reading-the-new-testament-in-the-church.md))*
- The 1985 *Notes* as one scholar's opinion (they are a real directive on preaching), or as a ruling on history (they are not). They do not show Pharisees absent from the passion narratives (John 18:3; Matt 27:62), only from the trial. *([1.2](lessons/01-02-second-temple-judaism.md))*
- *Nostra Aetate* 4 as "defined dogma" (overstates it), or as a translation rule (it governs preaching). *([2.5](lessons/02-05-the-theology-of-john.md), [3.4](lessons/03-04-the-passion-and-resurrection-narratives.md))*
- A general audience (Benedict XVI, 2008), a pope's book (*Jesus of Nazareth*), or a Douay note treated as a definition. *([3.1](lessons/03-01-the-quests.md), [5.4](lessons/05-04-the-new-perspective-on-paul.md))*
- The early Biblical Commission responses treated as definitions, or as dead letters: name the response and state the weight question. *([1.1](lessons/01-01-reading-the-new-testament-in-the-church.md), [4.3](lessons/04-03-paul-at-work.md), [6.1](lessons/06-01-the-disputed-pauline-letters-and-the-pastorals.md), [6.2](lessons/06-02-hebrews.md))*
- Trent's "entire with all their parts" read as settling the Johannine comma. *([6.4](lessons/06-04-peter-jude-and-the-letters-of-john.md))*
- Trent's canons on the hierarchy read as deciding what *episkopos* and *presbyteros* meant in Titus 1. *([6.1](lessons/06-01-the-disputed-pauline-letters-and-the-pastorals.md))*
- Trent VI ch. 10's use of James 2:24 read as a defined exegesis. *([6.3](lessons/06-03-james-faith-and-works.md))*
- "Paul was simply wrong about the end, as the Church admits": no Church document says so. *([4.3](lessons/04-03-paul-at-work.md))*

### Misreading the text

- "Historical" read as "stenographic": two forms of a saying do not make one gospel wrong. *([1.1](lessons/01-01-reading-the-new-testament-in-the-church.md))*
- Stage 2 read as invention; or Dei Verbum's three stages as an endorsement of Bultmann; or Bauckham as "the Catholic position". *([1.1](lessons/01-01-reading-the-new-testament-in-the-church.md), [1.5](lessons/01-05-from-oral-tradition-to-written-gospel.md))*
- "Form criticism assumes the gospels are fiction": the method classifies forms; the rejected presuppositions are Bultmann's verdict. Setting of use is not setting of origin. *([1.5](lessons/01-05-from-oral-tradition-to-written-gospel.md))*
- "Luke 21:20 is after the event, so Luke is untrustworthy": dating and judging history are different questions. *([1.5](lessons/01-05-from-oral-tradition-to-written-gospel.md))*
- Reading Griesbach or Markan priority into Papias. *([1.4](lessons/01-04-the-synoptic-problem.md))*
- "Messianic secret" taken to mean Mark doubts Jesus is Messiah: Wrede's claim was about pre-Markan history. *([2.1](lessons/02-01-mark.md))*
- "Fulfil" read as "predict", making 2:15 and 2:23 blunders; counting the Douay's Vulgate addition at 27:35. *([2.2](lessons/02-02-matthew.md))*
- "Their synagogues" read as "the Jews"; Matthew 23 as a verdict on the Jewish people. *([2.2](lessons/02-02-matthew.md))*
- *Kathexēs* read as strict chronology; "L" treated as a document. *([2.3](lessons/02-03-luke.md))*
- "John is theology, the Synoptics history": on the day of death and the ministry's length many historians prefer John. *([2.4](lessons/02-04-john-and-the-synoptics.md))*
- Counting Irenaeus as saying "son of Zebedee": he says "disciple of the Lord". *([2.4](lessons/02-04-john-and-the-synoptics.md))*
- Arguing from the Douay's "I am he"; or overcorrecting (9:9 has the same words, no divine claim). *([2.5](lessons/02-05-the-theology-of-john.md))*
- "Pilate ruled Galilee": Galilee was Antipas's until 39. Three Herods (plus Philip and Agrippa II) confused. *([1.3](lessons/01-03-under-rome.md))*
- "The Pharisees were legalists who thought they earned salvation": a picture from later controversy, not the sources. "The Essenes wrote the Scrolls" as fact. "The Messiah" as one job description. *([1.2](lessons/01-02-second-temple-judaism.md))*
- "Historical Jesus" confused with "real Jesus". Tyrrell's deep well credited to Schweitzer. The three-quest story taken as more than a periodization. *([3.1](lessons/03-01-the-quests.md))*
- "Failed dissimilarity, so inauthentic": the criteria are one-way. Counting sources without checking independence. *([3.2](lessons/03-02-sources-and-criteria.md))*
- "Josephus proves Jesus existed, which is why the Church believes it": reverses the order. *([3.2](lessons/03-02-sources-and-criteria.md))*
- "Scholars agree" as a trump card: the eschatological prophet reading is not on Sanders's list. Bin 3 read as failed bin 1. *([3.3](lessons/03-03-what-historians-broadly-agree-on.md))*
- "Scholars have shown 1 Cor 15:3-5 dates within five years of the crucifixion": a strong majority judgment, not proof. Reading the Douay's "the eleven" as Paul's word. *([3.4](lessons/03-04-the-passion-and-resurrection-narratives.md))*
- "Archaeology proves Acts" or "Theudas disproves Acts": each check is local; checked setting is not checked event. "We" read as a signature. *([4.1](lessons/04-01-acts-as-history.md))*
- "Undisputed" read as "more inspired". Gallio taken to date Paul's arrival. Galatians read as a neutral diary. *([4.2](lessons/04-02-pauls-life-and-letters.md))*
- "Spiritual body" read as no body. Mirror-reading every exhortation as a faction. *([4.3](lessons/04-03-paul-at-work.md))*
- "Pedagogue" read as teacher. "The Law was a mistake" (3:21 says the opposite). Settling Antioch's outcome by what you need. *([5.1](lessons/05-01-galatians-faith-law-and-the-gentiles.md))*
- "Luther invented 'faith alone'" and "Trent condemned the words 'faith alone'": both false. *Hilastērion* read as God's mind being changed. *([5.2](lessons/05-02-romans-1-4-the-righteousness-of-god.md))*
- Romans 7 as Paul's tormented pre-conversion conscience (Phil 3:6). "All Israel will be saved" read as a timetable. *([5.3](lessons/05-03-romans-5-11-adam-spirit-israel.md))*
- Stendahl upside down (he gave Paul a *robust* conscience). "Sanders proved Judaism was about grace, so the debate is over". The New Perspective treated as one position. *([5.4](lessons/05-04-the-new-perspective-on-paul.md))*
- "Disputed" Paulines confused with the ancient *antilegomena*. Douay's "priests" and "bishop" read back into Titus 1. *([6.1](lessons/06-01-the-disputed-pauline-letters-and-the-pastorals.md))*
- "Melchizedek had no parents": Hebrews argues from Genesis's silence. "Hebrews says the old covenant was always worthless": a shadow is cast by something real. *([6.2](lessons/06-02-hebrews.md))*
- "Luther removed James from his Bible": he demoted it and printed it last. *([6.3](lessons/06-03-james-faith-and-works.md))*
- "Antichrist" in 1 John read as the end-time figure. "The other scriptures" in 2 Pet 3:16 read as a closed canon. Jude's "prophesied" read as canonizing Enoch. *([6.4](lessons/06-04-peter-jude-and-the-letters-of-john.md))*

## Conventions

- **One card per course**, covering every lesson. The linter checks that every lesson file is cited somewhere on this card.
- **Intuition first**, same as the lessons: a plain-English line before any Greek.
- **Headings are anchors.** Renaming a `###` breaks inbound lesson links. Headings are ASCII only: no parentheses, dashes, Greek or diacritics, and verse colons drop out of the slug ("Romans 3:28" becomes `romans-328`).
- **No prose dollar signs** (see CLAUDE.md); money is "denarii", and the only math is the gematria.
- **Boss problems are reserved.** Entries touching boss material stay at outline level: the rich man's question and Peter's confession are not synopsized; the Testimonium's interpolations are not listed phrase by phrase; Acts 15 and Galatians 2 are not tabulated side by side; the empty-tomb accounts are not tabulated; the readings of *pistis Christou* and *dikaiosynē theou* are kept in separate entries, and the named scholars' readings of "works of the law" are not lined up one sentence each; Eusebius's classes are described, not filled book by book; the 2 Peter case and reply are named, not argued.
- **Modern scholars and post-1930 documents are paraphrased**, cited by work and year and, for documents, section only where verified. Quotations are public domain and short: Douay-Rheims (Challoner), Whiston's Josephus, NPNF and ANF, Church and Brodribb's Tacitus, Crawley's Thucydides, Schaff and Waterworth for Trent.
- **No Denzinger numbers.** A DH number points into one edition of one handbook; a wrong one looks authoritative and cannot be caught by anyone who trusts it.
- Length is not capped the way a lesson's is: this is a lookup surface, not a read-through.

**Hedges this card carries, and will not quietly drop.**

- The date of Claudius's expulsion (c. 49 only from Orosius; Dio's 41 may be another measure).
- The year of the crucifixion (30 or 33).
- The day of death (14 or 15 Nisan).
- The present force of the early Biblical Commission responses, and the standing of the 1955 clarification.
- Luther's breakthrough date (1513 to 1519).
- The Muratorian fragment's date.
- The 1927 Holy Office declaration on the comma is reported through the *Enchiridion Biblicum*, not seen directly.
- Sanders's list is paraphrased from secondary report; his wording is not quoted.
- The early response on Mark's ending was not consulted.
- The 1913 response on the pastorals is known through secondary sources.
