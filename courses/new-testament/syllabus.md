# The New Testament — Syllabus

> Catholic Theology · Tier 1 · ~27 lessons · Prereqs: [`fundamental-theology`](../fundamental-theology/syllabus.md) · Roadmap id: `new-testament`

## Goal

This course reads the New Testament the way the Church asks it to be read: as the inspired word of God, and as texts written by real authors in a real time and place, open to serious historical-critical study. You will finish able to place any New Testament book in its world (Second Temple Judaism under Roman rule), lay a gospel passage beside its parallels and say what each evangelist did with it, state what historians broadly agree about Jesus and by which criteria, read Paul's arguments on justification in Galatians and Romans closely enough to name the exact word each rival reading turns on, and tell how twenty-seven books became a canon. Throughout, it keeps apart what the Church teaches (the gospels' historical character, the canon as defined) from what scholars propose (Markan priority, Q, the dates and authors of disputed books), and presents disputed authorship and dating as disputed. It deliberately skips inspiration and the canon as *doctrines* (owned by [`fundamental-theology`](../fundamental-theology/syllabus.md)), the resurrection argued as a historical case (`apologetics-foundations`), the Christological and Marian dogmas the texts later fed (`christology`, `ecclesiology-and-mariology`), justification as a Catholic–Protestant dispute (`apologetics-catholic-claims`, `creation-grace-last-things`), the Church's history after Acts ([`church-history`](../church-history/syllabus.md)), and New Testament Greek as a language: Greek words appear only when the word is the point, in the lesson's own translation. It also skips a book-by-book commentary: Philemon, 2–3 John and most of the deutero-Pauline letters get a paragraph, not a lesson.

## How practice works in this course

Problems follow [HUMANITIES-BUILD-BRIEF.md](../../HUMANITIES-BUILD-BRIEF.md). Most practice is **exegetical** and strict: what a passage says, what an evangelist added or left out compared with his parallels, which Greek word a reading turns on, what a Church document actually affirms about the gospels and at what level. **Close reading** is the workhorse, on scripture in the Douay–Rheims (Challoner) and on public-domain witnesses (Josephus in Whiston's translation; Papias, Irenaeus, Eusebius and Athanasius in the *Ante-Nicene* and *Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers*), with the lesson's own translation of the Greek wherever the word decides the reading. Two archetypes are distinctive here: the **synopsis comparison** (a pericope in parallel columns, and which direction of dependence explains the changes), a form of close reading; and **find the crux** between named scholars (Sanders vs Westerholm on "works of the law", Goodacre vs the Two-Source hypothesis). **Evaluative** problems, verdict-neutral, are the questions Catholic exegetes genuinely dispute: Markan priority and Q, the authorship and date of the pastorals, 2 Peter and Hebrews, the historicity of a given episode, *pistis Christou*, the New Perspective. Any answer passes if the moves are made. There are occasional **formal** parts (Revelation's gematria, a Pauline chronology from fixed dates) and no apologetic ones: defending the gospels' reliability against sceptics belongs to `apologetics-foundations`. Where a claim's doctrinal weight matters it carries its level from [`fundamental-theology`](../fundamental-theology/syllabus.md). Stating a scholarly hypothesis as Church teaching, or Church teaching as a hypothesis, is an exegetical error.

## Dangerous Checklist

When you finish, you can:

- [ ] Explain the three stages of gospel formation (Jesus, apostolic preaching, the evangelists' writing) from Dei Verbum 19 and CCC 126, and say what that teaching affirms about the gospels' history and what it leaves to scholars
- [ ] Place a New Testament text in its world: which Jewish group, which Roman official, which crisis (the Temple's fall, the Gentile mission) it presupposes
- [ ] Lay a pericope out in synoptic parallel, describe each evangelist's changes, and argue which hypothesis (Two-Source, Farrer, Griesbach) explains them best, while marking all three as hypotheses
- [ ] Give each gospel's portrait of Jesus and of discipleship in a paragraph, and name the passages that carry it
- [ ] State the criteria of authenticity, apply them to a saying, and state the main objections to the criteria approach
- [ ] List what historians of every stripe broadly agree on about Jesus, and separate it from what they dispute
- [ ] Compare the resurrection narratives and 1 Corinthians 15:3–8 accurately: who, where, in what order, and what the differences are
- [ ] Build Paul's chronology from his letters and the Gallio inscription, and say where it strains against Acts
- [ ] Read Galatians 2:16 and Romans 3:21–4:8 closely, naming the Greek words (*dikaiosynē theou*, *pistis Christou*, *erga nomou*, *logizomai*) each rival reading turns on
- [ ] State the Old and New Perspectives on Paul as their best defenders state them, and name the crux between them
- [ ] Give the arguments for and against the traditional authorship of a disputed letter, and explain why canonicity does not by itself settle human authorship
- [ ] Tell how the twenty-seven-book canon emerged, from Irenaeus's four gospels through Eusebius's categories to Athanasius's list of 367, and which books were long disputed

## Modules

### Module 1: The world of the New Testament and the making of the gospels

How the Church asks the New Testament to be read, the Jewish and Roman world it was written in, and what scholars reconstruct about how four gospels came to be.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1.1 | Reading the New Testament in the Church | Say what the Church affirms about the gospels and what it leaves to historical-critical study | *Sancta Mater Ecclesia* (1964) and Dei Verbum 19 on the three stages, CCC 126, the gospels' "historical character" and what it does not require (verbatim reports, one chronology), the early Biblical Commission responses on gospel authorship as weight cases (the method's documents and their weight recapped from `old-testament` 1.3) |
| 1.2 | Second Temple Judaism | Name the Jewish groups, institutions and hopes the gospels presuppose | Temple and Torah, Pharisees, Sadducees and Essenes (Josephus's "philosophies"), Qumran, varied messianic expectations, apocalyptic and the kingdom of God, synagogue and diaspora, the Septuagint |
| 1.3 | Under Rome | Place the New Testament in its political world and fix its dating anchors | Herod the Great and his sons, the prefects and Pilate, Galilee under Antipas, taxation and patronage, the revolt of 66–70 and the Temple's fall, Roman roads and cities of the Pauline mission |
| 1.4 | The Synoptic problem | Describe the data and weigh the rival solutions without mistaking any for doctrine | triple and double tradition, order and wording agreements, arguments for Markan priority, the Two-Source hypothesis and Q, Farrer–Goulder, Griesbach, the minor agreements, Papias on Matthew |
| 1.5 | From oral tradition to written gospel | Explain how scholars reconstruct the stage between Jesus and the texts, and how the gospels are dated | form criticism (Bultmann, Dibelius), redaction criticism, oral tradition and memory, eyewitness claims (Bauckham) and their critics, the gospels as ancient *bioi* (Burridge), dating arguments and the year 70 |

**Boss problem 1:** The rich man's question in all three Synoptics (Mark 10:17–22, Matthew 19:16–22, Luke 18:18–23, Douay–Rheims, with the Greek of the opening exchange supplied). (a) Set the three accounts out in parallel and list every change in the question and Jesus's reply, including what Matthew does with "good" — exegetical. (b) Say how each change is explained on Markan priority and on the Griesbach hypothesis, and say which hypothesis explains this pericope more economically, any verdict, naming the change that decides it and the minor agreement that cuts against it — evaluative. (c) Place features of the pericope at each of Dei Verbum 19's three stages, and say what the document commits a Catholic to about this episode and what it leaves open — exegetical. (d) An invented op-ed paragraph claims that "if Matthew rewrote Mark, the Church's teaching that the gospels are historical is finished." Diagnose the assumption doing the work and correct it from 1.1, marking the level of each claim — exegetical.

### Module 2: The four gospels

Each evangelist as an author with a portrait of Jesus: Mark's hidden Messiah, Matthew's fulfiller of the Law, Luke's Saviour of the poor, and John's Word made flesh.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2.1 | Mark | Read Mark as a narrative of a hidden Messiah and failing disciples | the messianic secret (Wrede) and its critics, discipleship failure, the way of the cross (8:27–10:52), the "brothers" of Mark 6:3 and the exegetical options (doctrine ceded), the endings of Mark and a first look at textual criticism |
| 2.2 | Matthew | Read Matthew as Jesus fulfilling Israel's scriptures and Law for a community in conflict | fulfilment quotations, the five discourses, the Sermon on the Mount and the antitheses, 5:17 on the Law, Peter and the *ekklēsia* in Matthew's story, the Lord's Prayer ("debts" vs Luke's "sins", theology ceded) |
| 2.3 | Luke | Read Luke as the first volume of a history of salvation reaching the poor and the nations | the prologue (1:1–4), the infancy narratives and the census problem, reversal of rich and poor, the Spirit and prayer, the parables only Luke tells, Luke-Acts as one work |
| 2.4 | John and the Synoptics | Say how John differs from the other three and how scholars explain it | chronology (three Passovers, the Temple incident placed early), signs and long discourses, the beloved disciple and authorship disputes, the Johannine community (Brown, Martyn), whether John knew the Synoptics |
| 2.5 | The theology of John | Read John's prologue, discourses and passion as John's own theological argument | *logos* in the prologue (lesson's translation), the "I am" sayings, John 6 as text (sacrament ceded), *hoi Ioudaioi* and how to translate it, the passion as glorification, the purpose statement (20:31) |

**Boss problem 2:** Peter's confession: Mark 8:27–33, Matthew 16:13–23, Luke 9:18–22, with John 6:66–69 as its Johannine counterpart (Douay–Rheims). (a) Close reading: list what Matthew adds and what Luke omits relative to Mark, and what John's version shares with and lacks from the other three — exegetical. (b) Say what each difference shows about that evangelist's portrait of Peter and of discipleship, tying Mark's version to the messianic secret — exegetical. (c) Whether Matthew 16:17–19 goes back to Jesus or to Matthew's community is disputed among Catholic exegetes. In 150 words, state the strongest case on each side and say what evidence would move the question, any verdict; the argument for papal primacy from the passage is ceded and not to be argued — evaluative. (d) In 300 words, compare how Mark and John each handle Jesus's identity: hidden and disclosed in Mark, proclaimed from the first verse in John, citing three passages from each — exegetical.

### Module 3: The historical Jesus

What historians working without faith commitments can establish about Jesus, how they decide, and how the passion and resurrection texts relate to that work.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3.1 | The quests | Tell the story of the search for the historical Jesus and what each phase assumed | Reimarus and Strauss, Schweitzer's verdict (1906), Bultmann and the "no quest", Käsemann's new quest (1953), the third quest (Sanders, Meier, Wright), the Jesus Seminar, Benedict XVI's *Jesus of Nazareth* on the method's limits |
| 3.2 | Sources and criteria | Weigh the sources outside the gospels and apply the criteria of authenticity | Josephus (*Antiquities* 18 and 20) and the Testimonium debate, Tacitus, the criteria (multiple attestation, embarrassment, dissimilarity, coherence, rejection and execution), the critique of the criteria and memory approaches |
| 3.3 | What historians broadly agree on | List the near-consensus about Jesus and separate it from the live disputes | baptism by John, Galilean preacher of the kingdom, reputation as healer and exorcist, the Twelve, table fellowship, the Temple incident, crucifixion under Pilate (Sanders's list), the eschatological-prophet reading vs its rivals |
| 3.4 | The passion and resurrection narratives | Compare the four passion and resurrection accounts with 1 Corinthians 15, and map the scholarly readings | trial scenes and Roman responsibility, Matthew 27:25 and Nostra Aetate on the charge against the Jewish people, the empty-tomb accounts compared, 1 Cor 15:3–8 as early tradition, the scholarly readings mapped (historical case ceded) |

**Boss problem 3:** (a) Close reading: Josephus, *Antiquities* 18.3.3 (Whiston, public domain). Identify the phrases most scholars judge to be later Christian additions, give the reason for each, and state the majority partial-authenticity view and what it leaves standing — exegetical. (b) Apply the criteria of authenticity to three items: Jesus's baptism by John (noting what Matthew 3:14–15 adds), Mark 13:32 on the day or hour known not even to the Son, and the Temple incident. For each, say which criteria apply and what they do and do not establish — exegetical. (c) Name the strongest objection to the criteria approach and say, in 150 words and any verdict, whether a social-memory approach escapes it — evaluative. (d) Tabulate the empty-tomb accounts in the four gospels: who comes to the tomb, what they find, whom they meet, and what they are told. State two differences scholars treat as significant and how two scholars of different views read them, without arguing the historical case (ceded to `apologetics-foundations`) — exegetical.

### Module 4: Acts and Paul's churches

Luke's second volume as a history, Paul's life rebuilt from his own letters, and the apostle at work in his earliest churches.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4.1 | Acts as history | Read Acts as ancient historiography and say what it can establish | genre and the speeches (the Thucydidean convention), structure from Jerusalem to Rome, Pentecost, the "we" passages, where Acts can be checked against external evidence |
| 4.2 | Paul's life and letters | Rebuild Paul's chronology and sort his letters by scholarly confidence | Galatians 1–2 as autobiography, the Gallio inscription, the seven undisputed letters, the disputed six, the ancient letter form, Acts' Paul vs the letters' Paul |
| 4.3 | Paul at work: Thessalonica, Corinth, Philippi | Read Paul's gospel as it meets concrete community problems | the parousia in 1 Thessalonians 4, divisions and the cross in 1 Corinthians 1, the Lord's Supper tradition (1 Cor 11, doctrine ceded), the resurrection body (1 Cor 15), the Philippians hymn (2:6–11) and the early high Christology debate |

**Boss problem 4:** Acts 15:1–29 and Galatians 2:1–14 (Douay–Rheims). (a) Close reading: list the agreements and the differences between the two accounts: who attends, whether the meeting is public or private, what is decided, and what Paul says was added to him — exegetical. (b) Scholars identify Galatians 2 either with Acts 15 or with the famine visit of Acts 11:30. State each identification and the crux between them, and say what each implies for the date of Galatians — exegetical. (c) Using a date range for Paul's call supplied with the problem and his own "after three years" and "fourteen years" (Galatians 1:18, 2:1), compute the range for the Jerusalem meeting under inclusive and exclusive counting and under both readings of what the fourteen years count from, then check the result against the Gallio anchor for Paul's stay in Corinth — formal. (d) In 300 words, any verdict: what does this comparison do to the reliability of Acts as history? Use the conventions of ancient historiography from 4.1, and state the best case for the view you reject — evaluative.

### Module 5: Justification in Galatians and Romans

What Paul's two great arguments about righteousness, faith and the Law actually say, and the modern debate over how to read them.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5.1 | Galatians: faith, Law, and the Gentiles | Reconstruct Paul's argument against circumcising Gentile converts | the Antioch incident, Galatians 2:16, *erga nomou* and *pistis Christou* (objective vs subjective genitive), Abraham and the promise (Gal 3), the Law as *paidagōgos* |
| 5.2 | Romans 1–4: the righteousness of God | Read Paul's thesis and his argument that all are under sin and justified through faith | *dikaiosynē theou* as attribute, gift or saving action, Romans 3:21–26 and *hilastērion*, boasting excluded (3:27–31), Abraham "reckoned" (*logizomai*, Rom 4), Luther's "alone" in 3:28 as history of interpretation |
| 5.3 | Romans 5–11: Adam, Spirit, Israel | Follow Paul from the reign of sin to the Spirit and the destiny of Israel | Romans 5:12 and *eph' hō* vs the Latin *in quo* (doctrine ceded), the "I" of Romans 7, life in the Spirit (Rom 8), Romans 9–11 and "all Israel shall be saved" |
| 5.4 | The New Perspective on Paul | State the Old and New Perspectives at their best and name the crux | Stendahl on the introspective conscience, Sanders's covenantal nomism, Dunn and "works of the law" as boundary markers, Wright, the traditional reply (Westerholm) and the *Variegated Nomism* volumes, Barclay on grace, the apocalyptic reading (Martyn) |

**Boss problem 5:** Galatians 2:15–16 and Romans 3:27–4:8, with the Greek of the key phrases supplied. (a) Give the grammatical options for *pistis Christou* and for *dikaiosynē theou*, the lesson's own translation under each, and the words in the context each reading appeals to — exegetical. (b) State in one sentence each how Luther, Sanders, Dunn and Barclay read "works of the law", as each would sign it, and name the crux between the Old Perspective and Dunn — exegetical. (c) Romans 4:4–5 sets the one who works and receives wages as due against the one whose faith is reckoned as righteousness. In 300–400 words, any verdict, argue which reading of "works of the law" fits Romans 3:27–4:8 best, meeting the strongest verse against you — evaluative. (d) Say what Trent's decree on justification (Session VI) defines at the level of dogma, and what it leaves Catholic exegetes free to hold about *pistis Christou* and the New Perspective; overstating either level is an error. ### Module 6: The other letters, Revelation, and the canon

|---|---|---|---|

**Boss problem 6:** Eusebius, *Church History* 3.25, and Athanasius, Festal Letter 39 (both in *Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers*, public domain). (a) Close reading: sort the twenty-seven books into Eusebius's categories as he gives them, noting where he wavers, and list the books whose standing differs between the two lists — exegetical. (b) Say what Athanasius recommends for the Shepherd and the Didache and what that shows about categories between "canon" and "rejected" — exegetical. (c) Take 2 Peter. Give the strongest case against Petrine authorship and the strongest reply, with the evidence each uses (its ancient reception from 6.6 included) — evaluative, verdict-neutral. (d) In 300–400 words: if 2 Peter is pseudonymous, does that threaten its inspiration or canonicity? Use what [`fundamental-theology`](../fundamental-theology/syllabus.md) 3.1–3.3 teaches about author, inspiration and canon, mark the level of each doctrinal premise you use, and state the strongest objection. ## Notes

- **Two kinds of claim, kept apart.** Every lesson marks which claims are Church teaching and at what level (the gospels' historical character in Dei Verbum 19; the canon as defined at Trent) and which are scholarly hypotheses with no magisterial standing either way (Markan priority, Q, the dates of the gospels, the authorship of the pastorals, Hebrews, James and 2 Peter). The early responses of the Pontifical Biblical Commission on gospel authorship and the Synoptic question are weighed with the tools of [`old-testament`](../old-testament/syllabus.md) 1.3 and [`fundamental-theology`](../fundamental-theology/syllabus.md) 4.5; lessons do not state which particular responses still bind unless that is certain, and name the responses by subject rather than by date where the date is not checked.
- **Disputes stay disputes.** Where Catholic scholars divide (authorship of the disputed letters, the date of Revelation, Matthew 16:17–19's origin, *pistis Christou*, the New Perspective, the Muratorian fragment's date), lessons present the positions with their best evidence and let problems be evaluative. Lessons give the scholarly majority where it exists (for example, most critical scholars hold Markan priority and doubt Petrine authorship of 2 Peter) and say plainly that a majority is not a proof.
- **Scripture and Greek.** Quotations are Douay–Rheims, which follows the Vulgate; where the Latin rendering changes the reading (Romans 5:12's *in quo*, the Lord's Prayer), the lesson gives its own translation of the Greek beside it and says why the two differ. Transliterated Greek stays out of reference-card `###` headings.
- **Bridges.** The Jubilee and "forgive us our debts" hand off to `theology-of-debt` (with [`history-of-debt`](../history-of-debt/syllabus.md) 1.3 for the release laws as law); Romans and James hand the justification dispute to `apologetics-catholic-claims` and `creation-grace-last-things`; the prologue, Philippians 2 and Hebrews hand doctrine to `christology`; 3.4 and 4.3 hand the historical case for the resurrection to `apologetics-foundations`; Acts 15 and Galatians 2 meet [`church-history`](../church-history/syllabus.md) 1.1, and 6.6 meets its 1.2 on Marcion; [`old-testament`](../old-testament/syllabus.md) 6.4 reads the fulfilment quotations from the other side, 4.3 the Servant Songs that Acts 8 quotes, and 5.4 the Wisdom 7:26 that Hebrews 1:3 echoes; `patristics` reads Irenaeus and Athanasius as theologians.
- **Helpful but not required.** [`philosophical-method`](../philosophical-method/syllabus.md) 1.3 (reconstruction), 2.1 (inference to the best explanation, which is how the Synoptic hypotheses and the criteria are weighed) and 4.3 (the crux) are used without being retaught.
- **Lesson count.** The roadmap estimated ~24; the syllabus runs 27 (+12.5%, within the ±25% band). The extra lessons are the ones the steering requires and no module could absorb: John needs two (its relation to the Synoptics and its theology are separate skills), justification needs four (Galatians, Romans 1–4, Romans 5–11 and the New Perspective each carry their own close reading), and the Catholic epistles need two because James against Paul is a lesson on its own.
