# Neuroscience · Lesson 1.3: The action potential

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 1: The neuron & the action potential · Builds on: [1.2](01-02-resting-membrane-potential.md) · Unlocks: 1.4 (the Hodgkin–Huxley model)

## Why this matters

The action potential was *described* in 1939 and *explained* in 1952. Thirteen years separate seeing the thing from knowing what it is, and almost all of that gap was closed by one instrument. **This lesson is about the instrument, not the trace** — because the trace you can look up, and the logic you cannot.

The reason it took an instrument is worth stating precisely. The spike is generated by conductances that depend on voltage, and those conductances *change* the voltage. You cannot measure how conductance depends on voltage while the voltage is being driven by the conductance; the two variables are locked in a feedback loop that runs to completion in a millisecond. **The voltage clamp is the trick that breaks the loop**, and every quantitative statement in [1.4](01-04-hodgkin-huxley-model.md) descends from it. Patch clamp, the modern single-channel version, is the same idea shrunk ([4.5](04-05-methods-a-taste.md)).

There is also a falsification worth carrying. Bernstein's 1902 hypothesis said the spike is a **breakdown of selectivity** — the membrane briefly becomes permeable to everything, and the potential collapses to zero. Plug equal permeabilities for all ions into GHK ([1.2](01-02-resting-membrane-potential.md)) with squid-axon concentrations and you get

$$V \approx 26.7\ \text{mV}\times\ln\frac{440+20}{50+400} = 26.7\times\ln(1.022) = +0.6\ \text{mV},$$

i.e. essentially zero, exactly as Bernstein predicted. But the first intracellular recording showed the membrane **overshooting** to about $+40$ mV. **A loss of selectivity cannot produce an overshoot; only a new, different selectivity can.** One number killed a fifty-year-old theory and told you what to go looking for.

## The idea

**The problem.** Inject a small current into an axon and the membrane behaves like the RC circuit of [circuits 3.2](../../circuits/lessons/03-02-first-order-rc-rl-transients.md): the voltage relaxes exponentially to a new level proportional to the current. Inject a bit more and it does something no passive circuit does — it takes off on its own and produces a stereotyped 100 mV excursion that has nothing to do with how hard you pushed. The response is **nonlinear and regenerative**. That means the ordinary experiment — apply a known stimulus, record the response, infer the element — is unavailable, because the element is being driven by its own output.

**The fix: make voltage the independent variable.** Instead of injecting current and watching voltage, *command* the voltage to a value you choose and inject whatever current is needed to hold it there. A high-gain feedback amplifier compares the measured membrane potential to your command and pumps in current to null the difference — a textbook servo loop ([control-systems 1.1](../../control-systems/lessons/01-01-feedback-and-the-control-problem.md)). Because the injected current exactly cancels the current the membrane passes, **the amplifier's output is a direct, real-time readout of the membrane current** — equal in size and opposite in sign to what the ion channels are carrying.

That single move buys three things at once, and they are worth counting:

1. **It kills the feedback loop.** $V$ is now imposed, so conductance can be measured as a function of a variable that is no longer free to run away.
2. **It kills the capacitive current.** With $V$ held fixed, $dV/dt = 0$, so $I_C = C\,dV/dt = 0$ everywhere except at the step edges. The record is *pure ionic current* — the capacitor has been subtracted out by the experiment rather than by arithmetic.
3. **It kills the spatial dimension.** Threading a silver wire down the squid giant axon makes the whole membrane isopotential ("space clamp"), so there is no propagation to confound the measurement — the cable problem is deferred to [1.5](01-05-cable-theory-conduction.md).

**What the record shows.** Step the membrane from $-65$ mV to a depolarized level and the current does something with obvious structure: a brief **inward** transient that peaks in under a millisecond and dies away even though the voltage is still held depolarized, followed by a **sustained outward** current that switches on with a delay and stays on for as long as the step lasts. Two components, opposite signs, different kinetics.

**The dissection.** Total current is all you ever measure; the components must be pried apart. Two methods did it:

- **Ion substitution.** Replace most of the external $\text{Na}^+$ with an impermeant cation (choline). The late outward current is untouched; the early inward current shrinks and its **reversal potential moves by exactly the amount the Nernst equation predicts for $\text{Na}^+$.** That is the identification: not "sodium looks likely" but "this current's reversal potential tracks $E_{\text{Na}}$ quantitatively."
- **Selective blockers.** Later, tetrodotoxin (TTX, from pufferfish) abolished the early current and left the late one; tetraethylammonium (TEA) did the reverse. Now each component could be recorded *in isolation*, not by subtraction.

**Worth pausing on: all of this happened before anyone knew channels were proteins.** The currents were named, their voltage dependence measured, their kinetics fitted, and the whole mechanism written down as equations — with no structure, no sequence, no direct evidence that discrete pores existed at all. The molecules arrived twenty years later and confirmed a description derived entirely from electrical measurements.

**The mechanism the records imply.** Two conductances:

- A **fast $\text{Na}^+$ conductance** with **two independent gates of opposite voltage dependence**: an *activation* gate that opens on depolarization within tenths of a millisecond, and an *inactivation* gate that **closes** on depolarization, more slowly. Depolarization opens one and shuts the other; the conductance is the product, so it is transient by construction.
- A **slower $\text{K}^+$ conductance** that activates with a delay and **does not inactivate** — it stays on as long as the depolarization does.

**The single most important structural fact about the spike: inactivation is a separate process, not the reverse of activation.** A channel that has inactivated is closed, but it is *not* back where it started — it is in a distinct, unavailable state, and getting out of it requires repolarization plus time. That asymmetry is the entire source of the refractory period, and confusing it with simple closing is the most common error in this material.

**Now the spike assembles itself: positive feedback with delayed negative feedback.** Depolarization opens $\text{Na}^+$ channels; $\text{Na}^+$ rushes in toward $E_{\text{Na}} \approx +58$ mV, depolarizing further; more channels open. The loop runs away — that is the upstroke, and it needs no further input. Meanwhile two brakes engage on a delay: $\text{Na}^+$ inactivation removes the drive, and the delayed $\text{K}^+$ conductance drags the membrane back toward $E_{\text{K}} \approx -80$ mV. **The spike is a relaxation oscillation with a one-shot structure: fast autocatalysis, slow recovery.**

## The formal version

**The current-balance equation.** Take outward current as positive. Charge conservation on a patch of membrane:

$$C_m \frac{dV}{dt} = I_{\text{inj}} - \big[\,I_{\text{Na}}(V,t) + I_{\text{K}}(V,t) + I_{L}(V)\,\big]$$

where $C_m$ is membrane capacitance per unit area (about $1\ \mu\text{F}/\text{cm}^2$), $I_{\text{inj}}$ is injected current density, and $I_L$ is the leak. *In words: the voltage changes at whatever rate the net current charges the membrane capacitor.*

**Under clamp, $dV/dt = 0$ by construction**, so

$$\boxed{\;I_{\text{inj}} = I_{\text{Na}} + I_{\text{K}} + I_{L}\;}$$

*In words: the current you must supply to hold the voltage still is precisely the current the membrane is passing.* The unmeasurable feedback loop has become a direct measurement.

**Each current is Ohmic in its driving force.** For ion $X$,

$$I_X = g_X\,(V - E_X)$$

with $E_X$ the reversal potential from Nernst ([biophysics 4.4](../../biophysics/lessons/04-04-membrane-potentials-nernst-goldman.md)) and $g_X$ the conductance per unit area. **The quantity $(V - E_X)$ is the driving force**, and it carries the sign: $I_X$ is inward (negative) when $V$ lies below $E_X$. Note that $I_X = 0$ at $V = E_X$ *whatever* $g_X$ is — which is exactly why measuring a reversal potential identifies a carrier without knowing anything about the channel.

**The two-gate factorization.** The $\text{Na}^+$ conductance is written

$$g_{\text{Na}}(V,t) = \bar g_{\text{Na}}\; a(V,t)\; h(V,t)$$

where $\bar g_{\text{Na}}$ is the maximum, $a$ is the **activation** variable (rises with depolarization, fast) and $h$ is the **availability**, one minus inactivation (falls with depolarization, slow). *In words: a sodium channel conducts only if the fast gate has opened and the slow gate has not yet shut.* The $\text{K}^+$ conductance has no $h$:

$$g_{\text{K}}(V,t) = \bar g_{\text{K}}\; n(V,t).$$

[1.4](01-04-hodgkin-huxley-model.md) supplies the rate equations for $a$, $h$, $n$ and the exponents Hodgkin and Huxley needed to fit the sigmoidal *delay* in the $\text{K}^+$ onset — that delay is a clue that several independent things must move before a channel conducts, and 1.4 turns the clue into an exponent.

**How you know the two gates are independent processes.** The **two-pulse protocol**. Hold at a conditioning voltage $V_1$ for long enough that $h$ reaches steady state, then step to a fixed test voltage $V_2$ and measure the peak $\text{Na}^+$ current. The *shape and kinetics* of the test current depend only on $V_2$; its *amplitude* depends only on $V_1$. **Two experimentally separable dependencies means two processes**, not one gate doing two jobs. Sweeping $V_1$ traces out the steady-state availability curve $h_\infty(V)$, a descending sigmoid — the mirror image of the ascending activation curve $a_\infty(V)$.

**Threshold, properly defined.** Freeze the slow variables ($h$, $n$) at their resting values and plot the instantaneous total ionic current against voltage. Because $I_{\text{Na}}$ turns on steeply and inward while $I_{\text{K}} + I_L$ grows gently and outward, the curve is **N-shaped** and crosses zero three times:

$$I_{\text{ion}}(V) = 0 \quad\text{at}\quad V_{\text{rest}} \;<\; V_{\theta} \;<\; V_{\text{peak}}$$

$$\boxed{\;\text{Threshold } V_\theta \text{ is the \textbf{middle, unstable} zero of the instantaneous } I\text{--}V \text{ curve.}\;}$$

*In words: threshold is the voltage above which a further depolarization produces net inward current, which produces further depolarization.* It is a fixed point of a one-dimensional flow ([dynamical-systems 1.1](../../dynamical-systems/lessons/01-01-flows-on-the-line.md)) — the unstable one sitting between the resting state and the excited state, acting as a separatrix.

**Three consequences follow immediately, and none of them follow from "threshold is $-55$ mV":**

- **Threshold is not a property written into the membrane.** It is where two currents happen to balance, so it moves whenever either does.
- **Accommodation.** Depolarize slowly instead of abruptly and $h$ falls (fewer $\text{Na}^+$ channels available) and $n$ rises (more outward current) *as you go*. The inward hump of the N-curve flattens; the middle and upper zeros can merge and vanish. **A slow enough ramp reaches voltages far above the "threshold" and never fires**, because by the time it arrives, there is no threshold there.
- **History dependence.** Recent spikes, recent hyperpolarization, and background synaptic conductance all shift $V_\theta$. A neuron's threshold measured 5 ms after a spike is not the number you measured at rest.

**Refractoriness.** Immediately after a spike, $h \approx 0$: no $\text{Na}^+$ channels are available, so **no stimulus of any size fires the cell** — the **absolute refractory period**, lasting roughly the recovery time of inactivation (order 1 ms). As $h$ recovers and the elevated $n$ decays, spikes become possible again but need larger stimuli and come out smaller — the **relative refractory period**, several milliseconds. Two functions:

1. **One-way propagation.** The membrane behind the advancing wave is inactivated, so the spike cannot re-excite where it came from ([1.5](01-05-cable-theory-conduction.md)). Without inactivation an axon would ring rather than conduct.
2. **A ceiling on firing rate.** The absolute refractory period is a hard upper bound on spikes per second, and therefore on how much a rate code can carry ([3.1](03-01-transduction-neural-coding.md)).

**Afterhyperpolarization (AHP).** $n$ decays more slowly than the voltage recovers, so the extra $\text{K}^+$ conductance **outlasts the spike** and pulls $V$ below rest, toward $E_{\text{K}}$. *In words: the brake is still on after the car has stopped.* The AHP deepens refractoriness (it is further from threshold) and, in cells with slow calcium-activated $\text{K}^+$ channels, accumulates across a train to produce spike-frequency adaptation.

**All-or-none, and what it costs.** Above threshold the amplitude is set by $E_{\text{Na}}$, the channel densities, and the kinetics — **not by the stimulus**. So the stimulus magnitude cannot survive in the spike's amplitude; it is erased at the first spike. **This is a design choice, not a limitation**: a regenerated, stereotyped pulse is immune to the attenuation that destroys graded signals over distance ([1.5](01-05-cable-theory-conduction.md)). The price of immunity is that all information must be re-encoded in **which** neuron fires, **how often**, and **when** — the subject of [3.1](03-01-transduction-neural-coding.md). Where distances are short, evolution keeps the analog signal: retinal bipolar cells and many invertebrate interneurons never spike at all.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (mechanical — reading conductances out of a clamp record, then proving the carrier).** Squid axon at 6 °C. Concentrations in mM: $[\text{Na}^+]_{\text{out}} = 440$, $[\text{Na}^+]_{\text{in}} = 50$, $[\text{K}^+]_{\text{out}} = 20$, $[\text{K}^+]_{\text{in}} = 400$. Use $RT/F = 26.7$ mV. The membrane is clamped from $-65$ mV to $-5$ mV; the early current peaks at $-1.00\ \text{mA}/\text{cm}^2$ and the late current settles at $+0.60\ \text{mA}/\text{cm}^2$ (inward negative). (a) Find $E_{\text{Na}}$ and $E_{\text{K}}$. (b) Find the peak $g_{\text{Na}}$ and the plateau $g_{\text{K}}$. (c) Two-thirds of the external $\text{Na}^+$ is replaced by choline. Predict the new $E_{\text{Na}}$ and the new peak inward current.

**(a)** Both are monovalent, so $E_X = 26.7\ \text{mV}\times \ln\!\big([X]_{\text{out}}/[X]_{\text{in}}\big)$:

$$E_{\text{Na}} = 26.7\ln\frac{440}{50} = 26.7 \times 2.175 = \mathbf{+58\ \text{mV}}, \qquad E_{\text{K}} = 26.7\ln\frac{20}{400} = 26.7\times(-2.996) = \mathbf{-80\ \text{mV}}.$$

**(b)** Driving forces at the clamp voltage $V = -5$ mV:

$$V - E_{\text{Na}} = -5 - 58 = -63\ \text{mV}, \qquad V - E_{\text{K}} = -5 - (-80) = +75\ \text{mV}.$$

$$g_{\text{Na}} = \frac{I_{\text{Na}}}{V-E_{\text{Na}}} = \frac{-1.00\ \text{mA}/\text{cm}^2}{-63\ \text{mV}} = 0.0159\ \frac{\text{mA}}{\text{mV}\cdot\text{cm}^2} = \mathbf{15.9\ \text{mS}/\text{cm}^2}$$

$$g_{\text{K}} = \frac{+0.60\ \text{mA}/\text{cm}^2}{+75\ \text{mV}} = \mathbf{8.0\ \text{mS}/\text{cm}^2}$$

(Unit check: $1\ \text{mA}/\text{mV} = 1\ \text{A}/\text{V} = 1\ \text{S}$, so $\text{mA}\,\text{mV}^{-1}\text{cm}^{-2}$ is $\text{S}/\text{cm}^2$.) **Both signs came out positive, as conductances must** — the negative current went with a negative driving force. That sign agreement is itself evidence: an inward current at $-5$ mV is only Ohmic-with-positive-conductance if its reversal potential is above $-5$ mV, which already restricts the carrier to an ion concentrated *outside*.

**(c)** $[\text{Na}^+]_{\text{out}}$ falls from 440 to $440/3 = 147$ mM:

$$E'_{\text{Na}} = 26.7\ln\frac{147}{50} = 26.7 \times 1.078 = \mathbf{+28.8\ \text{mV}}, \qquad \Delta E_{\text{Na}} = -26.7\ln 3 = \mathbf{-29.3\ \text{mV}}.$$

The channels have not changed, so $g_{\text{Na}}$ at this step is still $15.9\ \text{mS}/\text{cm}^2$; only the driving force moved:

$$I'_{\text{Na}} = 15.9\ \frac{\text{mS}}{\text{cm}^2}\times(-5 - 28.8)\ \text{mV} = 15.9\times(-33.8) = -537\ \frac{\mu\text{A}}{\text{cm}^2} = \mathbf{-0.54\ \text{mA}/\text{cm}^2},$$

a **46 percent reduction**, while the late outward current is unchanged. **This is the whole identification argument in three numbers.** A threefold dilution must shift a sodium-selective reversal potential by $26.7\ln 3 = 29$ mV and nothing else; measure that shift and you have named the carrier without ever seeing a channel. Do the same dilution and find the late current unmoved, and you have shown the two components are carried by different ions.

**Example 2 (why you'd care — what a spike costs, and why the answer depends on geometry).** How much ion actually moves per spike, and does it matter?

**Step 1: the capacitive minimum.** To swing the voltage by $\Delta V = 100$ mV across $C_m = 1\ \mu\text{F}/\text{cm}^2$:

$$Q = C_m \Delta V = (1\times10^{-6}\ \text{F}/\text{cm}^2)(0.1\ \text{V}) = 1\times10^{-7}\ \text{C}/\text{cm}^2.$$

$$\frac{Q}{F} = \frac{1\times10^{-7}\ \text{C}/\text{cm}^2}{96{,}485\ \text{C}/\text{mol}} = 1.04\times10^{-12}\ \text{mol}/\text{cm}^2 \approx \mathbf{1\ \text{pmol}/\text{cm}^2}.$$

**Step 2: the real cost.** In the squid axon the $\text{Na}^+$ and $\text{K}^+$ currents overlap in time, so some $\text{Na}^+$ entry is cancelled by simultaneous $\text{K}^+$ exit and does no work on the capacitor. Measured entry runs about three to four times the minimum; take $\mathbf{4\ \text{pmol}/\text{cm}^2}$ per spike.

**Step 3: convert to a concentration change.** For a cylinder of radius $a$, surface area per unit length is $2\pi a$ and volume is $\pi a^2$, so the surface-to-volume ratio is $2/a$. Then $\Delta[\text{Na}^+]_{\text{in}} = (4\ \text{pmol}/\text{cm}^2)\times(2/a)$.

| Axon | radius $a$ | $2/a$ | $\Delta[\text{Na}^+]_{\text{in}}$ per spike | as a fraction of $[\text{Na}^+]_{\text{in}}$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| squid giant | 250 μm $=0.025$ cm | $80\ \text{cm}^{-1}$ | $3.2\times10^{-4}$ mM | $6.4\times10^{-6}$ of 50 mM |
| thin mammalian | 0.5 μm $=5\times10^{-5}$ cm | $4\times10^{4}\ \text{cm}^{-1}$ | 0.16 mM | $1.1\times10^{-2}$ of 15 mM |

**Read the two rows against each other, because that is the point.** In the squid axon a spike changes internal sodium by **six parts per million**: the gradients are a battery so large that a single discharge is invisible, which is exactly why the axon can fire thousands of times with the pump poisoned. In a thin unmyelinated axon the *same* current density moves the *same* charge per unit membrane, but there is eighty thousand times less volume per unit membrane to dilute it into — so one spike shifts internal $\text{Na}^+$ by **about one percent**. **Nothing about the spike changed; only the geometry did.** Small structures — thin axons, boutons, dendritic spines — live near the edge of gradient depletion and must pump continuously.

**Step 4: spike versus rest.** The $\text{Na}^+/\text{K}^+$ pump exports 3 $\text{Na}^+$ per ATP, so 4 pmol/cm² of entry costs $4/3 = 1.3$ pmol ATP per cm² per spike. Now the resting cost. At rest the membrane still has a small standing sodium permeability — the same nonzero $P_{\text{Na}}$ that keeps $V_{\text{rest}}$ above $E_{\text{K}}$ in GHK ([1.2](01-02-resting-membrane-potential.md)). Take a resting sodium leak conductance of $0.01\ \text{mS}/\text{cm}^2$ against a driving force of $-65 - 58 = -123$ mV:

$$I_{\text{Na,leak}} = (0.01\ \text{mS}/\text{cm}^2)(123\ \text{mV}) = 1.23\ \mu\text{A}/\text{cm}^2 \;\Longrightarrow\; \frac{1.23\times10^{-6}}{96{,}485} = 1.3\times10^{-11}\ \frac{\text{mol}}{\text{cm}^2\,\text{s}} = 13\ \frac{\text{pmol}}{\text{cm}^2\,\text{s}}.$$

$$\frac{4\ \text{pmol}/\text{cm}^2\ \text{per spike}}{13\ \text{pmol}/\text{cm}^2\ \text{per second}} = \mathbf{0.31\ \text{s}}.$$

**One spike costs about as much sodium as a third of a second of doing absolutely nothing.** So a neuron firing at 3 Hz has roughly doubled its sodium bill; below that, *rest dominates*. This is the accounting behind a fact that surprises people: the brain's enormous resting metabolic rate is not overhead waiting for activity — **maintaining the readiness to spike is more expensive than spiking.** The gradient is a charged capacitor with a leaky dielectric, and you pay to keep it charged whether or not you ever discharge it.

## Watch out

- **You might think inactivation is just the channel closing again.** It is a *different closed state*. Deactivation (repolarization reversing activation) returns the channel to a **ready** closed state it can leave immediately; inactivation leaves it **unavailable**, and recovery needs repolarization *plus* time. If they were the same process there would be no refractory period, no one-way propagation, and no such thing as accommodation.
- **You might think threshold is a voltage stored in the membrane, like $-55$ mV.** It is the crossing point where inward current overtakes outward current, so it moves with $h$, with $n$, with background conductance, and with how fast you got there. Quote a threshold without saying what the cell was doing beforehand and you have quoted a number with no referent.
- **You might read the clamp record as the membrane's natural behaviour.** It is the opposite: the clamp shows what the membrane *would* do if voltage were prevented from responding. The free-running spike is what happens when you let the loop close, and it is reconstructed from clamp data, not read off them.
- **You might expect the peak to reach $E_{\text{Na}}$.** It stops around $+40$ mV, roughly 18 mV short, because inactivation and the delayed $\text{K}^+$ current are already biting before activation is complete. The peak is a race outcome, not an equilibrium.
- **You might reverse the current sign convention.** Inward current is **negative** and plotted **downward**; $I_X = g_X(V-E_X)$ is negative when $V < E_X$. Conductances are always positive — if one comes out negative, a sign is wrong upstream.
- **You might think the spike depletes the gradients.** For a fat axon, six parts per million per spike. The pump's job is not to refill after each spike; it is to fight the continuous resting leak.

## One-liner

> Hold the voltage still and the current you must supply *is* the membrane current — and that record says the spike is a fast inward $\text{Na}^+$ conductance with two independent gates of opposite voltage dependence, running away against a slow non-inactivating $\text{K}^+$ conductance, with threshold the unstable balance point between them.

| $-55$ | $+1.429$ | 4.173 | **0.193** |
| $-45$ | $+2.857$ | 17.41 | **0.054** |

**(b)** $$\frac{h_\infty(-80)}{h_\infty(-45)} = \frac{0.895}{0.054} = \mathbf{16.5\text{-fold}}.$$

Thirty-five millivolts of holding potential changes the available sodium conductance by more than an order of magnitude — before any stimulus arrives.

**(c)** **Why $-55$ mV can be harder to fire than $-65$ mV.** Moving from $-65$ to $-55$ closes 10 mV of the gap to threshold, but it drops availability from 0.50 to 0.19 — a **2.6-fold cut in the maximum inward current the cell can generate.** Threshold is where inward current overtakes outward current, so shrinking the inward term raises $V_\theta$. If availability falls enough, the inward hump of the $I$–$V$ curve never rises above the outward current at any voltage, the middle zero disappears, and the cell is **inexcitable no matter how hard you push** — depolarization block. Chronic depolarization is not a mild head start; past a point it is an off switch.

**Why released inhibition can trigger a burst.** Hyperpolarizing to $-80$ mV *removes* inactivation — availability climbs from 0.50 to 0.895, nearly a doubling. When the inhibition ends and the cell relaxes back toward rest, it arrives with an abnormally large pool of available $\text{Na}^+$ channels, so the same depolarization now produces far more inward current. That is **post-inhibitory rebound**, and it is the reason inhibition is a legitimate way to *cause* spikes on a delay.

**(d)** A synaptic input's effect cannot be summarized by the millivolts it moves the membrane, because it simultaneously changes the *state* of the voltage-gated machinery — availability, activation, the outward conductance — and that state, not the voltage alone, determines what the next input does.

**P3 (a)** $$r_{\max} = \frac{1}{1.2\ \text{ms}} = \frac{1000\ \text{ms/s}}{1.2\ \text{ms}} = \mathbf{833\ \text{Hz}}.$$

(A hard ceiling, not an operating point: real cortical pyramidal cells rarely sustain more than a few tens of hertz because of adaptation and metabolic cost.)

**(b)** With 1 ms bins and a mean rate of 50 Hz, the probability of a spike in a bin is $p = 50\ \text{s}^{-1} \times 0.001\ \text{s} = 0.05$. The binary entropy is

$$H(p) = -p\log_2 p - (1-p)\log_2(1-p)$$

$$= -0.05\log_2(0.05) - 0.95\log_2(0.95) = 0.05(4.322) + 0.95(0.0740)$$

$$= 0.2161 + 0.0703 = \mathbf{0.286\ \text{bits per bin}}.$$

With 1000 bins per second:

$$\text{information rate} \le 0.286 \times 1000 = \mathbf{286\ \text{bits/s}}.$$

**This is an upper bound in two senses at once**: it assumes every bin is independent (no refractoriness, no correlations), and it counts the *entropy* of the output, which bounds the mutual information with any stimulus from above ([information-theory 1.1](../../information-theory/lessons/01-01-entropy-uncertainty-surprise.md)). Measured rates in real sensory neurons run from a few bits per second to a few tens — well under this ceiling.

**(c)** **Why all-or-none forces the accounting.** Because the spike's amplitude is set by $E_{\text{Na}}$ and the channel population rather than by the stimulus, amplitude carries zero information — every spike is the same symbol. The only degrees of freedom left in a single neuron's output are the *presence or absence* of that symbol in each moment, which is exactly the binary-string description above.

**What the bound ignores:** the refractory period makes consecutive bins **not independent** — a spike forbids a spike in the next bin or two — which *reduces* the number of admissible sequences and therefore lowers the true entropy below 286 bits/s. (Refractoriness also has a compensating effect worth naming: by suppressing bursts it regularizes the spike train, which can *increase* the reliability, and hence the mutual information, of the spike times that do occur. The bound is a ceiling, not an estimate.)

</details>

## Flashback

**From Lesson 1.2 (the resting membrane potential):** Cortical neurons switch chloride transporters during development. A neonatal neuron expressing NKCC1 accumulates chloride, holding $[\text{Cl}^-]_{\text{in}} = 25$ mM; the mature neuron expressing KCC2 extrudes it, holding $[\text{Cl}^-]_{\text{in}} = 6$ mM. In both, $[\text{Cl}^-]_{\text{out}} = 120$ mM and $V_{\text{rest}} = -68$ mV. Use $RT/F = 26.7$ mV.

(a) Compute $E_{\text{Cl}}$ in each. (b) Compute the driving force on chloride at rest in each, and state which way $\text{Cl}^-$ ions move and which way the membrane potential moves when a $\text{GABA}_\text{A}$ receptor (a chloride channel) opens. (c) A drug blocks KCC2 in an adult neuron so that $[\text{Cl}^-]_{\text{in}}$ creeps back to 25 mM. Predict the effect on the neuron's excitability in one sentence.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**(a)** Chloride has $z = -1$, so the valence flips the ratio:

$$E_{\text{Cl}} = \frac{RT}{zF}\ln\frac{[\text{Cl}^-]_{\text{out}}}{[\text{Cl}^-]_{\text{in}}} = -26.7\ln\frac{120}{[\text{Cl}^-]_{\text{in}}} = 26.7\ln\frac{[\text{Cl}^-]_{\text{in}}}{120}.$$

$$\text{Neonatal:}\quad E_{\text{Cl}} = 26.7\ln\frac{25}{120} = 26.7\times(-1.569) = \mathbf{-41.9\ \text{mV}}$$

$$\text{Mature:}\quad E_{\text{Cl}} = 26.7\ln\frac{6}{120} = 26.7\times(-2.996) = \mathbf{-80.0\ \text{mV}}$$

**(b)** Driving force is $V_{\text{rest}} - E_{\text{Cl}}$:

| | $E_{\text{Cl}}$ | $V - E_{\text{Cl}}$ | $\text{Cl}^-$ moves | current | effect on $V$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Neonatal | $-41.9$ mV | $-26.1$ mV | **out** of the cell | inward | **depolarizing** |
| Mature | $-80.0$ mV | $+12.0$ mV | **into** the cell | outward | **hyperpolarizing** |

Watch the double sign flip in the neonatal row and make sure it is not a slip. $V - E_{\text{Cl}} < 0$ makes $I_{\text{Cl}} = g_{\text{Cl}}(V-E_{\text{Cl}})$ negative, i.e. **inward current** by convention — and inward current depolarizes. Physically: intracellular chloride is above its equilibrium value, so $\text{Cl}^-$ (negative) leaves, and removing negative charge from inside makes the inside **more positive**. The membrane is dragged from $-68$ mV up toward $-41.9$ mV.

**So GABA is depolarizing in the neonate and hyperpolarizing in the adult, with the identical receptor.** *Nothing about the receptor changed — only the gradient it reports.* This is the cleanest possible demonstration of the 1.2 principle that a channel does not have a sign; **a channel has a reversal potential, and the cell's transporters decide what that means.**

Two refinements worth holding: depolarizing does not automatically mean excitatory, because the open conductance also **shunts** — it clamps the membrane near $E_{\text{Cl}}$ and short-circuits other inputs, which can be inhibitory even while depolarizing. And in the mature neuron, $E_{\text{Cl}} = -80$ mV happens to coincide with $E_{\text{K}}$, so GABAergic and $\text{K}^+$-mediated inhibition pull toward the same floor.

**(c)** Chloride would accumulate, $E_{\text{Cl}}$ would rise from $-80$ mV to $-41.9$ mV, and GABAergic synapses would flip from hyperpolarizing to depolarizing — collapsing the brain's principal inhibitory mechanism and making the network **markedly more excitable**, which is precisely the mechanism implicated in some post-injury and neonatal seizures.

</details>
