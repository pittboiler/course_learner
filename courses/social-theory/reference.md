# Social Theory · Reference Card

> Open book. This card is meant to be open while you work — including during
> quizzes. Nothing here is something you're expected to recall cold.

The course asks three questions of every claim about society: does the explanation run through individuals or through social wholes (and is that a claim about what exists or about what explains)? Does it explain by cause, by function, or by meaning? And what comparison would test it? Marx, Durkheim, Weber, Simmel, Polanyi, Tocqueville, Coleman, Putnam, the secularization theorists and Taylor are each read first exegetically (what they claimed, in which translation), then classified on that grid, then put on trial against the record, with the strength of the evidence stated in words. Entries never give a verdict on a theorist, and never a judgment on religious truth: whether a social explanation of a belief bears on its truth is flagged ([genetic fallacy question](#genetic-fallacy-question)) and left to philosophy-of-religion. Use the card mid-problem to look up a term, a thinker's argument in premises, a rival explanation, a critic, a translation, or the evidence standard a comparison must meet. Where a boss problem asks for a reconstruction, the card gives only an outline and says so.

## Kinds of explanation and evidence

The course's master vocabulary. Every later entry says which kind of explanation a theorist offers and which evidence standard bears on it.

### Kinds of social explanation

The grid the whole course uses to classify an explanation before judging it. Two questions from 1.1, plus two further kinds from 1.2 and 1.3.

| Kind | What it claims or cites | What it owes | Course examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ontological individualism | Society contains nothing beyond people, their relations and the material things they use; no group mind | Nothing about explanation follows by itself | Weber, Coleman; Simmel (society = reciprocal influence); Durkheim denies a group mind but calls society *sui generis* |
| Ontological holism | Social wholes are real in their own right, with properties and powers of their own | An account of what these wholes are | Durkheim on the strong reading |
| Explanatory individualism | Every good explanation of a social fact bottoms out in individuals' beliefs, desires and actions | Explanans stated without social predicates (Lukes: rarely possible) | Weber's *Verstehen*; Popper, Watkins; Elster; Coleman's boat |
| Explanatory holism | Some good explanations cite social facts that cannot be eliminated | Why the social fact cannot be cashed out in individuals | Durkheim's rule (*Rules* ch. 5); Polanyi; Simmel's forms do the explaining |
| The "both" position | Individualist about what exists, holist about what explains | Coherence (the gas-law analogy: molecules only, yet temperature explains) | Lukes 1968 |
| Causal | Earlier conditions produce X | Comparison; ruling out confounders | Famine produces migration |
| Intentional | People adopt X because they *expect* Y | Evidence of what people actually aimed at | A firm adopts piece rates to raise output |
| Functional, with a feedback mechanism | Unintended consequence Y of X keeps X going through a loop (selection, reinforcement, imitation) | Naming and testing the loop (Elster), or a consequence law (Cohen) | Market selection of firms; Durkheim on rites renewing sentiments (3.3); Coleman's norms (6.2) |
| Functional, no mechanism (bare benefit claim) | Y is good for Z, therefore X | Incomplete as it stands: only praises the practice | "The ritual survives because it unites the village" |
| Interpretive | X is explained by the meaning it has for actors, checked against outcomes | Both adequacies: meaning and causal (Weber) | Weber's Protestant ethic; Taylor's *Secular Age* |

Two cautions. Labels attach to the **explanans**, not to the size of the topic: a rate pooling ten thousand acts explained by integration is holist; one bank run explained by depositors' beliefs is individualist. And none of these kinds is a verdict: saying a practice has a function, or that a belief has a social cause, is neither praise nor debunking.

### Evidence standards

The checks a comparison must pass before it shows a cause (1.4), used in every later module.

| Standard | The question | Classic case in the course |
|---|---|---|
| Comparison | Do cases that differ in X differ in Y? (Mill's agreement, difference, concomitant variation) | Durkheim's Bavarian provinces (3.2); Polanyi's factory laws across countries (5.3) |
| Confounding | Could a third factor drive both? | Durkheim's own schooling-and-suicide example: weakening religious traditionalism as common cause |
| Selection | Did units sort into X on traits tied to Y, or were cases picked by outcome? | Married men (3.2); civic people joining associations (6.1); Catholic-school families (6.3) |
| Ecological fallacy | Is a relation among group rates being read as one among individuals? | Robinson 1950; Durkheim's provinces (3.2); Putnam's regions (6.3); existential security (7.2) |
| Reverse causation | Could Y produce X? | Trust and good government (6.4) |
| Mechanism | By what chain through beliefs and actions would X produce Y, and do cases with X but no mechanism lack Y? | Literacy as mechanism or rival to Weber (4.2) |
| Case study vs large-N | Is the evidence within-case sequence or cross-case covariation? | Skocpol's revolutions vs county-level regressions |

### Social facts

Ways of acting, thinking and feeling that no one person makes and everyone runs into.

Durkheim, *Rules* ch. 1 (1895; my translation in the lesson): "every way of acting, fixed or not, capable of exerting an external constraint on the individual; or again, every way of acting which is general throughout a given society while having an existence of its own, independent of its individual manifestations." "Fixed or not" admits crowd currents and fashions; "capable of" makes constraint a disposition, felt on resistance; "or again" gives a second test (generality plus independent existence); "independent of its individual manifestations" is where holism enters. A social fact is in each part because it is in the whole. "Things" (2nd-ed. preface) means known from outside, not material. Rates are the pure case: private circumstances cancel out, leaving a state of the group. In 3.2 a stable group-specific suicide rate is the example.

*Lessons:* [1.1](lessons/01-01-individuals-and-social-facts.md), [3.2](lessons/03-02-suicide.md)

### Methodological individualism

Collectivities are courses of action of individuals; explanations should run through what individuals mean and do.

Weber, *Economy and Society* ch. 1 §1: for interpretive sociology a state or company is "merely processes and connections of the specific action of individual persons, since these alone are for us intelligible bearers of meaningfully oriented action" (my translation); there is no "acting" collective personality. Note 9: sociology may use collective concepts ("state", "firm", "nation") but must cash them out in individuals' action; functional analysis from the whole is only preliminary work (*Vorarbeit*); an individualistic method does not imply individualistic values. The label is Schumpeter's (1908), not Weber's. Popper and his student J. W. N. Watkins defended it; Watkins demanded "rock-bottom" explanations in individuals' dispositions. A claim about explanation, not a political creed.

*Lessons:* [1.1](lessons/01-01-individuals-and-social-facts.md), [1.3](lessons/01-03-understanding-webers-interpretive-method.md)

### Holism

Social wholes have properties and causal powers of their own, so an explanation may run from one social fact to another.

Durkheim's rule (*Rules* ch. 5, my translation): "The determining cause of a social fact must be sought among antecedent social facts, and not among the states of individual consciousness." His case, exegetically: (1) social facts exist; (2) they are neither organic nor psychological; (3) they arise from association, individual minds being necessary but not sufficient; (4) an explanation must cite what produces the thing explained; (5) hence the rule. A footnote to ch. 5 says the collective conscience need not be hypostasized, so the view reads as emergentism rather than a group soul; whether he keeps to that is a live interpretive question.

**Weak point:** the step from 3 to 5. The critic grants that combination matters and denies it must be cited *as a social fact*. Lukes's rejoinder: individualist models quietly take social facts as given (a bank-run model assumes a deposit contract). **Crux:** whether the individualist's explanans can be stated without social predicates.

*Lessons:* [1.1](lessons/01-01-individuals-and-social-facts.md)

### Ontological and explanatory individualism

"Is society more than individuals?" is two questions: what exists, and what a good explanation must cite.

Ontological individualist: nothing beyond people, their relations and things. Explanatory individualist: every adequate explanation bottoms out in individuals. Explanatory holist: some good explanations cite ineliminable social facts. Steven Lukes, "Methodological Individualism Reconsidered" (*British Journal of Sociology*, 1968): the doctrine is a truism if it means society consists of people, too strong if it bans social predicates (*cashier*, *voter*, *debtor*). That leaves the "both" position. Gas analogy: nothing but molecules, yet temperature and pressure explain, and many molecular arrangements realize one macrostate. Later uses: Simmel is individualist in ontology (society = reciprocal influence) while configurations do the explaining (5.1); rational-choice sociology is explanatory individualism that still admits corporate actors (6.2).

*Lessons:* [1.1](lessons/01-01-individuals-and-social-facts.md), [5.1](lessons/05-01-simmel-sociation-and-the-forms-of-social-life.md), [6.2](lessons/06-02-rational-choice-sociology.md)

### Functional explanation

Explaining a practice X by its consequence Y for a group Z.

Looks backward, since Y comes after X; legitimate only with a loop (the heart pumps blood, and ancestors whose hearts pumped well left more descendants). Durkheim, *Rules* ch. 5: "seek separately the efficient cause that produces it and the function it fulfils"; find the cause first; "function" rather than "end" because social facts do not exist for their results; yet "if the usefulness of the fact is not what makes it exist, it must generally be useful in order to maintain itself", defended by an organism analogy ("the budget of the organism"), not a mechanism. Durkheim also calls crime normal and useful (*Rules* ch. 3) and gives punishment the function of keeping sentiments strong.

Elster's critique ("Marxism, Functionalism, and Game Theory", *Theory and Society*, 1982), as the lesson reconstructs it:

1. A functional explanation explains X by a consequence Y benefiting Z.
2. A later Y explains an earlier X only through a feedback loop through Z.
3. If Z keeps X going because it foresees Y, the explanation is intentional, not functional.
4. So functional explanation proper needs Y unintended and unrecognized and a loop through no one's foresight.
5. Such loops are established in biology and sometimes markets; most sociological functional explanations show the benefit but not the loop.
6. ∴ Those explanations are incomplete: name and test the loop, or reject them.

Elster's conditions in summary: Y is an effect of X; Y benefits Z; Y is unintended; Y (or the X-to-Y link) is unrecognized by Z; Y maintains X through a loop through Z. **Weak point:** 5 to 6. Cohen (*Karl Marx's Theory of History: A Defence*, 1978): a consequence law confirms a functional explanation before the mechanism is known; Elster replies that real consequence laws are rare and easy to fake. Later uses: Marx's ideology claim explains ideas by what they do for the ruling class (2.2); Polanyi's "society protected itself" (5.3); Coleman defines social capital by function but denies the benefit explains its existence, since builders do not capture the payoff (6.3).

### Manifest and latent functions

Consequences the participants intend and recognize, versus consequences they neither intend nor recognize.

Merton, *Social Theory and Social Structure* (1949; revised 1957, 1968), paraphrased. Hopi rain ceremony: manifest function rain (which it does not bring), latent function reinforcing group identity by regular gathering. Latent functions are meant to explain why practices whose stated purpose fails persist. Political machine: its functions are latent mainly to reformers. Add dysfunctions, and ask "for whom". In 6.1 the service associations do for democracy is read as a latent function, not their cause. A latent function is not a hidden purpose: once someone pursues it, the explanation is intentional.

### Functional equivalents

Other arrangements could meet the same need, so the need alone cannot explain why this practice exists.

Merton against the "indispensability" postulate: reforming the political machine away requires providing its equivalents. In 7.1, premise 1 of classical secularization theory (differentiation) has secular institutions taking over religion's tasks as equivalents.

### Social action

Behaviour with a meaning the actor intends, oriented to what others do.

Weber, *E&S* ch. 1 §1 (my translation): sociology is "a science that seeks to understand social action interpretively and thereby to explain it causally in its course and its effects"; "'Social' action shall mean action which, by the meaning its agent or agents intend, is related to the behaviour of others and is oriented to it in its course." The others may be unknown and many: accepting money is social action. Not social: mere events (cyclists colliding), uniform action of many (umbrellas opening in the rain), action merely influenced by a crowd. "Intend" (*gemeint*): the actor's meaning, not an objectively correct one.

*Lessons:* [1.3](lessons/01-03-understanding-webers-interpretive-method.md)

### Verstehen

Weber's interpretive understanding: grasping what an act is and in what context of meaning it belongs.

Direct understanding (*aktuelles Verstehen*): chopping wood, aiming a gun. Explanatory understanding (*erklaerendes Verstehen*): chopping for a wage, for fuel, to work off anger; aiming to execute, fight, take revenge. Needs no empathy: "you need not be Caesar to understand Caesar". It is the first half of causal explanation, not an alternative to it; by itself only "a particularly evident causal hypothesis". Critics: Winch (*The Idea of a Social Science*, 1958): understanding grasps constitutive rules, a conceptual not causal relation; Abel ("The Operation Called Verstehen", *AJS* 1948): understanding only suggests hypotheses. Defenders: reasons can be causes (Davidson 1963).

*Lessons:* [1.3](lessons/01-03-understanding-webers-interpretive-method.md)

### Adequacy at the level of meaning

The sequence of conduct hangs together as a typical, intelligible course by our ordinary habits of thought and feeling (*Sinnadaequanz*).

Alone it yields only an evident hypothesis: motives are misreported, one act can carry opposite meanings, competing motives are each intelligible. Ideal types supply the typical meaning. In 7.3, Taylor's account is interpretive, aiming at what it is like to believe; its causal adequacy (the Reform narrative) is the weak point.

*Lessons:* [1.3](lessons/01-03-understanding-webers-interpretive-method.md), [7.3](lessons/07-03-taylors-a-secular-age.md)

### Causal adequacy

By rules of experience, there is a real probability the sequence actually occurs that way (*Kausaladaequanz*).

Alone it yields only "an unintelligible statistical probability". A correct causal interpretation of typical action needs both adequacies. Weber's method, exegetically (*E&S* §1, notes 5-9):

1. Sociology studies social action.
2. Intended meaning can be grasped directly and explanatorily.
3. An evident interpretation is not thereby the causally valid one.
4. So check interpretation against outcomes: experiment, statistics, comparison of cases alike except in the motive, or failing these a thought experiment.
5. An unintelligible regularity is not a sociological rule.
6. Only individuals bear meaningfully oriented action.
7. ∴ An explanation of typical action must be adequate in meaning and causally adequate, and run through individuals.

**Weak point:** 3-4, that a meaning-interpretation is a causal hypothesis like any other (Winch and Abel attack from opposite sides). In 4.1 Weber's predestination reconstruction is meaning-adequate; its causal adequacy needs comparison, and that is the half of the Protestant-ethic thesis critics attack (4.2).

### Ideal types

A deliberately one-sided pure case, found nowhere in reality, used as a yardstick for real cases.

Weber's "Objectivity" essay (1904): one or a few aspects accentuated into a consistent construct. Not an average, not a moral ideal, not a hypothesis. *E&S* §1: the "laws" of pure economic theory are ideal types; to explain a stock panic, construct the purely purposive-rational course, then enter emotions as "disturbances". This "rationalist" method is not a belief that rationality dominates. Deviations are data, not refutations. Used again for the three types of authority (4.3) and bureaucracy (4.4). Cousin of Mill's isolated economic man, with a difference: Mill claims a real tendency, Weber a yardstick.

### Value freedom and value relevance

Values pick the question; they must not be smuggled into the answer.

Value relevance (*Wertbezogenheit*, credited in *E&S* §1 to Heinrich Rickert): values select what is worth studying and which features count. Value freedom (1917 essay): a finding about what people do and why must not be passed off as a verdict on what is good. It does not mean the researcher has no values. Explaining why people return wallets neither praises nor debunks their honesty.

*Lessons:* [1.3](lessons/01-03-understanding-webers-interpretive-method.md)

### Comparison and concomitant variation

Hold the setting fixed, let the suspected cause vary, see whether the effect follows.

Durkheim, *Rules* ch. 6: social facts cannot be produced at will, so sociology uses indirect experiment. Exegetically:

1. Causation is shown only by comparing cases where the phenomena are present or absent together.
2. Sociology must use the comparative method.
3. Agreement and difference need cases alike or different in one respect only; in sociology they give only conjectures.
4. Concomitant variation needs no inventory: a parallel across enough sufficiently varied cases proves a relation.
5. The relation must be interpreted: deduce a link and check it against new comparisons; if none is intelligible, seek a common cause or intermediary.
6. (Assumed) one effect always has one cause; apparent multiple causes mean several types.
7. ∴ Concomitant variation over long, regular series is sociology's main proof: "to illustrate an idea is not to prove it".

**Weak points:** 5 (the trigger for seeking a confounder is implausibility *to him*, but confounding is a property of the world) and 6 (modern analysis allows several causes; splitting effects into types risks defining types by causes). Uses: the Bavarian provinces (3.2); Simmel's own test of form and content, hold form fixed and vary content (5.1); Polanyi's quasi-experiment (5.3: near-identical factory laws passed by Conservatives and Liberals in England, Catholics and Social Democrats in Germany, the Church in Austria, anticlericals in France; threats: diffusion between countries, a democratization confound); Tocqueville's ranking of circumstances, laws and mores via South America, Mexico and East vs West (6.1).

### Mills methods

Mill, *A System of Logic* (1843): agreement, difference, joint method, residues, concomitant variations.

Agreement: cases sharing the outcome share only one circumstance. Difference: cases alike except one circumstance differ in outcome. Durkheim rejects residues and finds agreement and difference yield only conjectures in sociology. Skocpol, *States and Social Revolutions* (1979): France, Russia, China by agreement and difference, with negative cases (17th-c. England, 19th-c. Prussia, 19th-c. Japan). Lieberson, "Small N's and Big Conclusions" (*Social Forces*, 1991): small-N use assumes deterministic, single, non-interacting, error-free causes. The single-cause assumption is Durkheim's premise 6.

*Lessons:* [1.4](lessons/01-04-testing-a-social-explanation.md)

### Confounding

A third factor drives both X and Y.

Ice cream and drownings both peak in summer. Durkheim's "common cause": schooling and suicide both from weakening religious traditionalism. Formalized as omitted-variable bias in econometrics. In 6.4: inherited-trust designs close reverse causation, but whatever else is inherited along with trust rides along.

*Lessons:* [1.4](lessons/01-04-testing-a-social-explanation.md), [6.4](lessons/06-04-social-capital-on-trial.md)

### Selection

Units sort into X for reasons tied to Y, or the researcher picks cases by outcome.

Fix: include negative cases (Skocpol), or find assignment not chosen by the units. Course uses: married men already less at risk (3.2); princely choice of religion after 1555, *cuius regio, eius religio*, as Cantoni's guard (4.2); routinization evidence comes from movements that survived (4.3); civic people joining associations would fake a schooling effect (6.1); families in close-knit communities or Catholic schools differ in other ways, the standing objection to Coleman's comparisons (6.3).

### Ecological fallacy

Reading a relation among group rates as a relation among the individuals in the groups.

W. S. Robinson, *American Sociological Review* 1950, 1930 census, 48 states (as Freedman summarizes it): percent foreign-born and percent literate correlate about +0.53 across states, about -0.11 across individuals; immigrants settled where natives were relatively literate. Group data are the right evidence for group claims; only the slide to individuals fails, and it needs an assumption group data cannot supply (that each group's rate does not itself vary with the mix). Aggregates bound a subgroup's rate tightly only where that subgroup dominates. Course uses: Durkheim's province and canton comparisons (3.2); county-level tests such as Becker and Woessmann (4.2); Putnam's region-level evidence (6.3); social-capital variables measured for whole places (6.4); existential security as a cross-country correlation backed by an individual mechanism (7.2). See [Ecological-inference arithmetic](#ecological-inference-arithmetic).

### Mechanisms

The chain of steps, usually through beliefs and actions, by which X produces Y.

Tells you where else to look: if X works through M, cases with X but no M should lack Y. Durkheim's "interposed phenomenon" (a mediator) is a mechanism, not a rival; finding it strengthens the causal claim. Links 1.3's adequacies: a mechanism that makes sense to the actors is meaning-adequate; comparison makes it causally adequate. In 2.2, class formation (concentration, communication, combination) is a mechanism through individuals an explanatory individualist can accept.

*Lessons:* [1.4](lessons/01-04-testing-a-social-explanation.md), [2.2](lessons/02-02-class-class-consciousness-and-ideology.md)

### Case study and large-N

Many units compared for strength of covariation, versus few cases traced for mechanism and sequence.

Large-N often speaks only at the group level. Small-N has more candidate causes than cases (Lieberson's critique); defenders reply that within-case sequence can rule rivals out. How much small-N comparison can prove is contested; that its evidence differs in kind and should be judged as such is not.

*Lessons:* [1.4](lessons/01-04-testing-a-social-explanation.md)

### Reverse causation

Y produces X.

Named in 1.4, central in 6.4: good institutions and growth may produce trust. Rothstein and Stolle (*Comparative Politics* 2008): institutional theory of generalized trust; effective, impartial, fair street-level bureaucracies breed it. Design answer: a cause measured before, and not shaped by, the outcome, e.g. Algan and Cahuc, "Inherited Trust and Growth" (*AER* 2010): trust of descendants of US immigrants by origin country and arrival timing, with country fixed effects. That closes the reverse path, not confounding.

*Lessons:* [1.4](lessons/01-04-testing-a-social-explanation.md), [6.4](lessons/06-04-social-capital-on-trial.md)

## Marx

Marx as a social theorist: what historical materialism and class analysis explain, what they predicted, and how the tradition revised itself. The Preface and the political Marx are [history-of-political-thought 6.5](../history-of-political-thought/lessons/06-05-marx-from-hegel-to-historical-materialism.md) and [6.6](../history-of-political-thought/lessons/06-06-marx-the-state-revolution-and-after.md).

### Historical materialism

How a society produces explains its law, its state and its forms of consciousness.

Three layers: forces of production, relations of production, superstructure. The 1859 Preface states the theory (Stone translation; reconstructed in HPT 6.5, and Boss 2 asks for the reconstruction, so the card gives it only in outline: production relations corresponding to a stage of the forces form the base; the superstructure rises on it; when relations fetter the forces an epoch of social revolution begins). *Capital* vol. 1, 1867 preface (Moore-Aveling): "tendencies working with iron necessity towards inevitable results"; "the country that is more developed industrially only shows, to the less developed, the image of its own future" (a comparative prediction; Gerschenkron 1962 argued late industrializers took other routes, through banks and the state); law can "shorten and lessen the birth-pangs" but not clear the phases "by bold leaps"; individuals appear "only in so far as they are the personifications of economic categories" (explanatory, not ontological, holism). The puzzle: relations plainly shape forces and law shapes property, so what can "primacy" mean? Two answers follow.

*Lessons:* [2.1](lessons/02-01-historical-materialism.md)

### Forces and relations of production

Forces: what a society can do with nature. Relations: who effectively controls the forces and labor.

Forces: tools, machines, skills, know-how, labor power. Relations: lord and serf, master and slave, owner and wage-worker. Both readings below agree that relations causally affect forces (capitalism built the railways); they disagree about *why the relations are there*.

*Lessons:* [2.1](lessons/02-01-historical-materialism.md)

### Base and superstructure

The economic structure is the base; law, the state and forms of consciousness are the superstructure.

The base is the sum of the relations of production. On the 1867 preface, the superstructure changes the speed and cost of development, not its direction ("birth-pangs"). A functional reading needs the superstructure to have effects: a superstructure with none could not be explained by its effects. Not technological determinism.

*Lessons:* [2.1](lessons/02-01-historical-materialism.md)

### Functional reading of historical materialism

G. A. Cohen, *Karl Marx's Theory of History: A Defence* (1978): the primacy of the forces is explanatory, and the explanation is functional.

Cohen's Marx, as the lesson reconstructs it:

1. Development thesis: the forces tend to develop (people are somewhat rational, live under scarcity, and can improve their tools).
2. Primacy thesis: the relations are explained by the level of the forces.
3. The explanation is functional: relations obtain *because* they suit the forces' development at that level.
4. Relations that fetter the forces are replaced by relations that suit them.
5. The superstructure is explained functionally too, by stabilizing the relations.
6. ∴ Epochs succeed one another by the growth of the forces, with relations and superstructures selected for fit.

In words: technology is not the engine that pushes institutions; it is the test institutions are selected by. Kind: functional, explanatory holist. Cohen held it could be confirmed by a regularity before its mechanism was known (as Darwin before genetics). Elster (1982; Cohen's reply in the same issue) demands the [feedback mechanism](#feedback-mechanism): candidates are purposive choice by rulers, competition between states, class struggle won by whichever class's rule would develop the forces. **Weak point:** 1 joined to 3; rational peasants and lords pursue their own reproduction, not society's productivity. **Test:** strict primacy fails if similar forces sit under persistently different relations.

*Lessons:* [2.1](lessons/02-01-historical-materialism.md)

### Relations-first readings

Property relations, fixed by the outcome of class conflict, decide whether the forces develop at all.

Robert Brenner, "Agrarian Class Structure and Economic Development in Pre-Industrial Europe" (*Past and Present* 70, 1976): secure peasants and rent-taking lords face no systematic pressure to raise productivity; only producers who must compete in a market to keep their land do. The same post-Black-Death shock gave declining serfdom in the West and a second serfdom east of the Elbe, in regions of broadly similar technique: on this reading class power, not the forces, did the selecting, and capitalist relations were an unintended result of class struggle. E. P. Thompson (*The Making of the English Working Class*, 1963): class is made in experience and struggle, not read off a structure.

*Lessons:* [2.1](lessons/02-01-historical-materialism.md)

### Map of rival readings of historical materialism

| Reading | Core claim | What it predicts | Main objection | Lesson |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Technological determinism (the misreading) | The machine decides; law changes nothing | Superstructure inert | Cannot explain law functionally if law has no effects | [2.1](lessons/02-01-historical-materialism.md) |
| Cohen, functional | Relations are there because they develop the forces | Similar forces, similar relations; relations change when outgrown | No feedback mechanism (Elster) | [2.1](lessons/02-01-historical-materialism.md) |
| Brenner, relations-first | Class conflict fixes relations; relations set incentives for the forces | Same shock, different relations, depending on class power | Gives up the forces' primacy | [2.1](lessons/02-01-historical-materialism.md) |
| Thompson | Class is made in experience and struggle | Class identity varies with history, not structure alone | Hard to state what it forbids | [2.1](lessons/02-01-historical-materialism.md) |

### Class

Marx: your class is fixed by your relation to the means of production, not by income, schooling or lifestyle.

Owners of factories, land and tools against those with only labor power to sell. A well-paid salaried engineer and a cleaner are both wage-workers; a struggling shopkeeper who owns his shop is not. Classes are defined against each other, as creditor needs debtor. Positions fitting neither pole are 2.3's problem.

*Lessons:* [2.2](lessons/02-02-class-class-consciousness-and-ideology.md)

### Class in itself and for itself

A class by position is not yet a class in action.

*The Poverty of Philosophy* (1847), ch. II §5, Quelch translation (Kerr printing 1913): "this mass is already a class, as opposed to capital, but not yet for itself. In the struggle … this mass unites, it is constituted as a class for itself." **"For itself" is Marx's phrase; only "in itself" is a later Hegelian gloss** (Marx's words are "as opposed to capital"). Negative case: the *Eighteenth Brumaire* (1852; De Leon's 1897 translation) on the "allotment farmers", a class insofar as their conditions oppose them to other classes, not one insofar as they have only local ties and no organization, so that they "must themselves be represented" ("much as a bag with potatoes constitutes a potato-bag").

Marx's account of class formation (*Poverty of Philosophy*; *Manifesto* I), as the lesson reconstructs it:

1. Capitalist production creates a mass of wage-workers with common situation and interests opposed to capital.
2. Shared interest does not by itself produce joint action (competition, isolation).
3. Modern industry concentrates workers, equalizes conditions, connects localities.
4. Repeated struggle builds combinations valued above any single wage gain.
5. Linked local struggles become a national, hence political, struggle.
6. ∴ A class becomes an actor when common situation is joined by contact and organization.

Ideology rider: ruling ideas present the ruling interest as everyone's and slow steps 4-5. **Weak point:** 1-2 to 6, the move from common interest to common action ([free-rider problem](#free-rider-problem)); a second line attacks 1 (nation, religion, ethnicity as rival bases of interest). Kind: holist vocabulary, individualist [mechanism](#mechanisms), testable by comparing concentrated and scattered workers.

*Lessons:* [2.2](lessons/02-02-class-class-consciousness-and-ideology.md)

### Ideology

"The ruling ideas of each age have ever been the ideas of its ruling class" (*Manifesto* II, Moore).

Three mechanisms read into Marx: (1) control: owners of material production own the means of producing ideas (*German Ideology*, written 1845-46, published in full 1932, paraphrased); (2) appearance: the market makes a wage look like fair exchange (commodity fetishism, 2.3); (3) function: ideas persist because they stabilize the relations, a [functional explanation](#functional-explanation) that owes a [feedback mechanism](#feedback-mechanism) (Elster, *Making Sense of Marx*, 1985). The ideology claim explains why a belief is *widespread*; a belief can serve a class and still be true. Gramsci's [hegemony](#hegemony) is the most developed answer to how ruling ideas win consent.

*Lessons:* [2.2](lessons/02-02-class-class-consciousness-and-ideology.md)

### False consciousness

Engels's phrase, not Marx's: letter to Franz Mehring, 14 July 1893 ("consciously, indeed, but with a false consciousness").

An explanatory claim about why a belief is held, not by itself a proof that the belief is false. Whether an idea's origin in class interest bears on its truth is an evaluative question the course does not settle (compare the [genetic fallacy question](#genetic-fallacy-question)).

*Lessons:* [2.2](lessons/02-02-class-class-consciousness-and-ideology.md)

### Free-rider problem

In a large group each member gets the shared benefit whether or not she contributes, so common interest need not yield common action.

Mancur Olson, *The Logic of Collective Action* (1965). Contact alone does not cure it; selective incentives or norms do. The model is owned by political-economy 3.2; here it is the objection to Marx's step from class position to class action. Defender's reply: combinations come to be valued for themselves, building solidarity norms (6.2 asks how norms beat free riding, and meets the second-order version).

*Lessons:* [2.2](lessons/02-02-class-class-consciousness-and-ideology.md), [6.2](lessons/06-02-rational-choice-sociology.md)

### Commodification

The spread of buying and selling into areas once run by custom, kinship or obligation, above all labor-power sold for a wage.

*Manifesto*: no tie left between people but cash payment. The empirical drift of work into wage employment is among the best-documented trends of two centuries. Whether commodifying a good corrupts it is evaluative and belongs to philosophy-of-economics 5.1; Polanyi's sociological version is 5.3.

*Lessons:* [2.3](lessons/02-03-capitalism-and-the-test-of-history.md), [5.3](lessons/05-03-polanyis-great-transformation.md)

### Alienation

1844 Manuscripts: alienation from the product, the activity, species-being and other people.

Taught in history-of-political-thought 6.5. Note only its kind: a claim about the *structure* of work, not the size of the wage, so no wage series confirms or refutes it.

*Lessons:* [2.3](lessons/02-03-capitalism-and-the-test-of-history.md)

### Commodity fetishism

A social relation between producers appears as a relation between things.

*Capital* vol. 1, ch. I §4 (Moore-Aveling): "a definite social relation between men, that assumes, in their eyes, the fantastic form of a relation between things." A rise in the price of coffee looks like a fact about coffee; for Marx it is a fact about growers, roasters and buyers in a division of labor none sees whole. Kind: interpretive (how the relation appears) with a structural cause (production for exchange). Comparative prediction: the mystification vanishes where dependence is personal (the serf knew his labor for the lord was a definite quantity). Polanyi's ch. 6 footnote says his fictitious commodities are unrelated to it (5.3).

*Lessons:* [2.3](lessons/02-03-capitalism-and-the-test-of-history.md)

### Polarization and immiseration

Marx's forecast: two hostile camps, the lower middle strata sinking into the proletariat, the worker sinking toward pauperism.

*Manifesto* I (Moore): society "more and more splitting up into two great hostile camps"; "the lower strata of the middle class … sink gradually into the proletariat" by scale and by technique. The forecast (*Manifesto*; *Capital* ch. XXV), as the lesson reconstructs it:

1. Capitalists compete; one who does not cut costs is ruined.
2. Costs fall with scale and machinery, so each must accumulate and mechanize.
3. ∴ Capital concentrates; small capital and craft skill sink into wage labor.
4. Machinery displaces workers faster than accumulation re-employs them (reserve army).
5. The reserve army holds wages near subsistence.
6. ∴ Immiseration and polarization; with 2.2's communication, class action.

Kind: individualist mechanism (1-2), holist explanandum. Marx hedged: "the absolute general law of capitalist accumulation … modified in its working by many circumstances" (ch. XXV §4), and the labourer's lot worsens "be his payment high or low", the root of **relative** immiseration (falling share or position). **Weak points:** 4-5 (the reserve army as transitional); 6's silent assumption that new jobs are all workers' jobs.

| Prediction | Record (strength) | Revision |
|---|---|---|
| Absolute immiseration | Allen, "Engels' pause" (*Explorations in Economic History* 2009): British real wages flat while output per worker rose, profit rate doubled, to about mid-century; then wages rose with productivity. Long real-wage rise since: about as robust as economic history gets | Relative immiseration |
| Old middle class sinks | Long decline robust; but Steinmetz and Wright (*AJS* 1989): US self-employment rose every year after the early 1970s (one country, a reversal at the margin) | |
| Two camps | Salaried managerial, professional, technical work grew through the 20th century (robust); Wright 1984 calls the middle class Marxism's "embarrassment" | Wright, [contradictory class locations](#contradictory-class-locations) |
| Shared situation yields class action | Durable working-class conservatism | Gramsci, [hegemony](#hegemony) |

Bernstein (*Evolutionary Socialism*, 1899) accepted the data and dropped the collapse forecast.

*Lessons:* [2.3](lessons/02-03-capitalism-and-the-test-of-history.md)

### Hegemony

Gramsci (prison notebooks 1929-35; English selections 1971): in the West the ruling class governs largely by consent organized in civil society.

Churches, schools, press and parties organize consent, kept up by real concessions to subordinate groups' economic interests that never touch the core. Workers who accept capitalism are led, not simply deceived. Overturning it takes a long "war of position" in civil society. Testing it: consent can be invoked for whatever workers do, so ask what pattern of working-class politics it *forbids*.

*Lessons:* [2.3](lessons/02-03-capitalism-and-the-test-of-history.md)

### Contradictory class locations

Erik Olin Wright: some positions sit in two classes at once.

Introduced 1976 (*New Left Review*, "Class Boundaries in Advanced Capitalist Societies"); rebuilt in *Classes* (1985) around exploitation of three assets: means of production, organization (control of how work is coordinated) and skills or credentials. A manager is exploited as a wage-earner and exploits through organization assets. It passes Lakatos's test in the lesson's account: it predicts income and pro-capitalist attitudes rising step by step with assets, a prediction Wright took to survey data.

*Lessons:* [2.3](lessons/02-03-capitalism-and-the-test-of-history.md)

### Progressive and degenerating revisions

Lakatos (1970): a revision is progressive if it predicts new facts, degenerating if it only absorbs old failures.

Popper (*Conjectures and Refutations*, 1963) charged that Marxists reinterpreted theory and evidence until nothing could refute them. Applied in the course: Wright's revision predicts a gradient with assets and was tested; hegemony is harder to test. A revision is judged by what it predicts next, not by its content. The general theory belongs to philosophy-of-science.

*Lessons:* [2.3](lessons/02-03-capitalism-and-the-test-of-history.md)

## Durkheim

Society as a moral reality with causal power of its own: over the division of labor, over the suicide rate, and over the sacred.

### Mechanical and organic solidarity

A group can hang together by likeness or by interdependence.

*De la division du travail social* (1893), Book I chs. 2-3. Mechanical: everyone believes, works and worships alike; named by analogy with the cohesion of inert bodies whose molecules move together because none moves on its own. Organic: people differ and need each other like organs; unity grows *with* individuation of the parts. The two vary inversely. As societies grow in **volume** and **density**, organic solidarity becomes preponderant.

The cause (Book II), as the lesson reconstructs it: (8) growing volume and density bring more similar people into competition for the same things; (9) competition is fiercest among the most similar (Durkheim cites Darwin), and specializing lets them coexist; (10) ∴ "the division of labor varies in direct ratio to the volume and density of societies" (Book II ch. 2). Book II ch. 1 rejects explaining it by the happiness it brings: Book I's function and Book II's causes are kept apart. Kind: explanatory holist (social facts on both sides), with a mechanism through competing individuals and a modest ontology. Against Spencer (Book I ch. 7), non-contractual relations grow alongside contract; the Conclusion keeps a common conscience: the cult of the person.

*Lessons:* [3.1](lessons/03-01-the-division-of-labor-and-social-solidarity.md)

### Collective conscience

The beliefs and sentiments common to the average members of a society, forming a system with its own life.

Book I ch. 2 (paraphrased): the same across regions and trades, outlasting generations, yet realized only in individuals; Durkheim leaves open whether it is a consciousness like an individual's. French *conscience* means both consciousness and conscience. In modern societies it shrinks and grows more general, but the cult of the person remains. Crime is defined relative to it: an act offending strong, defined states of the collective conscience; "it is a crime because we condemn it". 3.3 says where its force comes from and how it is renewed.

*Lessons:* [3.1](lessons/03-01-the-division-of-labor-and-social-solidarity.md)

### Repressive and restitutive law

Durkheim's index: law that punishes tracks mechanical solidarity; law that repairs tracks organic solidarity.

Solidarity is invisible, so "substitute for the internal fact that escapes us an external fact that symbolizes it … This visible symbol is law" (Book I ch. 1, my translation); compared to studying heat through the expansion of bodies. Law is classified by sanction: **repressive** (pain on the offender; penal law) vs **restitutive** (restore the normal state: performance, damages, annulment; civil, commercial, procedural, administrative, constitutional law). The index argument (Book I), as the lesson reconstructs it:

1. Solidarity cannot be observed or measured directly.
2. Lasting relations take organized form, law most stably, so law varies with the relations it regulates ("necessarily proportional").
3. Repressive sanctions answer offenses against strong collective sentiments; restitutive ones restore relations between parties who differ.
4. Custom diverging from law is rare and pathological.
5. ∴ The relative extent of the two kinds of law measures the two solidarities.
6. The oldest codes are almost all repressive (his Pentateuch count); modern codes are mostly restitutive.
7. ∴ Organic solidarity has progressively displaced mechanical.

**Weak point:** 2 and 4, the bridge from solidarity to law (law may track power, state organization, or which societies wrote things down); calling every awkward case pathological makes the index unfalsifiable. Evidence: Schwartz and Miller, "Legal Evolution and Societal Complexity" (*American Journal of Sociology* 70, 1964): across societies, damages and mediation appear early, police and counsel later, the reverse of Durkheim's order; Baxi (*Law & Society Review* 1974) argued this does not definitively refute Durkheim, and Schwartz replied in the same issue.

*Lessons:* [3.1](lessons/03-01-the-division-of-labor-and-social-solidarity.md)

### Anomie

A lack of regulation: of the relations between specialized functions (3.1), and of desires and expectations (3.2).

Origin (Book III ch. 1): the anomic division of labor, specialized functions with no rules adjusting them (commercial crises, bankruptcies, labor-capital conflict), because rules form only from sufficient, sustained contact between functions; a producer serving a vast unseen market lacks it. Remedy (2nd-ed. preface): occupational groups. In *Le suicide* it becomes a cause of a rate: suicide rises after crashes **and** booms (Italy, 29 per million in 1864-70 to 40.6 in 1877 as industry took off), so not poverty; poverty may even protect. Anomie is not hardship.

*Lessons:* [3.1](lessons/03-01-the-division-of-labor-and-social-solidarity.md), [3.2](lessons/03-02-suicide.md)

### Anomic and forced division of labor

Book III's abnormal forms: the division of labor binds only under conditions.

Anomic: no regulation between functions (too little contact). Forced: tasks assigned by external constraint, not aptitude, producing class war. Solidarity needs spontaneity, which requires equality in the external conditions of competition. Durkheim's rival to Marx on class conflict: for Marx it is built into capitalist relations; for Durkheim it is an abnormal form, removable by equalizing external conditions. Both forms are explanatory claims about when specialization binds, not verdicts on modern society.

*Lessons:* [3.1](lessons/03-01-the-division-of-labor-and-social-solidarity.md)

### Four types of suicide

Integration and regulation can each fail in two directions.

| Axis | Too little | Too much |
|---|---|---|
| Integration | **Egoistic**: own ends the only ones; Protestants, the unmarried | **Altruistic**: self counts for too little; soldiers, duty-bound deaths |
| Regulation | **Anomic**: desires unlimited after sudden change; crashes, booms, divorce | **Fatalistic**: "future pitilessly walled in", oppressive discipline; slaves (a footnote only, *Le suicide* Bk II ch. 5) |

Durkheim's definition turns on knowledge, not intention, so a soldier going to certain death counts. The army case strains the theory: Austria 1876-90, 1,253 per million soldiers against 122 civilians of the same age; Durkheim labels it altruistic. Without an independent measure of integration taken before the rates are seen, a high rate can be fitted in either direction; access to weapons and a fatalistic regime are rivals.

*Lessons:* [3.2](lessons/03-02-suicide.md)

### Integration and regulation

Durkheim's two axes in *Le suicide* (1897), Book II chs. 2-5.

Integration: how far a group draws members into common beliefs, practices and ends. Regulation: how far it limits their desires. Each axis is U-shaped: the rate is lowest at a middle level. The integration claim, in the Source passage (Book II ch. 3 §VI, my translation): religious, domestic and political society all protect when strongly integrated, so the cause "can only lie in a property that all these social groups possess"; "suicide varies inversely with the degree of integration of the social groups of which the individual is a part". The form is Mill's method of agreement; "only" can be contested by naming another shared property (such as how deaths were recorded). The argument runs from religious groups (Protestants above Catholics though both forbid suicide; *libre examen*), families (the married, especially with children, lower) and political crises (rates fell in 1830, 1848, 1870-71) to integration as the common property, with a mechanism through individuals (a weakly integrated group leaves members only their own ends). Boss 3 asks for the full premise reconstruction, so the card stays at this outline. Kind: explanatory holist, not functional. **Weak points:** the step from area rates to individual mechanism ([ecological fallacy](#ecological-fallacy)); official data ([recording bias](#recording-bias)); integration never measured independently.

*Lessons:* [3.2](lessons/03-02-suicide.md)

### Coefficient of preservation

Durkheim's ratio: the rate of a comparison group divided by the rate of the protected group at the same age.

E.g. unmarried men's rate over married men's rate, France 1889-91, by age band; below 1 it is a coefficient of aggravation. Durkheim uses its shape across ages as a test against selection into marriage: if selection were the cause, he argues, the coefficient should start small and peak when sorting into marriage is complete. His printed coefficients differ slightly from ratios of his printed rates; compute from the rates.

*Lessons:* [3.2](lessons/03-02-suicide.md)

### Recording bias

The rival that official suicide counts depend on who classifies a death, and integrated groups may hide suicides.

Jack Douglas, *The Social Meanings of Suicide* (1967): coroners, doctors and families decide; tightly knit communities are better placed to keep a death off the books, so integration may lower the *recorded* rate. Frans van Poppel and Lincoln Day (*ASR* 1996), with Dutch individual death records: much of the religious gap reflects classification (more Catholic deaths filed as sudden or of ill-defined cause). Miles Simpson (*ASR* 1998) disputed their comparison. Status: contested. Durkheim's own reply in one case: recording was harder in the countryside, so under-recording should show up there.

*Lessons:* [3.2](lessons/03-02-suicide.md)

### Sacred and profane

Two absolutely heterogeneous worlds, kept apart by interdicts.

Not higher versus lower: slaves are inferior to masters and nobody calls that religious; a miser making "a religion" of his gold is metaphor. The mark is absolute heterogeneity. Works without gods (Buddhism), which is why "belief in God" cannot define religion. A thing can be prized without being sacred.

*Lessons:* [3.3](lessons/03-03-religion-as-social-the-elementary-forms.md)

### Durkheims definition of religion

*The Elementary Forms of the Religious Life* (1912; Swain translation 1915), Book I ch. 1.

"A unified system of beliefs and practices relative to sacred things, that is to say, things set apart and forbidden … which unite into one single moral community called a Church, all those who adhere to them." "Church" = any moral community of the faithful, not a Christian institution. It separates religion from magic: a magician has clients, and "there is no Church of magic". Religion is "something eminently social".

*Lessons:* [3.3](lessons/03-03-religion-as-social-the-elementary-forms.md)

### Totemism

Each clan bears the name of an animal or plant; the emblem is more sacred than the animal.

Durkheim chose Central Australia as the simplest case, using Spencer and Gillen, *Native Tribes of Central Australia* (1899) and *Northern Tribes* (1904), checked against Carl Strehlow on the Arunta (Aranda). The *churinga*, engraved with the totem's design and hidden from women and the uninitiated, are more sacred than the totem itself. The emblem is the god's sign and the clan's flag; Durkheim's inference is that the god of the clan is the clan itself, personified. Critics: van Gennep (1913 review, 1920 survey: Australian societies are not simple; peoples with simpler technology have no totems; clans without totems); Evans-Pritchard (*Theories of Primitive Religion*, 1965), following Lowie: hordes and tribes, not clans, acted together; Stanner (1967, Murinbata): their religion was not totemism and society was not its real object. Evidence strength: the origin claim about Australian totemism is widely judged unsupported by later fieldwork.

*Lessons:* [3.3](lessons/03-03-religion-as-social-the-elementary-forms.md)

### Collective effervescence

Heightened feeling in periodic assemblies, caused by the group but felt as an external force.

Australian life alternates dispersal (small groups, profane life) and concentration (ceremonies lasting days or weeks). The force is fixed on the emblem and renewed at each rite. Source (Book II ch. 7 §III, Swain, after a Warramunga fire ceremony): "everything is just as though he really were transported into a special world … How could such experiences … fail to leave in him the conviction that there really exist two heterogeneous and mutually incomparable worlds?"

Durkheim's explanation of religion, as the lesson reconstructs it:

1. Nothing in ordinary experience suggests the absolute sacred/profane split.
2. Assemblies produce feeling caused by the group but felt as an external superior force.
3. Repeated and contrasted with dispersal, it yields the conviction that two worlds exist.
4. The force is fixed on the nearest concrete symbol of the group, its emblem.
5. The emblem stands for both the totemic principle and the clan.
6. ∴ The sacred power is the clan personified; religious representations are collective representations of society.
7. ∴ (functional) Rites persist because they periodically re-create the sentiments the group's unity depends on.

Kind: explanatory holist and functional, with a named [feedback mechanism](#feedback-mechanism); pointedly not interpretive (believers' reasons are "generally mistaken"). **Weak points:** 2-3 (Goldenweiser 1915: crowd excitement alone makes no religion; secular dances never become rites; effervescence presupposes the sacred) and 4-5 (Evans-Pritchard, Lowie). The narrower mechanism lives on in Randall Collins's interaction-ritual theory, with some lab support (Wiltermuth and Heath, *Psychological Science* 2009: synchrony raised cooperation); one set of studies, scaling contested.

*Lessons:* [3.3](lessons/03-03-religion-as-social-the-elementary-forms.md)

### Reductionism objection

An explanatory objection: society cannot account for all of religion.

Doctrinal content, the solitary mystic, universal faiths that break with the believer's group. Durkheim's Conclusion replies that individual and universalist religion reflect the individual and international groups of later societies. Distinct from the truth question ([genetic fallacy question](#genetic-fallacy-question)). Malinowski's 1913 review and later critics also ask whether crowd psychology fits the *Rules*' demand that social facts be explained by social facts: a consistency question, not proof of individualism.

*Lessons:* [3.3](lessons/03-03-religion-as-social-the-elementary-forms.md)

### Genetic fallacy question

Whether a correct social explanation of a belief bears on its truth or warrant. This course flags it and never settles it.

Durkheim says the believer is not deceived about a moral power on which he depends, but misidentifies it; every religion is "true in its own way". Two readings the lessons record without choosing: a theist can accept the mechanism as the route by which belief arises (as neural causes of seeing a tree do not exclude the tree); a naturalist can argue an explanation that never mentions God leaves belief in God without evidential support. The question is symmetric: a social explanation of *secular* belief raises it too. Where it recurs: 3.3 (effervescence), 4.4 (the Lourdes bureau: a medical finding versus a bishop's judgment), 7.1 (plausibility structures), 7.2 (explaining churchgoing by supply or insecurity says nothing about truth), 7.3 (secularity 3 describes how belief is held, not whether it is true). Owned by [philosophy-of-religion 3.4](../philosophy-of-religion/lessons/03-04-the-argument-from-religious-experience.md) (projection theory) and [5.5](../philosophy-of-religion/lessons/05-05-religious-diversity.md) (the birthplace argument), and [philosophical-method 3.4](../philosophical-method/lessons/03-04-intuitions-and-reflective-equilibrium.md) (when debunking works).

## Weber

Ideas, interests and the making of the modern West. Method (Verstehen, ideal types, the two adequacies) is in [Kinds of explanation and evidence](#kinds-of-explanation-and-evidence).

### Spirit of capitalism

Weber's explanandum: acquisition as a duty and an end in itself, pursued methodically, with enjoyment refused.

Not acquisitiveness, which is found in every age. Franklin ("Remember, that time is money") against Jacob Fugger, who wanted to make money as long as he could (commercial daring, morally neutral). Weber, *PE* ch. II (Parsons): "The infraction of its rules is treated not as foolishness but as forgetfulness of duty. … It is not mere business astuteness, that sort of thing is common enough, it is an ethos." A prudential rule broken makes you a fool; an ethic broken makes you guilty. From the individual's happiness the maxim is irrational: work to accumulate, do not consume. Franklin, a deist, is the ethic without the theology. The starting clue, not the explanandum: Baden 1895, taxable capital of 954,000 marks per 1,000 Protestants against 589,000 per 1,000 Catholics (Offenbacher 1901, in Weber's footnote; reliability is 4.2's question). Florence vs Pennsylvania (ch. II): capital-rich Florence tolerated acquisition, backwoods Pennsylvania commanded it, so the ethic is no simple reflection of local commerce ("patent nonsense"); two chosen cases cannot show where the ethic came from.

*Lessons:* [4.1](lessons/04-01-the-protestant-ethic-and-the-spirit-of-capitalism.md), [4.2](lessons/04-02-the-protestant-ethic-on-trial.md)

### The calling

Luther's *Beruf*: a worldly occupation as a task set by God, duty in it the highest moral activity.

On Weber's account Luther's Bible translation gave the word its modern sense; monastic withdrawal was no longer superior to ordinary work. Luther's own version was traditionalist: stay in the station God gave you. Weber gives Lutheranism a minor role in the thesis.

*Lessons:* [4.1](lessons/04-01-the-protestant-ethic-and-the-spirit-of-capitalism.md)

### Predestination and the anxiety of proof

An unchangeable, unknowable decree left believers anxious; pastoral advice turned methodical work into the sign of election.

Westminster Confession as Weber quotes it: "By the decree of God, for the manifestation of His glory, some men and angels are predestinated unto everlasting life, and others foreordained to everlasting death." No priest, sacrament or church can change it: "inner loneliness". Pastoral answer: it is a duty to consider oneself chosen (doubt is temptation), and intense worldly activity drives doubt out. Works are signs, not causes: "the technical means, not of purchasing salvation, but of getting rid of the fear of damnation." Calvin himself rejected inferring election from conduct.

*Lessons:* [4.1](lessons/04-01-the-protestant-ethic-and-the-spirit-of-capitalism.md)

### Worldly asceticism

Methodical, whole-life self-control inside a worldly calling.

Baxter's *Christian Directory*: waste of time the deadliest sin; even the rich must work; wealth dangerous only as a temptation to idleness and enjoyment; profit in a calling a sign of blessing. A life under control, not good deeds offset by confession. Acquisition released plus consumption restrained yields accumulation, and gives profit-seeking a good conscience.

*Lessons:* [4.1](lessons/04-01-the-protestant-ethic-and-the-spirit-of-capitalism.md)

### Elective affinity

Weber's *Wahlverwandtschaften* between forms of religious belief and the ethic of the calling: a fit between ideas and conduct, each drawn to the other, not a law.

**Parsons never writes "elective affinity"**: he renders the word as "certain correlations" (ch. III). "Elective affinity" is later English usage. Cite it as Weber's concept in later translation, not as Parsons's wording.

*Lessons:* [4.1](lessons/04-01-the-protestant-ethic-and-the-spirit-of-capitalism.md)

### Protestant ethic thesis

At Weber's claimed strength: ascetic Protestantism was *one* contributor to the formation and spread of the spirit of capitalism.

Weber's chain (*PE*, 1904-05; Parsons 1930), as the lesson reconstructs it:

1. Modern Western capitalism has a distinctive spirit (acquisition as duty, methodical, enjoyment refused).
2. Luther's *Beruf* made duty in a worldly calling the highest moral activity.
3. Calvinist predestination produced intense anxiety about election.
4. Pastoral practice treated systematic work as the sign of election: a "psychological sanction".
5. That sanction produced worldly asceticism.
6. Acquisition plus restrained consumption yields saving, reinvestment and a good conscience about profit.
7. ∴ Ascetic Protestantism (Calvinism, Pietism, Methodism, Baptists) was one cause of the formation and spread of the spirit; not the only cause, not a cause of capitalism as a system.

Ch. III rejects "such a foolish and doctrinaire thesis … as that … capitalism as an economic system is a creation of the Reformation"; important capitalist business forms are older. German *mit beteiligt*, letter-spaced: religion took part *along with* other forces (Parsons drops the emphasis). Boss 4 asks for the thesis at the strength the closing pages allow, so the card does not restate those pages. Kind: interpretive first (3-4 are meaning-adequacy), explanatorily individualist, **not functional**: market selection explains the ethic's spread, not its origin. **Weak point:** 4, reconstructed from prescriptive sources (what pastors urged, not what merchants felt); defenders reply that the pastoral literature answered real parishioners' questions. Keep apart from the starting fact and the folk version (see the map below).

*Lessons:* [4.1](lessons/04-01-the-protestant-ethic-and-the-spirit-of-capitalism.md), [4.2](lessons/04-02-the-protestant-ethic-on-trial.md)

### Tawneys reversal

R. H. Tawney, *Religion and the Rise of Capitalism* (1926), and his foreword to Parsons's translation (1930): the Puritan ethic was shaped by commerce as much as it shaped it.

As the lesson reconstructs it: (1) capitalist enterprise and spirit flourished in 14th-century Venice and Florence and 15th-century Antwerp, before Calvin; (2) Calvin's Geneva put economic life under strict discipline ("iron collectivism"); early Calvinism was collectivist; (3) Weber's pious businessman comes mostly from later-17th-century English Puritans, three generations after Calvin, by then reconciled to prosperity; (4) commercial expansion and the rise of commercial classes came between, and Catholic writers shifted too; ∴ the ethic was moulded by economic change as much as it moulded it. Not mirror-image materialism: Tawney calls the pure economic derivation equally one-sided; his model is "action and reaction". He grants the association and Weber's reconstruction of meaning; he attacks causal adequacy and the arrow's direction. **Weak point:** 4 to C (co-movement is not causation); and 1 may equivocate on "spirit", though Tawney replies that Weber narrows "capitalism" to suit his case, leaving the dispute partly definitional.

*Lessons:* [4.2](lessons/04-02-the-protestant-ethic-on-trial.md)

### Literacy explanation

Becker and Woessmann, "Was Weber Wrong? A Human Capital Theory of Protestant Economic History" (*QJE* 2009): Bible reading raised literacy, and literacy raised prosperity.

All 452 Prussian counties; literacy from the 1871 census; outcomes from the 1870s-80s (income tax per head, teachers' incomes, non-agricultural share). Income-tax revenue per head 9.1% higher in mostly-Protestant counties. Instrument: distance to Wittenberg (the Reformation spread in rough rings); supported by distance being unrelated to schools, universities, monasteries and urbanization before 1517. Finding: literacy accounts for practically the whole Protestant economic lead. Strength in words: the association is well documented; the channel claim rests on one influential study and an exclusion restriction no statistic can prove. Cautions: county aggregates ([ecological fallacy](#ecological-fallacy)); about 94% of Prussian Protestants were Lutheran before the 1817 union. It targets the starting fact, and finds that Protestantism *did* raise prosperity, through another channel.

*Lessons:* [4.2](lessons/04-02-the-protestant-ethic-on-trial.md)

### Cantoni city size test

Cantoni, "The Economic Effects of the Protestant Reformation" (*JEEA* 2015): Protestant and Catholic cities grew alike.

272 Holy Roman Empire cities, 1300-1900. After Augsburg (1555) princes chose each territory's religion, guarding against [selection](#selection). City population as Malthusian proxy for prosperity. A precise null that rules out sizeable effects, robust to an IV version and to counting only the 21 Reformed cities as Protestant (163 Lutheran). Strength: one careful study, a precise null on one outcome. Targets something near the folk version.

*Lessons:* [4.2](lessons/04-02-the-protestant-ethic-on-trial.md)

### Power and domination

Power is any chance of imposing your will; domination is a standing relationship of command and obedience.

*E&S* Kap. I §16 (my translation): *Macht* is any chance of carrying through one's will against resistance, whatever it rests on (a mugger has it); *Herrschaft* is "the chance of finding obedience for a command of specific content among specifiable persons". Every genuine domination involves some minimum will to obey. Weber's definition of the state (Kap. I §17, monopoly of legitimate physical coercion) belongs to comparative-politics.

*Lessons:* [4.3](lessons/04-03-domination-and-the-three-types-of-authority.md)

### Legitimacy as belief

Rule lasts when it is believed rightful; Weber's legitimacy is a fact about belief, not a verdict.

Kap. III §1: custom, interest, affect and value commitment each motivate obedience, but none alone is reliable (bonds of pure interest are relatively unstable); normally a belief in legitimacy is added, and every domination seeks to cultivate it. Descriptive ("a belief that stabilizes domination"). Names a mechanism (rulers deliberately cultivating belief), so not a bare benefit claim. **Weak point:** compliance may need no belief in rightfulness. Michael Mann (*ASR* 1970): subordinate classes in liberal democracies accept the order pragmatically. David Beetham (*The Legitimation of Power*, 1991): if legitimacy just is belief, a regime that manufactures belief becomes legitimate by definition; legitimacy should be justifiability by the subjects' own standards. Testing problem: belief inferred from compliance cannot explain compliance. Status: contested. Whether anyone *ought* to obey is political-philosophy 1.1.

*Lessons:* [4.3](lessons/04-03-domination-and-the-three-types-of-authority.md)

### Three types of authority

Kap. III §2 (my translation): "There are three pure types of legitimate domination", by the primary ground of their legitimacy.

| Type | Belief | Obeyed | Staff |
|---|---|---|---|
| Legal-rational | "the legality of enacted orders" | The impersonal order and whoever it appoints | Officials (bureaucracy) |
| Traditional | "the everyday belief in the sanctity of traditions valid from of old" | The person tradition calls, within tradition's bounds | Retainers, kin, favourites |
| Charismatic | "extraordinary devotion to the sanctity or the heroic power or the exemplary character of a person" | The leader as such | Disciples, a following |

"Primarily": mixtures are normal; classify by the primary ground and say what the secondary ones add. These are [ideal types](#ideal-types). The argument (§§1-2): domination needs obedience; no motive alone is reliable; belief in legitimacy is added and cultivated; the kind of legitimacy claimed fixes the kind of obedience, staff and rule; ∴ classify by claim to legitimacy. The classification is not yet an explanation; the explanatory claims are that belief stabilizes domination and that succession forces routinization.

*Lessons:* [4.3](lessons/04-03-domination-and-the-three-types-of-authority.md)

### Charismatic authority

Authority resting on an extraordinary quality as judged by the followers.

§10: value-free; the gift's "objective" worth is indifferent (Weber names the Mormon founder as a case treated value-free). It must be proved (*Bewaehrung*): if the leader's rule brings no well-being, it is likely to vanish. The great revolutionary force in tradition-bound epochs. Plebiscitary leadership is charisma under democratic form. Not charm or popularity: a well-liked mayor obeyed because she holds office is legal-rational.

*Lessons:* [4.3](lessons/04-03-domination-and-the-three-types-of-authority.md)

### Routinization of charisma

*Veralltaeglichung*, "making everyday": lasting charisma becomes traditionalized or legalized.

§11: the followers' and especially the staff's ideal and material interests in a durable footing become acute at succession; charisma "becomes traditionalized or rationalized (legalized) or both". Six solutions: (a) search by marks (the Dalai Lama), (b) oracle or lot, (c) designation by the leader, recognized by the community, (d) designation by the qualified staff, recognized by the community (a duty to identify the right one, not a majority election), (e) hereditary charisma, (f) office charisma (transferable by anointing or ordination). Case: Latter Day Saints after Joseph Smith's death (1844). The regularity fares better than the legitimacy claim but rests on case material open to [selection](#selection): movements that never routinized vanished from the record.

*Lessons:* [4.3](lessons/04-03-domination-and-the-three-types-of-authority.md)

### Ideal type of bureaucracy

The purest form of legal authority: rule through a bureaucratic staff (*E&S* Part I ch. III §§3-5).

Features: (1) rules and fixed competences; (2) hierarchy with appeal and discipline; (3) office as vocation: appointed on certified qualification, money salary by rank, career, main occupation; (4) separation: no ownership of the means of administration or the post; bureau apart from home; (5) files. Ethos (§5, my translation): "sine ira et studio, without hatred and passion … 'without regard to the person', formally equal for 'everyone'". The top is never purely bureaucratic: ministers are officials only formally. A measuring rod, not a portrait or a recommendation.

Why it spreads (§5), as the lesson reconstructs it:

1. File-based bureaucracy is "by all experience" the most precise, stable and calculable form.
2. Mass administration requires calculability (capitalism created the need; a socialism would need more).
3. Organizations compete and the less calculable lose: "bureaucratization" or "dilettantization".
4. The advantage is expertise ("domination by virtue of knowledge", guarded as official secrets), so the apparatus is indispensable and serves revolutions and occupiers alike.
5. ∴ Bureaucratization advances wherever administration is on a mass scale; the open question is who controls it.

Kind: functional *with* a [feedback mechanism](#feedback-mechanism) (competition selects), plus an interest mechanism; explanatorily individualist. **Weak point:** 1 and with it 3. Merton, "Bureaucratic Structure and Personality" (*Social Forces* 1940): displacement of goals; and an inefficient ministry does not go bankrupt, so selection is weak for states. Evidence: Weberian recruitment and careers go with less corruption and faster growth across countries, robust correlation, contested direction (political-institutions 6.1).

*Lessons:* [4.4](lessons/04-04-bureaucracy-rationalization-and-disenchantment.md)

### Formal and substantive rationality

Formal: how far calculation is possible and applied. Substantive: how far outcomes meet some value standard.

*E&S* Part I ch. II §9. Formal rationality is relatively unambiguous; substantive (*materiale*) rationality is plural, one per value postulate (ethical, political, egalitarian, status, any other). They come apart, an "antinomy". Inside bureaucracy: the governed demand formalism (the alternative is arbitrariness), officials lean to substantive, utilitarian handling, which ends in demands for new rules. Formal does not mean efficient or sensible; it means calculable. Simmel's money as calculability (5.2) is formal rationality in exchange.

*Lessons:* [4.4](lessons/04-04-bureaucracy-rationalization-and-disenchantment.md)

### Iron cage

**Parsons's 1930 rendering of *stahlhartes Gehaeuse*, not Weber's words.**

The phrase occurs in the closing pages of *The Protestant Ethic* (Parsons: "fate decreed that the cloak should become an iron cage"). Peter Baehr, "The 'Iron Cage' and the 'Shell as Hard as Steel'" (*History and Theory* 2001), proposes "shell as hard as steel": steel is a human product, and a shell, unlike a cage, suggests an order that remakes the creature inside rather than merely confining it. Cite "iron cage" as Parsons's phrase. What the closing pages claim about rationalization is Boss 4's question.

*Lessons:* [4.4](lessons/04-04-bureaucracy-rationalization-and-disenchantment.md)

### Disenchantment

*Entzauberung der Welt*: not more knowledge, but the belief that no incalculable powers are in principle at play.

"Science as a Vocation" (lecture, Munich, 7 November 1917; published 1919), my translation: rationalization "does not … mean an increasing general knowledge of the conditions of life … It means … the knowledge or the belief that, if one only wanted to, one could find out at any time; that there are therefore in principle no mysterious, incalculable powers at play … That, however, means: the disenchantment of the world." The tram passenger knows less about his tools than the "savage" (Weber's period word) did, yet can count on them. Test: what you reach for to get something done; technical means replace magic. Later in the lecture the highest values withdraw from public life into mystical experience or personal relations, and the old gods, now impersonal powers, resume their quarrel. In *PE* ch. IV Parsons renders it "elimination of magic from the world". Kind: interpretive. It does not say religion declines or is false. Uses: Weber's first root of secularization theory, read as privatization not extinction (7.1); Taylor's porous-to-buffered shift is disenchantment seen from inside a life (7.3).

*Lessons:* [4.4](lessons/04-04-bureaucracy-rationalization-and-disenchantment.md), [7.1](lessons/07-01-classical-secularization-theory.md), [7.3](lessons/07-03-taylors-a-secular-age.md)

## Simmel and Polanyi

What money does to social relations, and whether markets can stand free of the society around them.

### Form and content

Content is what brings people together; form is the pattern of their interaction. Simmel claims they vary independently.

Content: hunger, love, profit, faith, curiosity. Form: superiority and subordination, competition, party structure, insider and outsider. A church, guild, gang and family can share "one leader, many followers"; "make money" can take the form of competition or cartel. Sociology studies forms, as geometry studies shapes whatever they are made of. Inseparable in reality, separated by abstraction. Simmel's own justifying test (Small's translation, *AJS* 15, 1909, p. 299): "similar forms of socialization occur with quite dissimilar content … and per contra … interests similar in content clothe themselves in quite unlike forms": a comparison design (hold form fixed, vary content). Number is the cleanest form: a group of n has n(n-1)/2 pairs (1 for two people, 3 for three, 45 for ten, 4,950 for a hundred), so mutual monitoring easy at 3 is impossible at 4,950. In 5.2 form analysis is applied to money, the most content-free thing.

*Lessons:* [5.1](lessons/05-01-simmel-sociation-and-the-forms-of-social-life.md), [5.2](lessons/05-02-simmels-philosophy-of-money.md)

### Sociation

*Vergesellschaftung*, "society-making": society is nothing but reciprocal influence among individuals.

From a group out walking to a medieval guild, more or less intense. **Albion Small (1909) renders it "socialization"** (his footnote: "His word here is Vergesellschaftungen"); later English usually says "sociation", avoiding the child-rearing sense. Ontologically individualist: society is not a thing over and above people.

*Lessons:* [5.1](lessons/05-01-simmel-sociation-and-the-forms-of-social-life.md)

### Dyad and triad

A group of two and a group of three differ in kind, not degree.

"The Number of Members as Determining the Sociological Form of the Group" (*AJS* 8, 1902, Small): in the dyad "the departure of each single individual would destroy the whole"; in a triad "each individual element operates as a court of appeal between the two others … both in combining and separating". The number thesis, as the lesson reconstructs it:

1. Some features of a relationship depend only on number and position, not on why people are together.
2. In a dyad each faces only the other; either's exit ends it.
3. So no majority, arbiter or anonymous collective responsibility; intimacy and all-or-nothing stakes (the chief seat of jealousy).
4. A third creates indirect ties that bind or split.
5. Three roles become possible: non-partisan or mediator; *tertius gaudens* (profits from a quarrel he did not start; a small pivotal party "turns the scale"); *divide et impera* (creates the quarrel to rule).
6. ∴ Some outcomes are explained by number and position, whatever the motives.

Kind: individualist ontology, configuration does the explaining; not functional (what a position *allows*, not why mediators exist). **Weak point:** 1; Simmel's own examples show content overriding form (the woman courted by two rivals cannot exploit the *tertius* position), so failures can be blamed on content. Defender: write in scope conditions and test (Caplow 1956 on triad coalitions; Vinacke and Arkoff 1957 in the lab; small-group evidence). The voting-power idea (Shapley-Shubik) states the pivotal third formally.

*Lessons:* [5.1](lessons/05-01-simmel-sociation-and-the-forms-of-social-life.md)

### The stranger

"The one who comes today and stays tomorrow" (*Soziologie*, 1908, excursus; the lesson's translation).

Not the wanderer who comes and goes: a member who did not belong from the start, near in space, far in origin. From the position alone Simmel derives: historically the stranger appears as the **trader** (local positions on the land are taken); he is credited with **objectivity** (no kinship or faction binds him; Italian cities hired outside judges, the podesta); people **confide** in him. A position, not a verdict on outsiders. In *Philosophie des Geldes* (1900, pp. 207-210) money is long the business of strangers, people outside the circle of personal ties.

*Lessons:* [5.1](lessons/05-01-simmel-sociation-and-the-forms-of-social-life.md), [5.2](lessons/05-02-simmels-philosophy-of-money.md)

### Money as pure means

Money has no use of its own except conversion into other things: "the purest form of the tool".

*Philosophie des Geldes* (1900), ch. 3, p. 191 (my translation): "a social institution into which the individual pours his doing or having, to reach through this point of passage ends inaccessible to his effort aimed at them directly." Abstraction and calculability follow: every good has a price, qualitative differences become more and less; the modern mind is "calculating" (ch. 6, pp. 472-473), a cousin of Weber's formal rationality. Simmel sets aside money's origin as history's business (preface, p. viii), so the thesis is about consequences of a form, not functional. The preface aims to build "a storey beneath historical materialism" (p. x).

*Lessons:* [5.2](lessons/05-02-simmels-philosophy-of-money.md)

### Money and freedom

A money claim reaches only a sum, so it leaves the debtor free how to earn it; dependence widens and depersonalizes.

Ch. 4 ranks obligations by reach into the person: labor itself (the serf's unmeasured services) → a specific product (a tenth sheaf, a quantity of honey) → a sum of money. The lord who demands honey decides what the peasant produces; with money rent he may keep bees or cattle as he likes (pp. 279-283). Simmel's synthetic thesis (chs. 4-6), as the lesson reconstructs it:

1. Money is a pure means.
2. A money claim reaches only a sum.
3. Divisibility lets each depend on very many others, each replaceable.
4. So each relation narrows to its objective performance; the person behind it becomes replaceable.
5. Money works only while holders trust the wider circle.
6. ∴ More dependent on society as a whole, less on any particular person: freedom grows as personal bonds thin.
7. ∴ The freedom is largely negative; the style of life grows calculating and impersonal.

The bill (ch. 5): Oldenburg cottagers (*Heuermann*) paid in wages felt declassed; threshers paid by harvest share cared for the estate, the money wage gave more income and less self-respect (pp. 419-420); freedom is "an empty form" (p. 421); selling a farm yields freedom in an almost purely negative sense (p. 422). Simmel argues both sides; the evidence is illustration, not controlled comparison, though testable (monitoring should fall and partner-switching rise when obligations are monetized).

*Lessons:* [5.2](lessons/05-02-simmels-philosophy-of-money.md)

### Objectification of relations

Money's divisibility and indifference reduce each relation to its impersonal performance; the person becomes interchangeable.

P. 294 (my translation): "On how many 'suppliers' alone … does the person of the money economy depend! But of any single, particular one of them he is incomparably more independent, and changes him easily and as often as he likes." **Main objection:** Viviana Zelizer, *The Social Meaning of Money* (1994): American households, charities and courts around the turn of the twentieth century earmark money (pin money, allowance, gifts of cash, poor relief); social relations shape money as much as money flattens them; impersonality belongs to certain institutions (anonymous markets), not money as such. Defender: earmarking happens inside households and gifts; Simmel's claim concerns money obligations replacing personal bonds elsewhere.

*Lessons:* [5.2](lessons/05-02-simmels-philosophy-of-money.md)

### Money rests on trust

Even metal money is a promise; it differs from a cheque only in the size of the circle that guarantees it.

Ch. 2 §III, pp. 148-150 (my translation): "metal money, too, is a promise, and … it differs from a cheque only in the size of the circle that guarantees its redemption"; money exchange has three terms: buyer, seller and the social circle; "The feeling of personal security that the possession of money gives is perhaps the most concentrated and pointed form and expression of trust in the organization and order of state and society." The Maltese inscription *non aes sed fides*. The analogy of credit with religious faith concerns the *form* of trust only; it implies nothing about whether faith is warranted. Simmel denies the credit-versus-sound-metal contrast.

*Lessons:* [5.2](lessons/05-02-simmels-philosophy-of-money.md)

### Embeddedness

Two senses: Polanyi's institutional embeddedness and Granovetter's relational embeddedness.

**Polanyi** (*The Great Transformation*, 1944, chs. 4-5): for most of history goods moved by reciprocity, redistribution and householding, inside kinship, religion and politics, with markets as hedged adjuncts. Market society reverses this: "instead of the economy being embedded in social relations, social relations are embedded in the economy". Ch. 1 calls the self-adjusting market a "stark utopia" that could not exist for long without annihilating society: disembedding was a project never finished. **Granovetter**, "Economic Action and Social Structure: The Problem of Embeddedness" (*AJS* 1985): economic action is embedded in ongoing networks of personal relations that generate trust and deter cheating; against the "undersocialized" actor of economics and the "oversocialized" norm-follower; embeddedness was lower in nonmarket societies, and changed less with modernization, than Polanyi's school claimed, and remains substantial in modern markets. Granovetter's networks lead into social capital (6.3).

*Lessons:* [5.3](lessons/05-03-polanyis-great-transformation.md)

### Self-regulating market

A system of markets in which prices alone organize production, including its inputs: labor, land, money.

Not markets as such (they always existed) but a system requiring a market society. Built by the state: "laissez-faire was planned; planning was not" (ch. 12). Polanyi treats the Poor Law Amendment Act 1834, Peel's Bank Act 1844 and Corn Law repeal 1846 as one package; the protective reaction arose piecemeal, in many places, without a shared ideology.

*Lessons:* [5.3](lessons/05-03-polanyis-great-transformation.md)

### Fictitious commodities

Labor, land and money are traded as if produced for sale, though none is.

Ch. 6: a commodity is defined empirically as something produced for sale. Labor is human activity that comes with life and cannot be detached from the person; land is nature; money is a token of purchasing power created by banking or state finance. The fiction is not that they are not traded (they are, daily) but that they were *produced for sale*. Left unchecked, the fiction lets the market dispose of people, landscapes and firms like cloth. Polanyi's ch. 6 footnote: unrelated to Marx's commodity fetishism. Philosophy-of-economics 5.1 cites it and owns whether commodification corrupts.

*Lessons:* [5.3](lessons/05-03-polanyis-great-transformation.md)

### Double movement

Market expansion in genuine commodities, plus a protective countermovement restricting markets in the fictitious ones.

End of ch. 6 and ch. 11. Protection: factory acts and unions (labor), land laws and agrarian tariffs (land), central banking (money). Two organizing principles: economic liberalism (trading classes; laissez-faire, free trade) vs social protection (chiefly workers and landowners; protective legislation, associations). Polanyi's explanation of the countermovement, as the lesson reconstructs it:

1. A self-regulating market requires labor, land and money to be traded as if produced for sale.
2. Left wholly to the market, they would destroy their own substance: people, nature, productive organization.
3. The most exposed groups press for protection; coalitions win by serving interests wider than their own (ch. 13).
4. So protective measures arose wherever the market advanced, under whatever ideology was to hand.
5. Each measure impaired self-regulation.
6. ∴ Market society generated its own countermovement; after 1918 the strain broke the system (gold standard collapse; fascism one exit; chs. 2, 20).

Kind: holist and [functional](#functional-explanation) ("society protected itself"; classes' parts "cut out" by functions of the whole, ch. 11). Candidate [feedback mechanism](#feedback-mechanism): political selection (3), thin: why society's protection rather than the best-organized group's? Test (ch. 12): similar factory laws under very different politics ([comparison](#comparison-and-concomitant-variation)). Status: similar legislation is well documented; that it shows a spontaneous protective reaction is contested. **Weak point:** 4's evidence: countries copied one another (diffusion), widened the suffrage as they industrialized (democratization confound), and "protection" is loose enough that almost anything counts (no failure condition). The liberal rival (Spencer, Sumner, Mises, Lippmann) accepts the countermovement and reads it as protectionism that wrecked a self-correcting market; the sides disagree about a counterfactual. Polanyi also makes an evaluative claim (protection was right); keep it apart. Interest-group explanations of protective law are the modelled rival (political-economy Module 4).

*Lessons:* [5.3](lessons/05-03-polanyis-great-transformation.md)

### Speenhamland

The Berkshire magistrates' bread scale of 6 May 1795, which Polanyi read as a "right to live" that blocked a labor market until 1834.

The scale, as the lesson states it: with the gallon loaf at 1 shilling, a poor man was to have 3 shillings a week from wages or the rates, each dependant 1s 6d; for every penny the loaf rose, 3d more for him and 1d more per dependant. Polanyi (chs. 7-8): never enacted, it spread widely; employers paid less knowing the parish would top up; productivity and self-respect collapsed; the New Poor Law of 1834 built a competitive labor market at savage cost. His evidence leaned on the 1834 Royal Commission report. Critics: Mark Blaug, "The Myth of the Old Poor Law and the Making of the New" (*Journal of Economic History* 1963); Fred Block and Margaret Somers, "In the Shadow of Speenhamland" (*Politics & Society* 2003), sympathetic to Polanyi's larger project, conclude from four decades of research that the policies could not have had the consequences attributed to them, and trace the story's use before the 1996 US welfare reform. On this episode the weight of later research runs against Polanyi. (Polanyi's printed second row of the scale is inconsistent with the per-penny rule; the lesson states only the rule.)

*Lessons:* [5.3](lessons/05-03-polanyis-great-transformation.md)

## Tocqueville, Coleman and Putnam

From Tocqueville's associations to rational-choice explanations of norms and the claim that networks and trust are a form of capital. Tocqueville as a political theorist is [history-of-political-thought 6.2](../history-of-political-thought/lessons/06-02-tocqueville-equality-of-conditions.md) and [6.3](../history-of-political-thought/lessons/06-03-tocqueville-tyranny-of-the-majority-and-soft-despotism.md).

### Equality of conditions

A social state with no hereditary ranks, in which families rise and fall and people regard one another as the same kind of person. Not equal wealth.

Reloaded from history-of-political-thought 6.2 as the macro starting point of the associations argument: equality abolishes the aristocrat's ready-made compulsory association of dependants, leaving everyone independent and individually weak.

*Lessons:* [6.1](lessons/06-01-tocqueville-on-associations.md)

### Individualism as withdrawal

Tocqueville's "individualism": a calm withdrawal into a small private circle that equality encourages.

*Democracy in America* Vol. 2, Part II, ch. 2 (Reeve): "a mature and calm feeling". A misjudgment of one's situation, not egotism or greed. Not methodological individualism (a claim about explanation) either.

*Lessons:* [6.1](lessons/06-01-tocqueville-on-associations.md)

### Art of association

The learned skill and taste for combining voluntarily: proposing a common object, keeping order among many, subordinating exertions to a common plan.

Vol. 2, Part II, chs. 5-7. Acquired by practice, part of mores; "the art of associating together must grow and improve" (ch. 5). Newspapers are its infrastructure (ch. 6, Reeve): many small people "cannot see, and know not where to find, one another"; a newspaper takes up a feeling that had occurred "simultaneously, but singly" and becomes a beacon. The obstacle is group size and visibility, not apathy: in modern terms (a gloss) a coordination problem with the newspaper as public signal. Tocqueville's argument (chs. 4-7), as the lesson reconstructs it:

1. Equality leaves most people independent and individually weak.
2. Equality disposes each to withdraw into a private circle.
3. Under equality, public ends need many to combine.
4. Shared interest does not by itself produce combination (scattered people cannot find one another; novices fear losses).
5. Combining is a learned skill and taste, part of mores.
6. Local self-government and political associations supply cheap, compulsory practice; necessity becomes habit, then taste; local powers multiply newspapers.
7. The skill transfers both ways between political and civil life.
8. ∴ With local liberty and free political association, equals build a dense web of associations that counters individualism; where suppressed, equality yields the isolated mass despotism needs.

Kind: macro to micro (weakness, learning) to macro, with mores as a collective habit no one chooses; claimed by both rational-choice and social-capital traditions. Not functional: associations serve democracy as a [latent function](#manifest-and-latent-functions). **Weak point:** 6-7: [selection](#selection) (the already cooperative join), and mores identified by elimination in a handful of cases (a residual category).

*Lessons:* [6.1](lessons/06-01-tocqueville-on-associations.md)

### Mores

Reeve renders *moeurs* as "manners", glossed with the ancients' *mores*: "the whole moral and intellectual condition of a people" (Vol. 1, ch. 17).

Not etiquette. Tocqueville ranks mores above laws, and laws above physical circumstances, as causes of American democratic stability, by comparison: South America (similar circumstances, no democracy), Mexico (a similar federal constitution), and East versus West within the US. Mores are identified partly by elimination: what circumstances and laws fail to explain.

*Lessons:* [6.1](lessons/06-01-tocqueville-on-associations.md)

### Civil and political associations

Civil: private or social ends (companies, schools, churches, charities). Political: aimed at the state (parties, conventions, campaigns).

Vol. 2, Part II, ch. 7 claims two-way causation: civil associations teach the habit politics uses; political associations are the better school (joining risks no money, and their size shows the weak what combination can do). Prediction: where political association is forbidden, civil associations will be few, feeble and badly run, whatever the law allows them. Tocqueville also held that unlimited political association brings a nation to the verge of anarchy. Vol. 1, ch. 12 (Reeve) on Europe: "The exercise of the right of association becomes dangerous in proportion to the impossibility which excludes great parties from acquiring the majority": a conditional claim, about political associations, matter of degree.

*Lessons:* [6.1](lessons/06-01-tocqueville-on-associations.md), [6.4](lessons/06-04-social-capital-on-trial.md)

### Colemans boat

James Coleman, *Foundations of Social Theory* (1990): a macro correlation is explained only by running it through individuals and back up.

Arrow 1, macro to micro: the situation shapes what individuals believe, want and can do. Arrow 2, micro to micro: purposive action. Arrow 3, micro to macro: choices combine into the system-level outcome. Hedstrom and Swedberg (*Social Mechanisms*, 1998) named them situational, action-formation and transformational mechanisms. Coleman's showcase is Weber: doctrine shapes values, values produce disciplined conduct, conduct adds up to capitalist organization (Coleman's compression; Weber's claim is narrower). Coleman's complaint: arrow 3 is the hardest and most often skipped. Weber agreed on origin: the ethic "had to originate somewhere … This origin is what really needs explanation" (*PE* ch. 2, Parsons). Explanatory, not ontological, individualism: Coleman admits corporate actors. Course uses: Coleman's dropout argument is one trip around it (6.3); classical secularization theory as macro (pluralism) to micro (belief held as choice, transmitted less) to macro (falling rate) (7.1).

*Lessons:* [6.2](lessons/06-02-rational-choice-sociology.md), [6.3](lessons/06-03-social-capital-coleman-and-putnam.md), [7.1](lessons/07-01-classical-secularization-theory.md)

### Norms as solutions to externalities

An action with externalities others cannot transact over creates a demand for a norm; a norm exists when control is held socially and backed by sanctions.

Coleman's explanation of a norm, as the lesson reconstructs it:

1. A macro pattern is explained only when all three boat links are supplied.
2. Individuals choose the action that best serves their goals, given beliefs and options.
3. Where an action imposes costs on others who cannot transact over it, they want control: demand for a norm.
4. A norm exists when that control is held socially and backed by sanctions; sanctions are supplied when relations are ongoing and closed.
5. In an ongoing relation, the threat to stop cooperating makes cooperation self-enforcing if people weigh the future enough ([grim trigger](#grim-trigger)).
6. ∴ Norms and the trust they support are equilibria among purposive people who expect to keep dealing with each other.

Sanctioning is itself a public good (the second-order free-rider problem); [closure](#closure) answers it. Kind: intentional at the micro level, causal in aggregation; it names its [feedback mechanism](#feedback-mechanism) (those harmed demand, those watching enforce), so not a disguised benefit claim. **Weak point:** 3-4. Elster (*The Cement of Society*, 1989): many norms serve no one (codes of revenge), and norms work through shame and guilt, not calculated sanctions. Colemanian reply: in closed networks sanctions are nearly free (gossip, a cold shoulder); an outlived norm reflects transition costs. Ellickson's Shasta County cattle-trespass norms are the lesson's worked case.

*Lessons:* [6.2](lessons/06-02-rational-choice-sociology.md)

### Closure

A network has closure when the people a person deals with also deal with each other.

Reputations travel, sanctions are coordinated and cheap: Coleman's answer to the second-order free-rider problem. Intergenerational closure: the parents of children who are friends know each other, so they can share information, agree on norms and back one another's sanctions (Coleman 1988). Behind it sits repeated-game enforcement. In 6.4 it is premise 2 of Putnam's chain; it produces trust in *known* people, which is why the generalization step is the weak point. Coleman's diamond merchants show it; Portes's exclusion examples include the same diamond trade.

*Lessons:* [6.2](lessons/06-02-rational-choice-sociology.md), [6.3](lessons/06-03-social-capital-coleman-and-putnam.md), [6.4](lessons/06-04-social-capital-on-trial.md)

### Grim trigger

Cooperate until the other defects, then defect forever.

In a repeated prisoner's dilemma with T > R > P > S and discount factor $\delta$, cooperation is self-enforcing when the one-time gain T - R is outweighed by the permanent loss R - P weighted by patience. Formula: [Grim-trigger threshold](#grim-trigger-threshold). Derivation and the folk theorem: game-theory-refresher 2.3. Boss 6 extends this to a community with a meeting probability and closure; the card gives only the two-player threshold.

*Lessons:* [6.2](lessons/06-02-rational-choice-sociology.md)

### Thin and thick rationality

Elster, *Sour Grapes* (1983): **thin** rationality asks only that beliefs and desires be consistent and action fit them; Elster's own word for the richer theory is **"broad"** (often relabeled "thick"), which also asks that beliefs be well grounded and desires autonomous.

Thin is not egoism: goals may be altruistic. But the thinner the theory, the more behavior it can absorb and the less it risks (a "duty" term rescues any turnout). The consistency axioms behind thin rationality are decision-theory 1.3.

*Lessons:* [6.2](lessons/06-02-rational-choice-sociology.md)

### Social capital

Resources that inhere in relations between people, not in persons or things.

**Coleman** defines it by function: features of social structure that facilitate certain actions of people within it ("Social Capital in the Creation of Human Capital", *AJS* 94 supplement, 1988). Diamond merchants hand each other bags of stones uninsured; a cheat would lose family, community and synagogue ties at once. Coleman's closure argument for dropout, as the lesson reconstructs it: (1) a child's progress depends on adults attending to her; (2) parents' human capital helps only if connected to her through family relations; (3) intergenerational closure lets parents share information, agree on norms and back sanctions; (4) norms backed by several adults are enforced more reliably; (5) ∴ other things equal, students in structures with closure drop out less: social capital creates human capital. Defining by function is not a [functional explanation](#functional-explanation): builders do not capture the payoff. **Weak point:** 3 to 5, [selection](#selection) (families in close-knit communities or Catholic schools differ otherwise).

**Putnam** treats it as trust, norms of reciprocity and networks of civic engagement that ease coordinated action for a whole society. *Making Democracy Work* (1993, with Leonardi and Nanetti), in outline only (Boss 6 asks for the premise reconstruction): after the 1970 reform Italy's regions got governments with the same powers; north and centre performed far better than the south; performance tracked civic community better than wealth; civic community tracked traditions back through 19th-century mutual aid to the medieval communes; mechanism borrowed from game theory (horizontal networks make reputations visible; vertical patron-client networks cannot), with regions settling into cooperative or distrustful equilibria. Macro to macro across regions, so individual readings face the [ecological fallacy](#ecological-fallacy); critics doubt the arrow's direction and an eight-century chain. *Bowling Alone* (2000): American ties thinned since about the 1960s.

6.4 puts Putnam's chain on trial, one charge per link: networks → norms (closure) → generalized trust → collective action → good government and growth. Measurement attacks the evidence for networks and generalization; [reverse causation](#reverse-causation) denies the outcome correlation comes from the chain; Skocpol disputes why networks shrank; the [dark side](#dark-side-of-social-capital) attacks generalization and collective action. **Weak point:** generalization: closure produces trust in known people, not strangers. Defender: [bridging](#bonding-and-bridging) ties should generalize. Deeper worry: if social capital is whatever facilitates action, finding that it facilitates action is no discovery.

*Lessons:* [6.3](lessons/06-03-social-capital-coleman-and-putnam.md), [6.4](lessons/06-04-social-capital-on-trial.md)

### Forms of social capital

Coleman 1988's three forms: obligations and expectations; information channels; norms with effective sanctions.

Obligations are credit slips, worth something only if the structure makes people trustworthy. Information channels carry what would be costly to acquire alone. Norms that others enforce let everyone act on them. Closure makes the first and third work. Warnings: not fully fungible (a structure that helps one action may harm another); largely a public good, so underinvested and often a by-product. A family that moves town keeps its human capital and loses its social capital (residential mobility as a cost to children).

*Lessons:* [6.3](lessons/06-03-social-capital-coleman-and-putnam.md)

### Bonding and bridging

Putnam, *Bowling Alone* (2000): bonding ties link similar people and reinforce exclusive identities; bridging ties cross social divides.

Bonding helps people get by, bridging helps them get ahead (labels and phrase borrowed from earlier writers). Dimensions of degree, not exclusive types: one group can bond on class and bridge on religion. Not "bonding bad, bridging good". In 6.4 bridging is the defender's reply to the generalization objection, strained by Weimar, where every club type predicted Nazi entry; whether the reply refines or shields the thesis depends on measuring bridging before the outcome.

*Lessons:* [6.3](lessons/06-03-social-capital-coleman-and-putnam.md), [6.4](lessons/06-04-social-capital-on-trial.md)

### Measuring social capital

Two proxies carry the literature: the generalized-trust survey question (most people can be trusted, or one can't be too careful) and membership counts.

Glaeser, Laibson, Scheinkman and Soutter, "Measuring Trust" (*QJE* 2000): the survey question predicted subjects' trust*worthiness* better than their trusting behavior (one experimental study of Harvard undergraduates). Knack and Keefer (*QJE* 1997), 29 market economies: trust and civic norms go with economic performance; group memberships (Putnam's measure) go with neither trust nor performance; trust is higher in richer, more equal, better-educated nations with constrained executives.

*Lessons:* [6.4](lessons/06-04-social-capital-on-trial.md)

### Membership to management

Skocpol, *Diminished Democracy: From Membership to Management in American Civic Life* (2003): a top-down account of the American decline in joining.

Cross-class membership federations with local chapters (Masons, Odd Fellows, PTA) dominated civic life from the late 19th to the mid-20th century; from the 1960s educated elites moved into professionally managed advocacy groups seeking donors, not members. Putnam's bottom-up account (*Bowling Alone*): about half generational replacement, perhaps a quarter television, plus sprawl and time and money pressure. Skocpol does not deny social capital matters; she gives a different kind of explanation of the trend (organizational supply vs dispositions) and leaves most of Putnam's chain alone.

*Lessons:* [6.4](lessons/06-04-social-capital-on-trial.md)

### Dark side of social capital

The same mechanisms that sustain cooperation can exclude, oppress and serve any end.

Portes, "Social Capital: Its Origins and Applications in Modern Sociology" (*Annual Review of Sociology* 1998): four negative consequences: exclusion of outsiders, excess claims on group members, restrictions on individual freedoms, downward leveling norms. Weimar: Sheri Berman (*World Politics* 1997): a robust civil society helped scuttle Weimar; associationism and democratic stability can be inversely related under some political conditions. Satyanath, Voigtlaender and Voth, "Bowling for Fascism" (*JPE* 2017): 229 towns; one standard deviation more association density, at least 15% faster Nazi Party entry; all club types; no association in states with stable governments. Tocqueville anticipated the condition (Vol. 1, ch. 12): association becomes dangerous in proportion to exclusion from any hope of a majority. "Club networks sped a party's growth" is an explanatory, conditional claim, not a verdict that associations are bad.

*Lessons:* [6.4](lessons/06-04-social-capital-on-trial.md)

## Secularization and its critics

The classical prediction that modernity erodes religion, its rivals, and Taylor's argument that the question was framed wrongly. Every entry here is an explanation of the social conditions of belief; none bears on whether any belief is true (see [Genetic fallacy question](#genetic-fallacy-question)).

### Secularization thesis

Modernization produces differentiation, privatization and decline of religion, and they hang together.

Roots: Weber's [disenchantment](#disenchantment) and [value spheres](#value-spheres); Durkheim's dying gods (3.3). Theorists: Bryan Wilson, *Religion in Secular Society* (1966): religious thinking, practice and institutions losing *social significance*, compatible with private belief. Peter Berger, *The Sacred Canopy* (1967): [plausibility structures](#plausibility-structures) and pluralism. Steve Bruce, *God Is Dead* (2002): the "secularization paradigm", predicting indifference in the West, generation by generation, with [exceptions](#cultural-defence-and-cultural-transition). The classical theory (synthesizing Wilson, Berger 1967 and Bruce), as the lesson reconstructs it:

1. Differentiation: education, welfare, law, health care and records move to secular institutions (religion loses its jobs).
2. Societalization: life organized in large impersonal systems, not local communities whose rites expressed shared belief.
3. Rationalization: science and technique reduce occasions to turn to supernatural means.
4. Pluralism: no faith has the whole society's backing; each loses taken-for-grantedness.
5. Plausibility: belief becomes choice, held less certainly, transmitted less reliably.
6. ∴ C1: religion loses social significance (privatization).
7. ∴ C2: over generations belief and practice decline, ending in the West in indifference.

Kind: explanatorily holist, best run through individuals ([Colemans boat](#colemans-boat)); 1 is functional ([functional equivalents](#functional-equivalents)); 5 is interpretive. Test: more differentiated and pluralist societies, and later cohorts, should be less religious. **Weak points:** 4-5 (religious economies: pluralism raises religiosity; the US) and C1 to C2 (Casanova). Berger, *The Desecularization of the World* (1999), withdrew the general prediction: apart from Western and Central Europe and a Western-educated international elite, the world was as religious as ever, in places more so. Evidence: the decline of attendance and belief across most of Western Europe over the 20th century is well documented and not in dispute; whether modernization causes it, and whether it generalizes beyond Europe, is contested. Weber's own text (Source, "Science as a Vocation", my translation): the highest values "have withdrawn from public life" into mystical life or brotherly relations, where "something pulses": relocation, not extinction. Classical versions differ: Weber relocation, Wilson loss of significance, Bruce indifference and only for the West.

*Lessons:* [7.1](lessons/07-01-classical-secularization-theory.md)

### Casanovas three claims

José Casanova, *Public Religions in the Modern World* (1994): "secularization" bundles three logically separate claims, to be tested separately.

(1) Differentiation of secular spheres from religion; (2) decline of belief and practice; (3) privatization. Casanova keeps (1) as the defensible core and denies (2) and (3) follow. Each needs its own evidence: legal and institutional records for differentiation, surveys and attendance for decline, the conduct of public life for privatization (a law closing monasteries tells you about the state, not belief). Best separating case: the French Revolution differentiated hard (church property 1789), tried to force decline (dechristianization 1793-94), and never privatized (civic cults; Concordat of 1801 restored public worship). In 7.2: believing without belonging means decline of practice need not mean decline of belief; American vigor is compatible with differentiation. Taylor's secularity 1 and 2 roughly match claims (1) and (2).

*Lessons:* [7.1](lessons/07-01-classical-secularization-theory.md), [7.2](lessons/07-02-critics-and-rival-models.md)

### Deprivatization

Religion re-entering public life in differentiated societies.

Casanova 1994: the 1980s; five cases in four countries (Spain, Poland, Brazil, and the United States, evangelical and Catholic). His evidence that differentiation does not entail privatization.

*Lessons:* [7.1](lessons/07-01-classical-secularization-theory.md)

### Plausibility structures

A world of meaning stays real only while a community keeps confirming it, by sharing it, talking in its terms and acting it out.

Berger, *The Sacred Canopy* (1967): religion legitimates a society's order by grounding it in a sacred cosmos; secularization removes sectors of society and culture from religious domination, in institutions and consciousness. Key mechanism: pluralism under a neutral state makes each faith one option in a market. Berger 1999 withdrew the general prediction (exceptions: Western and Central Europe, a Western-educated elite). The premise is symmetric: secular convictions need a social base too (Berger points to Western universities). The sociologist's cousin of the birthplace argument (philosophy-of-religion 5.5); whether either debunks belief is that course's question. Taylor's fragilization is a near relative.

*Lessons:* [7.1](lessons/07-01-classical-secularization-theory.md)

### Value spheres

Weber, *Zwischenbetrachtung* ("Intermediate Reflection", 1915; revised for the collected essays in the sociology of religion, 1920).

Economy, politics, art, erotic love and science each develop their own internal laws (*Eigengesetzlichkeit*) and clash with a religious ethic of brotherliness that would govern them all. Weber's root of the differentiation claim.

*Lessons:* [7.1](lessons/07-01-classical-secularization-theory.md)

### Cultural defence and cultural transition

Bruce's conditions under which religion keeps a public role in modern societies.

Cultural defence: religion carries a group's identity against an outside power (Quebec before 1960; Poland 1978-89 on Bruce's reading). Cultural transition: religion helps migrants through a new society. The lesson attributes them to Bruce without pinning a book.

*Lessons:* [7.1](lessons/07-01-classical-secularization-theory.md)

### Religious economies

Stark, Finke and Iannaccone: demand for religion is roughly stable; supply varies.

The supply-side model (Stark and Iannaccone, *JSSR* 1994; Finke and Stark, *The Churching of America, 1776-1990*, 1992), as the lesson reconstructs it:

1. Demand for religion is roughly stable across modern societies.
2. Religious organizations behave like firms: clergy effort depends on whether income depends on members.
3. Regulation protects one church: subsidized clergy are paid regardless, competitors few.
4. Monopolies underperform; markets specialize and serve many niches.
5. Participation rises with the effort and variety of supply.
6. ∴ Participation varies with regulation; Europe's "secularization" is a failure of supply, not loss of demand.

Stark and Iannaccone proposed dropping the concept of secularization. Iannaccone, "Why Strict Churches Are Strong" (*AJS* 1994): costly rules screen out free riders. Kind: explanatory individualism (Coleman's boat), thin rationality. Ancestor: Adam Smith, *Wealth of Nations* Book V ch. 1 (endowed clergy lose zeal). Tocqueville (Vol. 1, ch. 17, Reeve) shares the variable (church-state tie) but not the mechanism: an established church inherits "animosity" and shares the government's "vicissitudes"; supply-siders can claim his variable, not his mechanism. Evidence on the key link is weak: Chaves and Gorski (*Annual Review of Sociology* 2001): no general positive pluralism-participation association; Voas, Olson and Crockett (*ASR* 2002): many published correlations arise mechanically from how the indices are computed. **Weak point:** 1 (stable demand). Reply: supply shapes demand over time; cost: the model then absorbs almost any outcome unless it fixes the lag and measures supply independently.

*Lessons:* [7.2](lessons/07-02-critics-and-rival-models.md)

### Existential security

Norris and Inglehart, *Sacred and Secular* (2004): religiosity tracks vulnerability.

People exposed to poverty, disease and violence without a safety net seek religion's reassurance; secure societies secularize. The US is religious for a rich country because unusually unequal with a thinner welfare state. Fertility twist: secure, secular societies have few children, so the world can grow more religious while each rich country secularizes. Kind: a cross-country macro correlation backed by an individual mechanism, so the [ecological fallacy](#ecological-fallacy) warning applies.

*Lessons:* [7.2](lessons/07-02-critics-and-rival-models.md)

### Believing without belonging

Grace Davie ("Believing without Belonging", *Social Compass* 1990; *Religion in Britain since 1945*, 1994): in Britain belief persisted while attendance and membership fell.

A claim about measurement: if true, attendance overstates decline. Voas and Crockett ("Religion in Britain: Neither Believing nor Belonging", *Sociology* 2005) found belief fell at the same rate as belonging and is lower than nominal belonging.

*Lessons:* [7.2](lessons/07-02-critics-and-rival-models.md)

### Cohort replacement

Decline as generational: each cohort starts less religious and the average falls as older cohorts die.

Voas and Crockett 2005 (*Sociology* 39(1)): British decline is generational; only about half of parental religiosity is transmitted, and non-religion is almost always passed on. Voas and Chaves (*AJS* 2016): the US has been declining by the same cohort pattern for decades, so on trends rather than levels it is no exception. A mechanism of aggregation, not a cause: compatible with several causes of weak transmission. Its real rival is a period account.

*Lessons:* [7.2](lessons/07-02-critics-and-rival-models.md)

### Age period and cohort effects

Three ways a population average can move.

Age effect: change over a life (people grow more devout as they age). Period effect: hits every age at once (a war, a scandal). Cohort effect: a generational difference carried through life. Because cohort = survey year minus age, linear age, period and cohort effects cannot all be separated without an extra assumption (perfect collinearity, econometrics 1.3). US nones: Hout and Fischer (*ASR* 2002) found the nones rising from 7% to 14% in the 1990s, confined to political moderates and liberals, most holding conventional beliefs; Voas and Chaves (2016) read the US as generational decline.

*Lessons:* [7.2](lessons/07-02-critics-and-rival-models.md)

### Three senses of secularity

Charles Taylor, *A Secular Age* (2007): secularity 1, public institutions emptied of reference to God; secularity 2, decline of belief and practice; secularity 3, a change in the conditions of belief, from unchallenged default to one option among others, often not the easiest.

Same definitions as apologetics-foundations 8.3. Secularity 1 and 2 roughly match Casanova's differentiation and decline; the book is about 3. Comparison: Latin Christendom around 1500 against the North Atlantic world around 2000; no claim the story travels. Each sense has its own evidence: law and constitutions (1); surveys and attendance (2); how belief is held, how dissenters are treated, which alternatives can be lived (3, hard to test and easy to assert). A society can be high on belief and attendance and fully inside secularity 3.

Taylor's argument, as the lesson reconstructs it:

1. Around 1500 belief was the default: nature testified to God, society was grounded in the sacred, the self was porous.
2. Around 2000 belief is one fragilized option within an immanent frame.
3. The passage was a series of constructions (buffered self, disciplined society, impersonal moral order), many built by religious reformers ("Reform").
4. They made exclusive humanism livable, and positions multiplied (the nova).
5. ∴ Secularity is chiefly a change in the conditions of belief produced by that history; subtraction stories misdescribe it.

Kind: interpretive (aims at [adequacy at the level of meaning](#adequacy-at-the-level-of-meaning)); explanatorily holist (a "social imaginary"); a genealogy of unintended consequences like Weber's; not functional. **Weak points:** 3's causal weight and 1's evidence: history told through elite texts (a selection problem), and no comparison tests whether reformers or differentiation built the buffered self; a secular critic rejects the parity of "takes" (a universal search for "fullness" an unbeliever need not grant). Taylor is a Catholic; readers dispute whether the book tilts; a description of how belief is held leaves its truth where it was.

*Lessons:* [7.3](lessons/07-03-taylors-a-secular-age.md)

### Subtraction stories

Accounts in which moderns shed illusions and what remains is human nature as it always was underneath.

Taylor rejects the *form*: exclusive humanism (no goal beyond human flourishing) was constructed, partly by religious Reform, not uncovered, and became for the first time a widely livable option. The objection applies equally to a believer's mirror-image story in which faith reappears as the natural default once distractions are stripped away. Keith Thomas, *Religion and the Decline of Magic* (1971): English belief in magic waned before technical remedies existed, which a simple subtraction story does not predict. A claim about history, not an argument for religion.

*Lessons:* [7.3](lessons/07-03-taylors-a-secular-age.md)

### Porous and buffered self

The enchanted self, open to spirits and forces with meanings located in things, versus the modern self with a firm boundary between mind and world.

Porous: a relic heals, a curse wounds, melancholy *is* black bile. Buffered: meanings live in minds; a person can disengage. Disenchantment (4.4) described from inside a life. Buffered is not unbelieving: believers are buffered too. Example: the Witchcraft Act of 1603-04 (capital, invoking evil spirits) against the Act of 1735/36 (pretending to witchcraft, at most one year).

*Lessons:* [7.3](lessons/07-03-taylors-a-secular-age.md)

### Immanent frame

The shared background of modern life (natural science, market economy, self-governing polity, impersonal moral order) that makes the world intelligible in its own terms.

It can be lived *open*, pointing beyond itself, or *closed*. Taylor's sharpest claim: neither reading is forced by the frame; each is a "take", and presenting either as obvious is "spin".

*Lessons:* [7.3](lessons/07-03-taylors-a-secular-age.md)

### Nova effect

Once exclusive humanism became livable, positions multiplied between and beyond orthodoxy and unbelief.

First among elites from the 18th century, then for whole populations after the 1960s (the "supernova"). Every option is "fragilized" by decent people visibly holding another. Periods (after Durkheim): paleo-Durkheimian (sacred embodied in society); neo-Durkheimian "Age of Mobilization" (c. 1800-1960, church belonging tied to nation or class); post-Durkheimian "Age of Authenticity" (personal spiritual search detached from political identity). Religious diversity, the nova's epistemic shadow, is philosophy-of-religion 5.5.

*Lessons:* [7.3](lessons/07-03-taylors-a-secular-age.md)

## Formulas

Only what the lessons compute.

### Rates per 100,000

A rate divides events by the population at risk, so it survives changes in population and territory where yearly totals do not.

$$\text{rate per }100{,}000 = \frac{\text{events in a year}}{\text{population at risk}} \times 100{,}000, \qquad \text{per }100{,}000 = \frac{\text{per million}}{10}$$

Percentage change from $r_0$ to $r_1$: $(r_1 - r_0)/r_0$. A ratio of two rates compares groups (Prussia 1849-55: $159.9/49.6 \approx 3.2$, Protestants to Catholics per million). Durkheim's Bavarian provinces, 1867-75, per million: 192, 135, 75 by rising Catholic share (19.2, 13.5, 7.5 per 100,000); these are his averages, not simple means of the listed provinces.

[Coefficient of preservation](#coefficient-of-preservation): $c = r_{\text{comparison}} / r_{\text{protected}}$ at the same age; $c > 1$ means the protected group is protected, $c < 1$ is a coefficient of aggravation. Compute it from the rates, not from Durkheim's printed coefficients, which differ slightly.

*From* [3.2](lessons/03-02-suicide.md)

### Ecological-inference arithmetic

A group's overall rate is the weighted average of its subgroups' rates, so area rates can move with the mix even when every subgroup ranks the other way.

For an area with subgroup share $s$ (say, newcomers) whose subgroup rates are $r_1$ (newcomers) and $r_2$ (natives):

$$\text{area rate} = s\,r_1 + (1-s)\,r_2$$

The 1.4 toy example (invented, 200 adults per region; natives 10 points above newcomers in every region): Region B, $0.7 \times 85 + 0.3 \times 75 = 82\%$; regions at 10, 30, 50% newcomers have literacy 69, 82, 90%. Pooled, newcomers are $142/180 = 78.9\%$ literate, natives $340/420 = 81.0\%$. A line fitted through the region points, $\text{literacy} = 64.6 + 52.5 \times \text{newcomer share}$, "predicts" natives at 64.6% and newcomers at 117.1%: the step from groups to individuals needs an assumption (that each subgroup's rate does not vary with the mix) the group data cannot supply. Robinson 1950: about +0.53 across states, about -0.11 across individuals. Aggregates pin down a subgroup's rate tightly only where that subgroup dominates.

*From* [1.4](lessons/01-04-testing-a-social-explanation.md)

### Counting pairs

A group of $n$ members contains

$$\binom{n}{2} = \frac{n(n-1)}{2}$$

pairs: 1 for two people, 3 for three, 45 for ten, 4,950 for a hundred. Why Simmel treats number as a form: mutual monitoring easy at 3 is impossible at 4,950.

*From* [5.1](lessons/05-01-simmel-sociation-and-the-forms-of-social-life.md)

### Grim-trigger threshold

In a repeated prisoner's dilemma with temptation $T$, reward $R$, punishment $P$, sucker's payoff $S$ ($T > R > P > S$) and discount factor $\delta \in (0,1)$, grim trigger sustains cooperation when cooperating forever beats defecting once and being punished forever:

$$\frac{R}{1-\delta} \ge T + \frac{\delta P}{1-\delta} \iff \delta \ge \delta^* = \frac{T-R}{T-P}$$

The one-time gain $T - R$ must be outweighed by the permanent loss $R - P$, weighted by patience. Two players who meet every period only; Boss 6's community version (meeting probability, closure) is not given here. Derivation and folk theorem: [game-theory-refresher 2.3](../game-theory-refresher/lessons/02-03-repeated-games-folk-theorem.md).

*From* [6.2](lessons/06-02-rational-choice-sociology.md)

## Pitfalls

### Confusing kinds of explanation

- Holism is not belief in a group mind: explanatory holism is compatible with ontological individualism, and Durkheim refuses to hypostasize the collective conscience. Check which question a claim answers. *([1.1](lessons/01-01-individuals-and-social-facts.md))*
- An explanation is not holist because it talks about many people, nor individualist because it mentions persons: classify by what the explanans cites. *([1.1](lessons/01-01-individuals-and-social-facts.md))*
- "Methodological individualism" is not a political creed and "constraint" is not a complaint; both are claims about explanation. Tocqueville's "individualism" (withdrawal into private life) is a third thing again. *([1.1](lessons/01-01-individuals-and-social-facts.md), [6.1](lessons/06-01-tocqueville-on-associations.md))*
- Showing a practice benefits a group does not explain why it exists; the benefit explains nothing until the return path is shown. "If it didn't pay it would have died out" assumes a selection mechanism. *([1.2](lessons/01-02-functional-explanation-and-its-critics.md))*
- A latent function is not a hidden purpose pursued by insiders: it is neither intended nor recognized. Once pursued, the explanation is intentional. *([1.2](lessons/01-02-functional-explanation-and-its-critics.md))*
- Calling crime "normal" or "useful" does not endorse it; Durkheim's footnote compares it to pain. A function claim is explanatory, not evaluative. *([1.2](lessons/01-02-functional-explanation-and-its-critics.md))*
- *Verstehen* is not empathy or a soft alternative to causal explanation; it is the first half of causal explanation. *([1.3](lessons/01-03-understanding-webers-interpretive-method.md))*
- An ideal type is not an ideal or an average; using the purposive-rational type as yardstick is not a claim that people are rational. *([1.3](lessons/01-03-understanding-webers-interpretive-method.md))*
- Value freedom does not mean the researcher has no values: values select the question; the rule forbids passing off a verdict as a finding. *([1.3](lessons/01-03-understanding-webers-interpretive-method.md))*

### Evidence and inference

- Durkheim's two alternatives to direct causation are not both rivals: the interposed phenomenon is a mechanism and strengthens the causal claim; only the common cause competes. *([1.4](lessons/01-04-testing-a-social-explanation.md))*
- The ecological fallacy does not make group data worthless: they are the right evidence for group claims. The fallacy is the slide from groups to individuals. *([1.4](lessons/01-04-testing-a-social-explanation.md))*
- A test that supports an explanation does not show the thing explained is good or bad, and a social explanation of religious change is not evidence about religious truth. *([1.4](lessons/01-04-testing-a-social-explanation.md))*

### Marx pitfalls

- Historical materialism is not technological determinism: Cohen's functional reading needs the superstructure to have real effects. *([2.1](lessons/02-01-historical-materialism.md))*
- "Functional" does not mean "good for society": relations function for the development of the forces, not for the people under them. *([2.1](lessons/02-01-historical-materialism.md))*
- Cohen's reconstruction is not simply what Marx said; keep exegetical (did Marx hold primacy?), explanatory (is it true?) and evaluative (was it progress?) apart. *([2.1](lessons/02-01-historical-materialism.md))*
- Class is not income: a rich salaried manager sells labor power, a poor shopkeeper owns his means of production. *([2.2](lessons/02-02-class-class-consciousness-and-ideology.md))*
- Calling a belief "ideology" does not show it is false; the claim explains why it is widespread. *([2.2](lessons/02-02-class-class-consciousness-and-ideology.md))*
- The rise of the middle class refutes one sentence of the *Manifesto*, not all of it: the lower-strata prediction concerned the old middle class, which did shrink for a century. *([2.3](lessons/02-03-capitalism-and-the-test-of-history.md))*
- Fetishism and alienation did not fail with immiseration: one is interpretive, the other structural; neither predicts a wage series. *([2.3](lessons/02-03-capitalism-and-the-test-of-history.md))*
- A revision is not honest or ad hoc by its content: the test is what it predicts next. *([2.3](lessons/02-03-capitalism-and-the-test-of-history.md))*

### Durkheim pitfalls

- Organic solidarity is not a society of contracts with nothing in common: non-contractual relations grow alongside contract, and the cult of the person remains. *([3.1](lessons/03-01-the-division-of-labor-and-social-solidarity.md))*
- "The division of labor produces solidarity" is a claim about function, not the explanation of why it exists; Durkheim's explanation is causal (density and competition). *([3.1](lessons/03-01-the-division-of-labor-and-social-solidarity.md))*
- The anomic and forced forms are not a verdict that specialization is bad, nor is the book a proof that modern society is fine. *([3.1](lessons/03-01-the-division-of-labor-and-social-solidarity.md))*
- Durkheim explains rates, not why particular people die; reading him as a theory of individual cases is the ecological fallacy. *([3.2](lessons/03-02-suicide.md))*
- "Integration protects" is an explanatory claim, not a value judgment for religion or traditional families; Durkheim held old beliefs cannot be artificially restored. *([3.2](lessons/03-02-suicide.md))*
- Anomie is not poverty or hardship but a lack of regulation: booms raise the rate as crashes do. *([3.2](lessons/03-02-suicide.md))*
- "Sacred" is not "very valuable": the test is absolute separation enforced by interdicts. *([3.3](lessons/03-03-religion-as-social-the-elementary-forms.md))*
- Durkheim does not argue that religion is false (or true); treating his explanation as a verdict on God's existence confuses explanation with evaluation. *([3.3](lessons/03-03-religion-as-social-the-elementary-forms.md))*
- The theory is not individualist because its mechanism is psychological: it runs from how the group is arranged to what people believe. *([3.3](lessons/03-03-religion-as-social-the-elementary-forms.md))*

### Weber pitfalls

- Weber did not say Protestantism caused capitalism; he calls that thesis "foolish and doctrinaire". His claim is one contributor to a *spirit*. *([4.1](lessons/04-01-the-protestant-ethic-and-the-spirit-of-capitalism.md))*
- "Interpretive" does not mean untestable: meaning-adequacy and causal adequacy are separate claims, and a brilliant reconstruction of meaning is not yet evidence of cause. *([4.1](lessons/04-01-the-protestant-ethic-and-the-spirit-of-capitalism.md))*
- The thesis neither praises nor indicts Calvinism; that a belief had economic consequences says nothing about its truth. *([4.1](lessons/04-01-the-protestant-ethic-and-the-spirit-of-capitalism.md))*
- Pre-Reformation capitalism does not refute Weber: he concedes it. The price is that the thesis is only as testable as the ethos is measurable. *([4.2](lessons/04-02-the-protestant-ethic-on-trial.md))*
- The economists did not show Protestantism did not matter: Becker and Woessmann find it raised prosperity through literacy; Cantoni finds no effect on one outcome. *([4.2](lessons/04-02-the-protestant-ethic-on-trial.md))*
- Tawney did not simply reverse Weber: he called the pure economic reading equally one-sided. *([4.2](lessons/04-02-the-protestant-ethic-on-trial.md))*
- "Legitimate domination" means believed-rightful rule, not justified rule. *([4.3](lessons/04-03-domination-and-the-three-types-of-authority.md))*
- Charisma is not charm or popularity but a recognized extraordinary gift grounding a duty to follow. *([4.3](lessons/04-03-domination-and-the-three-types-of-authority.md))*
- The three types are ideal types, not boxes: real authority mixes them. *([4.3](lessons/04-03-domination-and-the-three-types-of-authority.md))*
- The ideal type of bureaucracy neither describes nor recommends; officials bending rules are what it helps you see. *([4.4](lessons/04-04-bureaucracy-rationalization-and-disenchantment.md))*
- Formal rationality means calculable, not efficient or sensible. *([4.4](lessons/04-04-bureaucracy-rationalization-and-disenchantment.md))*
- Disenchantment does not say moderns know more, nor that they have stopped believing in God. *([4.4](lessons/04-04-bureaucracy-rationalization-and-disenchantment.md))*

### Simmel and Polanyi pitfalls

- Formal sociology does not ignore what people want: form and content are inseparable in reality; the abstraction is methodological. *([5.1](lessons/05-01-simmel-sociation-and-the-forms-of-social-life.md))*
- "The stranger" names a position, not a verdict on outsiders. *([5.1](lessons/05-01-simmel-sociation-and-the-forms-of-social-life.md))*
- The triad roles are not functional explanations: Simmel says what the third's position allows, not that mediators exist because groups need them. *([5.1](lessons/05-01-simmel-sociation-and-the-forms-of-social-life.md))*
- Simmel is not simply a critic of money: chapter 4 presents it as the vehicle of freedom; the costs come in chapters 5-6. *([5.2](lessons/05-02-simmels-philosophy-of-money.md))*
- "Money rests on trust" does not set credit money against sound metal: coin is a promise too. *([5.2](lessons/05-02-simmels-philosophy-of-money.md))*
- Simmel's conclusion is not that money makes people less dependent: more dependent overall, less on any particular person. Nor is it functional. *([5.2](lessons/05-02-simmels-philosophy-of-money.md))*
- "Fictitious" does not mean labor, land and money are not bought and sold; the fiction is that they were produced for sale. Nor is it Marx's fetishism. *([5.3](lessons/05-03-polanyis-great-transformation.md))*
- "Society protected itself" is not a report of a collective decision but a holist, functional explanation that owes a mechanism; keep it apart from Polanyi's evaluative claim. *([5.3](lessons/05-03-polanyis-great-transformation.md))*
- Polanyi does not say the economy was ever fully disembedded: the self-adjusting market was a "stark utopia", a project never finished. *([5.3](lessons/05-03-polanyis-great-transformation.md))*

### Associations, rational choice and social capital

- Tocqueville does not explain associations functionally (because democracy needs them): he explains them by interests, legal freedom and learned skill; the service to democracy is a latent function. *([6.1](lessons/06-01-tocqueville-on-associations.md))*
- Tocqueville's civil associations do not work best kept apart from politics: ch. 7 argues political association is the better school. *([6.1](lessons/06-01-tocqueville-on-associations.md))*
- Showing Tocqueville's argument coherent does not show associations make democracy work: exegesis is not explanation. *([6.1](lessons/06-01-tocqueville-on-associations.md))*
- Rational-choice sociology does not assume only individuals exist: its claim is explanatory, and Coleman admits corporate actors. *([6.2](lessons/06-02-rational-choice-sociology.md))*
- "The norm exists because it benefits the group" is not Coleman's explanation: his needs the feedback (demand from those harmed, someone paying to sanction). *([6.2](lessons/06-02-rational-choice-sociology.md))*
- Explaining a norm by self-interest neither debunks nor endorses it, and thin rationality does not say people are selfish. *([6.2](lessons/06-02-rational-choice-sociology.md))*
- Social capital is not an asset a person owns: it is in the structure of relations. *([6.3](lessons/06-03-social-capital-coleman-and-putnam.md))*
- Coleman and Putnam do not give one explanation at two scales: Coleman's runs through individuals in a network; Putnam's links a regional trait to a regional outcome. *([6.3](lessons/06-03-social-capital-coleman-and-putnam.md))*
- Not "bonding is bad, bridging is good": both are useful, and the distinction is one of degree. *([6.3](lessons/06-03-social-capital-coleman-and-putnam.md))*
- A trust-and-growth correlation does not show trust causes growth: reverse causation predicts the same correlation. *([6.4](lessons/06-04-social-capital-on-trial.md))*
- Skocpol does not deny social capital matters: she disputes where its decline came from. *([6.4](lessons/06-04-social-capital-on-trial.md))*
- Weimar does not show associations are bad for democracy: the finding is explanatory and conditional. *([6.4](lessons/06-04-social-capital-on-trial.md))*

### Secularization pitfalls

- Secularization theory does not predict that religion will disappear: Weber describes relocation, Wilson loss of social significance, Bruce indifference and only for the West. *([7.1](lessons/07-01-classical-secularization-theory.md))*
- Evidence of differentiation is not evidence of decline: each of Casanova's claims needs its own evidence. *([7.1](lessons/07-01-classical-secularization-theory.md))*
- If the theory is true it does not show religion false, and its failure does not show religion true: it explains the social conditions of belief. *([7.1](lessons/07-01-classical-secularization-theory.md))*
- Cohort replacement is not a fourth cause: it is a mechanism of aggregation whose rival is a period account. *([7.2](lessons/07-02-critics-and-rival-models.md))*
- High American religiosity does not by itself refute secularization theory: it depends on levels versus trends and on which of Casanova's claims is meant. *([7.2](lessons/07-02-critics-and-rival-models.md))*
- Explaining churchgoing by markets or insecurity does not show religion to be a product or a crutch. *([7.2](lessons/07-02-critics-and-rival-models.md))*
- Secularity 3 does not mean fewer believers; that is secularity 2. *([7.3](lessons/07-03-taylors-a-secular-age.md))*
- Rejecting subtraction stories is not an argument for religion; the objection is to a form and applies to a believer's mirror-image story too. *([7.3](lessons/07-03-taylors-a-secular-age.md))*
- "Buffered" does not mean unbelieving: believers are buffered too. *([7.3](lessons/07-03-taylors-a-secular-age.md))*

### Famous misattributions and translations

- "Iron cage" is Parsons's 1930 rendering of *stahlhartes Gehaeuse*, not Weber's words; Baehr (2001) proposes "shell as hard as steel". *([4.4](lessons/04-04-bureaucracy-rationalization-and-disenchantment.md))*
- "Class for itself" IS Marx's phrase (*Poverty of Philosophy*, Quelch); only "class in itself" is a later Hegelian gloss (Marx wrote "as opposed to capital"). *([2.2](lessons/02-02-class-class-consciousness-and-ideology.md))*
- "False consciousness" is Engels's (letter to Mehring, 1893), not Marx's. *([2.2](lessons/02-02-class-class-consciousness-and-ideology.md))*
- "Elective affinity" is not in Parsons: he renders *Wahlverwandtschaften* as "certain correlations". *([4.1](lessons/04-01-the-protestant-ethic-and-the-spirit-of-capitalism.md))*
- "Opium of the people" is Marx's (Introduction to the *Critique of Hegel's Philosophy of Right*, 1844) but is not taught in this course; in context it is not simply a sneer (religion is also "the sigh of the oppressed"). See [history-of-political-thought 6.5](../history-of-political-thought/lessons/06-05-marx-from-hegel-to-historical-materialism.md); this course's account of ideology is *([2.2](lessons/02-02-class-class-consciousness-and-ideology.md))*.
- "Methodological individualism" is Schumpeter's label (1908), not Weber's coinage. *([1.1](lessons/01-01-individuals-and-social-facts.md))*
- Elster's own word for the richer theory of rationality is "broad", not "thick". *([6.2](lessons/06-02-rational-choice-sociology.md))*
- Small (1909) renders *Vergesellschaftung* as "socialization"; "sociation" is later English. *([5.1](lessons/05-01-simmel-sociation-and-the-forms-of-social-life.md))*
- De Leon's *Brumaire* (1897) says "allotment farmers" and "a bag with potatoes … a potato-bag", not "smallholding peasants" or "sack of potatoes" (a later translation). *([2.2](lessons/02-02-class-class-consciousness-and-ideology.md))*
- Reeve renders Tocqueville's *moeurs* as "manners", glossed as the ancients' *mores*. *([6.1](lessons/06-01-tocqueville-on-associations.md))*
- "The elimination of magic from the world" is Parsons's rendering of *Entzauberung der Welt* in *PE* ch. IV; "disenchantment" is the usual later rendering. *([4.4](lessons/04-04-bureaucracy-rationalization-and-disenchantment.md))*

### Ecological inference and evidence

- Reading an area correlation (Protestant provinces, civic regions, secure countries) as a claim about individuals is the ecological fallacy; the area claim may still be true. *([1.4](lessons/01-04-testing-a-social-explanation.md), [3.2](lessons/03-02-suicide.md), [6.3](lessons/06-03-social-capital-coleman-and-putnam.md), [7.2](lessons/07-02-critics-and-rival-models.md))*
- A straight line fitted through group points does not give subgroup rates: in the 1.4 toy example it "predicts" newcomers at 117% literacy. *([1.4](lessons/01-04-testing-a-social-explanation.md))*
- An inherited-trust design closes reverse causation, not confounding. *([6.4](lessons/06-04-social-capital-on-trial.md))*
- An instrument's exclusion restriction cannot be proved by any statistic; placebo checks only support it. *([4.2](lessons/04-02-the-protestant-ethic-on-trial.md))*
- Mill-style small-N reasoning assumes single, deterministic, non-interacting, error-free causes (Lieberson). *([1.4](lessons/01-04-testing-a-social-explanation.md))*
