# Social Theory — Syllabus

> Politics & Society · Tier 1 · ~24 lessons · Prereqs: none · Roadmap id: `social-theory`

## Goal

How societies are explained: the classical sociologists and the frameworks built on them that social scientists still reach for. You will finish able to say what kind of explanation a claim about society is offering (individualist, holist, functional, interpretive), read Marx, Durkheim, Weber, Simmel and Tocqueville closely and state what they actually claimed, lay rival explanations of the same social fact side by side and name the premise they disagree about, and judge an explanation against the evidence that would bear on it. The course runs through the classical founders (Marx as a social theorist, Durkheim, Weber, Simmel), their twentieth-century heirs (Polanyi on embeddedness, rational-choice sociology, social capital), and the long argument over secularization, ending with Charles Taylor's *A Secular Age*. It stays verdict-neutral throughout, including on religion: sociological accounts of religion are assessed as explanations of social facts, never as evidence for or against religious truth. It deliberately skips Marx and Tocqueville as political theorists (owned by `history-of-political-thought`), formal collective-action models (owned by [`political-economy`](../political-economy/syllabus.md) Module 3), the history of debt (`history-of-debt`), causal-identification technique (owned by [`econometrics`](../econometrics/syllabus.md)), the theology of secularism (`catholic-social-teaching`, and political theology later), and the twentieth-century schools after the classics (Parsons's systems theory, Frankfurt School critical theory, Foucault, Habermas, Luhmann) except where a lesson needs one as a foil.

## How practice works in this course

Problems follow [HUMANITIES-BUILD-BRIEF.md](../../HUMANITIES-BUILD-BRIEF.md): every part declares its kind (exegetical strict, evaluative graded on moves regardless of verdict, formal as in math), and every solution is a rubric plus a model answer. Four archetypes carry this course:

- **Close reading** (exegetical) of public-domain texts: Marx's 1859 Preface (N. I. Stone translation, 1904), the *Communist Manifesto* (Samuel Moore, 1888), *Capital* vol. 1 (Moore and Aveling, 1887) and *The Eighteenth Brumaire* (Daniel De Leon, 1898); Durkheim's *Elementary Forms of the Religious Life* (J. W. Swain, 1915); Weber's *Protestant Ethic* (Talcott Parsons, 1930 — public domain in the US since January 2026); Tocqueville's *Democracy in America* (Henry Reeve). Simmel is read through Albion Small's early translations of his essays in the *American Journal of Sociology*. Durkheim's *Rules*, *Division of Labor* and *Suicide*, Weber's *Economy and Society*, and Simmel's *Philosophy of Money* exist in English only in copyrighted translations: lessons paraphrase them, or give the lesson's own short translation from the public-domain French or German original and say so. Polanyi, Coleman, Putnam and Taylor are copyrighted and are paraphrased with section citations.
- **Find the crux** (exegetical): two explanations of the same social fact — Protestant suicide rates, Protestant prosperity, American churchgoing — and the single premise one side must deny.
- **Diagnose** (exegetical): an invented op-ed or policy memo; name the framework it silently assumes (holist, functionalist, rational-choice, interpretive, materialist).
- **Evaluate against evidence** (evaluative, built on apply-to-a-hard-case and steelman-and-reply): say what pattern each rival predicts, what evidence would discriminate, and whether a named study does — any verdict passes if the moves are made. Religious and secular readings of the secularization evidence get identical treatment.

Formal problems are rare and self-contained (6.2 only). Invented material (op-eds, commentaries, memos) appears only in diagnose parts and is never attributed to a real person; every other case is a real one.

## Dangerous Checklist

When you finish, you can:

- [ ] Classify a social explanation as individualist or holist, and say whether the dispute in a given case is about what exists or about what explains
- [ ] Tell a functional explanation that names its feedback mechanism from one that merely points to a benefit, and repair the second or reject it
- [ ] Construct a Weberian ideal type for a social pattern and use it to explain an action both by its meaning to the actor and by its causes
- [ ] Say what comparison would test a claim about society, and spot confounding, selection and the ecological fallacy in a prose argument
- [ ] Reconstruct Marx's historical materialism from the 1859 Preface, and test one of its predictions against the historical record
- [ ] Explain a social rate (suicide, crime, churchgoing) with Durkheim's concepts of integration and regulation, and state the strongest objection to the inference
- [ ] State the Protestant ethic thesis at the strength Weber actually claimed it, and find the crux between Weber and his best modern critics
- [ ] Place a real case of authority on Weber's traditional / charismatic / legal-rational map, and predict what routinization does to it
- [ ] Use Simmel's account of money and Polanyi's of embeddedness to read a market or credit arrangement, and say where they disagree
- [ ] Explain how social capital is supposed to produce good outcomes — through Tocqueville's associations, Coleman's closure, and Putnam's civic networks — and say what would show the arrow runs the other way
- [ ] Separate the three claims packed into "secularization" (differentiation, decline, privatization) and match each rival model — classical, religious-economies, existential-security — to the evidence it predicts
- [ ] State Taylor's "conditions of belief" sense of secularity and his objection to subtraction stories, and say what kind of evidence could bear on it

## Modules

### Module 1: What a social explanation is

Before the theorists, the tools for telling their explanations apart: what does the explaining, and how you would know if it worked.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1.1 | Individuals and social facts | Say whether an explanation runs through individuals or through social wholes, and whether the dispute is ontological or explanatory | Durkheim's social facts (externality, constraint), methodological individualism, holism, ontological vs explanatory individualism |
| 1.2 | Functional explanation and its critics | Tell a legitimate functional explanation from a disguised benefit claim | function vs cause, Merton's manifest and latent functions, Durkheim's "crime is normal", the missing feedback mechanism (Elster), functional equivalents |
| 1.3 | Understanding: Weber's interpretive method | Explain an action by its meaning to the actor without giving up on causes | social action, *Verstehen*, adequacy at the level of meaning vs causal adequacy, ideal types, value freedom vs value relevance |
| 1.4 | Testing a social explanation | Say what evidence would discriminate between rival explanations of a social pattern | comparison and concomitant variation, confounding, selection, ecological fallacy, mechanisms, case study vs large-N |

**Boss problem 1:** A persistent social fact — the rise in mid-life deaths from drugs, alcohol and suicide among less-educated Americans since the 1990s — and three invented commentaries explaining it: one from individual incentives, one functionalist, one interpretive. (a) Diagnose which framework each explanation assumes, and whether each is individualist or holist in the explanatory sense. (b) Name the crux between the incentive account and the interpretive account. (c) Describe the comparison that would best discriminate between them, name one confound it must handle and one ecological-inference trap it must avoid, and say which explanation you think currently survives and why. Parts (a)–(b) are exegetical; (c) is evaluative.

### Module 2: Marx as social theorist

Historical materialism and class analysis as a research program: what they explain, what they predicted, and how the tradition responded when predictions failed.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2.1 | Historical materialism | Reconstruct the base-and-superstructure theory and choose between its main readings | forces and relations of production, base and superstructure, the 1859 Preface, Cohen's functional reading vs relations-first readings |
| 2.2 | Class, class consciousness, and ideology | Use class analysis to explain a political outcome, and say when a class acts as one | relation to the means of production, class in itself vs for itself, the smallholding peasants of the *Eighteenth Brumaire*, ruling ideas and ideology |
| 2.3 | Capitalism and the test of history | Test Marx's predictions about capitalist society, and assess the tradition's revisions | commodification, alienation, polarization and immiseration predictions, the growth of middle classes, Gramsci's hegemony, Wright's contradictory class locations |

**Boss problem 2:** Take the central paragraph of Marx's 1859 Preface to *A Contribution to the Critique of Political Economy* (Stone translation, public domain). (a) Reconstruct its theory of historical change in numbered premises. (b) The Preface holds that no social order passes away before the productive forces it has room for have developed: which reading from 2.1 does the paragraph's wording support, and which words decide it? (c) Take the 1917 revolution in a comparatively undeveloped Russia, or the durability of Western capitalism after 1945, as a hard case: is it a counterexample to the Preface, or can a reading absorb it without becoming unfalsifiable? Parts (a)–(b) are exegetical; (c) is evaluative.

### Module 3: Durkheim

Society as a moral reality with its own causal power: over the division of labor, over the private act of suicide, and over the sacred.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3.1 | The division of labor and social solidarity | Explain what holds a differentiated society together, and when it fails | mechanical vs organic solidarity, repressive vs restitutive law as an index, collective conscience, the anomic division of labor |
| 3.2 | Suicide | Explain a suicide rate with integration and regulation, and state the objections to the inference | egoistic, altruistic, anomic and fatalistic suicide; rates as social facts; the Protestant–Catholic comparison; ecological fallacy and recording bias |
| 3.3 | Religion as social: *The Elementary Forms* | Reconstruct Durkheim's definition and explanation of religion, and assess what it can and cannot show | sacred and profane, the definition of religion and the moral community, totemism, ritual and social renewal, the reductionism objection |

**Boss problem 3:** Durkheim explains higher Protestant than Catholic suicide rates in nineteenth-century Europe by weaker religious integration. (a) Reconstruct the argument from provincial rates to egoistic suicide in numbered premises, supplying the suppressed premise that links a group rate to individual acts. (b) State two rival explanations of the same rates — one from the ecological fallacy, one from differences in how deaths were recorded — and name the crux between each and Durkheim. (c) Read Swain's 1915 translation of Durkheim's definition of religion in *The Elementary Forms*: does the integration concept doing the work in *Suicide* match what religion does for a community there? (d) Say what evidence would most weaken Durkheim's account, and whether the account would survive it. Parts (a)–(c) are exegetical; (d) is evaluative.

### Module 4: Weber

Ideas, interests and the making of the modern West: the Protestant ethic, the forms of legitimate domination, and the rationalized world they leave behind.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4.1 | The Protestant ethic and the spirit of capitalism | State Weber's thesis at the strength he actually claimed it | the spirit of capitalism, the calling, predestination and the anxiety of proof, worldly asceticism, elective affinity |
| 4.2 | The Protestant ethic on trial | Find the crux between Weber and his critics, and weigh the evidence each side cites | Tawney's reversal, pre-Reformation Catholic commerce, Becker and Woessmann's literacy explanation, Cantoni's test in German cities, what the thesis predicts |
| 4.3 | Domination and the three types of authority | Classify a case of authority and predict what happens when its source changes | power vs domination, legitimacy as belief, traditional, charismatic and legal-rational authority, routinization of charisma |
| 4.4 | Bureaucracy, rationalization, and disenchantment | Use Weber's ideal type of bureaucracy to explain an organization's behavior, and say what disenchantment claims | features of bureaucracy, formal vs substantive rationality, the "iron cage" and its retranslation, disenchantment of the world |

**Boss problem 4:** Work from the closing pages of *The Protestant Ethic* (Parsons translation, 1930, public domain in the US). (a) Weber ends by disclaiming a one-sided causal interpretation of either kind: state his thesis at exactly the strength those pages allow. (b) Name the crux between that thesis and Becker and Woessmann's claim that Protestant economic advantage runs through literacy. (c) The same pages describe the ascetic ethic hardening into a compulsion that outlives its religious root; say what this claims about rationalization and disenchantment, connecting it to 4.4. (d) Design the evidence that would best discriminate between Weber and his critics, and say which way the current evidence points and how far. Parts (a)–(c) are exegetical; (d) is evaluative.

### Module 5: Money, markets, and embeddedness

What money does to social relations, and whether markets can ever stand free of the society around them.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5.1 | Simmel: sociation and the forms of social life | Explain a social situation by its form rather than its content | form vs content, sociation, the dyad and the triad, the stranger, group size |
| 5.2 | Simmel's *Philosophy of Money* | Use Simmel's account of money to read a credit or exchange relationship | money as pure means, abstraction and calculability, money and personal freedom, objectification of relations, money resting on trust in the social order |
| 5.3 | Polanyi's *Great Transformation* | Apply embeddedness and the double movement to a market reform and its backlash | embeddedness, fictitious commodities (land, labor, money), the self-regulating market, the double movement, Speenhamland and its critics, Granovetter's re-embedding |

**Boss problem 5:** The spread of storefront payday lending across the United States from the 1990s, and the interest-rate caps and bans that a number of states later imposed. (a) Read the lending relationship through Simmel: what does money-mediated, impersonal short-term credit do to the relations between lender, borrower and the borrower's kin and neighbors, and what freedom does it give the borrower? (b) Read the whole sequence through Polanyi: which fictitious commodity is involved, and do the state caps fit the double movement? (c) Name the crux between Simmel's and Polanyi's readings of the same facts. (d) An interest-group reading (see [`political-economy`](../political-economy/syllabus.md) Module 4) also fits the caps; say what evidence would discriminate it from Polanyi's, and which you find stronger. Parts (a)–(c) are exegetical; (d) is evaluative. The debt thread picks this case up in `history-of-debt` and `philosophy-of-debt`.

### Module 6: Civil society, rational choice, and social capital

From Tocqueville's associations to the claim that networks and trust are a form of capital — and the rational-choice program that tries to explain norms from individual action.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 6.1 | Tocqueville on associations | Explain what Tocqueville thought associations do for a democratic society | equality of conditions, individualism as withdrawal, the art of association, mores, civil vs political associations |
| 6.2 | Rational-choice sociology | Explain a macro pattern through individual choices and back up, and say what the approach struggles to explain | Coleman's macro-micro-macro diagram, norms as solutions to externalities, reputation and closure, repeated-game enforcement, thin vs thick rationality |
| 6.3 | Social capital: Coleman and Putnam | Say how social capital is supposed to produce outcomes, and distinguish its kinds | closure and obligations, Coleman's school-community findings, Putnam's Italian regions and *Bowling Alone*, bonding vs bridging |
| 6.4 | Social capital on trial | Evaluate the social-capital thesis against its best objections and evidence | measurement (trust surveys, membership counts), reverse causation, Skocpol's organizational-change account, associations in Weimar Germany, the dark side of bonding |

**Boss problem 6:** Putnam's argument that civic traditions explain the performance of Italy's regional governments. (a) Reconstruct it in numbered premises, paraphrasing *Making Democracy Work*. (b) Read the Reeve translation of Tocqueville's chapter in volume 2 on the use Americans make of associations in civil life: is Tocqueville's mechanism the one Putnam relies on, and which words decide it? (c) Formal: in a community where each pair of members meets with probability $p$ per period and plays a prisoner's dilemma with payoffs $T > R > P$, find the discount factor needed to sustain cooperation by grim trigger when only the victim punishes a defector, and when closure lets every member punish; say what the comparison shows about Coleman's closure. (d) Name the crux between Putnam and the reverse-causation reading (good government builds trust), and say what evidence would decide it. Parts (a)–(b) are exegetical; (c) is formal; (d) is evaluative.

### Module 7: The secularization debate

The classical prediction that modernity erodes religion, the critics who say it failed, and Taylor's argument that the question itself was framed wrongly.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 7.1 | Classical secularization theory | State what the classical theory predicted and why | Weber's disenchantment and differentiation, Berger's plausibility structures and his later retraction, Wilson and Bruce, Casanova's three claims (differentiation, decline, privatization) |
| 7.2 | Critics and rival models | Match each model of religious change to the evidence it predicts, and find their crux | religious economies (Stark, Finke, Iannaccone), American exceptionalism vs European decline, Norris and Inglehart's existential security, believing without belonging, cohort replacement |
| 7.3 | Taylor's *A Secular Age* | State Taylor's sense of secularity and his challenge to subtraction stories, and say what could count as evidence for it | the three senses of secularity, belief as one option among others, subtraction stories, the porous vs buffered self, the immanent frame, the nova effect |

**Boss problem 7:** Weekly religious attendance fell steeply across most of Western Europe after 1960 while remaining far higher in the United States. (a) Diagnose which of Casanova's three claims each of three invented commentaries on this pattern asserts. (b) Name the crux between the religious-economies explanation (a regulated European religious market vs a competitive American one) and the existential-security explanation (welfare states and economic security lowering demand), and say what a third country would have to look like to discriminate between them. (c) State what Taylor's third sense of secularity would say about the same pattern, and whether it competes with either model or answers a different question. (d) In 300–500 words, assess which account best survives the evidence — any verdict, religious or secular, if the moves in (b)–(c) are made. Parts (a)–(c) are exegetical; (d) is evaluative.

## Notes

- **Lesson count.** The roadmap estimated ~20; the syllabus runs 24 (+20%, inside the band). Four classical theorists each need their central works taught *and* put on trial against the evidence, and the course owns three topics outright (Marx's and Weber's sociology, social capital, secularization) that no other course will pick up. Durkheim's *Rules* is folded into 1.1–1.2 rather than given its own lesson to hold the line.
- **Ownership checks (2026-09-15).** A grep of every built lesson, syllabus and reference card found no existing coverage of Durkheim, Weber's sociology, Simmel, Polanyi, Tocqueville, social capital, rational-choice sociology or secularization. `political-institutions` already cites this course for Weber on bureaucracy (its 6.1); [`political-economy`](../political-economy/syllabus.md) Module 3 owns the formal collective-action models 6.2 leans on.
- **Where it connects.** Weber's types of authority (4.3) are the descriptive counterpart of the normative question in [`political-philosophy`](../political-philosophy/syllabus.md) 1.1. Marx's and Tocqueville's political theory is `history-of-political-thought`; state formation and modernization are `comparative-politics`; the institutional turn (North, Acemoglu–Robinson) is `institutions-and-development`. Durkheim's and Taylor's accounts of religion are the social-scientific neighbors of `philosophy-of-religion`, which owns the question of whether religious belief is true.
- **The debt thread.** Simmel's *Philosophy of Money* (5.2) and Polanyi's money as a fictitious commodity (5.3) are this course's contribution to the debt thread; the institutional history is `history-of-debt`, the moral argument `philosophy-of-debt`, the doctrine `theology-of-debt`, the models `economics-of-debt`.
- **Copyright.** The Parsons translation of *The Protestant Ethic* (1930) entered the US public domain in January 2026; lessons that quote it at length should confirm that status holds for the edition used. Every other modern source listed is paraphrased.
