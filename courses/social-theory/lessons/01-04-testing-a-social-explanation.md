# Social Theory · Lesson 1.4: Testing a social explanation

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 1: What a social explanation is · Builds on: [1.1 Individuals and social facts](01-01-individuals-and-social-facts.md), [1.2 Functional explanation and its critics](01-02-functional-explanation-and-its-critics.md), [1.3 Understanding: Weber's interpretive method](01-03-understanding-webers-interpretive-method.md) · Unlocks: [2.3 Capitalism and the test of history](02-03-capitalism-and-the-test-of-history.md), [3.2 Suicide](03-02-suicide.md), [4.2 The Protestant ethic on trial](04-02-the-protestant-ethic-on-trial.md), [6.4 Social capital on trial](06-04-social-capital-on-trial.md)

## Why this matters

Every theorist in this course says what causes what: weak religious integration raises suicide, a religious ethic helped make capitalism, civic networks make government work. Lessons 1.1 to 1.3 taught you to say what *kind* of explanation each one is. This lesson asks the next question: what would show it is *true*? Social scientists cannot rerun history with one thing changed, so they compare, and comparison has four classic ways of failing. Learn them once here; every "on trial" lesson after this one uses them.

## The idea

A causal claim is a claim about a difference: had X been otherwise, Y would have been otherwise. We never see the other history, so we compare cases that differ in X and ask whether Y differs too. John Stuart Mill catalogued the forms of **[comparison](../reference.md#comparison-and-concomitant-variation)** in *A System of Logic* (1843). Three of his five **[methods](../reference.md#mills-methods)** carry most of social science:

- **Agreement:** cases that share the outcome share only one circumstance, so that circumstance is the candidate cause.
- **Difference:** cases alike in everything but one circumstance differ in outcome, so that circumstance makes the difference.
- **Concomitant variation:** two quantities rise and fall together across many cases.

Four things can break the step from a comparison to a cause:

1. **[Confounding](../reference.md#confounding).** A third factor drives both X and Y. Ice-cream sales and drownings both peak in summer.
2. **[Selection](../reference.md#selection).** Units sort themselves into X for reasons tied to Y (people who join a training scheme expected to gain from it), or the researcher picks cases by their outcome (study only firms that survived, and every trait of the survivors looks like a cause of survival).
3. **The [ecological fallacy](../reference.md#ecological-fallacy).** A relationship among group rates is read as a relationship among the individuals in the groups.
4. **Reverse causation.** Y produces X. Named here; 6.4 makes it the main event.

The best single defence against all four is a **[mechanism](../reference.md#mechanisms)**: the chain of steps, usually through what people believe and do, by which X would produce Y. A mechanism tells you where else to look. If X works through M, then cases with X but no M should lack Y. This is where [1.3](01-03-understanding-webers-interpretive-method.md)'s two adequacies meet: a mechanism that makes sense to the actors is adequate at the level of meaning, and comparison is what makes it causally adequate.

Two research styles divide the labor (**[case study and large-N](../reference.md#case-study-and-large-n)**). Large-N studies compare many units and can say how strongly X and Y move together, often only at the group level. Case studies and small-N comparisons trace a mechanism step by step, but have more candidate causes than cases. The formal machinery of identification (potential outcomes, regression, instruments) belongs to [`econometrics`](../../econometrics/syllabus.md); this course uses the prose version.

## Source

Durkheim, *Les règles de la méthode sociologique* (1895), ch. 6, on the method of concomitant variations, which he calls the sociologist's chief instrument. My translation from the 1919 edition:

> The concomitance may be due not to one of the phenomena being the cause of the other, but to both being effects of the same cause, or else to a third phenomenon, interposed between them but unnoticed, which is the effect of the first and the cause of the second. […] For example, one can establish with the greatest certainty that the tendency to suicide varies with the tendency to education. But it is impossible to understand how education can lead to suicide […]. One thus comes to ask whether the two facts are not consequences of one and the same state. This common cause is the weakening of religious traditionalism, which strengthens at once the need to know and the inclination to suicide.

Read three things. First, he names both alternatives a modern methodologist would: a **common cause** and an **interposed phenomenon**, a mediator. Second, what sends him looking for a third factor is not the data but implausibility: he cannot see how schooling (*instruction*), which touches only the surface of the mind, could overcome so deep a drive as self-preservation. Third, his common cause is itself a hypothesis, which *Le suicide* (1897) then tries to test. P2 asks how well.

## The argument

Durkheim's case for concomitant variation, *Rules* ch. 6. These are exegetical claims: what he argued.

1. **P1.** The only way to show that one phenomenon causes another is to compare cases where they are present or absent together and ask whether their variations show that one depends on the other.
2. **P2.** Social facts cannot be produced at will, so sociology must use indirect experiment: the comparative method. (Mill, Durkheim notes, judged even this inapplicable to social science; Durkheim replies that society differs from living bodies only in being more complex.)
3. **P3.** Agreement and difference need cases that agree, or differ, in one respect only. No one can inventory everything that coexists in a society, so in sociology these methods yield only conjectures.
4. **P4.** Concomitant variation needs no such inventory. A parallel between the values two phenomena take, established in enough sufficiently varied cases, proves that they are related. *In words:* you cannot hold everything else fixed, but steady co-movement still shows a connection.
5. **P5.** The relation must then be interpreted: deduce how one could produce the other and check the deduction against new comparisons; if no direct link is intelligible, look for a common cause or an intermediary.
6. **P6.** (Assumed throughout the chapter.) One effect always has one cause. Where suicide seems to have several causes, there are really several types of suicide.

**∴ C.** Concomitant variation, applied to long, regular series rather than scattered examples, is sociology's main method of proof. As he puts it, "to illustrate an idea is not to prove it" (my translation).

**What kind of method is this?** In logic it is neutral between individualism and holism ([1.1](01-01-individuals-and-social-facts.md)). Durkheim yokes it to holist explanation: group rates explained by other social facts. That pairing is exactly where the ecological fallacy waits, because the data are group rates and the conclusion is often read as being about individuals. [3.2](03-02-suicide.md) presses this on his own religious comparisons.

**Where the argument is weakest.** At P5. Durkheim's trigger for seeking a common cause is whether a direct link is intelligible *to him*. A critic replies that confounding is a property of the world, not of our imagination: where a direct link seems plausible, P5 never prompts the search, and plausible stories are the ones most in need of it. A second line of attack targets P6. Modern causal analysis routinely allows one effect several causes, and Durkheim's escape, splitting the effect into types, risks defining the types by their causes. A defender answers that P5's demand for *new comparisons* is a genuine test, and that *Le suicide* runs them.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean): Robinson's census.** W. S. Robinson, "Ecological Correlations and the Behavior of Individuals" (*American Sociological Review*, 1950), used the 1930 US census. As David Freedman summarizes it, Robinson took, for each of the 48 states, the percent of residents aged 10 or older who were foreign-born and the percent who were literate. Across states the two correlate at about +0.53: states with more immigrants were *more* literate. Across individuals the correlation is about −0.11: the foreign-born were *less* likely to be literate. The sign flips because immigrants tended to settle in states where the native-born were relatively literate. As evidence this is one computation from one census; the logical point it illustrates is uncontroversial.

The figure has the same structure, and its arithmetic shows how far group data can mislead. A region's rate is the weighted average of its two groups:

$$\text{Region B: } 0.7 \times 85 + 0.3 \times 75 = 82\%.$$

Pooling all 600 adults, newcomers are $142/180 = 78.9\%$ literate and natives $340/420 = 81.0\%$. Now read the regions as a naive analyst would and fit a straight line through the three region points:

$$\text{literacy} = 64.6 + 52.5 \times \text{newcomer share}.$$

Setting the share to 0 and then to 1 "predicts" natives at 64.6% and newcomers at 117.1%, an impossible number. The group data are accurate. The step to individuals needs an assumption they cannot supply: that natives' literacy does not itself vary with how many newcomers live nearby. Here it does, because something about Region C (its schools, its jobs) both draws newcomers and raises everyone's literacy. That is a confounder at the group level, and it is the whole fallacy. [`econometrics` 4.1](../../econometrics/lessons/04-01-panel-data-fixed-effects.md) shows the same reversal as Simpson's paradox.

**Example 2 (hard): Skocpol's revolutions.** Theda Skocpol's *States and Social Revolutions* (1979) explains the French, Russian and Chinese revolutions using Mill's methods of agreement and difference. In her account, revolution came where an old-regime state broke down under foreign military pressure, a landed upper class having blocked its reforms, and village communities with some autonomy could then revolt. To avoid selecting on the outcome she adds crises that did *not* end in social revolution: seventeenth-century England, nineteenth-century Prussia, nineteenth-century Japan. That is the selection lesson done right.

Here is the strain. Three positive cases, a few negative ones, and many candidate causes. Stanley Lieberson ("Small N's and Big Conclusions", *Social Forces*, 1991) argued that Mill-style reasoning on so few cases is valid only if causes are deterministic, there is a single cause, causes do not interact, and cases are measured without error, assumptions he judged unrealistic for most social processes. Notice the second: it is Durkheim's P6. Defenders of comparative history reply that a case study's evidence is not the cross-case table alone but the sequence of events inside each case, which can rule rivals out. How much small-N comparison can prove is contested; that its evidence differs in kind from a large-N correlation, and should be judged as such, is not.

## Watch out

- **You might think Durkheim's two alternatives to direct causation are both rivals, but actually only the common cause is.** The interposed phenomenon, effect of the first and cause of the second, is a mechanism through which the first *does* cause the second. Finding it strengthens the causal claim.
- **You might think the ecological fallacy makes group-level data worthless, but actually they are the right evidence for group-level claims.** Durkheim's rates are social facts ([1.1](01-01-individuals-and-social-facts.md)). The fallacy is only the slide from "groups with more X have more Y" to "individuals with X are more likely to have Y."
- **You might think a test that supports an explanation shows the thing explained is good or bad, but actually those are separate claims.** What Durkheim argued is exegetical; whether weakening religious tradition raised suicide is empirical; whether that weakening is a loss or a liberation is evaluative and not this course's question. A social explanation of religious change is not evidence about religious truth, which is [`philosophy-of-religion`](../../philosophy-of-religion/syllabus.md)'s question.

## One-liner

> A comparison shows a cause only once you have ruled out a common cause, a selected sample, a slide from groups to individuals and an arrow running backwards; a mechanism tells you where to look.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢) *(Formal (a)-(b) · Exegetical (c).)*** Invented numbers. A city has three wards of 1,000 registered voters each. Council-election turnout by housing tenure:

| Ward | Renters' share of voters | Renters' turnout | Owners' turnout |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 20% | 40% | 55% |
| 2 | 50% | 47% | 62% |
| 3 | 80% | 55% | 70% |

(a) Compute each ward's turnout, and citywide turnout among renters and among owners. (b) Suppose you had only each ward's renter share and total turnout. What range of renter turnout is consistent with Ward 3's totals? With Ward 1's? (c) An invented campaign memo, not the words of any real person or party: "The more renters a ward has, the higher its turnout, so renters are our most reliable voters." In two sentences, name the error and say what the table shows.

**P2 (🟡) *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)*** Evaluate against evidence. In the Source, Durkheim explains the co-variation of suicide and education by a common cause. In *Le suicide* (1897, Book II, ch. 2) he meets a group that breaks the pattern. In the Prussian figures he cites for 1875–76, Jews were 1.3% of the population but 9.6% of secondary-school pupils, and Judaism had the lowest suicide rate of any confession. He explains that their schooling had a different origin, a religious minority's effort to hold its own against hostility, laid over a tradition that kept its force. So, he concludes, "the exception is only apparent; indeed it only confirms the law" (my translation).

(a) Which reading of the schooling–suicide link does this case count against, and why? Two sentences. (b) Is Durkheim's handling a genuine test of his common-cause account or a rescue of it? 120 words or fewer: say what each reading predicts for this group, name one confound the comparison leaves uncontrolled, and describe one further comparison that would discriminate.

**P3 (🟡) *(Exegetical (a)-(b).)*** Diagnose. An invented memo to a regional development agency, not the words of any real person or body:

> "We studied the twelve oldest worker cooperatives in the region. Every one holds a weekly members' assembly. Assemblies give members a sense of ownership, so they stay, and the cooperative survives. We recommend funding only cooperatives that commit to weekly assemblies."

(a) Name the evidence standard the memo's comparison violates, and the comparison it needs. Two sentences. (b) The memo also offers a mechanism. Say whether it is individualist or holist in [1.1](01-01-individuals-and-social-facts.md)'s explanatory sense, and name one observation inside a single cooperative that would support or weaken it. Two sentences.

<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Formal (a)-(b) · Exegetical (c))*

(a) A ward's turnout is the weighted average of its two groups:

| Ward | Turnout |
|---|---|
| 1 | $0.2 \times 40 + 0.8 \times 55 = 8 + 44 = 52\%$ |
| 2 | $0.5 \times 47 + 0.5 \times 62 = 23.5 + 31 = 54.5\%$ |
| 3 | $0.8 \times 55 + 0.2 \times 70 = 44 + 14 = 58\%$ |

Citywide, renters cast $80 + 235 + 440 = 755$ votes of $200 + 500 + 800 = 1{,}500$, a turnout of $50.3\%$. Owners cast $440 + 310 + 140 = 890$ of $1{,}500$, a turnout of $59.3\%$.

(b) Write $s$ for the renter share, $t$ for ward turnout and $r$ for renter turnout. Owners' turnout is then $(t - sr)/(1-s)$, and it must lie between 0 and 1, so

$$\max\!\left(0,\ \frac{t-(1-s)}{s}\right) \le r \le \min\!\left(1,\ \frac{t}{s}\right).$$

Ward 3: $(0.58 - 0.20)/0.80 = 0.475$ and $0.58/0.80 = 0.725$, so renter turnout lies between 47.5% and 72.5% (the true value, 55%, is inside). Ward 1: $(0.52 - 0.80)/0.20$ is negative and $0.52/0.20 = 2.6$, so the range is 0% to 100%: the totals say nothing. Aggregates are informative about a group only where it dominates.

**Must hit, strict (c):**

- The error is the ecological fallacy: a ward-level pattern (renter-heavy wards turn out more) read as an individual-level one (renters turn out more).
- The table shows the reverse at the individual level: renters vote 15 points less than owners in every ward and 9 points less citywide (50.3% against 59.3%); turnout rises with renter share because both groups vote more in the renter-heavy wards.

**Wrong turns:** treating the ward pattern as false (it is accurate; only the inference fails); in (b), reporting a single number for renter turnout, which the totals cannot give. A straight line through the three wards would imply owners at about 49.8% and renters at about 59.8%, the wrong sign.

---

**P2** *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b))*

**Must hit, strict (a):**

- It counts against the direct reading, that schooling itself raises suicide: on that reading the most heavily schooled group should have high suicide, and it has the lowest.
- It leaves standing the common-cause reading, which predicts low suicide wherever tradition remains strong, whatever the level of schooling; so the case discriminates between the two.

**Must hit, any verdict (b):**

- The predictions: direct reading, high suicide; common cause, low suicide *if* the group's tradition is intact.
- The test is genuine only if the strength of tradition is established independently. If the evidence that tradition is intact is the low suicide rate itself, or a story about the origin of the schooling told after the fact, the case cannot fail, and it rescues rather than tests.
- One uncontrolled confound, for example: a small minority facing hostility may be unusually cohesive (which is integration, Durkheim's own variable, under another name), or may differ in family structure, urban concentration or age profile.
- One discriminating comparison, for example: a group whose schooling rose for a reason unconnected to weakening tradition, such as a compulsory-schooling law (common cause predicts no rise in suicide, the direct reading a rise); or an independent measure of tradition (observance, intermarriage) checked against suicide within a level of schooling.

**Wrong turns:** treating "the exception confirms the law" as a fallacy on sight (it is a legitimate move when the exception's special cause is shown independently); treating the case as evidence about the truth of any religion, when it bears only on social integration.

**Model answer (b), one of several:** If schooling caused suicide, this heavily schooled group should have high rates; on the common-cause account it should have low rates as long as its tradition is intact. Durkheim gets the second, but his evidence that the tradition is intact is largely his account of why the group sought schooling, offered after the fact, so the case confirms more weakly than he claims. It also leaves a confound open: a minority under hostility may be unusually cohesive, which is integration under another name. A sharper test would compare suicide before and after a compulsory-schooling law in a population whose religious practice was measured independently: a rise favors the direct reading, no change the common cause.

---

**P3** *(Exegetical (a)-(b))*

**Must hit, strict (a):**

- Selection on the dependent variable: only survivors were studied, so the memo cannot show that assemblies are rarer among cooperatives that failed (Mill's method of agreement with no variation in the outcome).
- The needed comparison: all cooperatives founded in some period, failed and surviving, comparing survival rates with and without weekly assemblies (and, ideally, checking that committed founders do not choose both assemblies and the habits that keep a cooperative alive).

**Must hit, strict (b):**

- Individualist: the mechanism runs through members' sense of ownership and their decisions to stay.
- One observation, for example: whether members who attend assemblies leave less often than members who do not, or whether departures fell after a cooperative began holding assemblies; leavers attending as often as stayers would weaken it.

**Wrong turns:** calling the flaw confounding alone (confounding is a further worry; the first problem is that the outcome never varies); calling the mechanism holist because the outcome is the cooperative's survival, when the explanation runs through individual choices.

**Model answer:** (a) The memo studies only cooperatives that survived, so it selects on the outcome and cannot tell whether failed cooperatives held assemblies just as often. It needs a full cohort of cooperatives, failed and surviving, compared on survival with and without assemblies. (b) The mechanism is individualist, running through members' feelings of ownership and their choices to stay. If within one cooperative the members who skip assemblies leave no more often than those who attend, the mechanism is in trouble.

## Flashback

**From Lesson [1.2](01-02-functional-explanation-and-its-critics.md) (Functional explanation and its critics):** *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)* Find the crux. Bronisław Malinowski, "Magic, Science and Religion" (1925), paraphrased: in the Trobriand Islands, villages on the inner lagoon fished by an easy, reliable method with no danger, and used no magic for it; on the open-sea shore, where fishing was dangerous and catches uncertain, it came with extensive magical ritual meant to secure safety and a good catch. The function of magic, he argued, is to let people carry out vital tasks with confidence, holding hope against fear; and that, he concluded, is why magic is found in every society of the kind he studied. (a) Using Elster's conditions, which does this confidence account assert, and which does it leave unshown? How would Cohen use the lagoon and open-sea contrast? Three sentences. (b) A rival: the fishermen perform the rites because they believe the rites secure fish and safety, and they believe this only where skill cannot. Say what kind of explanation the rival is, whether it predicts the same contrast, and name one observation that would favor one account over the other. 100 words or fewer.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):**

- Asserted: the confidence is an effect of the rites, it benefits the fishermen, and it is unintended, since the rites are performed to secure safety and a catch (the manifest function), not to calm anyone. Calm is a latent function in Merton's sense, and that it goes unrecognized is assumed rather than shown.
- Unshown: the feedback condition. Nothing says how the confidence keeps the rites going, and "that is why magic is found everywhere" moves straight from benefit to existence, the bare benefit claim's shape.
- Cohen: the contrast looks like a small consequence law (magic present where it would help, absent where it would not), so it is some evidence that a loop exists before anyone names it. Elster's reply is that such laws are easy to read off cases that fit, and this is one society and two settings.

**Must hit, any verdict (b):**

- The rival is intentional: the actors' beliefs and aims do the explaining. It needs the belief, not the belief's truth.
- It predicts the same contrast: nobody reaches for a rite where a reliable method already works. So the contrast cannot discriminate, and that is the crux. Cohen's evidence counts for a function only if no rival predicts the same pattern.
- A discriminating observation, for example: whether the rites persist among men who no longer believe they secure fish or safety (the confidence account predicts they persist in dangerous fishing, the rival that they fade with the belief); or whether crews that skip a rite show more fear, turning back or hesitating more often, which is the reinforcement loop the confidence account needs.

**Wrong turns:** counting the lagoon's lack of magic as confirming the function on its own, when the rival predicts it too. Calling the rival functional because the rites have a purpose: a purpose the actors hold makes an explanation intentional. Treating either part as a question about whether magic works or whether its beliefs are true; both accounts are assessed only as explanations of a practice.

**Model answer:** (a) The account asserts an effect (confidence), a beneficiary (the fishermen) and that the benefit is unintended, a latent function beside the manifest aim of safety and fish; it never shows the loop by which confidence sustains the rites, and its step to universality is a bare benefit claim. Cohen would read the lagoon and open-sea contrast as a consequence law, evidence that some loop exists. Elster would answer that one society and two settings is thin. (b) The rival is intentional and predicts the same contrast, so the contrast does not decide between them. Watch men who stop believing: if they keep the rites before dangerous voyages and report steadier nerves, the confidence account gains; if the rites lapse with the belief, the rival gains.

</details>
