# Phenomenology & Personalism · Lesson 4.1: Scheler: values given in feeling

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 4: Values, empathy and the person: Scheler and Stein · Builds on: [1.1 Intentionality](01-01-intentionality-from-brentano-to-husserl.md), [ethics 2.1–2.3](../../ethics/lessons/02-01-the-good-will-and-acting-from-duty.md), [modern-philosophy 6.5](../../modern-philosophy/lessons/06-05-nietzsche-perspectivism-and-genealogy.md) · Unlocks: [4.2 Sympathy, love and the person](04-02-scheler-sympathy-love-and-the-person.md), [5.1 Wojtyła and Scheler](05-01-wojtyla-and-scheler.md)

## Why this matters

Kant left ethics a dilemma: a moral law is either formal and a priori, or it has content and rests on experience, and then it is hedonism in disguise ([ethics 2.2](../../ethics/lessons/02-02-the-formula-of-universal-law.md)). Max Scheler (1874–1928) used Husserl's method to deny the dilemma. His *Der Formalismus in der Ethik und die materiale Wertethik* (Part I 1913, Part II 1916, both in Husserl's *Jahrbuch*) argues that values are objects given in a kind of feeling, ranked in an order as strict as logic. Wojtyła wrote his habilitation on it ([5.1](05-01-wojtyla-and-scheler.md)).

## The idea

**What Scheler grants Kant.** An ethics of goods or purposes fails: things and outcomes change, and a morality pegged to them would be empirical and relative. And "good" cannot be the content of the will. In Part I Scheler says the value *good* appears when we realize the higher value, and sits as if "on the back" of that act, so it can never be what the act aims at. The Pharisee who acts in order to be good misses it.

**What he denies.** Kant equated *a priori* with *formal*: whatever is necessary must come from reason's form, so any content must be empirical. Scheler calls this identification a fundamental error of Kant's teaching (Part I, ch. II). For a phenomenologist, an a priori is an essential connection given in intuition, and it can hold among contents. That a colour cannot exist without extension is a content truth, not a formal one. This is the [material a priori](../reference.md#material-a-priori).

**Values are not goods.** A value is a quality, like a colour, given independently of the thing that bears it. A good is a "value-thing" (*Wertding*). The distinction shows when a painting's colours fade: the thing survives, but the good is destroyed ([values and goods](../reference.md#values-and-goods)). Goods come and go; the values and their order do not.

**Feeling is cognition.** Scheler separates feeling-states (*Gefühle*: a pain, a mood) from intentional feeling (*Fühlen*: the act that grasps something). The same pain can be suffered, endured or even enjoyed, and an *observed* pain is almost the opposite of a *suffered* one. A state is linked to its cause only afterwards, by thought. Feeling the beauty of snowy mountains at sunset is different, because the feeling reaches its object directly. It is intentional in exactly Husserl's sense ([1.1](01-01-intentionality-from-brentano-to-husserl.md)):

> Here we do not feel *about* something; we feel something immediately, a determinate value-quality [*Wertqualität*]. (translation ours, from the 1916 German; Part II, V.2)

Built on feeling is **preferring** (*Vorziehen*), the act in which one value is given as higher than another. It is not choosing. We choose only between actions, but we prefer fine weather to foul. Part I insists the rank of values can never be deduced. Which is higher must be grasped anew in preferring, with an intuitive evidence no logic replaces ([feeling and preferring](../reference.md#feeling-and-preferring)).

**The hierarchy.** Part I names four modalities, from lowest: the **sensible** (pleasant/unpleasant); the **vital** (noble/vulgar: health, vigour, the felt rise and decline of life); the **spiritual** (beautiful/ugly, right/wrong, and the value of pure knowledge of truth); and the **holy** (holy/unholy), given only in objects intended as absolute. The useful is no rung. It is a consecutive value founded on the pleasant ([hierarchy of values](../reference.md#hierarchy-of-values)). Then come five marks of height, which Scheler offers as signs, possibly reducible to one: a value seems higher the more it **lasts**, the less it is **extended and divisible**, the less it is **founded** on other values, the **deeper the satisfaction** in feeling it, and the less **relative** it is to the kind of being who feels it (the pleasant exists only for sensing beings, the noble only for living ones) ([criteria of value height](../reference.md#criteria-of-value-height)).

**Ressentiment.** Scheler's 1912 essay, revised as "Das Ressentiment im Aufbau der Moralen", explains how the order gets falsified. [Ressentiment](../reference.md#ressentiment) is a lasting self-poisoning of the mind. It forms when revenge, envy and spite are repeatedly held down by a sense of impotence, and it ends in fixed **value-delusions** (*Werttäuschungen*). Sour grapes is only the first step: the fox says the grapes are sour, not that sweetness is bad. Ressentiment proper falsifies the values themselves. Scheler takes over the mechanism from Nietzsche ([modern-philosophy 6.5](../../modern-philosophy/lessons/06-05-nietzsche-perspectivism-and-genealogy.md)) and calls it the most profound modern discovery about moral judgment,

> even if his more specific claim, that Christian morality, and Christian love in particular, are the finest "flower of ressentiment" [*Blüte des Ressentiment*], should prove false. (translation ours, from the 1923 printing of *Vom Umsturz der Werte*)

He argues that it is false. Christian love does reverse the Greek direction of love. Instead of the lower striving toward the higher, the noble stoops to the lowly. On his reading, though, the stooping flows from fullness and inner security, not from impotence. He finds ressentiment instead at the root of modern bourgeois morality, and in a modern "love of humanity" that loves mankind only to set it against something hated.

## Source

Scheler, *Formalismus*, Part II (*Jahrbuch* II, 1916), V.2 "Fühlen und Gefühle", p. 120. He has just cited Pascal's "the heart has its reasons".

> Rather: there is a kind of experience [*Erfahrungsart*] whose objects are wholly closed to the "understanding", which is as blind to them as ear and hearing are to colour. Yet it is a kind of experience that brings us genuine objective objects and an eternal order among them: namely values, and an order of rank [*Rangordnung*] among them. And the order and laws of this experiencing are as determinate, exact and evident as those of logic and mathematics. That is, there are evident connections and conflicts between values and value-attitudes, and the acts of preferring built on them, on the basis of which a genuine grounding of moral decisions, and of laws for them, is possible and necessary. (translation ours, from the 1916 German)

Three claims sit in it: a distinct organ (feeling, not intellect), objective objects (values and their rank), and evidence strict enough to *ground* moral decisions. The last is what Kant thought only reason's form could supply.

## The argument

**Against formalism** ([Scheler against formalism](../reference.md#scheler-against-formalism)).

1. **Kant: a material ethics must take its content from goods or purposes, so it is a posteriori and heteronomous; only a formal law is a priori.** *In words:* content means experience, and experience means inclination.
2. **"A priori" means given as an essential connection in intuition, and not "contributed by reason's form".** *In words:* necessity can live in contents.
3. **Values are such contents: qualities distinct from their bearers, given in intentional feeling, with evident relations of higher and lower given in preferring.**
4. **These relations hold whatever goods happen to exist, and whatever the organization of the being that feels them.** *In words:* they are not abstracted from goods, so they are not a posteriori.
5. **Every ought rests on a value, not the reverse.** In Part II's words, "every ought is founded on values, whereas values are in no way founded on an ideal ought" (translation ours, from the 1916 German; IV.2a).
6. **∴ A non-formal ethics can be a priori and non-hedonistic, and Kant's ethics of law inverts the true order of value and ought.**

Premise 5 meets Kant head-on. The *Critique of Practical Reason* (Analytic, ch. 2) holds that good and evil must be determined after and by means of the moral law, not before it. Each side calls the other's order the root error.

**Where the argument is weakest.** Premise 3, the claim that feeling and preferring yield *evidence*. J. L. Mackie's argument from relativity (*Ethics: Inventing Right and Wrong*, 1977, ch. 1; [ethics 6.3](../../ethics/lessons/06-03-error-theory.md)) is aimed at every view of this shape. Rankings vary across cultures, and different ways of life explain the variation better than some people perceiving values others miss. A special organ of value-perception is also queer. Scheler's reply is built in. Ages differ in their *dominant* ranking, not in the order itself. Error is explained by value-blindness and value-delusion, as optical illusion is explained without denying sight. And love widens what a person can feel. The critic answers that a theory which can redescribe every dissent as blindness has made itself unfalsifiable.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean: running the marks).** Compare a box of chocolates, a recovered runner's vigour, and a string quartet. *Duration:* the chocolate's pleasantness is gone as it is eaten. Vigour lasts a season. The quartet's beauty is given as something that persists through every hearing. *Divisibility:* chocolate shared is chocolate halved. Beauty shared with a thousand listeners is not diminished. *Foundation:* Scheler says the pleasant, as a value, is founded on a vital value such as health, so the founding vital value is the higher. *Depth:* a sweet gives a pleasure at the surface. Vigour colours a whole day. *Relativity:* the chocolate's value needs a sensing being, vigour's a living one, and the quartet's is given free of body and surroundings. All five marks agree: quartet over vigour over chocolate. The verdict still comes from preferring. The marks only confirm it.

**Example 2 (hard: height against urgency).** A village has one grant. It can restore a fresco cycle (spiritual), or install a clean-water pump that ends recurring dysentery (vital). Read flat, the ladder says frescoes, and Part I even says there is clear evidence that vital values *ought* to be sacrificed for spiritual ones. Most readers recoil. Scheler's defenders note that the hierarchy ranks *values*, not options. Preferring is not choosing, and an ought also depends on what is realizable and by whom. Nicolai Hartmann, who built on Scheler (*Ethik*, 1926), added a principle Scheler does not state: lower values are *stronger*, so violating them is graver, while fulfilling higher values is more meritorious. On Hartmann's terms leaving the dysentery is the graver failure, which favours the pump. A Schelerian who declines Hartmann's addition owes another account of urgency. Here the ladder by itself stops deciding.

## Watch out

- **You might think a value (*Wert*) is whatever someone values, but for Scheler it is an objective quality given in feeling.** Valuing (*Werthaltung*) can be blind or deluded. The value is what it misses.
- **You might think feeling (*Fühlen*) means having an emotion, but Scheler separates intentional feeling from feeling-states.** Anger wells up in me and runs its course. It grasps nothing, and some evil must already be felt for anger to arise. Preferring is not choosing either: it ranks values, not actions.
- **You might think *ressentiment* is ordinary resentment or envy, and that Scheler simply rejects Nietzsche.** It is a lasting attitude that produces value-delusion, which is neither error nor lying. And Scheler accepts Nietzsche's mechanism; he rejects the diagnosis of Christian love and relocates ressentiment in modern morality.

## One-liner

> Scheler keeps Kant's a priori but moves it from reason's form into the contents of feeling: values are given as qualities, ranked in preferring from pleasant to holy, and ressentiment is how that order gets falsified.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢) *(Exegetical (a) · Exegetical, then evaluative (b).)*** Apply to a hard case. Mira, a translator, has one free week and four options: (i) a beach resort, chosen for the food, sun and rest; (ii) a week with her oldest friend, whom she has not seen in years; (iii) one dinner at a celebrated restaurant; (iv) the Easter vigil at the abbey where she was baptized. (a) Place each option under Scheler's modalities, rank them, and apply at least three of his five marks. One sentence per option. (b) Her sister argues: "The resort comes first. Without rest she will be useless to her friend and asleep at the vigil. The lower value *founds* the higher, so it is higher." Is this Scheler's foundation? Then say whether her practical point survives. 100 words or fewer.

**P2 (🟡) *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)*** Close reading. Scheler, "Das Ressentiment im Aufbau der Moralen", in *Vom Umsturz der Werte* I (Leipzig, 1923 printing), pp. 76–77:

> The phenomenal peculiarity of the value-delusion [*Werttäuschung*] produced by ressentiment [...] does not consist in the other's positive values not being given to him in experience "as" positive, as "high" values at all, as though for his experience they were simply "not there". In that case there could be no talk of a "delusion". But neither does it consist in his feelingly experiencing them and merely passing and stating false judgments about them, judgments that conflict with what he experienced. That would be "error" or a lie. If we want to grasp his attitude descriptively, we can say only this: the values are still there for him as positive and high, but covered over, as it were, by the delusive values, through which they shine only faintly, as if "transparently". (translation ours, from the 1923 German)

(a) The passage rejects two descriptions of the ressentiment-person and accepts a third. Name all three and quote the words that decide each. Three sentences. (b) A Nietzschean replies: "'Transparency' just restates Scheler's objectivism. He assumes there are true values for the false ones to cover." Does the description beg the question? Any verdict; 120 words or fewer.

**P3 (🔴) *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)*** Find the crux. An invented exchange:

> **Kantian:** If moral knowledge rests on an evidence of preferring, two sincere people who prefer differently have no court of appeal. The Universal Law test is a procedure any rational agent can run on any maxim.
> **Schelerian:** A test for contradiction tells you nothing about what is worth doing. And the duty you test is already founded on values you felt before you tested anything.

(a) Name the single premise about the order of *good* and *ought* on which they part ways, with each side's textual source. Two sentences. (b) Give the Kantian's objection at full strength, then Scheler's best reply, and say whether the reply also helps the Kantian. 150 words or fewer.

<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical (a) · Exegetical, then evaluative (b))*

**Accept:** any ranking that puts the dinner lowest and the vigil highest, with the resort and friendship placed for stated reasons.

**Must hit, strict (a):**

- Dinner: sensible (pleasant), the lowest. It is brief, divisible (eaten portions are not shared), relative to sensing beings, and its satisfaction is shallow.
- Resort: a *good* bearing values of two modalities, sensible (food, sun) and vital (rest, restored vigour). The ladder ranks its values, not the week as a whole.
- Friendship: spiritual (for credit, not required: Part I ties friendship to spiritual sympathy). Sharing it does not halve it, and it lasts.
- Vigil: holy, the highest. It is given in an object intended as absolute, least relative, indivisible among participants, with the deepest satisfaction.
- At least three marks applied by name. Ranking: vigil > friendship > resort > dinner.

**Must hit, strict (b), first step:**

- No. Scheler's foundation is an essential relation of givenness: value *a* is founded on *b* when *a* can be given only if *b* already is. The founding value is the *higher* one (the useful is founded on the pleasant). The sister means a causal precondition between goods, which is a different relation, and her rule runs the wrong way.

**Must hit, any verdict (b), second step:**

- The practical point may survive on other grounds. Preferring is not choosing, and rest may be the means to realizing the higher values. It does not survive as a claim about *height*.

**Wrong turns:** ranking the resort as merely pleasant and ignoring its vital values. Accepting the sister's "foundation" because it sounds Schelerian.

**Model answer (b), one of several:** Not Scheler's foundation. For him a value is founded on another when it can only be *given* on the basis of the other, and the founding value is the higher one. The sister means that rest causally enables later goods, which is a fact about realizing goods, not about the rank of values. Her practical point can still stand. The hierarchy ranks values, not weeks. Choosing rest as a means to the friendship and the vigil prefers nothing lower; it serves the higher values.

---

**P2** *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b))*

**Must hit, strict (a):**

- Rejected, *blindness*: the values not given "as" positive at all, simply "not there". Ruled out because then there could be "no talk of a 'delusion'".
- Rejected, *false judgment*: he feels them and states judgments that "conflict with what he experienced". That would be "error" or a lie.
- Accepted, *overlay*: the values are "still there for him as positive and high", "covered over" by delusive values, shining through "as if 'transparently'". The delusion sits in feeling, not in judgment.

**Must hit, any verdict (b):**

- Say what the passage assumes: that there are true values for the delusion to cover, given in feeling. It is a description from inside Scheler's framework.
- Say what the passage offers that is not simply assumed: a reported datum, that the disparaged values still "shine" faintly through. A pure invention of new values would leave no such residue.
- Then judge whether that residue needs objective values to explain it, or whether a rival account (suppressed desire, say) explains it as well.

**Wrong turns:** treating (a)'s third option as "he knows the truth and lies". The text classes lying with error and rejects both. Calling the passage an argument for objectivism; it is a description.

**Model answer (b), one of several:** Partly. As a description it presupposes what the Nietzschean denies: that there are high values for the delusion to cover. But it also reports something a Nietzschean must explain too: the ressentiment-person half-sees what he disparages, since the high values still shine faintly through. If ressentiment were only the creative invention of new values, that residue would be odd. The Nietzschean can reply that it is the trace of suppressed desire, not of seen values. So the passage does not settle the dispute. It moves it to the best explanation of a shared datum, the felt "transparency".

---

**P3** *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b))*

**Must hit, strict (a):**

- The crux is whether good is prior to ought or ought to good. Scheler: every ought is founded on values, not the reverse (*Formalismus* Part II, IV.2a).
- Kant: good and evil are determined only after and by means of the moral law (*Critique of Practical Reason*, Analytic, ch. 2).

**Must hit, any verdict (b):**

- The objection at strength: intuitive evidence without a public procedure leaves sincere disagreement unresolvable. Mackie's relativity point sharpens it.
- Scheler's reply: the dominant rankings of an age differ, not the order itself; blindness and ressentiment explain error; and the formal test gets its verdicts only from contents it smuggles in.
- Whether the reply helps the Kantian: Kantians can answer that the test needs no values, only the will's consistency with itself. Or concede that the formula of humanity already treats rational nature as a value.

**Wrong turns:** making the crux "reason vs emotion". Scheler's feeling is cognitive, and both sides claim a priori status. Treating Kant's test as having no content at all, which ignores the formula of humanity ([ethics 2.3](../../ethics/lessons/02-03-humanity-autonomy-and-the-lie.md)).

**Model answer (b), one of several:** The Kantian's worry is that the evidence of preferring is not public. When two people prefer differently, each can call the other blind, and nothing settles it. The Universal Law test asks only that a maxim be willable by any rational agent. Scheler replies that disagreement shows differing dominant rankings, distorted by blindness and ressentiment, as perceptual disputes show illusion without refuting sight. And the test is not neutral: what counts as a contradiction in a willed world depends on ends already valued. That reply cuts both ways. If Kant's test needs prior values, then Scheler still owes a public check on preferring. Whether any runnable moral procedure can do without felt values is exactly what divides them.

## Flashback

**From Lesson [3.3](03-03-marcel-mystery-presence-and-fidelity.md) (Marcel: mystery, presence and fidelity):** *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)* Apply to a new case. At her friend's son's christening, Irena promised the parents she would always stand by the boy, Tobi. Tobi is now sixteen, sullen, and they have little in common. Three ways her commitment could be going:

- **(i)** Every birthday she sends the same card with the same cheque she sent when he was eight. She never asks what he is like now. "I promised, so I do it."
- **(ii)** She calls him "my godson" to everyone, shows off his school prizes as if they were hers, and is wounded and resentful when he picks a different adult as his mentor.
- **(iii)** She drops the cards and starts coming to the climbing gym where he spends his weekends, mostly to listen. What grows between them is nothing either of them had when she made the promise.

(a) Name each in Marcel's terms and give the feature of the case that decides it. One or two sentences each. (b) Irena's sister objects to (iii): "She has broken her promise. She dropped the one thing she always did, and she never promised to take up climbing." On Marcel's account, has she? Any verdict; 80 words or fewer.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):**

- **(i) Constancy, not fidelity.** She keeps the behaviour against the grain and is faithful to her past act and its routine, not to Tobi as he now is; nothing in it is renewed availability. Marcel thinks such constancy can become a lie to herself and to the other.
- **(ii) Having, and unavailability.** The relation has become a possession: inventoried ("my godson", his prizes as her credit), defended like property, and it possesses her in turn. She is encumbered with herself (*indisponible*), so Tobi is before her as something she holds, not given as a thou.
- **(iii) Creative fidelity.** She is faithful to the person, not to the form the promise took on the day she made it, and keeps it by renewed availability (*disponibilité*). It makes something that did not exist at the vow, which is what Marcel means by *créatrice*.

**Must hit, any verdict (b):**

- State what the commitment binds to on Marcel's account: the person, not her feelings on the day (which she could not guarantee) and not a fixed behaviour (which is constancy).
- Apply it: dropping the cards breaks the promise only if the cards were what she promised; changing form can be how fidelity is kept.
- Say where the account stops deciding: it gives no rule for which changes count as renewal and which as abandonment, so a reader could still hold she has let something go.

**Wrong turns:** sorting the three by warm feeling, so that (i) fails because she feels nothing. A vow cannot bind feelings, so what decides is whether she is renewing her availability to Tobi, not what she happens to feel. Reading (ii) as presence because she is so involved: involvement held as possession is the opposite of availability.

**Model answer:** (a) (i) is constancy: the same card for an eight-year-old who no longer exists keeps a routine, not faith with Tobi. (ii) is having: the relation is held as her property, and the self-encumbrance of pride makes her unavailable. (iii) is creative fidelity: kept by renewed availability to Tobi, it creates a relation that did not exist when she vowed. (b) One of several: No. She promised to stand by Tobi, not to post cards, and Marcel holds that fidelity is to the person, so a change of form can be the very way the promise is kept. The sister is right about one thing: Marcel offers no test for when a change of form becomes abandonment. That judgment rests on whether Irena is actually available to him.

</details>
