# Phenomenology & Personalism — Syllabus

> Philosophy · Tier 2 · ~21 lessons · Prereqs: [`modern-philosophy`](../modern-philosophy/syllabus.md) · Roadmap id: `phenomenology-and-personalism`

## Goal

This course follows one twentieth-century idea from its method to its moral payoff. Husserl proposed to set aside theories about the mind and the world and describe experience exactly as it is given, and the result, phenomenology, split several ways. Heidegger turned it into an analysis of human existence. Sartre, Marcel and Levinas turned it toward freedom and the encounter with another person. Scheler and Edith Stein used it to describe values, empathy and the person, and Stein joined it to Aquinas. Karol Wojtyła, later John Paul II, built on that last line: *Love and Responsibility* and *The Acting Person* argue that a person is revealed in action and may never be merely used. Kierkegaard comes in as the precursor the existentialists read. You will finish able to run the epoché on an experience and describe what remains, place a case under Heidegger's structures of care, diagnose Sartrean bad faith, say what Levinas means by the face, rank a choice on Scheler's hierarchy of values, tell Stein's empathy from inference by analogy, and apply Wojtyła's personalistic norm to a hard case and say where it stops deciding. The course is verdict-neutral like the rest of the field. Personalism is presented at the strength its best readers would recognize, and so are its critics. It deliberately skips Merleau-Ponty, Beauvoir, Buber, Gadamer, Ricoeur and the deconstructive tradition (each named once at most), Heidegger's later thought after the "turn" (named only), the analytic philosophy of consciousness, and John Paul II's teaching as pope.

## How practice works in this course

Problems follow [HUMANITIES-BUILD-BRIEF.md](../../HUMANITIES-BUILD-BRIEF.md), verdict-neutral. Husserl and his realist critics, Heidegger and Sartre, Sartre and Marcel, Scheler and Wojtyła, the Thomist and the phenomenologist are each presented at the strength their best readers would recognize, and no rubric rewards siding with any of them. Four archetypes carry the course:

- **Apply to a hard case** is the workhorse. Phenomenology is a method of description, so most problems give an invented experience or situation (seeing a die from one side, a hammer that breaks, a waiter playing at being a waiter, a colleague's grief, an employer who values workers only for their output) and ask for the right description in the thinker's own terms. Graded strictly on the terms, then evaluatively on where the account stops deciding.
- **Diagnose** uses invented memos, op-eds and dialogue to find bad faith, *das Man*, *ressentiment* or "use" in Wojtyła's sense. Invented material is never attributed to a real person.
- **Find the crux** lines up the course's rival accounts of the same phenomenon: the other person as threat (Sartre), presence (Marcel) or command (Levinas); empathy as projection (Lipps), inference or a sui generis act (Stein); the person as act-center (Scheler) or as agent whose action reveals a self-possessing subject (Wojtyła).
- **Reconstruct** the few load-bearing arguments: the epoché is not doubt, death individualizes, formalism cannot ground ethics, the personalistic norm.

Steelman-and-reply and counterexample appear in every module; each module uses at least three archetypes. A recurring exegetical discipline is **terms of art**: "phenomenon", "authentic", "value", "empathy" and "person" each mean something exact in each author, and a term read in its everyday sense is graded as a misreading.

**Texts and copyright.** Almost everything in this course is modern and copyrighted, so **paraphrase with a section citation is the default**, and problems give the paraphrase plus the citation. That covers Husserl's *Cartesian Meditations* and *Crisis*, all of Sartre, Marcel, Levinas and Wojtyła, Stein's *Finite and Eternal Being*, and every English translation (Macquarrie and Robinson or Stambaugh for Heidegger, the Hongs for Kierkegaard, Barnes for Sartre, Lingis for Levinas, Willetts or Grabowski for *Love and Responsibility*). **Close reading** is rarer here than elsewhere in the field and is limited to the lesson's own translation, 150 words or fewer, of an original published in 1930 or earlier and so public domain in the United States, with the key German or Danish term given alongside: Brentano's *Psychologie vom empirischen Standpunkte* (1874), Husserl's *Logische Untersuchungen* (1900–01) and *Ideen* I (1913), Scheler's *Ressentiment* essay (1912), *Formalismus* (1913–16) and *Wesen und Formen der Sympathie* (1923), Stein's *Zum Problem der Einfühlung* (1917), Heidegger's *Sein und Zeit* (1927), and Kierkegaard's Danish originals. The course does not rely on the older English Kierkegaard translations being public domain.

## Dangerous Checklist

When you finish, you can:

- [ ] State the thesis of intentionality, analyse an experience into act, content and object, and say what it means for an empty intention to be fulfilled
- [ ] Perform the epoché on an ordinary perception, say what is bracketed and what remains, and explain why it is neither Cartesian doubt nor a denial that the world exists
- [ ] Explain Husserl's charge that the mathematization of nature forgot the lifeworld it grew from, and say what it does and does not claim about physics
- [ ] Place an invented situation under Heidegger's structures (ready-to-hand vs present-at-hand, *das Man*, thrownness and projection, anxiety vs fear) and reconstruct his argument that being-toward-death individualizes
- [ ] State the facts of Heidegger's involvement with National Socialism accurately, and separate them from the disputed question of what, if anything, they show about *Being and Time*
- [ ] Say what Kierkegaard's three stages and his accounts of anxiety and despair contribute to existentialism, and what Heidegger keeps and drops
- [ ] Diagnose bad faith in a case as a misuse of facticity or transcendence, and state Sartre's claim that existence precedes essence without turning it into a slogan
- [ ] Put one encounter under Sartre's look, Marcel's presence and Levinas's face, and name the premise about the other person on which they part ways
- [ ] Rank goods on Scheler's hierarchy of values using his criteria, explain his case against Kant's formalism, and tell his account of *ressentiment* from Nietzsche's
- [ ] Tell Stein's account of empathy from projection and from inference by analogy, and say how she describes the person as body, soul and spirit
- [ ] Apply Wojtyła's personalistic norm to a hard case, separate the two senses of "use", and say what it adds to the Formula of Humanity
- [ ] Explain how *The Acting Person* reads the person from action (efficacy, self-determination, integration, participation), and assess whether Wojtyła's join of phenomenology and Thomism is a synthesis or a juxtaposition

## Modules

### Module 1: Husserl and the phenomenological method

What phenomenology is: a return from theories to what is given, the discipline of bracketing that makes the return possible, and Husserl's late diagnosis of what modern science forgot.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1.1 | Intentionality: from Brentano to Husserl | State the thesis that consciousness is always of something, and say why it rejects both the theory of ideas and psychologism | Brentano's intentional inexistence (*Psychology from an Empirical Standpoint*, 1874, Book II ch. 1), the neo-Kantian backdrop named (Marburg and Baden), the attack on psychologism in the *Prolegomena* of the *Logical Investigations*, "phenomenon" here vs in Kant (6.1) and Hegel (6.4), the object as not an idea (against [`modern-philosophy`](../modern-philosophy/syllabus.md) 3.1, 4.1) |
| 1.2 | The anatomy of an experience | Analyse a perception, a memory and a judgment into act, content and object, and say what fulfils each | act quality and matter (Fifth Investigation), empty vs fulfilled intention and categorial intuition (Sixth Investigation), profiles (*Abschattungen*) and horizons, noesis and noema (*Ideas* I), inner time-consciousness named (retention, primal impression, protention), "to the things themselves" |
| 1.3 | The natural attitude, the epoché and the reductions | Perform the epoché on a case and explain why it is not doubt | the natural attitude and its general thesis (*Ideas* I §§27–32), bracketing vs Cartesian doubt ([`modern-philosophy`](../modern-philosophy/syllabus.md) 1.1), the principle of all principles (§24), eidetic variation, the transcendental ego vs Kant's "I think" (5.4), the *Cartesian Meditations* as a restaging of Descartes, the Göttingen realists' objection that the turn is idealism |
| 1.4 | The lifeworld and the crisis of the sciences | Say what Husserl means by the lifeworld and by the claim that Galileo's mathematization of nature concealed it | *Crisis* (1936) Part II §9, idealization and the "garb of ideas", the lifeworld as ground of meaning for science, what the charge does not claim (that physics is false), "The Origin of Geometry" named |

**Boss problem 1:** (a) An invented sequence of experiences: seeing a die from one side, turning it over, later remembering it, imagining a seven-faced die, and judging "this die has six faces." For each, give the act quality, the object, what is intended emptily and what fulfils it (1.2). (b) Reconstruct Husserl's reasons that the epoché differs from Descartes's method of doubt, in standard form, marking what each procedure does to the belief that the world exists (1.3). (c) Close-read the principle of all principles (*Ideas* I §24, in the lesson's own translation from the 1913 German). Does the text make intuition a source of justification for every claim, or does it restrict what intuition justifies? Point to the words that decide it. (d) The Göttingen realists held that the transcendental reduction led Husserl into idealism. State their objection at full strength, give Husserl's best reply from 1.3, and assess it, in 300–500 words. Parts (a)–(c) are exegetical; (d) is evaluative.

### Module 2: Existence: Kierkegaard and Heidegger

A turn from consciousness to existence: Kierkegaard's single individual facing a choice no system can make, and Heidegger's analysis of the being for whom its own being is an issue.

|---|---|---|---|
| 2.1 | Kierkegaard: the single individual | Say what the aesthetic, ethical and religious stages are and what anxiety and despair disclose about the self | pseudonymity, *Either/Or* (1843), Abraham and the teleological suspension of the ethical (*Fear and Trembling*, Problema I), the knight of faith vs the knight of infinite resignation, anxiety and freedom (*The Concept of Anxiety*, 1844), the self as a relation that relates itself to itself (*The Sickness unto Death*, 1849), truth as subjectivity; the leap assessed as fideism ceded to [`philosophy-of-religion`](../philosophy-of-religion/syllabus.md) 5.4 |
| 2.2 | Heidegger: Dasein and being-in-the-world | Explain the question of Being and describe a situation in terms of equipment and world rather than subject and object | *Being and Time* (1927), the ontological difference, Dasein and existence (§9), phenomenology as hermeneutic (§7), the ready-to-hand and the present-at-hand, the hammer and the broken tool (§§15–16), against the Cartesian subject ([`modern-philosophy`](../modern-philosophy/syllabus.md) 1.2), the break with Husserl, Davos (1929) named |
| 2.3 | The they, mood and care | Place a case under *das Man*, thrownness, projection and fallenness, and state the structure of care | being-with, *das Man* (§27), idle talk and curiosity, attunement and thrownness, understanding as projection, fallenness, anxiety vs fear (§40), care as the being of Dasein (§41); Kierkegaard's anxiety (2.1) as the source |
| 2.4 | Being-toward-death, authenticity, and 1933 | Reconstruct the argument that death individualizes, say what authenticity is, and state the political record accurately | death as ownmost, non-relational, certain, indefinite possibility (§§46–53), the they's evasion of death, the call of conscience and resoluteness, temporality and the "turn" named; the record: party membership (1933–45), the Freiburg rectorate (1933–34) and the rectoral address, the post-war teaching ban, the *Black Notebooks* (published from 2014); the debate over whether the philosophy is implicated (Löwith, Farías, Faye, and defenders who separate the work from the man), facts graded strictly and interpretation evaluatively |

**Boss problem 2:** (a) An invented week in a carpenter's life: a workshop, a snapped chisel, an evening of gossip, a sleepless night after a diagnosis. Place each episode under Heidegger's terms (ready-to-hand or present-at-hand, *das Man* and idle talk, fear or anxiety) and say what each discloses (2.2–2.3). (b) Reconstruct Heidegger's argument that anticipating death individualizes Dasein, in standard form, marking where "non-relational" does the work (2.4). (c) Find the crux between Kierkegaard's anxiety and despair (2.1) and Heidegger's anxiety (2.3): what does Heidegger's account keep, and which premise about what the self stands before does it drop? (d) Critics beginning with Heidegger's former student Karl Löwith charged that resoluteness is a decision without content, and so offered no resistance to 1933. State the charge precisely, keeping the documented facts separate from the interpretation, give the strongest reply a defender of *Being and Time* makes, and assess it, in 300–500 words. Parts (a)–(c) are exegetical; (d) is evaluative.

### Module 3: Freedom and the other: Sartre, Marcel, Levinas

Three answers to what another person is for me: a gaze that turns me into an object, a presence I can only meet by being available, or a face that commands before I choose.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3.1 | Sartre: existence precedes essence, and bad faith | State Sartre's account of freedom and diagnose bad faith in a case | "Existentialism Is a Humanism" (1945) and the student torn between his mother and the Free French, anguish and abandonment, being-in-itself vs being-for-itself, facticity and transcendence, bad faith (*Being and Nothingness*, 1943, Part One ch. 2): the waiter and the woman on a date; *The Transcendence of the Ego* against Husserl's transcendental ego (1.3) named |
| 3.2 | Sartre: the look | Explain how the other's gaze makes me an object for myself, and why Sartre thinks relations with others are conflict | the look and shame at the keyhole (*Being and Nothingness*, Part Three ch. 1), being-for-others, the other as subject I cannot perceive, love and its failure, Heidegger's "Letter on 'Humanism'" (published 1947) rejecting Sartre's reading, Beauvoir's *Ethics of Ambiguity* named |
| 3.3 | Marcel: mystery, presence and fidelity | Tell a problem from a mystery and say what availability to another person involves | Marcel's conversion (1929), "On the Ontological Mystery" (1933), problem vs mystery, being vs having (*Being and Having*, 1935), my body as incarnate existence, availability (*disponibilité*), creative fidelity and hope (*Homo Viator*), the label "Christian existentialist" and Marcel's later distance from it, Buber's *I and Thou* named |
| 3.4 | Levinas: the face of the other | Say what Levinas means by the face and why he calls ethics first philosophy | Levinas as Husserl's translator and Heidegger's student, the critique of ontology as totality ("Is Ontology Fundamental?", 1951), *Totality and Infinity* (1961) Section III, the face as expression and command against murder, Descartes's idea of the infinite ([`modern-philosophy`](../modern-philosophy/syllabus.md) 1.3) as the model, asymmetry, the third party and justice, *Otherwise than Being* (1974) named |

**Boss problem 3:** (a) Diagnose an invented memo in which a hospital administrator explains that denying a patient's appeal "was not really my choice; I am only the policy's instrument." Name the structure of bad faith at work, facticity or transcendence, and say what Sartre would call the honest description (3.1). (b) The administrator then meets the patient in the corridor. Describe the encounter as Sartre (3.2), Marcel (3.3) and Levinas (3.4) would each describe it, in each thinker's terms. (c) Find the crux between Sartre and Levinas: which premise about how the other first appears to me must one of them deny? (d) A critic objects that Levinas's asymmetry, an infinite responsibility to each face, cannot survive the third party, since justice among many requires comparing faces. State the objection at full strength, give Levinas's own account of the third, and assess whether it answers the objection or changes the subject, in 300–500 words. Parts (a)–(c) are exegetical; (d) is evaluative.

### Module 4: Values, empathy and the person: Scheler and Stein

The branch of phenomenology that looked at values and persons rather than existence: Scheler's ethics of felt values and love, and Stein's account of empathy and her road from Husserl to Aquinas.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4.1 | Scheler: values given in feeling | Rank goods on Scheler's hierarchy and state his case against Kant's formalism | *Formalism in Ethics and Non-Formal Ethics of Values* (1913–16), the material a priori, values vs goods, feeling and preferring as cognition, the hierarchy (the pleasant, the vital, the spiritual, the holy) and its criteria (duration, indivisibility, foundation, depth of satisfaction, non-relativity), against formalism (citing [`ethics`](../ethics/syllabus.md) 2.1–2.3), *ressentiment* (1912) as value-deception and his reply to Nietzsche's genealogy ([`modern-philosophy`](../modern-philosophy/syllabus.md) 6.5) |
| 4.2 | Scheler: sympathy, love and the person | Tell the forms of fellow-feeling apart and explain why Scheler says the person is never an object | *The Nature of Sympathy* (1913; expanded 1923): emotional contagion, identification, fellow-feeling; love as a movement toward higher value, the *ordo amoris*, the person as the concrete unity of acts, Scheler's Catholic period and later break named, von Hildebrand's value-response named |
| 4.3 | Stein: empathy | Tell Stein's account of empathy from projection and from inference by analogy, and apply its three levels to a case | *On the Problem of Empathy* (1917), against Lipps's inner imitation and against the argument from analogy, empathy as a sui generis act (a non-primordial experience of another's primordial experience), the three levels (emergence, fulfilling explication, comprehensive objectification), the lived body (*Leib*) vs the physical body, her work on Husserl's *Ideas* II, Husserl's later "pairing" (*Cartesian Meditations* V) compared |
| 4.4 | Stein: the person, and the meeting with Aquinas | Describe Stein's person as body, soul and spirit, and say what she thought phenomenology and Thomism each lacked | biography in brief (assistant to Husserl, baptism 1922, Carmel 1933, murdered at Auschwitz 1942; context cited from [`church-history`](../church-history/syllabus.md) 9.1), the 1929 essay comparing Husserl and Aquinas, *Finite and Eternal Being* (written 1935–37): finite being as pointing to eternal being, essence and existence ([`ancient-medieval-philosophy`](../ancient-medieval-philosophy/syllabus.md) 7.2 cited), the person as body, soul and spirit, the individual form |

**Boss problem 4:** (a) An invented choice: a doctor must decide whether to spend an evening on a well-paid consultation, a hard run that keeps her fit, finishing an honest report that will cost her a friendship, or attending Mass. Rank the goods on Scheler's hierarchy and apply at least three of his criteria (4.1). (b) Diagnose *ressentiment* in an invented op-ed that praises "humble" people for despising ambition, and say how Scheler's account of Christian love differs from Nietzsche's (4.1). (c) A friend reads you a letter announcing her father's death. Describe what you grasp of her grief at each of Stein's three levels, and say what the argument from analogy and Lipps's projection theory would each get wrong about the case (4.3). (d) Stein's late work places the phenomenological description of the person inside a Thomist metaphysics of finite and eternal being. State the strongest objection a strict Husserlian would make, the strongest a strict Thomist would make, and assess whether Stein's synthesis answers both, in 300–500 words. Parts (a)–(c) are exegetical; (d) is evaluative.

### Module 5: Wojtyla: the acting person

Karol Wojtyła's personalism: a Thomist who tested Scheler's ethics and found it wanting, an account of love that forbids using a person, and a philosophy of the person read from action. This is the philosophy the rest of the library needs for John Paul II.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5.1 | Wojtyła and Scheler | Reconstruct Wojtyła's verdict on whether Scheler's system can ground a Christian ethics | Wojtyła's Thomist formation (the Angelicum doctorate on faith in St John of the Cross, 1948), the habilitation thesis on Scheler, phenomenology as method vs as system, the charge that Scheler gives value-experience without efficacy or obligation, the Lublin school named |
| 5.2 | The personalistic norm | State the personalistic norm in its negative and positive forms and apply it to a case | *Love and Responsibility* (1960) ch. | 5.3 | The anatomy of love | Tell the elements of love apart and say what Wojtyła means by betrothed love as self-gift | *Love and Responsibility* ch. II: attraction, desire, goodwill, sympathy vs friendship, reciprocity, betrothed love; shame and chastity (ch. | 5.4 | Action reveals the person | Explain the difference between "man acts" and "something happens in man" and what it shows about the person | *The Acting Person* (Polish 1969; the disputed English edition of 1979) Parts I–II: consciousness in its mirroring and reflexive functions, efficacy, self-determination, self-possession and self-governance, the transcendence of the person in action, the experience of the human being as the starting point |
| 5.5 | Integration, participation and the bridge | Explain integration and participation, and assess the join of phenomenology and Thomism | *The Acting Person* Parts III–IV: integration of somatic and psychic dynamisms, participation, authentic attitudes (solidarity, opposition) vs inauthentic (conformism, avoidance); the essay "Subjectivity and the Irreducible in Man": the cosmological vs the personalist approach to the human being, Boethius's definition ([`metaphysics`](../metaphysics/syllabus.md) 6.5) and *operari sequitur esse*; the critics from both sides; destinations named: *Fides et Ratio* ([`fundamental-theology`](../fundamental-theology/syllabus.md) 2.4), the theology of the body and the moral encyclicals ([`moral-theology`](../moral-theology/syllabus.md)), solidarity and work (`catholic-social-teaching`) |

**Boss problem 5:** (a) Apply the personalistic norm to three invented cases: a manager who treats employees generously only because it raises output; a couple who stay together because each finds the other pleasant company and nothing more; a donor who funds a clinic for the publicity. For each, say which sense of "use" is in play, what the norm forbids and what it requires, and where it stops deciding (5.2). (b) Reconstruct Wojtyła's objection to Scheler in standard form: what does value-experience lack, on his reading, and why does ethics need it (4.1–4.2, 5.1)? (c) Find the crux between the personalistic norm and Kant's Formula of Humanity ([`ethics`](../ethics/syllabus.md) 2.3): name the premise about the adequate response to a person on which they part ways (5.2). (d) Is Wojtyła's bridge between phenomenology and Thomism a synthesis or a juxtaposition? State the strongest objection from a Thomist who holds that lived experience cannot ground metaphysics, and the strongest from a phenomenologist who holds that the Thomist *suppositum* is an unbracketed presupposition, then assess whether 5.4–5.5 answer both, in 300–500 words. Parts (a)–(c) are exegetical; (d) is evaluative.

## Notes

- **What this course assumes from [`modern-philosophy`](../modern-philosophy/syllabus.md),** exactly as that syllabus's Notes list it: Cartesian doubt and the cogito (1.1–1.2), answered by the epoché and the *Cartesian Meditations* in 1.3; the empiricist theory of ideas and Hume's bundle self (3.1, 4.1, 4.4), rejected by intentionality in 1.1 and by Scheler's person in 4.2; Kant's intuition/concept distinction, synthesis, the transcendental method and the "I think" (5.1, 5.3–5.4), reworked as fulfilment in 1.2 and the transcendental ego in 1.3; the thing in itself (6.1), against which "to the things themselves" is read in 1.2; Hegel's *Phenomenology* (6.4) as the older sense of the word in 1.1; Nietzsche's genealogy (6.5), answered by Scheler's *ressentiment* in 4.1. As that note assigns, Brentano and the neo-Kantians are owned here (1.1), briefly.
- **What [`moral-theology`](../moral-theology/syllabus.md) and `catholic-social-teaching` can assume.** The personalistic norm and the two senses of "use" (5.2), the analysis of love as self-gift (5.3), self-determination and self-possession (5.4), and participation and solidarity as philosophical notions (5.5). Those courses own what the Church teaches with them and at what level of authority. This course never marks doctrinal authority, because it teaches Wojtyła as a philosopher.
- **Order.** The modules run by theme, not date. Scheler (from 1912) and Stein (from 1917) come before Heidegger chronologically, but sit in Module 4 so that the road from Husserl through value ethics and Stein's Thomism lands directly on Wojtyła, who read Scheler closely. Marcel's early work predates Sartre's; he is placed after Sartre's look because the module compares their accounts of the other.
- **Heidegger and 1933.** The steering asks for the record honestly and briefly, so it is the closing section of 2.4, not a lesson. Documented facts (party membership, the rectorate and its address, the teaching ban, the *Black Notebooks* and their antisemitic passages) are exegetical and graded strictly. Whether those facts bear on *Being and Time* is evaluative and verdict-neutral; the lesson presents the case that they do and the case for separating the work from the man, each at full strength. The same discipline applies to Scheler's and Marcel's changing relations to the Church in 4.2 and 3.3.
- **Copyright.** The close-reading pool rests on the rule that works published in 1930 or earlier are public domain in the United States. *Sein und Zeit* (1927) is still in copyright in Europe, and every English translation used by the course is in copyright, so lessons translate only short passages themselves and paraphrase everything else. Where a lesson writer is unsure of a work's status, the default is paraphrase. No boss problem quotes a primary text: Boss 1(c) is the only one to use a passage, and it uses the lesson's own translation of a 1913 original.
- **Accuracy discipline.** Section numbers are given only where they are certain (*Ideas* I §24 and §§27–32; *Being and Time* §7, §9, §§15–16, §27, §40–41, §§46–53; *Crisis* §9; the part and chapter divisions of *Being and Nothingness*, *Totality and Infinity*, *Love and Responsibility* and *The Acting Person*). Lessons must not add section numbers for Scheler's *Formalism*, *The Concept of Anxiety* or Stein's works without checking them against an edition. The English *Acting Person* (1979) is known to diverge from the Polish text; lessons should say so and cite by part, not page.
- **Lesson count.** The roadmap estimated ~18; this runs 21 (+17 percent, inside the ±25 percent band). The steering lists eight thinkers plus Kierkegaard and Brentano, and Wojtyła needs five lessons: his critique of Scheler, the norm, the analysis of love and the two halves of *The Acting Person* are each a full ten minutes, and this course is the library's only home for them. Heidegger gets three lessons and Husserl four because the later modules use their vocabulary constantly. Levinas and Marcel get one each, as the steering's framing implies.
- **Ownership collisions checked.** No built lessons in the library teach any of this course's thinkers. On-disk syllabi that touch its ground are [`ethics`](../ethics/syllabus.md) (cedes Wojtyła here), [`modern-philosophy`](../modern-philosophy/syllabus.md) (cedes Husserl through Wojtyła and excludes Kierkegaard), [`philosophy-of-religion`](../philosophy-of-religion/syllabus.md) 5.4 (owns Kierkegaard's leap as fideism, so 2.1 does not evaluate it), [`metaphysics`](../metaphysics/syllabus.md) 6.5 (owns what a person is as a metaphysical question, cited in 5.5), [`church-history`](../church-history/syllabus.md) 9.1 (names Edith Stein in its account of the Dutch bishops' protest, cited in 4.4), [`moral-theology`](../moral-theology/syllabus.md) (cedes Wojtyła's philosophy here and teaches the theology of the body in its 6.1, so 5.3 stops at the philosophy of love), and [`philosophy-of-mind`](../philosophy-of-mind/syllabus.md) (cedes Husserl and the Brentano school here while stating Brentano's thesis as a live claim in its 4.1).
