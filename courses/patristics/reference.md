# The Church Fathers · Reference Card

> Open book. This card is meant to be open while you work — including during
> quizzes. Nothing here is something you're expected to recall cold.

This course reads the Fathers in their own words, so the card is built for the questions a passage actually raises mid-problem: what this Greek or Latin word meant to the man using it, who he was and what he was fighting, which text you are holding and what it can and cannot establish, which translation the words in front of you come from, which passages three traditions each claim and on what words, which famous sentences nobody ever wrote, and where a ceded topic (what a council *defined*, what a passage *proves*) is actually taught. Contested passages here are **maps, not verdicts** — the arguing belongs to [`apologetics-catholic-claims`](../apologetics-catholic-claims/syllabus.md). Where an entry touches a boss problem it stops at outline level.

## Concepts and distinctions

One `###` per concept: the plain-English line first, then what the texts actually say, then the lesson.

### The four marks of a Father

The four things the manual tradition asks of a writer before calling him a Father.

**Antiquity, orthodoxy of doctrine, holiness of life, approval of the Church.** No council ever promulgated the list and there is no official roll. Joseph Fessler, stating the marks in the nineteenth century, shrugged at the last and noted that antiquity is required "at the present day" — by convention, not definition. What functions in practice is a set of working questions: has a general council cited him, does the Roman Martyrology praise his holiness and learning, was he read publicly in churches, do later Fathers quote him as an authority on the faith. A settled judgement of the tradition, not a juridical status: treat borderline cases as borderline and say so.

*Introduced:* [1.1](lessons/01-01-how-to-read-a-church-father.md)

### Father, Doctor and ecclesiastical writer

Three titles answering three different questions.

A **Father** is an ancient witness with the four marks. A **Doctor of the Church** is a later, narrower honour — eminent learning, holiness of life, and a formal declaration, in practice always papal — and antiquity is *not* required, which is why most Doctors are not Fathers and most Fathers are not Doctors. An **ecclesiastical writer** is read, quoted and often indispensable but missing a mark: Tertullian ended outside the Great Church, Origen's name was anathematized in the sixth century. Their testimony is not worthless — a writer later found wanting can be an excellent witness to what the churches were doing in his lifetime, sometimes a better one for having no stake in the later question — but it is not a Father's witness, and an argument that needs it to be will fail.

*Introduced:* [1.1](lessons/01-01-how-to-read-a-church-father.md); Tertullian [2.3](lessons/02-03-tertullian.md), Origen [2.5](lessons/02-05-clement-of-alexandria-and-origen.md)

### Doctor of the Church

A title commending a saint's teaching as eminent for the whole Church. **It endorses no particular opinion of the holder's.**

Boniface VIII raised the feasts of the four Latin Doctors in 1298 (Ambrose, Jerome, Augustine, Gregory the Great), Pius V's Breviary of 1568 those of the four Greek (Basil, Nazianzen, Chrysostom, Athanasius). The list stands at thirty-seven; Irenaeus was added on 21 January 2022 as *Doctor unitatis*, Ephrem in 1920, Leo the Great in 1754. The test cases for what the title does not do: Irenaeus's millenarianism, Cyprian on rebaptism, Jerome's canon, the late Augustine on predestination, Chrysostom's Judaizing homilies.

*Introduced:* [1.1](lessons/01-01-how-to-read-a-church-father.md); pressed at [2.2](lessons/02-02-irenaeus-recapitulation.md), [3.4](lessons/03-04-ephrem-and-syriac-christianity.md), [4.1](lessons/04-01-ambrose-of-milan.md), [4.5](lessons/04-05-augustine-against-the-pelagians.md)

### Unanimous consent of the Fathers

A restraint on how you may read Scripture, not a method for building doctrine out of the Fathers.

Trent, Session IV (8 April 1546) forbids interpreting Scripture, in matters of faith and morals, contrary to the Church's sense "or even contrary to the unanimous consent of the Fathers" (Waterworth, 1848). *Dei Filius* ch. 2 (1870) notes Trent had been wrongly understood and renews the prohibition in the same words. Taken apart: it is a restraint, not a method; its scope is fixed and narrow (faith and morals, not astronomy or chronology); it forbids contradiction and does not require anyone to assemble a consensus; and **"unanimous" needs a sense before the clause can be worked** — numerical, moral, on the doctrine drawn from a verse, or on its literal sense. These give different verdicts on the same evidence and the decree does not choose, so whoever invokes the criterion owes you his reading of it before he counts. The normal state of the patristic record is divergence on the letter with convergence on the doctrine, and any usable account has to handle that.

*Introduced:* [1.1](lessons/01-01-how-to-read-a-church-father.md); tested on a real case at [4.2](lessons/04-02-jerome.md); Boss 5(d) in the [syllabus](syllabus.md)

### How to check a patristic quotation

Five steps, in order, before a quotation is allowed to do any work.

1. **Locate** the work, book, chapter and section. If you cannot, do not use it.
2. **Test whether the work is genuine.** This step fails more often than beginners expect — see [Spurious and misattributed works](#spurious-and-misattributed-works).
3. **Identify the recension and edition.** Ignatius is the standing case; for Origen the question is not "which edition" but "which translator, and which way did he bend" (see [The transmission stack](#the-transmission-stack)).
4. **Name the translation**, and check a critical text if a single word decides the reading.
5. **Ask what it is being made to prove.** One Father's view is a witness, never Church teaching; a claimed consensus must have "unanimous" defined and its spread tested.

The course's worked failures at step two: the Dionysian corpus ([5.4](lessons/05-04-maximus-and-john-of-damascus.md)), the long recension of Ignatius ([1.3](lessons/01-03-ignatius-of-antioch.md)), Cyril's Apollinarian formula ([5.1](lessons/05-01-cyril-of-alexandria.md)), Ambrosiaster ([4.1](lessons/04-01-ambrose-of-milan.md)).

*Introduced:* [1.1](lessons/01-01-how-to-read-a-church-father.md); at its hardest in [2.5](lessons/02-05-clement-of-alexandria-and-origen.md)

### Opinions the Church did not receive

A Father's argument can be sound, his holiness certain, his title secure, and his conclusion still not Church teaching. **Non-reception is not condemnation.**

| Father | Opinion | Status |
|---|---|---|
| Irenaeus | A literal earthly reign of the risen righteous (*AH* 5.32-36) | Never condemned; not received. Closed in the West by Augustine's figurative reading of Revelation 20 (*City of God* 20.7), who says he once held it himself ([2.2](lessons/02-02-irenaeus-recapitulation.md)) |
| Cyprian | Baptism given outside the Church is no baptism, so converts must be baptized (premise 6 of *On the Unity*) | Not received; Western councils within sixty years admitted such converts by the laying on of hands ([2.4](lessons/02-04-cyprian-of-carthage.md)) |
| Origen | The pre-existence of souls; universal restoration | The propositions stand condemned; whether Origen *held* them as doctrine is contested, since *On First Principles* announces itself as inquiry where the apostles left questions open ([2.5](lessons/02-05-clement-of-alexandria-and-origen.md)) |
| Gregory of Nyssa | Evil finally annihilated when God is all in all (1 Corinthians 15:28) | How far he extends it is disputed among scholars; never received as doctrine; he was never condemned ([3.3](lessons/03-03-the-two-gregories.md)) |
| Jerome | The books outside the Hebrew list are not canonical | Not received; the canon as defined is [`fundamental-theology` 3.3](../fundamental-theology/lessons/03-03-the-canon-as-defined.md) ([4.2](lessons/04-02-jerome.md)) |
| Augustine (late) | A fixed number of the elect known before creation; perseverance a further unmerited gift | Orange II (529) received the core — grace goes before every good movement, the beginning of faith included — and says nothing of a fixed number; its epilogue anathematizes predestination to evil ([4.5](lessons/04-05-augustine-against-the-pelagians.md)) |
| Augustine | The state should coerce religious dissenters (Letters 93, 185) | A Father's opinion, never a definition; channelled through Gratian and *ST* II-II q.10 a.8. The Church's teaching is *Dignitatis Humanae* (1965) ([4.4](lessons/04-04-augustine-against-the-donatists.md)) |

Doctrinal levels for every row come from [`fundamental-theology` 4.4](../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-04-the-ladder-of-doctrinal-authority.md) and the owning dogmatic course.

*Introduced:* [2.4](lessons/02-04-cyprian-of-carthage.md) as the type case

### Bishops, presbyters and deacons around 100

What each of the three earliest texts names, and what none of them says.

| Text | Officers named | Words to look at | What it does not say |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 Clement 42, 44, 47 (c. 95-97) | bishops and deacons; the same men called presbyters | "the office of the episcopate" and "presbyters" inside one chapter (44) | that anyone presides alone at Corinth or at Rome |
| Didache 15 (c. 50-150) | bishops and deacons, elected by the congregation, beside prophets and teachers | "appoint for yourselves bishops and deacons worthy of the Lord" | that presbyters exist, or that one bishop presides |
| Ignatius, *Smyrnaeans* 8 (c. 107-140) | one bishop, a presbytery, deacons | "follow the bishop, even as Jesus Christ does the Father, and the presbytery as you would the apostles" | how old the arrangement is, or how widely it held |

Two live readings of the shift, held open here: the threefold order is apostolic and becomes *visible* in Ignatius (a text argues for what is challenged and is silent about what nobody disputes); or it is a post-apostolic development, first in Syria and Asia, with presbyter-bishops as the original form. Catholic and Orthodox readers generally take the first, many Protestant historians the second, and scholars in all three traditions hold positions in between. Both must explain the same evidence. **Boss 1 territory: the crux, not the verdict.**

*Introduced:* [1.2](lessons/01-02-first-clement-and-the-didache.md), [1.3](lessons/01-03-ignatius-of-antioch.md)

### Monepiscopate

One bishop to a city, with a college of presbyters under him and deacons in ministry.

First clearly attested in Ignatius: *Magnesians* 6 (the bishop presides in the place of God, the presbyters in the place of the assembly of the apostles), *Trallians* 3, *Smyrnaeans* 8. Absent from 1 Clement, where bishop and presbyter name one office and Rome writes as a church rather than through a man. The limits are part of the reading: Ignatius writes to churches in western Asia Minor, his letter to Rome greets no bishop there, and every passage is an **imperative** — evidence that something was being urged, not proof that it was universal.

*Introduced:* [1.2](lessons/01-02-first-clement-and-the-didache.md), [1.3](lessons/01-03-ignatius-of-antioch.md)

### Docetism

From the Greek for "seem": Christ only appeared to have a body and to suffer.

Ignatius's target at Smyrna and Tralles, and the reason his argument runs from practice back to doctrine. Also Marcion's position, and the target of Tertullian's *On the Flesh of Christ*.

*Introduced:* [1.3](lessons/01-03-ignatius-of-antioch.md); Tertullian at [2.3](lessons/02-03-tertullian.md)

### Martyrdom as imitation

Ignatius wants to die because dying is how he becomes an imitator of the passion of his God.

*Romans* 4-6: he begs the Roman Christians not to intervene, calls himself God's wheat to be ground by the beasts' teeth into pure bread, and says that if the animals hold back he will provoke them. The idea the later cult of the martyrs is built on. **A first-rate witness to one man's mind, at no level on the ladder of doctrinal authority** ([`fundamental-theology` 4.4](../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-04-the-ladder-of-doctrinal-authority.md)); later Church discipline discouraged volunteering for martyrdom, and Ignatius nowhere tells anyone else to seek it.

*Introduced:* [1.3](lessons/01-03-ignatius-of-antioch.md)

### Argument from prophecy

Justin's public engine: the Hebrew prophets described a man's life centuries before he lived it, and you can check the books.

"We will now offer proof, not trusting mere assertions, but being of necessity persuaded by those who prophesied before these things came to pass" (*First Apology* 30). It works on the emperor because the books are old and public. Its cost is the polemic of the *Dialogue*: the engine needs the Hebrew Scriptures to be about Christ, so it forces Justin to explain why the people who hold those books do not read them so.

*Introduced:* [1.4](lessons/01-04-justin-martyr.md)

### Seeds of the Word

Reason itself is a share in the Word who is Christ, so whatever anyone reasoned rightly already belonged to him.

*Logos spermatikos*. "Those who lived reasonably are Christians, even though they have been thought atheists; as, among the Greeks, Socrates and Heraclitus" (*First Apology* 46); "each man spoke well in proportion to the share he had of the spermatic word" (*Second Apology* 13). Read the qualifiers, which do the limiting work: seed against whole, imitation against participation, and the philosophers "did not know the whole of the Word, which is Christ, they often contradicted themselves"; "no one trusted in Socrates so as to die for this doctrine" (*Second Apology* 10). A second-century theologian's construction, never defined. Justin also explains the same data a second, incompatible way — where the Greeks agree with us they borrowed from Moses (*First Apology* 60) — and never reconciles the two.

*Introduced:* [1.4](lessons/01-04-justin-martyr.md); the Latin refusal of the move is [2.3](lessons/02-03-tertullian.md)

### Rule of faith

A short, public summary of the apostolic faith that any believer can hold and apply to any reading.

Irenaeus, *Against Heresies* 1.10.1, also 1.22.1 and 3.4.2 — and he gives it differently each time, because what is fixed is the **content**, not the wording. Creed-shaped but not yet a creed. Read as a weapon, every clause lights up: *received from the apostles* (not discovered), *one God ... Maker* (against a lesser, ignorant world-maker), *became incarnate ... in the flesh* (against a Saviour who only seemed to have a body). It is a key to the arrangement of the Scriptures, **not a second source of content**: when Irenaeus asks at 3.4.1 what we would do if the apostles had left no writings, the tradition he reports from the unlettered churches is the same summary again. Tertullian states it in full at *Prescription* 13.

*Introduced:* [2.1](lessons/02-01-irenaeus-against-the-gnostics.md); used at [2.3](lessons/02-03-tertullian.md), [2.5](lessons/02-05-clement-of-alexandria-and-origen.md)

### Apostolic succession

Named teachers in named cities, from the apostles to the present, open to anyone to check.

*Against Heresies* 3.2.2 and 3.3.1-4: Rome's line from Linus to Eleutherius, "the twelfth place from the apostles"; Polycarp at Smyrna, whom Irenaeus heard himself; Ephesus. The premise that does the work is 3.3.1 — if the apostles had hidden mysteries they would have entrusted them above all to the men to whom they entrusted the churches. **Its evidential force is the convergence of many independent lines on one public summary** (1.10.2: Germany, Spain, Gaul, the East, Egypt, Libya, however unequal their teachers' eloquence), not any single line, and it cannot by itself prove that a particular later teaching is apostolic. The contrast it is built against: the rival systems have birthdays, and Irenaeus dates them (Valentinus under Hyginus, Pius and Anicetus; Cerdon under Hyginus; Marcion under Anicetus, 3.4.3). **Boss 2 territory.**

*Introduced:* [2.1](lessons/02-01-irenaeus-against-the-gnostics.md); recast in Roman legal terms at [2.3](lessons/02-03-tertullian.md); narrowed to present communion at [2.4](lessons/02-04-cyprian-of-carthage.md)

### The fourfold gospel

Irenaeus's four-zones, four-winds and four-cherubim defence of exactly four gospels — a bad argument and excellent evidence.

*Against Heresies* 3.11.8-9: four zones of the world, four principal winds, the four faces of the cherubim of Revelation 4:7, four covenants. *As an argument* it is worthless, since the same form would license any number you started from. *As evidence* it is first-rate: Irenaeus is not proposing four, he is defending a number already so fixed in the churches of Gaul and Asia by about 180 that he reaches for cosmology rather than concede one, against people who wanted fewer (Marcion) and more (the Valentinians, whose *Gospel of Truth* he says agrees with the apostles' gospels in nothing, 3.11.9). Weighing a bad argument as a good witness is the skill.

*Introduced:* [2.1](lessons/02-01-irenaeus-against-the-gnostics.md); the canon as formed is [`new-testament`](../new-testament/syllabus.md) 6.6

### Recapitulation

Christ sums up the whole human story in himself and reverses Adam's outcome at each matched point.

Greek *anakephalaiosasthai*, Ephesians 1:10; the root is *kephalaion*, the sum a scribe writes at the head of a column of figures. Irenaeus hears the neighbouring *kephale*, head, as well, and uses both: Christ is the total of the human story and its head. Not repetition — repetition would change nothing — but the same ground gone over with the opposite outcome.

| Adam's move | Christ's countermove | Where |
|---|---|---|
| formed by God from untilled, virgin soil | born of a virgin, formed by God without human seed | 3.21.10 |
| disobedient at a tree | obedient upon a tree; the debt contracted at a tree remitted by a tree | 5.16.3, 5.17.3 |
| the knot tied by Eve's unbelief | "the knot of Eve's disobedience was loosed by the obedience of Mary" | 3.22.4 |
| a man defeated by the adversary | a man defeating him, and defeating him out of the law | 5.21.1-2 |
| an infancy that could not hold the gift | an infancy passed through, so the Word could be taken as milk | 4.38.1-2 |

The premise underneath is that one God is both creator and redeemer, so salvation cannot be an exit from the flesh but only its repair.

*Introduced:* [2.2](lessons/02-02-irenaeus-recapitulation.md); the Greek heir is [3.1](lessons/03-01-athanasius.md)

### Growth and maturation in Irenaeus

Adam was created good but **infant** — made to grow — so salvation restores an interrupted ascent, not a lost Edenic perfection.

*Against Heresies* 4.38: a creature is by definition later than its creator, so it starts small; man was made able to grow and unable, as yet, to receive what growth would bring. "The glory of God is a living man; and the life of man consists in beholding God" (4.20.7, ANF) belongs here and has **two halves**: the second defines the first, and the sentence sits inside an argument about advance — man must always have something further to move towards. The circulating modern form, "the glory of God is man fully alive", drops the half that says what the life consists in.

*Introduced:* [2.2](lessons/02-02-irenaeus-recapitulation.md)

### Eve and Mary

Mary's obedience unties the knot of Eve's disobedience.

First in Justin, *Dialogue* 100: Eve, "a virgin and undefiled," conceived the serpent's word; Mary received the angel's word with faith and joy, the disobedience undone "in the same manner in which it derived its origin." Irenaeus makes it structural by fitting it into the matched moves of recapitulation (*AH* 3.22.4). Ephrem sings the same typology rather than arguing it. Read it as written — obedience undoing disobedience — rather than back-reading later Marian doctrine, which belongs to [`ecclesiology-and-mariology`](../ecclesiology-and-mariology/syllabus.md).

*Introduced:* [1.4](lessons/01-04-justin-martyr.md), [2.2](lessons/02-02-irenaeus-recapitulation.md); [3.4](lessons/03-04-ephrem-and-syriac-christianity.md)

### Millenarianism in the Fathers

The expectation of a literal earthly reign of the risen righteous before the last judgment.

Irenaeus, *AH* 5.32-36, argued from a strict application of his own first premise: it is just that in the very creation in which they suffered the righteous should receive their reward, and that the creation itself be restored to the dominion of the just (5.32.1). The picture — vines with ten thousand branches, each branch ten thousand twigs — he passes on from the elders via Papias, whom he names as his written source (5.33.3-4). Never condemned, not received; closed in the West by Augustine's figurative reading of Revelation 20 (*City of God* 20.7). What you cannot do is treat it as a detachable oddity without argument: it grows from the same root as everything else in his theology, that this creation and this flesh are really redeemed.

*Introduced:* [2.2](lessons/02-02-irenaeus-recapitulation.md)

### The prescription argument

Before you tell me what the verse means, show me by what right you hold the book.

Tertullian, *Prescription against Heretics* 15-21, 32, 37. The heretics are good at Scripture and a scriptural contest with them produces no verdict, so the appeal must not be made to Scripture first: the prior question is whose faith the Scriptures belong to (ch. 19). Ownership is traceable — Christ taught the apostles, the apostles founded churches, those churches hand on the rule of faith — and the test is public: let them unfold the roll of their bishops back to an apostle, while a church founded yesterday counts as apostolic if it is "akin in doctrine" (ch. 32). **A plea entered before the merits, not a verdict on any verse.** It is not a claim that Christians should not reason from Scripture — Tertullian spends five books doing exactly that against Marcion — and it is not self-executing, since everything rests on the rule of faith and succession really being what he says.

*Introduced:* [2.3](lessons/02-03-tertullian.md); the premise is [2.1](lessons/02-01-irenaeus-against-the-gnostics.md)'s. **Boss 2 territory.**

### Latin Trinitarian vocabulary

The words the West still uses for God, invented in a treatise against modalism and written from inside the New Prophecy.

*Against Praxeas* (c. 213). Praxeas held Father, Son and Spirit to be one person under three names, so that the Father himself was born and suffered; Tertullian's charge is that he put the Paraclete to flight and crucified the Father. To answer he supplied **trinitas**, **substantia** for what is one, **persona** for what is three (ch. 2: the three are three "not in condition, but in degree; not in substance, but in form; not in power, but in aspect," and yet of one substance, one condition and one power). Two disciplines. *Exactness:* **una substantia, tres personae** is a later condensation, and *tres personae* is not in the treatise. *Authority:* at ch. 9 the same work calls the Father the entire substance and the Son "a derivation and portion of the whole", with "my Father is greater than I" — a ranking Nicaea worked to exclude, and scholars divide over whether it belongs to the *oeconomia* or to the Godhead. **The West received his vocabulary; it did not receive his ranking.**

*Introduced:* [2.3](lessons/02-03-tertullian.md); the Greek settlement is [3.2](lessons/03-02-basil-the-great.md)

### The one episcopate

The episcopate is a single thing that each bishop holds **whole**, not a share.

Cyprian, *De unitate* 5: "The episcopate is one, each part of which is held by each one for the whole." *In solidum* is the Roman-law phrase for joint and several liability, where each party answers for the entire obligation — the **opposite** of independence. The cost shows in 256: an episcopate one in this strong sense has no procedure inside it for settling a question its members each hold whole, which is why Cyprian can open the Seventh Council of Carthage with "neither does any of us set himself up as a bishop of bishops."

*Introduced:* [2.4](lessons/02-04-cyprian-of-carthage.md)

### The Church as mother

There is exactly one place where Christians are made.

*De unitate* 6: "she is one mother ... from her womb we are born, by her milk we are nourished, by her spirit we are animated. ... He can no longer have God for his Father, who has not the Church for his mother. If any one could escape who was outside the Ark of Noah, then he also may escape who shall be outside of the Church" (ANF, Wallis). The images of ch. 5 — many rays and one light, many branches and one root, many streams and one spring — all say one thing: the many are real, the source is one, severance kills.

*Introduced:* [2.4](lessons/02-04-cyprian-of-carthage.md)

### No salvation outside the Church

In Cyprian's own texts a statement about **schismatics and heretics**, denying that their rites work.

Its job in his argument is that the outsider has nothing to give: water poured outside is not baptism, and even the blood of a heretic's martyrdom does not profit him (Letter to Jubaianus, ANF Epistle 72.21 = Hartel 73.21). It is not his general theory of who is saved. What the phrase means as Catholic doctrine, invincible ignorance included, is fixed by the Magisterium and owned by [`ecclesiology-and-mariology`](../ecclesiology-and-mariology/syllabus.md), not by Cyprian.

*Introduced:* [2.4](lessons/02-04-cyprian-of-carthage.md)

### The true gnostic

Clement of Alexandria keeps the word rather than ceding it: the real *gnostikos* is the mature Christian whose knowledge is inseparable from charity.

It pairs with his account of philosophy as a covenant: Greek philosophy was given to the Greeks "as a covenant peculiar to them", a stepping-stone to the philosophy according to Christ (*Stromata* VI.8), and "a schoolmaster to bring the Hellenic mind, as the law, the Hebrews, to Christ" (I.5, ANF, Wilson). Preparatory and subordinate — the handmaid Hagar, not the wife Sarah — and what sorts its results is the rule of faith.

*Introduced:* [2.5](lessons/02-05-clement-of-alexandria-and-origen.md)

### Eternal generation

The Father never existed, even for a moment, without having generated the Son.

Origen, *On First Principles* I.2.2 (ANF, Crombie, from Rufinus's Latin). The argument turns entirely on what God is like: Wisdom subsists — someone, not something; God does not acquire perfections; so suppose a moment when the Father had not generated Wisdom, and either he could not, which makes God advance from inability to ability, or he would not, which makes him withhold a good. Therefore the generation is a permanent relation, not an event: "without any beginning, not only such as may be measured by any divisions of time, but even that which the mind alone can contemplate within itself." The image is the brightness the sun never began to emit (I.2.4). **Keep the honest other half:** Origen also calls the Son "a second God" (*Against Celsus* V.39, surviving in Greek) and gives the Son and Spirit a lesser scope of operation. Pre-Nicene Greek had no settled way to say *distinct in person, equal in nature*; he supplied the premise Nicaea needed generations before anyone had the words to guard it.

*Introduced:* [2.5](lessons/02-05-clement-of-alexandria-and-origen.md); used at [3.1](lessons/03-01-athanasius.md), [3.2](lessons/03-02-basil-the-great.md)

### Universal restoration

*Apokatastasis*: every rational creature, perhaps including the devil, finally healed.

Attributed to Origen along with the pre-existence of souls, and quoted to that effect in the pipes that bend toward heresy (Jerome's Letter 124 to Avitus). In 543 Justinian issued an edict against Origenism with anathemas, endorsed by a synod at Constantinople; fifteen anathemas against Origenism are associated with Constantinople II in 553, and scholars dispute both whether the council or a synod just before it issued them and whether Origen's name in canon 11 is original. The target was in any case the developed Origenism of sixth-century Palestinian monks. **State the dispute; do not settle it.** What is not disputed: the condemned propositions are condemned, and neither pre-existence nor universal restoration is Catholic teaching. Gregory of Nyssa reaches something similar from 1 Corinthians 15:28 in *On the Soul and the Resurrection* — evil finally annihilated — and how far he extends it is disputed; he was never condemned, and the sixth-century anathemas were aimed at Origenism.

*Introduced:* [2.5](lessons/02-05-clement-of-alexandria-and-origen.md); Nyssa at [3.3](lessons/03-03-the-two-gregories.md)

### The soteriological argument pattern

Reason **backwards**, from the effect salvation claims to the nature the saviour must have to produce it.

Athanasius's one move: if an act can only be done by God, a creature cannot do it, so if the Son is a creature the act did not happen and nobody has been saved. "If the Son were a creature, man had remained mortal as before, not being joined to God; for a creature had not joined creatures to God" (*Orations against the Arians* II.69). The conclusion is not "the Son is divine because a text says so" but "the Son must be God, or what you say happened to you at baptism did not happen." The same machine with a different variable: Basil on the Spirit ([3.2](lessons/03-02-basil-the-great.md)), Nazianzen on the Spirit and on Christ's human mind ([3.3](lessons/03-03-the-two-gregories.md)), Leo on the union ([5.2](lessons/05-02-leo-the-great.md)), Maximus on the human will ([5.4](lessons/05-04-maximus-and-john-of-damascus.md)).

*Introduced:* [3.1](lessons/03-01-athanasius.md)

### The divine dilemma

God's truthfulness requires the sentence of death to stand; God's goodness forbids the ruin of his image. Only one move satisfies both.

*On the Incarnation* 6-9. Man was made out of nothing and holds together only by sharing in the Word, so sin cancels the loan and what was made from nothing drifts back toward nothing. Horn one: a threat God declines to execute makes God a liar. Horn two: abandoning his handiwork would show weakness, not goodness. Repentance satisfies neither (ch. 7) — forgiving the penalty leaves the sentence unexecuted, and repentance "merely stays them from acts of sin" without reaching a nature coming apart. So the Word takes a body of our kind, gives it over to death in the stead of all, and raises it: death happens, so truth is kept; the image is restored by its Artificer, so goodness is kept. **The picture of sin underneath is corruption of nature, not guilt in a courtroom** — importing the later Latin debt-and-satisfaction frame makes you hear what Athanasius does not say. Chapter 54's famous summary is **Boss 3 material** and is not worked here.

*Introduced:* [3.1](lessons/03-01-athanasius.md)

### There was when he was not

The Arian slogan, with its twin "he was not before his generation": the Son had a beginning, and so falls on the creature side of the God/creature line.

Quoted in Robertson's revision of Newman at *Orations against the Arians* I.11. Note whose pen it reaches us through: Athanasius is attacking it, and he also supplies the narrative of one continuous "Arian party" descending from Arius, which Lewis Ayres and R. P. C. Hanson argue he largely constructed. Take the rule: **a polemicist's summary of his opponents is evidence about the polemicist first.**

*Introduced:* [3.1](lessons/03-01-athanasius.md)

### Homoousios

"Of one essence" — a non-scriptural word adopted at Nicaea because every scriptural formula could be signed by opponents in the wrong sense.

*On the Decrees of the Nicene Synod* 20 tells the story: the bishops first tried to say only what Scripture says, that the Son is "from God," and the Arian party agreed cheerfully, since "from God" is said of everything (1 Corinthians 8:6); so they wrote "from the essence of God"; the opponents then accepted "like," "always," "power" and "in Him" while, Athanasius says, whispering and winking, since all those are used of us too. Hence the last step: the bishops "were again compelled ... to collect the sense of the Scriptures, and to re-say and re-write what they had said before, more distinctly still." **A fence around Scripture's sense, not an addition to it** — the classic patristic defence of non-scriptural vocabulary. The word also had a bad record: a synod at Antioch, conventionally dated 268, refused it in deposing Paul of Samosata, and Athanasius answers in *On the Synods* 43-45 by distinguishing the senses rather than denying the fact.

*Introduced:* [3.1](lessons/03-01-athanasius.md)

### Deification

Creatures adopted and made sharers by grace in what the Son is by nature.

The asymmetry is the point: **the Son deifies and is not deified** (*Orations* I.39; *On the Incarnation* 54). Make human beings divine in the Son's own sense and the whole argument collapses, since it needs the creature-God gap to be real. Basil's list of the Spirit's works climbs to the same place (*On the Holy Spirit* 9), and Nazianzen turns it on the Spirit at Oration 31.28. In Maximus it is extended past the individual soul to the whole cosmos gathered in Christ — the theme a modern reader meets under Balthasar's title *Cosmic Liturgy* — and it is still a share in God's life by grace, never a change of nature.

*Introduced:* [3.1](lessons/03-01-athanasius.md); extended at [5.4](lessons/05-04-maximus-and-john-of-damascus.md)

### Ousia and hypostasis

*Ousia* answers "what is it?" and is common; *hypostasis* answers "which one?" and is particular. One *ousia*, three *hypostaseis*.

Basil's settlement, Letters 236.6 and 214.4 (NPNF 2nd ser. vol. 8, Blomfield Jackson): "The distinction between *ousia* and *hypostasis* is the same as that between the general and the particular; as, for instance, between the animal and the particular man. ... We must, therefore, confess the faith by adding the particular to the common. The Godhead is common; the fatherhood particular." The problem it solves: at Nicaea the two nouns were interchangeable — the anathema pairs them as "substance or essence" — so "one hypostasis" sounded Sabellian to an Easterner and "three hypostases" tritheist to a Westerner, and orthodoxy had become unsayable. **What this buys is precision, not explanation:** "three" and "one" now answer different questions, and what a divine *hypostasis* is Basil has not said. Date your vocabulary: reading Basil's sense back into the 325 anathema makes the council forbid what Basil taught.

*Introduced:* [3.2](lessons/03-02-basil-the-great.md)

### Basil's argument from worship

From honour to nature, run through practice: the Church already prays this way, so what must be true for the prayer not to be blasphemy?

*On the Holy Spirit*, written 375 for Amphilochius of Iconium. (1) Christ conjoined the Spirit with the Father and the Son in the baptismal command, Matthew 28:19 (ch. 10). (2) Faith conforms to baptism and glory to faith, in an unbroken sequence, so the opponents must choose which link to break: "either not to baptize as we have received, or not to believe as we were baptized, or not to ascribe glory as we have believed" (chs. 12, 27). (3) Worship of this kind is not offered to creatures — Paul names angels as witnesses, not recipients of glory, and a fellow-slave can testify but cannot free (ch. 13). (4) What the Spirit does is not creaturely work: he sanctifies, adopts, illuminates and deifies (ch. 9). (5) Therefore the Spirit is numbered *with* the Father and the Son, not under them (chs. 17-18). The occasion was the doxology fight: "with the Son, together with the Holy Spirit" beside the older "through the Son, in the Holy Spirit".

*Introduced:* [3.2](lessons/03-02-basil-the-great.md)

### Basil's reticence about the Spirit

In the whole treatise Basil never calls the Spirit *homoousios* and never writes "the Spirit is God."

He argues that the Spirit is glorified with the Father and the Son, shares their operations and is no creature — and stops. Gregory of Nazianzus, preaching his funeral oration (*Oration* 43.68-69), calls it a deliberate *oikonomia*: enemies were watching for that phrase, which would have got Basil deposed and Caesarea handed to them, so he taught the same content in other terms; in private, Gregory adds, Basil confessed the Spirit consubstantial. **Weigh that as testimony:** a friend's eulogy answering critics who called the silence cowardice — our best evidence, and not disinterested. Beyond dispute is the text: the words are not there. Two years after his death Constantinople 381 confessed the Spirit as the one "who with the Father and the Son together is worshipped and glorified" — Basil's idiom, not Athanasius's word. Quoting the treatise as saying the Spirit is God is an attribution error.

*Introduced:* [3.2](lessons/03-02-basil-the-great.md)

### Gradual revelation

Scriptural silence read as staged pedagogy rather than as evidence against the doctrine.

Gregory of Nazianzus, Oration 31.26 (NPNF 2nd ser. vol. 7, Browne and Swallow): the Old Testament proclaimed the Father openly and the Son more obscurely; the New manifested the Son and suggested the Deity of the Spirit; now the Spirit dwells among us. The reason given is capacity — it was not safe to proclaim the Son before the Father's Godhead was acknowledged, lest hearers "loaded with food beyond their strength" lose what was within their powers. **It is not a theory of doctrinal development:** Gregory stages the deposit up to the apostolic age, not later growth in the Church's understanding. For that, see [`fundamental-theology` 5.2](../fundamental-theology/lessons/05-02-newmans-theory-of-development.md).

*Introduced:* [3.3](lessons/03-03-the-two-gregories.md)

### What is not assumed is not healed

Christ saves what he takes on, so whatever he left out of his humanity is unsaved.

Gregory of Nazianzus, Letter 101 to Cledonius (usually dated 382), against Apollinarius, who held that in Christ the Word took the place of the human mind: "For that which He has not assumed He has not healed; but that which is united to His Godhead is also saved." **Christological, not Trinitarian** — using it to argue about the Spirit misplaces it by one controversy. It is the soteriological pattern pointed at the Incarnation, and Module 5 runs it twice more: Nestorius is guarding it against Alexandrian one-nature talk, and Maximus uses it as premise 4 of the dyothelite argument (a humanity without a will could not heal human willing). **Boss 3 territory:** the argument pattern it shares with *On the Incarnation* 54 is not worked here.

*Introduced:* [3.3](lessons/03-03-the-two-gregories.md); reused at [5.1](lessons/05-01-cyril-of-alexandria.md), [5.4](lessons/05-04-maximus-and-john-of-damascus.md)

### Endless ascent

*Epektasis*: since God is infinite the soul never arrives, but stretches forward forever.

Gregory of Nyssa, *Life of Moses*: the approach to God ends not in vision but in the darkness of Sinai, God known as unknowable. Desire becomes a permanent feature of blessedness rather than a defect.

*Introduced:* [3.3](lessons/03-03-the-two-gregories.md)

### Raza

Ephrem's *raze*: real things in nature and Scripture made to carry something true of God.

The same Syriac word means symbol, mystery and later sacrament. A symbol is not an illustration of a truth you already have by another route; it is how a creature is given anything of the Creator at all. So it is **inexhaustible where a definition is not** — the pearl "proved faces on all sides" — and what contemplating it yields is not a definition of the Son but the discovery that he cannot be contained in one. Ephrem's *raze* are **types**, one real thing prefiguring another, not a hidden spiritual meaning behind a discarded letter: he prefers the literal sense and uses allegory sparingly, and the 1909 *Catholic Encyclopedia* calls his exegesis strongly Antiochene.

*Introduced:* [3.4](lessons/03-04-ephrem-and-syriac-christianity.md)

### Stumbling blocks

Origen's criterion: the Spirit planted impossibilities and offences in the letter so that the reader would climb.

*On First Principles* Book IV (ANF vol. 4, Crombie; the Greek, ANF Book IV section 15, modern IV.2.9): "the word of God has arranged that certain stumbling-blocks, as it were, and offenses, and impossibilities, should be introduced into the midst of the law and the history," so that a reader is neither drawn away by the attractive surface into "nothing worthy of God" nor held to the letter and brought "to the knowledge of nothing more divine." Read the grammar: the subject is the word of God, so these are **deliberate acts of the divine author**, not defects. This is a theory of inspiration before it is a theory of reading, and it gives the method an **entrance condition**: where the letter is impossible, unworthy or useless, a spiritual sense is not optional but commanded. **Boss 3 territory** — the application to Genesis 2-3 is not worked here.

*Introduced:* [3.5](lessons/03-05-allegory-and-history-alexandria-and-antioch.md)

### The three senses of Scripture

Body, soul and spirit, matched to simple, progressing and perfect readers.

Origen takes the pattern from Proverbs 22:20, read in his Greek as a command to write "in a threefold manner", and from the body-soul-spirit division of a human being. Two guards, often forgotten: the "soul" sense is rarely used in his own commentaries, which mostly run on two levels, letter and spirit; and he insists that the historical sense of most of Scripture stands, the stumbling blocks being scattered among narratives and laws true as they are. What is universal is the spiritual sense — "there are certain passages of Scripture which do not at all contain the corporeal sense" (ANF Book IV, Greek, section 12). **The four senses of the Latin tradition are not Origen's three;** they grow out of this material through Cassian, Gregory the Great and the medievals, and the four senses as Catholic teaching belong to [`old-testament`](../old-testament/syllabus.md) 1.2.

*Introduced:* [3.5](lessons/03-05-allegory-and-history-alexandria-and-antioch.md)

### Theoria

The Antiochene alternative to allegory: the prophet, and the reader after him, sees a further reference **through** the historical sense, which stays standing.

Hezekiah's deliverance really happened and really shows Christ's. Diodore of Tarsus wrote a treatise on the difference between *theoria* and allegory, now lost; Theodore of Mopsuestia read each book against its own historical setting; Chrysostom is the school in the pulpit. Their objection is not that figurative reading is illegitimate but that the figure must be found *in* the history, not *instead of* it — so they deny Origen's entrance condition: where he finds an impossibility, an Antiochene finds an idiom, a figure or a translator's artefact, solvable at the level of the letter, and the bolt never drops.

*Introduced:* [3.5](lessons/03-05-allegory-and-history-alexandria-and-antioch.md)

### Typology and allegory

The textbook line: a **type** is a real person or event prefiguring another real one; an **allegory** treats the narrative as a coded surface whose elements stand for truths of another order.

Typology keeps the history; allegory can spend it. Useful, and half true — see [The Young thesis](#the-young-thesis). Note the complication in the sources themselves: Paul says of Hagar and Sarah that the things "are an allegory" (Galatians 4), Origen quotes that verse as apostolic warrant for the whole procedure (ANF Book IV, Greek, section 13), and Chrysostom has to answer that Paul uses the word loosely for what is properly a type.

*Introduced:* [3.5](lessons/03-05-allegory-and-history-alexandria-and-antioch.md)

### Hypothesis and akolouthia

The Antiochene controls on meaning: a text's **governing subject** and its **narrative sequence**.

The complaint against allegory, stated this way, is not "too figurative" but "you have broken the sequence". The same word *hypothesis* names what Irenaeus was defending against the Valentinians: his mosaic image at *AH* 1.8.1 — the same jewels rearranged from a king into a dog or a fox — makes the dispute a dispute about the **arrangement** the texts are fitted into, not about which texts are Scripture.

*Introduced:* [2.1](lessons/02-01-irenaeus-against-the-gnostics.md), [3.5](lessons/03-05-allegory-and-history-alexandria-and-antioch.md)

### The Young thesis

Frances Young (*Biblical Exegesis and the Formation of Christian Culture*, 1997): the Alexandria/Antioch contrast is drawn in the wrong place, by modern anxieties about historicity rather than by the Fathers' own categories.

Compressed: both schools allegorize, both care about the letter, and both were trained in the same grammatical and rhetorical curriculum — Alexandrian allegory grew out of the philological commentary practised on Homer, Antiochene reading out of rhetoric — so their techniques of construing a text are the same techniques. What differs is the **mode of reference**, and the real fault line is the status of the *historia*. Widely adopted and not unanimous: readers who live in the commentaries still report a real difference in practice. A piece of evidence in its favour: Ephrem's symbols are neither Alexandrian allegory nor Antiochene *theoria*, and Syriac exegesis is usually left out of the contrast entirely.

*Introduced:* [3.5](lessons/03-05-allegory-and-history-alexandria-and-antioch.md)

### Christ in the poor

The hungry and naked are the same body as the one on the altar, because one speaker said both sentences.

Chrysostom, Homily 50 on Matthew, section 4 (NPNF 1st ser. vol. 10, Prevost rev. Riddle): "He that said, This is my body, and by His word confirmed the fact, This same said, You saw me an hungered, and fed me not." The argument turns on the identity of the speaker, not on a metaphor. Then: honour must be the kind the one honoured asked for (Peter at the washing of the feet); the altar does not need what the poor need — "This indeed needs not coverings, but a pure soul; but that requires much attention"; so adornment offered while the poor go without is insult, and the ordering is not negotiable. He is explicit that he is "not forbidding such offerings to be provided" but requiring alms "together with them, and before them." How far it goes: preaching on 1 Timothy 4 he argues God made the greatest goods common, that strife appears where someone appropriates, and that "mine and yours" are cold words (Homily 12 on 1 Timothy). **Mark the level:** one bishop's preaching, at no level on the ladder; the doctrine of property is [`catholic-social-teaching`](../catholic-social-teaching/syllabus.md)'s.

*Introduced:* [3.6](lessons/03-06-john-chrysostom.md)

### Reading for the speaker's purpose

Hold the letter, ask what the speaker was doing in that scene, and let the answer control the general use of the sentence.

Chrysostom on "The poor always you have with you, but me you have not always" (Homily 50 on Matthew, section 5). He does not allegorize the poor and does not deny the plain sense: the words "were uttered because of some economy", *oikonomia*, the arrangement of a particular moment — so the saying tells us the opposite of what the objector wants, give alms now precisely because Christ is not always here to be fed in person. The Antiochene method in one paragraph; an Alexandrian would sooner have looked for a second sense in "the poor."

*Introduced:* [3.6](lessons/03-06-john-chrysostom.md); method from [3.5](lessons/03-05-allegory-and-history-alexandria-and-antioch.md)

### Psogos

The ancient rhetorical genre of formal invective, whose convention was unrestrained vilification.

Robert Wilken (*John Chrysostom and the Jews*, 1983) argues from this that calling Chrysostom an antisemite imports a modern category; he used comparable abuse on heretics and bad Christians. **The genre argument answers a different question from the reception argument.** *Psogos* is about what the words were doing in 386; the reception history is about what the words do when read. Someone who says "it was just the convention" and stops has answered the first and pretended it settles the second. It explains the register of patristic polemic; it does not settle what the propositions say.

*Introduced:* [3.6](lessons/03-06-john-chrysostom.md)

### Mystagogy

Instruction on the sacraments given to the newly baptized **after** the rite, walking them back through what happened.

Ambrose's *On the Mysteries* and *On the Sacraments* are the West's earliest full examples: the opening of the ears, the renunciation facing west and the turn to the east, the water, the anointing and the washing of the feet (ch. 6), the white robe and the seal of the Spirit (ch. 7), then the altar (chs. 8-9). A running argument that the visible thing is not the whole thing.

*Introduced:* [4.1](lessons/04-01-ambrose-of-milan.md)

### Disciplina arcani

The early Church's deliberate reserve about the sacraments: not explained to the uninitiated.

Ambrose, *On the Mysteries* 2: teaching them before baptism would betray rather than portray them, and the rites strike harder on someone who did not know what was coming. The Didache's exclusion of the unbaptized from the thanksgiving (ch. 9) and Justin's "not as common bread ... to no one else is it permitted to partake" are the earlier end of the same reserve.

*Introduced:* [4.1](lessons/04-01-ambrose-of-milan.md)

### Hebraica veritas

For the Old Testament the **Hebrew**, not the Septuagint, is what a Latin version should be a translation of.

Jerome's grounds: the Greek is itself a translation; the legend that the Septuagint translators worked in sealed cells and produced identical texts is false, and with it the claim that they were inspired (preface to Genesis); and the apostles themselves sometimes follow the Hebrew against the Greek — his example is Paul at Romans 9:33, "a stumbling-stone and rock of offense" (Letter 57.9). What the Church did **not** receive is the corollary he drew about the canon.

*Introduced:* [4.2](lessons/04-02-jerome.md)

### Sense for sense translation

"I render sense for sense and not word for word" — with an exception carved out for Scripture, and then quietly eroded.

Jerome, Letter 57.5 (NPNF 2nd ser. vol. 6, Fremantle, Lewis and Martley): the rule applies "except in the case of the holy scriptures where even the order of the words is a mystery". Then sections 7-11 argue at length that the evangelists and apostles themselves quoted the Old Testament sense for sense, concluding that in dealing with the Scriptures it is the sense we look to and not the words. The consistent reading: the exemption governs **his own** translating, where he will not risk the mystery of the order, while the apostles' freedom shows that fidelity lives in the sense. Do not quote section 5 as if he were a literalist, or section 10 as if he had no exception.

*Introduced:* [4.2](lessons/04-02-jerome.md)

### The two senses of confession

Latin *confiteri* means to acknowledge a fault, to praise, and to profess a faith — and Augustine uses all three at once.

Which is why the *Confessions* is addressed to God and not to a reader: a confession of sin that was not also praise would be a memoir with a guilty conscience, and praise that skipped the sin would be a lie. It also explains the structure. Having confessed his past (Books 1-9) a man praying has to confess what he still is (10), and then what God is and what the Scripture says that made him (11-13) — so the last four books are not an appendix bolted on.

*Introduced:* [4.3](lessons/04-03-augustine-confessions.md)

### The restless heart

"Thou madest us for Thyself, and our heart is restless, until it repose in Thee" (*Confessions* 1.1 (1), Pusey, 1838).

The book's opening diagnosis, **inside a prayer** to a God already addressed in the previous clause — not an argument for God's existence, and used as a premise in natural theology it does work Augustine does not ask of it (that work is `philosophy-of-religion`'s). The English varies by translator, so quote a named one.

*Introduced:* [4.3](lessons/04-03-augustine-confessions.md)

### The divided will

One will willing incompletely, not two souls fighting.

*Confessions* 8.9 (21): the mind commands the body and is obeyed at once, commands itself and is resisted. Compare 8.7 (17), the prayer for chastity "but not yet". The target is the Manichaean reading Augustine attacks at 8.10 (22), which explained the conflict by two natures in one man. The general point it serves: knowing the good does not produce the will to do it.

*Introduced:* [4.3](lessons/04-03-augustine-confessions.md)

### What the Platonists lacked

*Confessions* 7.9 (14): in the Platonist books Augustine found the Word's eternity and divinity, and then — "that the Word was made flesh, and dwelt among us, I read not there" (Pusey).

The Incarnation, and with it humility. They could raise the mind and could not bend the will, which is why Book 7 is not the conversion and Book 8 is.

*Introduced:* [4.3](lessons/04-03-augustine-confessions.md)

### The garden at Milan

*Confessions* 8.12 (29), August 386: the chanted "take up and read", Romans 13:13-14, and "all the darkness of doubt vanished away."

**Notice what is not in the passage.** No new doctrine arrives; Augustine already had those verses and had heard Ambrose preach. What arrived was the ability to obey them, so what changed was the will and not the evidence. On its evidential value: Pierre Courcelle (*Recherches sur les Confessions de saint Augustin*, 1950) argued the scene is literary composition — the child's voice a device, the fig tree an allusion. Influential, **not the majority view**; most hold the narrative heavily shaped by Scripture and by the book's purpose without being invented. As evidence for what happened in 386 it is a shaped retrospect; as evidence for what Augustine held about grace in 397-401 it is nearly ideal, because the shaping *is* the doctrine.

*Introduced:* [4.3](lessons/04-03-augustine-confessions.md)

### Give what you command

*Da quod iubes et iube quod vis* — *Confessions* 10.29 (40), Pusey: "Give what Thou enjoinest, and enjoin what Thou wilt."

If knowing the good does not produce the will to do it, the only coherent thing to ask of a commandment is that God supply the obedience it demands. This is the sentence a brother bishop quoted in Pelagius's hearing at Rome, which Pelagius could not bear, nearly coming to a quarrel over it — Augustine tells the story himself at *On the Gift of Perseverance* 20 (53), and his point in retelling it is that he had written the words before the controversy existed. **The hinge from 4.3 to 4.5, and Boss 4 material:** the objection, the reply and the crux are not worked here.

*Introduced:* [4.3](lessons/04-03-augustine-confessions.md); reopened at [4.5](lessons/04-05-augustine-against-the-pelagians.md)

### Donatism

The premise that a sacrament carries the holiness of the man who gives it.

So a *traditor*'s hands are empty, his ordinations are empty, and everything flowing from them is void — hence rebaptism of anyone coming from the Catholic side, who on this account had never been baptized. The premise is not new: Cyprian had argued that baptism outside the Church is no baptism, and the Donatists claimed him. Augustine's strategy is to **divide** Cyprian rather than attack him — concede the holiness and the martyrdom, grant premises 1-5 of *On the Unity*, deny the sixth, and show that Cyprian himself forbade separating from a Church with bad men in it.

*Introduced:* [4.4](lessons/04-04-augustine-against-the-donatists.md); the source of the premise is [2.4](lessons/02-04-cyprian-of-carthage.md)

### Valid but not fruitful

"It is one thing not to have, another to have so as to be of no use."

*On Baptism, against the Donatists* IV.17 (NPNF 1st ser., J. R. King), answering a letter of Cyprian sentence by sentence: "He who has not must be baptized that he may have; but he who has to no avail must be corrected, that what he has may profit him." The premise underneath is IV.11: baptism is Christ's, not the minister's — of Christ the gospel says "the same is He which baptizes" — so we consider not who gives but what he gives. Therefore baptism given in the words of the gospel is **real** outside the Church and is not repeated when a schismatic is reconciled; what it lacks is charity, which only unity supplies. **Not "sacraments work automatically":** reading the validity without the fruitfulness turns Augustine into a magician. **Boss 4 territory** — which Donatist premise it denies is not worked here.

*Introduced:* [4.4](lessons/04-04-augustine-against-the-donatists.md)

### The Church as a mixed body

*Corpus permixtum*: wheat and tares, vessels of gold and of wood, sorted at the harvest and not before (Matthew 13:24-30).

The load-bearing move against Donatist purity. If the Church were the society of the pure, the Donatists would be right that one traitor bishop is fatal; Augustine answers that no visible society has ever been that, and that the Lord told the servants to let both grow together — arguing it, pointedly, from Cyprian's own letter to Antonianus. **A reason for patience with bad men inside**, which is not the same as a claim about what may be done to those outside.

*Introduced:* [4.4](lessons/04-04-augustine-against-the-donatists.md)

### The whole world judges securely

The three-word tag *securus iudicat orbis terrarum* is a compression that drops the object clause.

*Against the Letter of Parmenianus* III.4.24 (often cited III.24; not in Schaff, so 4.4 gives its own translation of the Latin): "the whole world judges securely that those are not good who divide themselves from the whole world, in whatever part of the world." Augustine's sentence has an object — the world judges securely **that separatists are not good** — and the argument behind it is about a party in one African province condemning Christians in Gaul, Egypt and Syria whose lives it could not know. In the summer of 1839 John Henry Newman met the three words in a review article on the Donatists and wrote in the *Apologia* (ch. 3) that they "went beyond the occasion of the Donatists" and applied to the Monophysites too, so that "the theory of the Via Media was absolutely pulverized." **The sentence is genuinely Augustine's; the free-standing maxim is not,** and reading it as "the majority is always right" imports a rule about counting his context never supplies.

*Introduced:* [4.4](lessons/04-04-augustine-against-the-donatists.md)

### Pelagius on the three faculties

The crux of the whole controversy, in Pelagius's own words.

From the lost third book of his *On Free Will*, quoted at Augustine, *On the Grace of Christ* 5 (NPNF 1st ser., Holmes): "The 'ability' we place in our nature, the 'volition' in our will, and the 'actuality' in the effect. The first ... properly belongs to God ...; the other two ... must be referred to man, because they flow forth from the fountain of the will." So being able, wanting and doing are three different things; the ability is God's permanent gift; the willing and the doing are ours. **God's help therefore attaches to the ability, not to the willing** — and a human being can, at any moment, not sin. Augustine denies the third step and answers with Philippians 2:13, "to will and to accomplish". Take Pelagius at his strongest: nature is God's good gift and a gift is not an alibi; God does not command the impossible; sin is an act and nobody inherits an act; and he affirmed grace loudly enough to be acquitted at Diospolis in 415. **Boss 4 territory** — the exchange and the crux are named, not worked.

*Introduced:* [4.5](lessons/04-05-augustine-against-the-pelagians.md)

### Prevenient, operative and cooperative grace

Grace goes **before** any good movement of the will, producing it rather than assisting it.

Augustine's vocabulary. Prevenient: it does not wait for a good will to help, it sheds love abroad in the heart (Romans 5:5). Operative and cooperative: "He operates, therefore, without us, in order that we may will; but when we will, and so will that we may act, He co-operates with us" (*On Grace and Free Will* 17.33, NPNF 1st ser., Holmes and Wallis rev. Warfield). Paired with *liberum arbitrium* against *libertas*: the sick man still chooses, and has lost the taste for health. Conclusion: the first movement of a good will is itself a gift, not a merit — otherwise grace is proportioned to something we brought, "and so grace is no more grace" (Romans 11:6). The doctrine and its weight belong to [`creation-grace-last-things`](../creation-grace-last-things/syllabus.md).

*Introduced:* [4.5](lessons/04-05-augustine-against-the-pelagians.md)

### Romans 5:12 in the Latin

Augustine's Old Latin ended the verse *in quo omnes peccaverunt*, Douay-Rheims "in whom all have sinned", so the race sinned in Adam and guilt, not only mortality, passes by generation.

How he uses it: *On Nature and Grace* 9, against Pelagius's claim that "all sinned" means all imitated. The Greek *eph' ho pantes hemarton* is now usually read causally, "because all sinned", which does not by itself say the sin is Adam's. **How much rested on it: less than the story implies.** Three planks stand without the clause — the Adam-Christ parallel of Romans 5:15-19; Ephesians 2:3, children of wrath by nature (*On Nature and Grace* 3); and the Church's practice of exorcising and baptizing infants for the remission of sins, which Caelestius conceded while refusing its reason. The mistranslation is not Augustine's; he received the text. The comment he quotes on this verse as "the sainted Hilary" is in fact [Ambrosiaster](#ambrosiaster)'s.

*Introduced:* [4.5](lessons/04-05-augustine-against-the-pelagians.md)

### Theotokos

God-bearer. **A claim about who Mary's son is, not about Mary's own nature.**

Affirmed at Ephesus in 431. Cyril denies twice over that Mary is the source of the Godhead; what the title asserts is that the one she bore is God. Nestorius's objection was that the word, taken flatly, sounds like a claim that the divine nature began in Mary, and that Arians had used it of a Son who came to be and Apollinarians of a Word who replaced a human soul.

*Introduced:* [5.1](lessons/05-01-cyril-of-alexandria.md)

### Christotokos

Nestorius's preferred title, Christ-bearer: Mary bore Christ, who is God and man.

The divine and the human are joined in one *prosopon*, one presented face, by a conjunction (*synapheia*) that leaves each nature intact. Socrates the historian — no admirer, calling Nestorius vain and ill-read — still refuses the charge that he denied Christ's divinity, saying he was frightened of the word and that his published homilies nowhere destroy the Word's personal existence (*HE* VII.32).

*Introduced:* [5.1](lessons/05-01-cyril-of-alexandria.md)

### Communication of idioms

What belongs to either nature may be said of the **one person**, named by a concrete term; the properties are never traded between the natures, named by abstract terms.

| Sentence | Verdict |
|---|---|
| "The Word died." | Passes: concrete subject, human predicate |
| "The divine nature suffered." | Fails: abstract subject; Cyril rejects it in the Second Letter in so many words |
| "God was two feet long." | Formally passes, but the tradition adds Cyril's own qualifier *according to the flesh*, since the hearer will attach the predicate to the Godhead |
| "Mary is the mother of the divine nature." | Fails, and Cyril says so himself |

Cyril reaches the rule from the one subject, Leo from the two forms, and they meet at Lazarus: the tears belong to the flesh, the command that empties a four-day grave to the Word, and one person does both.

*Introduced:* [5.1](lessons/05-01-cyril-of-alexandria.md), [5.2](lessons/05-02-leo-the-great.md)

### One incarnate nature of God the Word

*Mia physis tou theou logou sesarkomene* — Cyril's favourite formula, which he believed was Athanasius's and which came from the school of Apollinaris.

Two different things follow. On the **argument**: the misattribution does not make the formula false; it removes the authority Cyril thought it carried and throws the sentence back on its sense, which is ambiguous. Read *physis* as "concrete individual subject" and it says what "one hypostasis" says; read it in Chalcedon's sense and it says there is one nature after the union, which Chalcedon denies. On the **history**: both readings were taken. Cyril accepted the Formula of Reunion in 433, which speaks of a union of two natures — evidence he could say what Chalcedon would say. Dioscorus and the churches that refused Chalcedon took "one incarnate nature" as the test of Cyril's real mind. **A forged sentence sits under a division fifteen centuries old.** Boss 5(b) asks what the misattribution does and does not do; the entry stops here.

*Introduced:* [5.1](lessons/05-01-cyril-of-alexandria.md)

### Eutychianism

Eutyches of Constantinople's claim, as Leo reports it at *Tome* 6: two natures before the union, one after.

Condemned by Flavian in 448 and at Chalcedon in 451. **Not the same as the miaphysite position,** which rejects Eutyches. Against a claim that the two collapse into one, Leo's instrument — a sentence whose grammar keeps two clauses balanced and inseparable — *is* the argument.

*Introduced:* [5.2](lessons/05-02-leo-the-great.md)

### Miaphysite objection to the Tome

The grammatical subject of "does what is proper to it" is *each form*, not *the Word* — and natures that act look like persons.

Severus of Antioch and later miaphysite writers pressed exactly this, against Cyril's fourth anathema condemning the distribution of the Gospel sayings between two subjects; it is one reason the churches that rejected Chalcedon read the council as a retreat toward Nestorius. The Chalcedonian reply is textual and in the same paragraph: Leo writes there that one and the same is truly Son of God and truly son of man, and that in Christ God and man is one person, and *cum alterius communione* binds the two operations into a single agent rather than distributing them. **Boss 5(a) territory** — the crux is named, the reading is not worked.

*Introduced:* [5.2](lessons/05-02-leo-the-great.md)

### The moral or tropological sense

The reading of Scripture for conduct, and in Gregory the Great the point of arrival rather than a middle step.

The *Moralia on Job*, dedicatory letter to Leander, section 3: expound the historical sense first, then build the typical or allegorical on it, then finish with the *moralitas*. All three are old; what Gregory does is put the moral sense **last**, and it is his ordering, not Origen's, that the Latin Middle Ages inherits. The four senses as Catholic teaching belong to [`old-testament`](../old-testament/syllabus.md) 1.2.

*Introduced:* [5.3](lessons/05-03-gregory-the-great.md)

### Servus servorum Dei

"Servant of the servants of God." Gregory made it the standing papal style; he did not invent it, and the usual story about it is a ninth-century gloss.

John the Deacon's ninth-century *Life* says Gregory assumed it to shame John the Faster. But Gregory used it of himself writing to Leander in April 591, four years before the 595 controversy, and a document of 570 under John III already opens with it. The same ninth-century *Life* is also the earliest authority for his reform of the chant that bears his name, disputed for the same reason.

*Introduced:* [5.3](lessons/05-03-gregory-the-great.md)

### Natural will and gnomic will

The count of wills follows the **natures**; the count of agents follows the **person**.

Maximus: willing is a power of a nature, not of a person, so two complete natures mean two natural wills — and a humanity with a will subtracted could not heal human willing. The **natural will** is a nature's settled orientation toward its own good and away from death; the **gnomic will** is the deliberating, hesitating mode of a creature that does not know what is best and must make up its mind. Maximus gives Christ the first and denies him the second; John states the denial flatly — Christ, being God and knowing all things, had no need of inquiry and deliberation (*Exposition* III.14, III.18). Gethsemane is the test case: the shrinking from death is the human nature doing what a human nature does, "not as I will" marks the human willing and "as You will" the divine, and the same one says both. Constantinople III (681) defines two natural wills and two natural operations, the human following and not resisting the divine; the definition belongs to `christology`.

*Introduced:* [5.4](lessons/05-04-maximus-and-john-of-damascus.md)

### Latreia and proskynesis

The worship of *latreia*, due to God alone by nature, is distinguished from the honour or veneration paid to saints, places and images.

John of Damascus, *Exposition* IV.16 (NPNF 2nd ser. vol. 9, S. D. F. Salmond), quoting Basil: "the honour given to the image passes over to the prototype." Reverence travels through the picture to the person; it does not stop at the paint. Nicaea II (787) built its definition on the distinction, reserving *latreia* to God alone. The argument it completes: the Old Testament prohibition was on depicting the **invisible**; in Christ the invisible God became visible and circumscribed; so depicting him denies nothing about God, while refusing to depict him denies the Incarnation; and matter is not contemptible, since God saved through it.

*Introduced:* [5.4](lessons/05-04-maximus-and-john-of-damascus.md)

### The three ways

Pseudo-Dionysius's route to the divine names: **affirmation, negation, and eminence** correcting both.

Taken up by Maximus, by John of Damascus, and eventually by Aquinas, whose version is owned by [`thomistic-synthesis`](../thomistic-synthesis/syllabus.md) 4.2, with participation at 2.3. The content was never true because Paul's Athenian convert wrote it: what the exposure of the false name destroys is every argument of the form "the apostolic age already held this", and what it leaves standing is the argument.

*Introduced:* [5.4](lessons/05-04-maximus-and-john-of-damascus.md)

### The close of the patristic age

A convention of the manuals, not a fact of nature.

Gregory the Great (d. 604) or Isidore of Seville (d. 636) in the West; John of Damascus (d. c. 749) in the East. Nothing stopped in 749 and Greek theology kept working; John is called the last Greek Father because he **summarizes** the tradition, not because it ended. The convention is worth understanding anyway: after Maximus and John the tradition has a summary of itself, and a summary is what you appeal to when you say "the Fathers." Three claims in ascending order of cost: *this Father said this* (cheap, settled by the five-step check); *this Father taught this as the faith of the churches* (harder — it needs the genre, the opponent and the argument); *the Fathers agree on this* (expensive, and it needs a defensible sense of "agree" before any counting starts).

*Introduced:* [5.4](lessons/05-04-maximus-and-john-of-damascus.md); the method is [1.1](lessons/01-01-how-to-read-a-church-father.md)'s

## The Fathers

One `###` per figure: dates, city, language, the controversy he was in, his standing (Father, Doctor, ecclesiastical writer), and the works this course reads. Dates that are inferences are marked as inferences.

### Clement of Rome

**c. 95-97 (the letter's conventional date) · Rome · Greek · the Corinthian deposition · Father**

Known only through the letter. 1 Clement names **no author in its text**; the manuscripts head it with Clement's name and the ancient witnesses agree — Dionysius of Corinth about 170 (in Eusebius, *HE* 4.23.11), then Hegesippus, Irenaeus and Origen. But notice how Irenaeus puts it: "the Church in Rome dispatched a most powerful letter to the Corinthians" (*AH* 3.3.3). **The letter is the act of a church; Clement is the hand.** Cite it as 1 Clement and say, when it matters, that the attribution is early and traditional rather than internal.

*Read at:* [1.2](lessons/01-02-first-clement-and-the-didache.md)

### Ignatius of Antioch

**c. 107-140, traditionally "about 110" · bishop of Antioch in Syria · Greek · docetism and church order · Father**

Condemned — we are never told for what, or by whom — and sent to Rome for the arena under guard, by soldiers he calls ten leopards. He wrote at two stops: from **Smyrna** to the Ephesians, Magnesians, Trallians and to the Christians at Rome; from **Troas** to the Philadelphians, the Smyrnaeans and to Polycarp. Seven letters, exactly the list Eusebius gives with the two places of writing (*HE* III.36.5-10). The date is traditional, not documented — Eusebius sets him under Trajan (98-117) and nothing in the letters names a year. A live minority: Reinhard Hübner (1997) and Thomas Lechner (1999) argued the seven are a later second-century forgery; T. D. Barnes (2008) kept them as Ignatius's but moved him to the 140s. Most patristic scholars follow neither — **a scholarly consensus, not a doctrine.**

*Read at:* [1.3](lessons/01-03-ignatius-of-antioch.md); see [The three recensions of Ignatius](#the-three-recensions-of-ignatius)

### Justin Martyr

**born early 100s at Flavia Neapolis in Samaria; beheaded at Rome c. 165 · Greek · defending Christianity to the emperor, and to Trypho · Father**

A layman and philosopher, of a pagan family. He tried a Stoic, a Peripatetic who wanted his fee up front, a Pythagorean who demanded music and geometry first, and a Platonist — until an old man by the sea unpicked his Platonism and pointed him to the prophets (*Dialogue* 2-8). He kept the philosopher's cloak after baptism and taught in a rented room above a Roman bathhouse. *First Apology* 46 dates the work near 155 (roughly 150-160): Christ was born "one hundred and fifty years ago under Cyrenius." Tried with six companions under the prefect Q. Junius Rusticus; the *Acts of Justin* records his flat answer about where Christians met. **Works:** *First Apology*, *Second Apology*, *Dialogue with Trypho* (certainly his); *Address to the Greeks*, *Hortatory Address*, *On the Sole Government of God*, *On the Resurrection*, *Epistle to Diognetus* (ANF listed as disputed; the consensus is that none is his); *Against All Heresies* and a work against Marcion, lost.

*Read at:* [1.4](lessons/01-04-justin-martyr.md); see [Paris gr. 450](#paris-gr-450)

### Irenaeus of Lyons

**c. 175-189 for *Against Heresies*, conventionally about 180 · from Smyrna, bishop of Lyons in Gaul · Greek · the Valentinians · Father, and Doctor of the Church since 21 January 2022**

Heard Polycarp preach as a boy; presbyter and then bishop at Lyons after the persecution of 177. His title was *Detection and Overthrow of the Falsely So-Called Knowledge*; Book 3 names Eleutherius as bishop of Rome, which dates it. Declared *Doctor unitatis* by Francis for bridging East and West — and the Church still did not receive his millenarianism. **Works read here:** *Against Heresies* 1 (the Valentinian system and the rule of faith), 3 (succession, the Roman sentence, the fourfold gospel), 4-5 (recapitulation, the Eucharist, the thousand years).

*Read at:* [2.1](lessons/02-01-irenaeus-against-the-gnostics.md), [2.2](lessons/02-02-irenaeus-recapitulation.md)

### Tertullian

**c. 155-160 to c. 220 · Carthage · Latin · Gnostics, docetists, modalists · ecclesiastical writer, not a Father**

The most quoted Father who is not one. Almost everything biographical comes from one page written nearly two centuries later — Jerome, *On Illustrious Men* 53, which calls him a presbyter, the son of a proconsular centurion, and says he "lapsed to the doctrine of Montanus"; Timothy Barnes (*Tertullian*, 1971) showed most of it is unsupported, including the identification with the jurist Tertullianus of the *Digest*. What the writings prove is enough: a Carthaginian of high rhetorical training, at home in Roman legal language, writing Latin of enormous force. **Works, on conventional datings:** *Apology* (197), *Prescription against Heretics* (c. 200), *On the Flesh of Christ* (c. 206), *Against Praxeas* (c. 213, written from inside the New Prophecy, as ch. 2 says openly). Vincent of Lérins made him the standing example of the danger, calling him more eloquent by far than faithful (*Commonitorium* 18); Jerome reports that Cyprian never let a day pass without reading him and would ask for "the master."

*Read at:* [2.3](lessons/02-03-tertullian.md)

### Cyprian of Carthage

**converted c. 246, bishop 248/9, beheaded 14 September 258 · Carthage · Latin · the lapsed, Novatian's schism, rebaptism · Father and martyr, named in the Roman Canon**

A wealthy, rhetorically trained Carthaginian elected bishop fast enough that some of his presbyters never accepted it. Within a year Decius ordered everyone in the empire to sacrifice; Cyprian went into hiding and governed by letter while thousands of African Christians lapsed. **Works read here:** *On the Lapsed*; *On the Unity of the Catholic Church* (251), twenty-seven short chapters against Novatian's schism at Rome and a rival faction at Carthage; the letters to Quintus, Jubaianus and Pompey on rebaptism; the *Sentences of the Eighty-Seven Bishops* (1 September 256). His error did not damage his standing: he was never condemned. Firmilian of Caesarea's furious letter against Stephen (ANF Epistle 74 = Hartel 75) is the other side of the correspondence.

*Read at:* [2.4](lessons/02-04-cyprian-of-carthage.md)

### Clement of Alexandria

**c. 150 to c. 215 · Alexandria, then Asia Minor · Greek · the Gnostics, and Greek philosophy · venerated as a saint into the sixteenth century, then dropped**

A convert who settled in Alexandria and taught there until persecution struck the city around 202 — the supposed edict of Septimius Severus rests on a weak source. His programme is three books in sequence, and the sequence is the argument: the *Protrepticus* turns a pagan around, the *Paedagogus* puts Christ over the convert's manners, meals and dress, and the *Stromata* is deliberately unsystematic teaching for the mature. Whether he ever wrote the promised third stage, a *Teacher*, is disputed. **His name was dropped from the Roman Martyrology in the Baronian revisions of the late 1500s**, and Benedict XIV upheld the omission in 1748 — his life was little known, he had no public cult, and some doctrines were at least suspect; Photius had already censured errors in the lost *Hypotyposes*.

*Read at:* [2.5](lessons/02-05-clement-of-alexandria-and-origen.md)

### Origen

**c. 185 to c. 253 · Alexandria, then Caesarea in Palestine · Greek · the first system, and the first textual criticism · ecclesiastical writer, not a Father**

Alexandrian by birth; his father Leonides was martyred in the trouble that drove Clement out. Eusebius, the main and admiring source, has him teaching converts in his teens; the story of his self-castration (*HE* VI.8) is widely doubted today, as is the tidy picture of a "catechetical school" with an official succession of heads. Ordained in Palestine around 231 without his own bishop's consent, he lost Alexandria and settled at Caesarea. **Tortured under Decius in 250 and died of it a few years later, in the Church's communion** — he was not condemned in his lifetime, and the anathemas are three centuries later with contested conciliar standing. **Works read here:** the *Hexapla*; *On First Principles* (c. 220-230), whose preface states the method — the apostles delivered certain points plainly and left the grounds and the open questions for later inquiry — and Book IV on reading Scripture; *Against Celsus*, which survives in Greek. Porphyry reports that he studied under Ammonius Saccas, Plotinus's teacher, though whether he means this Origen is disputed ([`ancient-medieval-philosophy` 4.4](../ancient-medieval-philosophy/lessons/04-04-plotinus-the-one-intellect-and-soul.md)). **The one without whom the Fathers are unintelligible.**

*Read at:* [2.5](lessons/02-05-clement-of-alexandria-and-origen.md), [3.5](lessons/03-05-allegory-and-history-alexandria-and-antioch.md)

### Athanasius

**c. 296-373 · bishop of Alexandria from 328 · Greek · Arius and the long fourth-century argument · Father, Doctor, one of the four great Greek Doctors**

Attended Nicaea in 325 as deacon and secretary to Bishop Alexander, succeeded him in 328, and was driven from his see five times under four emperors — some seventeen of his forty-five years as bishop in exile. **Works read here:** *On the Incarnation* (the second half of a two-part work for one Macarius, after *Against the Nations*); *Orations against the Arians*; *On the Decrees of the Nicene Synod*; *On the Synods*. His *Life of Antony* and the politics of the exiles are ceded to [`church-history`](../church-history/syllabus.md); Festal Letter 39 of 367, with its list of the twenty-seven New Testament books, is named only. **He did not write the Athanasian Creed.**

*Read at:* [3.1](lessons/03-01-athanasius.md)

### Basil the Great

**c. 330-379 · bishop of Caesarea in Cappadocia from 370 · Greek · the Pneumatomachi, and the vocabulary deadlock · Father, one of the four great Greek Doctors**

Of a wealthy Christian family of Pontus and Cappadocia; studied rhetoric at Athens alongside Gregory of Nazianzus and was turned toward ascetic life by his sister Macrina. In 358 he gathered a community at Annesi; his *Asketikon*, in Rufinus's Latin of 397, is the "Rule of our holy father Basil" Benedict would cite. As bishop he built a hospice, hospital and poorhouse Gregory called a new city. With Gregory he is the traditional compiler of the *Philocalia*, an ascription some scholars doubt. **Works read here:** *On the Holy Spirit* (375, for Amphilochius of Iconium); Letters 214, 236 and the disputed 38; *Hexaemeron*. Chapter 29 of *On the Holy Spirit* shows him doing source criticism on earlier Fathers, pressing only what is verifiable in the works.

*Read at:* [3.2](lessons/03-02-basil-the-great.md)

### Gregory of Nazianzus

**c. 329-390 · Constantinople in 380 · Greek · Eunomians and Pneumatomachi · Father, Doctor, "the Theologian"**

A reluctant bishop and a trained rhetorician who spent 380 preaching in a house chapel to a Nicene minority in a largely Homoian city. **Works read here:** Orations 27-31, the *Theological Orations*; Letter 101 to Cledonius against Apollinarius (usually dated 382); Oration 43, the funeral oration for Basil. The title "the Theologian" marks, in the Greek tradition, a writer who speaks of God's own being; he earned it for these five orations, and it is **not** a verdict that he outranks Athanasius or Basil.

*Read at:* [3.3](lessons/03-03-the-two-gregories.md)

### Gregory of Nyssa

**c. 335 to after 394 · bishop of Nyssa by his brother Basil's arrangement · Greek · the counting objection · Father; venerated as a saint, never declared a Doctor**

The more original speculative mind of the two brothers. **Works read here:** *To Ablabius: On Not Three Gods*; *Life of Moses*; *On the Soul and the Resurrection*, in which his teacher is his dying sister **Macrina**, who argues him out of his grief after Basil's death in 379. The asymmetry with Nazianzen's Doctorate grades nobody's theology: no title makes an opinion Church teaching. Letter 38 among Basil's letters is widely argued to be his.

*Read at:* [3.3](lessons/03-03-the-two-gregories.md)

### Aphrahat

**the *Demonstrations* dated inside the text to 337 (nos. 1-10), 344 (11-22) and 345 (23) · Persian territory beyond Nisibis · Syriac · Christian life under Persian rule · "the Persian Sage"**

The earliest Syriac author we can read whole, and the course's hardest attribution lesson in miniature. **His name was lost:** fifth- and sixth-century copies call him only "the Persian Sage", an eighth-century scholar who studied him closely cannot discover the author's name, rank or home, and for a while the works travelled under Jacob of Nisibis's name; "Aphrahat" resurfaces only in the tenth century. He barely notices the Arian controversy convulsing an empire he did not live in. If a corpus this large could lose its author, **"everyone has always attributed this to X" is weak evidence about authorship.**

*Read at:* [3.4](lessons/03-04-ephrem-and-syriac-christianity.md)

### Ephrem the Syrian

**died 373 (a minority of authorities say 378) · Nisibis, then Edessa from 363 · Syriac · Bardaisan's hymns, and the Arian controversy as it reached Edessa · Father, Doctor of the universal Church since 5 October 1920**

A deacon, never a bishop. The conventional birth year in the early 300s is inference, not record; he was a disciple of Jacob, bishop of Nisibis (d. 338). When Jovian ceded Nisibis to Persia in July 363 its Christian population left, and Ephrem settled at Edessa for his last decade. He wrote commentaries (Genesis, Exodus, the *Diatessaron*), verse homilies (*mimre*) and hymn cycles (*madrashe*) — the *Nisibene Hymns*, *On the Nativity*, *On the Epiphany*, *On Faith*, *On Paradise*, *On Virginity*. Benedict XV conferred the Doctorate in *Principi Apostolorum Petro*; its own footnotes quote works of varying authenticity. Two things reported by later Syriac tradition rather than documented: the choirs of consecrated women he is said to have trained, and the visit to Basil at Caesarea around 370 (Sozomen and a panegyric transmitted among Gregory of Nyssa's works report it; some critics judge the evidence insufficient). **Say "the Syriac tradition reports", not "we know."**

*Read at:* [3.4](lessons/03-04-ephrem-and-syriac-christianity.md); see [The Greek Ephrem](#the-greek-ephrem)

### Diodore of Tarsus

**bishop from 378, died about 390 · Antioch · Greek · Alexandrian allegory · teacher of the Antiochene school**

Ran an ascetic study house at Antioch and taught the next two names. He wrote a treatise on the difference between *theoria* and allegory, **now lost**; a Psalms commentary survives whose preface states his preference for the historical reading plainly.

*Read at:* [3.5](lessons/03-05-allegory-and-history-alexandria-and-antioch.md)

### Theodore of Mopsuestia

**about 350-428 · Antioch, then Mopsuestia · Greek · the historical sense · condemned posthumously in 553**

Read each book against its own historical setting, cut the directly messianic psalms to a handful, and treated the Song of Songs as a marriage poem. His person and works were condemned in 553 as one of the Three Chapters, **for Christological reasons**, with his exegesis read in that light; the affair as an event is [`church-history` 2.2](../church-history/lessons/02-02-ephesus-chalcedon-and-leos-rome.md). **No condemnation and no Doctor's title settles a method.**

*Read at:* [3.5](lessons/03-05-allegory-and-history-alexandria-and-antioch.md)

### John Chrysostom

**born at Antioch c. 349 (estimates run from the mid-340s), died 14 September 407 at Comana in Pontus · Antioch, then Constantinople · Greek · wealth, Judaizing Christians, the imperial court · Father, one of the four great Greek Doctors**

Trained in rhetoric, drawn to the ascetic life, ordained priest in 386 by Flavian of Antioch. For twelve years he preached there; in Lent 387, after a riot in which the emperor's statues were thrown down, he preached *On the Statues* to a terrified city. Ordained bishop of Constantinople on 26 February 398, deposed at the Synod of the Oak in 403, recalled, exiled again in 404. The politics belong to [`church-history`](../church-history/syllabus.md). **More pages survive under his name than under any other Greek Father's, and almost all were spoken aloud before they were written down.** "Chrysostom", golden-mouthed, is an epithet his readers gave him. His homilies run in two movements — the **letter** (what the text says, why the evangelist put it here, what the speaker was trying to do) and then the **turn**, where he stops explaining and addresses the people in front of him, usually about money.

### Ambrose of Milan

**born about 340, consecrated 7 December 374, bishop of Milan for twenty-three years · Latin, reading Greek · Arianism at Milan, and catechizing the newly baptized · Father, one of the four original Latin Doctors**

Son of a praetorian prefect of Gaul (the city is disputed: Trier, Arles and Lyons all claim him). He trained as a lawyer and became consular governor of Liguria and Aemilia, resident at Milan. In 374 the Arian Auxentius, who had held the see some twenty years, died; Ambrose went to the basilica officially, to keep order at a contested election, and the crowd acclaimed him bishop — **an unbaptized catechumen.** So the West got a bishop with no theological training and an excellent Greek library, and that combination is his structural importance: the 1907 *Catholic Encyclopedia* notes his "thorough mastery of the Greek language and literature," which Augustine and later Leo lacked, and his reading of Scripture and the Fathers "with a marked preference for Origen and St. Basil." Jerome (Letter 41) calls him a disciple of Origen as modified by Hippolytus and Basil; Philo is a third source, visible in the Genesis commentaries, which follow Basil's *Hexaemeron*. **Works read here:** *On the Mysteries*; *On the Sacraments* (disputed); the hymns. Ambrose and Theodosius is ceded to [`church-history` 2.1](../church-history/lessons/02-01-nicaea-and-the-imperial-church.md).

*Read at:* [4.1](lessons/04-01-ambrose-of-milan.md)

### Jerome

**born at Stridon on the Dalmatian frontier around 347 (older reference books say 340-342; the date is not fixed), died at Bethlehem in 420 · Rome, Antioch, Bethlehem · Latin, Greek, Hebrew · translation, the canon, Origen, Helvidius · Father and Doctor**

Schooling at Rome; ascetic years in the Syrian desert, where he began Hebrew; ordination at Antioch; three years at Rome (382-385) as scholar and secretary to **Pope Damasus**, where he gathered the circle that shaped the rest of his life — the widow **Paula** and her daughter **Eustochium**, who later paid for his secretaries and copyists. When Damasus died in December 384 his position collapsed under the hostility his satire had earned him; he settled at Bethlehem in 386 beside Paula's monastery and stayed thirty-four years. **He did two different jobs on the Bible, and running them together is the commonest mistake made about him:** a revision of the Latin gospels against the Greek at Rome (c. 382-385, at Damasus's request, *not* a new version — he corrected Augustine on the point at Letter 75.20), and a translation of the Old Testament out of the Hebrew at Bethlehem (c. 391-404, with no ecclesiastical commission, at friends' requests and on his own judgement). **Works read here:** Letter 57 to Pammachius; the prefaces, including the "helmeted preface" to Samuel and Kings (c. 391) and the preface to Solomon (393); Letter 53 to Paulinus; *Against Helvidius* (c. 383); Letters 75 and 82 in the exchange with Augustine; the prologue to the *Commentary on Isaiah*; Letter 124 to Avitus. The quarrel with Rufinus ran 393-402, the correspondence with Augustine 394-405.

*Read at:* [4.2](lessons/04-02-jerome.md)

### Augustine of Hippo

**354-430 · Thagaste, Carthage, Rome, Milan, Hippo · Latin · Manichees, Donatists, Pelagians · Father and Doctor**

Taught rhetoric at Carthage, Rome and Milan; a Manichaean hearer for about nine years; converted in a Milan garden in 386; baptized by Ambrose at Easter 387; bishop of Hippo about 395. **Works read in this course:** the *Confessions* (c. 397-401, written as bishop, looking back); *On Baptism, against the Donatists*; *Against the Letter of Parmenianus*; Letters 93 (408, to Vincentius) and 185 (to Boniface, circulated as *On the Correction of the Donatists*); Letter 133; Sermon 131; and the anti-Pelagian works. *City of God* as political theory is ceded to [`history-of-political-thought` 2.2](../history-of-political-thought/lessons/02-02-augustine-the-two-cities.md); his philosophy of memory, time and the will to [`ancient-medieval-philosophy` 5.1](../ancient-medieval-philosophy/lessons/05-01-augustine-certainty-and-illumination.md), [5.2](../ancient-medieval-philosophy/lessons/05-02-augustine-evil-and-the-will.md) and [5.3](../ancient-medieval-philosophy/lessons/05-03-time-eternity-and-foreknowledge.md). **He is a Doctor of the Church, and his late predestinarian teaching was not received whole** — the cleanest case after Cyprian of what "the title endorses no particular opinion" means.

*Read at:* [4.3](lessons/04-03-augustine-confessions.md), [4.4](lessons/04-04-augustine-against-the-donatists.md), [4.5](lessons/04-05-augustine-against-the-pelagians.md)

### Cyril of Alexandria

**bishop from 412; the controversy runs 428-433 · Alexandria · Greek · Nestorius · Father and Doctor**

**Works read here:** the Second Letter to Nestorius (his Letter 4), read at the first session of Ephesus in 431 and approved by the bishops one by one, read again at Chalcedon and acclaimed with his peace letter to John of Antioch; the Third Letter with the Twelve Anathemas; the Formula of Reunion he accepted in 433. The events of Ephesus are ceded to [`church-history` 2.2](../church-history/lessons/02-02-ephesus-chalcedon-and-leos-rome.md) and the definitions to [`christology`](../christology/syllabus.md). **Neither his title nor his Doctorate endorses a formula** — and his favourite formula came to him under a false name.

*Read at:* [5.1](lessons/05-01-cyril-of-alexandria.md)

### Leo the Great

**consecrated bishop of Rome 29 September 440, died 10 November 461 · Rome · Latin · Eutyches · Father, Doctor of the Church (declared by Benedict XIV in 1754)**

A Roman deacon trusted enough to be sent to Gaul on an imperial commission; Sixtus III died while he was away. **Ninety-six genuine sermons and 143 letters survive.** Not a speculative theologian in the Greek sense: a trained Latin rhetorician who preached to the same congregation for twenty-one years, whose instrument is the **balanced antithesis** — two clauses of the same shape, one naming what is divine and one what is human, held in a sentence that refuses to choose. Against a heresy claiming that after the union you can no longer count to two, that grammar *is* the argument. **Works read here:** the *Tome* (Letter 28 to Flavian, 449) and Sermons 1-5, preached on the anniversary of his consecration. Canon 28 of Chalcedon and the meeting with Attila are ceded to [`church-history` 2.2](../church-history/lessons/02-02-ephesus-chalcedon-and-leos-rome.md).

*Read at:* [5.2](lessons/05-02-leo-the-great.md)

### Gregory the Great

**born at Rome about 540, city prefect about 573, elected pope February 590, died 12 March 604 · Rome · Latin · no heresy — pastoral method, exegesis, and a title · Father, the last of the four great Latin Doctors**

Of a senatorial family; he gave up Rome's highest civil office, turned the family house on the Caelian into a monastery and became a monk in it, served as papal envoy at Constantinople, and was elected pope against his own loud protest with plague in the city and the Lombards in Italy. **He is where the patristic age in the West stops being a series of arguments and becomes a set of working habits.** **Works read here:** the *Pastoral Rule* (c. 591, addressed to John of Ravenna); the *Moralia on Job* (35 books, begun as lectures at Constantinople, dedicated to Leander of Seville); the letters of 595-602 against the title "ecumenical"; the letters to Mellitus and to Æthelberht (601); the *Dialogues* (disputed). The mission to Kent as an event is [`church-history` 3.1](../church-history/lessons/03-01-gregory-the-great-and-the-conversion-of-the-peoples.md). **Isidore of Seville** (c. 560-636), brother of that Leander, wrote the *Etymologies*, packing what survived of ancient learning into a form the early Middle Ages could carry: with him the Latin Fathers end and the schoolbooks begin.

*Read at:* [5.3](lessons/05-03-gregory-the-great.md)

### Maximus the Confessor

**c. 580-662 · Constantinople, North Africa, exile in Lazica · Greek · monothelitism · a monk who held no see and defined nothing**

The Greek *Life* makes him a Constantinopolitan aristocrat turned imperial secretary; a hostile Syriac life, unknown until the twentieth century, makes him a Palestinian of obscure birth. He fled the Persian and then the Arab advance to North Africa, and in July 645 at Carthage debated the deposed patriarch Pyrrhus in public on whether Christ has one will or two. **The *Disputation with Pyrrhus* is the clearest statement of his case, and there is no public-domain English of it, so 5.4 paraphrases him and quotes him nowhere.** Tried at Constantinople for resisting the emperor's ban on the question; in 662, old, he had his tongue and right hand cut off — the organs of speech and of writing — and was exiled. **For roughly a quarter-century it was imperial policy that he was wrong**, an edict forbade even raising the question, and a pope had written a letter the Sixth Council would later condemn. Nineteen years after his death Constantinople III taught what he had argued. He also worked through Pseudo-Dionysius's hardest passages in the *Ambigua*; the scholia printed under his name are now largely assigned to John of Scythopolis.

*Read at:* [5.4](lessons/05-04-maximus-and-john-of-damascus.md)

### John of Damascus

**c. 675 to c. 749 · Damascus, then a monastery near Jerusalem · Greek · iconoclasm · Father and Doctor; the last Greek Father by convention**

Born into a Christian family that served the caliph's treasury. He wrote **under Muslim rule, outside the reach of the Byzantine emperors** who were then suppressing icons — which is exactly why his defence of images survived; his setting is [`church-history` 3.2](../church-history/lessons/03-02-the-rise-of-islam-and-the-christian-mediterranean.md). The *Fount of Knowledge* has three parts: philosophical chapters, a catalogue of heresies, and the *Exposition of the Orthodox Faith*, a hundred chapters arranging the Greek Fathers into a single argument. **He rarely claims originality** — he says he is setting out what the Fathers taught, appending florilegia, and his genius is arrangement, which is what makes the *Exposition* usable and what makes it weak evidence for what any individual earlier Father held. The iconoclast synod of Hieria anathematized him by name in 754; Nicaea II restored the images in 787. Peter Lombard used the *Exposition* in the *Sentences* and Aquinas cites it constantly by the Latin book-and-chapter division ("De Fide Orth.").

*Read at:* [5.4](lessons/05-04-maximus-and-john-of-damascus.md)

## Texts and what they establish

One `###` per text the course reads closely: what it is, and what it can and cannot show. Texts whose main interest is an authenticity problem are under [Authenticity and transmission](#authenticity-and-transmission); the ones three traditions each claim are under [Contested passages](#contested-passages).

### 1 Clement

A letter of sixty-five short chapters from the church of God sojourning at Rome to the church of God sojourning at Corinth, conventionally about **95-97**.

The occasion is stated plainly: the Corinthians have deposed presbyters who served blamelessly, and Rome writes to get them restored. The argument (chs. 42-44) runs from apostolic appointment through built-in succession to the conclusion that lawfully appointed men of blameless service "cannot be justly dismissed from the ministry" (ANF vol. 9, John Keith). **What it establishes:** that by the 90s a church could argue this way, and that the offices had not fixed their names. **What it does not:** that anyone presides alone at Corinth or at Rome — the leadership is always plural, chs. 47 and 57 speak of presbyters only, and there is no bishop of Corinth. **Note the warrant:** chapters 40 and 43 argue from the pattern in Scripture (high priest, priests, Levites, layman; Aaron's rod), not from Rome's office; only near the end does the letter speak with weight of its own, warning that whoever disobeys what God has said through it stands in danger (ch. 59) and sending named messengers to bring word back (chs. 63, 65). **The date is an inference**, resting mainly on reading the calamities of ch. 1 as troubles at Rome late under Domitian, helped by the letter treating the deaths of Peter and Paul as its own generation's (ch. 5): Thomas Herron argued for about 70, L. L. Welborn allows 80 to 140, the 90s is the majority view. **It is not Scripture** — read aloud in the assembly at Corinth in the 170s, standing in Codex Alexandrinus after Revelation, and still not received into the canon. **Boss 1 territory.**

*Read at:* [1.2](lessons/01-02-first-clement-and-the-didache.md)

### The Didache

"The Lord's Teaching Through the Twelve Apostles to the Nations": sixteen short chapters, anonymous, composite, roughly **50 to 150** with the core placed early by most scholars.

Contents: the Two Ways (1-6), baptism, fasting, prayer and the thanksgiving prayers (7-10), travelling apostles and prophets (11-13), the Sunday assembly (14), local officers (15), the last things (16). What a reader gets from chs. 7-14 (ANF vol. 7, M. B. Riddle): baptism in the triune name, in living water if there is any, in other water if not, in warm if cold is unavailable, and failing that pour three times on the head — **the formula is fixed, the mode is ranked by preference, and the fallback is given without apology**; fasting Wednesdays and Fridays, "not as the hypocrites do", a discipline defined against the synagogue's; the Lord's Prayer three times a day with a doxology; thanksgiving over the cup first and then the broken bread, the scattered grain gathered into one loaf as a figure of the Church, only the baptized admitted; and on the Lord's day gathering, breaking bread and giving thanks after confession, with Malachi's pure offering applied to it. **Because it is composite, the real question is a date per layer**, and an argument needing the earliest edge of the range stands on a contested reading. **What it cannot license:** "the earliest Christians did X." It shows one community, probably Syrian, at one moment.

*Read at:* [1.2](lessons/01-02-first-clement-and-the-didache.md)

### The Two Ways

The way of life and the way of death, Didache 1-6.

A Jewish-Christian catechetical tract that also circulated separately and survives in part in an old Latin version — which is part of the evidence that the Didache is an assembly of older material rather than one author's book.

*Read at:* [1.2](lessons/01-02-first-clement-and-the-didache.md)

### The Eucharist against docetism

Ignatius, *Smyrnaeans* 6-7 (ANF vol. 1, Roberts-Donaldson): the docetists "abstain from the Eucharist and from prayer, because they confess not the Eucharist to be the flesh of our Saviour Jesus Christ, which suffered for our sins, and which the Father, of His goodness, raised up again."

**Read the halves together.** The charge is behavioural before it is doctrinal — these people do not come to the Eucharist, and have no care for the widow, the orphan, the prisoner or the hungry (ch. 6) — and the doctrine is given as the *reason* for the behaviour. The argument runs from what the Church does back to what must be true for the practice to make sense, and it includes Ignatius's own body: if Christ only seemed to suffer, then Ignatius is only seemingly in chains (ch. 4). Basil argues the same way about the Holy Spirit three centuries later. The doctrine of the Eucharist belongs to [`sacraments-and-liturgy`](../sacraments-and-liturgy/syllabus.md).

*Read at:* [1.3](lessons/01-03-ignatius-of-antioch.md)

### Justin's Sunday liturgy

*First Apology* 61, 65-67 (ANF, Dods and Reith): **the earliest full description of Christian worship we possess**, Rome around 155.

Baptism after instruction, fasting and prayer, by washing "in the name of God, the Father and Lord of the universe, and of our Saviour Jesus Christ, and of the Holy Spirit" (61). Then on Sunday: all who live in cities or in the country gather in one place; the memoirs of the apostles or the writings of the prophets are read as long as time permits; the president instructs and exhorts; prayers; a kiss; bread and a cup of wine mixed with water brought to "the president of the brethren"; a long thanksgiving offered "according to his ability"; the people's *Amen*; deacons distributing and carrying a portion to the absent; and a collection for widows, orphans, the sick and strangers (65-67). **What it settles and what it does not:** it does not prove a fixed liturgy — the president prays extempore within a fixed shape. What it does settle, often overlooked, is that by about 155 at Rome the meal is restricted to the baptized who believe and live accordingly, that it is "not as common bread," and that it is bound to the Incarnation by an analogy. The ANF editors footnoted ch. 66 as "claimed alike by Calvinists, Lutherans, and Romanists"; stating each reading and the words it leans on is this course's job.

*Read at:* [1.4](lessons/01-04-justin-martyr.md)

### Nag Hammadi

Thirteen Coptic papyrus codices found in Upper Egypt in **December 1945**, including the *Gospel of Truth* and the *Apocryphon of John*.

A check on a hostile source, cutting both ways. **A real result:** where Irenaeus had documents he reported reasonably well — he names and mocks the *Gospel of Truth* (*AH* 3.11.9), and the *Apocryphon of John*'s myth matches the system he summarizes at 1.29. **A limited one:** the same library holds writings with little in common, which is bad news for his claim that all of it descends by a single genealogy of error from Simon Magus. Michael Williams (*Rethinking "Gnosticism"*, 1996) and Karen King (*What Is Gnosticism?*, 2003) argue that "Gnosticism" as a unified religion is largely an artifact of the heresiologists. **Irenaeus is a good reporter and a bad taxonomist.**

*Read at:* [2.1](lessons/02-01-irenaeus-against-the-gnostics.md)

### The Hexapla

Origen's six-column edition of the Old Testament at Caesarea: the Hebrew; the Hebrew transliterated into Greek; Aquila; Symmachus; the Septuagint, marked against the Hebrew; Theodotion.

So a disputed reading could be checked by eye. **The first work of biblical textual criticism**; it survives only in fragments.

*Read at:* [2.5](lessons/02-05-clement-of-alexandria-and-origen.md)

### On the Incarnation

Athanasius's treatise, the second half of a two-part work addressed to one Macarius, after *Against the Nations*.

It answers a question of taste rather than doctrine — why would God stoop to a body, and to that death — with the divine dilemma, and **there are no heretics in view**: the treatise names neither Arius nor any Nicene term. **The date is disputed and nothing should rest on it.** The older and commonest dating reads that silence as proof of composition around 318, before the controversy; Charles Kannengiesser and others argue for the 330s. Report it as disputed and give the traditional date as the traditional date. Chapter 54 is **Boss 3 material**.

*Read at:* [3.1](lessons/03-01-athanasius.md)

### The Theological Orations

Gregory of Nazianzus, Orations 27-31, preached at Constantinople in 380 against Eunomians and Pneumatomachi; the texts that won him the title "the Theologian".

Oration 27 opens by denying that theology is for everyone, anytime, before any audience. The heart of Oration 31 is an argument about **words and silence**: the objection is that Scripture does not call the Spirit God in so many words, and Gregory answers that the opponents' own load-bearing terms, *Unbegotten* and *Unoriginate*, "those two citadels of your position", are not there either (31.23); that things and names come apart, Scripture speaking of things it never names (31.22); that the silence is pedagogical (31.26); and that worship decides what the argument cannot, since a Spirit who deifies in baptism is adored, and if adored, God (31.28). At 31.10 it is a flat exchange — is the Spirit God, most certainly; consubstantial, yes if he is God — **exactly the sentence Basil would not write.** He closes by letting loose "the swarm of testimonies", the titles Scripture does give (31.29).

*Read at:* [3.3](lessons/03-03-the-two-gregories.md)

### To Ablabius: On Not Three Gods

Gregory of Nyssa's answer to the counting objection, and it refuses the analogy where everyone expects him to accept it.

(1) "Three men" is a customary **abuse of language**: the nature *man* is one, indivisible, and not increased by adding people, so the analogy breaks on the *human* side. (2) The habit is harmless below and dangerous above, and he concedes that ordinary speech cannot be unteached. (3) "Godhead" names an **operation**, not the nature, which is unnameable — our words reach its surroundings. (4) The divine operation is single and undivided: every act issues from the Father, is brought into operation through the Son, and is perfected in the Spirit. (5) A name taken from one undivided operation cannot be pluralized — Scripture says one Saviour, not three. (6) The three are still really distinct, **by cause alone**: one uncaused, one begotten, one proceeding. **What he is entitled to is narrower and stronger than a knock-down analogy:** the plural "gods" is barred by the grammar of a single undivided operation, while whether the human case really is loose talk stays open — a nominalist can decline premise 1, and premise 1 is a strong realism about the universal ([`ancient-medieval-philosophy` 8.1](../ancient-medieval-philosophy/lessons/08-01-porphyrys-questions-universals-to-aquinas.md)). **Read it as a Father arguing toward** the later defined principle that the Trinity's works toward creation are undivided, which is `trinity-and-god`'s.

*Read at:* [3.3](lessons/03-03-the-two-gregories.md)

### On the Priesthood

Chrysostom's six-book dialogue, c. 386-387 (NPNF 1st ser. vol. 9, Stephens).

The priest's hands do what angels are not commissioned to do: "they who inhabit the earth and make their abode there are entrusted with the administration of things which are in Heaven, and have received an authority which God has not given to angels or archangels" (III.5). **He draws the conclusion downward, not upward** — such an office is terrifying, and most men should run from it. The same move as Homily 50: a high doctrine of the sacrament cashed out as a demand on the man holding it. Its occasion, a story about evading ordination, may be literary rather than historical.

*Read at:* [3.6](lessons/03-06-john-chrysostom.md)

### The Judaizing homilies

Eight homilies preached at Antioch in 386-387, known in Latin as *Adversus Judaeos*. **What they say has to be said plainly, and their later use named, before any setting is offered.**

They are sustained invective, in language of contempt that runs well past the Christians he is trying to keep out of the synagogue: the synagogue as a place of demons, Jewish worship as something worse than pagan, the Jews as a people whose covenant is finished and whose sufferings since 70 are their punishment. **The abuse is the argument's main instrument, not decoration on it.** Later use: because they carry the authority of a saint and Doctor they were quarried by later writers building a case against Jews, and in the twentieth century the Nazi party excerpted and reprinted them (Walter Laqueur, *The Changing Face of Antisemitism*, 2006; Steven Katz makes the stronger claim that they helped shape it). *Nostra Aetate* 4 (1965) rejected the charge that the Jews as a people are collectively guilty of the death of Christ. Setting: the audience was his own congregation — baptized Christians at Antioch were attending synagogue on sabbaths and festivals, keeping the fasts and swearing oaths there — and the Benedictine editor Montfaucon noted long ago that the Latin title misdescribes the target; the register belongs to [*psogos*](#psogos). **The setting explains the occasion and the register. It softens no sentence, and it does not touch the fact that the homilies generalize far beyond the Judaizers to the Jews as such** — which is what made them reusable fifteen centuries later. Justin's *Dialogue* 16 is the same quarry a century and a half earlier.

*Read at:* [3.6](lessons/03-06-john-chrysostom.md); Justin at [1.4](lessons/01-04-justin-martyr.md)

### The Vulgate

*Vulgata*, the common edition: **a later name for a later composite, and not all of it is Jerome's.**

What is in it: his revision of the Latin gospels against the Greek (c. 382-385); his Old Testament from the Hebrew (c. 391-404); the **Old Latin** for the books he declined to translate; and the Greek-based **Gallican Psalter**, his earlier correction against the Greek, not his *iuxta Hebraeos* version, which never entered liturgical use. Trent's decree that the Vulgate is "authentic" is a **juridical ruling about which Latin edition to argue from**, not a claim that Jerome's Latin is inspired ([`fundamental-theology` 3.3](../fundamental-theology/lessons/03-03-the-canon-as-defined.md)).

*Read at:* [4.2](lessons/04-02-jerome.md)

### Against Helvidius

Jerome's tract of about 383 against a Roman who argued from Matthew 1 that Mary had other children. **The argument is lexical throughout.**

"Knew her not *till* she brought forth" settles nothing about what came after, because *till* in scriptural usage often marks a limit without implying a reversal beyond it; "first-born" is a legal and cultic term for the one who opens the womb, not a claim that others followed; and in Scripture there are **four kinds of brethren** — by nature, race, kindred and love (ch. 16) — so "brother" cannot decide the question by itself. He also holds that Joseph was Mary's husband putatively and that virginity is the higher state. **Jerome did not invent the belief; he supplied the exegetical defence the West then used.** The doctrine, and the assent it requires, belong to [`ecclesiology-and-mariology`](../ecclesiology-and-mariology/syllabus.md) read with [`fundamental-theology` 4.4](../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-04-the-ladder-of-doctrinal-authority.md).

*Read at:* [4.2](lessons/04-02-jerome.md)

### The Oea incident

A North African bishop had Jerome's new version of Jonah read in church, and at 4:6 the plant that shaded the prophet was no longer the gourd the congregation had heard all their lives.

Augustine reports the result (Letter 71.5): an uproar, loudest among the Greek-speakers; the bishop forced to consult the town's Jewish residents, who said the Greek and the old Latin were right; the new reading corrected back. Jerome's reply is pure philology (Letter 75.7): the Hebrew word is *ciceion*, the Septuagint made it a gourd and Aquila and the others ivy, and the plant is neither but a shrub that shoots up to the height of a small tree on its own stem. **Neither man is wrong about his own subject.** Jerome is right about the Hebrew; Augustine is right that a Bible read in church is a public text and that changing what a congregation prays is a pastoral act — which is why in the same letter he asks Jerome to give the Latins an accurate version of *the Septuagint* instead.

*Read at:* [4.2](lessons/04-02-jerome.md)

### Jerome and Augustine on Galatians 2

The decade-long dispute over Paul's rebuke of Peter at Antioch — **two Fathers, both later Doctors, publicly and sharply at odds.**

Jerome, following Origen and, he says, every commentator after him, reads the scene as *dispensatio*: Peter's withdrawal and Paul's protest were both staged for the audience, and the motive was to silence the pagan critic Porphyry, who used the passage to show the apostles squabbling. This defends no lie, he insists; it teaches the honourable exercise of a wise discretion (Letters 75.6, 75.11). Augustine will not have it: if Paul reported as real a rebuke that was not real, Scripture contains a useful falsehood, and once you admit one there is no principled place to stop — the same move would excuse the gospel history about Peter's denial, or David's adultery (Letter 82.5). **The tradition followed Augustine.** The exchange ran for over a decade, in letters that went astray or circulated before they arrived, and it hurt. **A test case for claimed patristic consensus:** a consensus is something you test, not something you assume.

*Read at:* [4.2](lessons/04-02-jerome.md)

### Ignorance of the Scriptures

*Ignoratio enim Scripturarum ignoratio Christi est* — "ignorance of the Scriptures is ignorance of Christ."

**Genuine**, from the prologue to Jerome's *Commentary on Isaiah* (PL 24, 17); quoted at Vatican II, *Dei Verbum* 25. 4.2 gives its own translation of the Latin. **What it is not:** a ruling on canon, on translation, or on who may read what. Where it stands it is an exhortation to study introducing a commentary. A genuine tag still has to be read in its place — the discipline that the garbled ones make obvious, applied to one that is not garbled.

### The coercion letters

Letters **93** (408, to Vincentius) and **185** (to Boniface, circulated as *On the Correction of the Donatists*): Augustine's case that the state should coerce religious dissenters, read as it stands.

Letter 93 states a change of mind and the reason: "originally my opinion was, that no one should be coerced into the unity of Christ ... But this opinion of mine was overcome not by words ... but by the conclusive instances to which they could point" (ch. 5 §17, NPNF, J. G. Cunningham). The instances are **results** — his own town came over under fear of the imperial edicts and now detests the schism. Fear, he argues, does not produce faith, but it breaks the grip of habit so that a man will examine what he had not examined. The measures in view are fines, confiscation of meeting places, exile for clergy and the lash. Letter 185 supplies the scriptural case: Luke 14:23, "compel them to come in" (§24); suffering settles nothing by itself, since Hagar suffered from Sarah and two thieves were crucified beside Christ, so "the punishment of the impious may be the same; but the cause of the martyrs is always different" (§9); hence "there is a persecution of unrighteousness ... and there is a righteous persecution, which the Church of Christ inflicts upon the impious ... she persecutes in the spirit of love, they in the spirit of wrath" (§11, NPNF, J. R. King). **Its use:** Gratian's *Decretum* gathered these texts, and Aquinas at *ST* II-II q.10 a.8 and ad 1 rules that Jews and pagans may not be compelled to believe while heretics and apostates "should be submitted even to bodily compulsion", quoting Letter 93's change of mind almost word for word and rereading the parable of the tares to allow what Augustine had used it to forbid. Pierre Bayle set out to demolish it in the *Philosophical Commentary* of 1686. **The rest of the record:** in 412, writing about Circumcellions who had confessed to murder, Augustine begged the commissioner not to execute or mutilate them, praising him for extracting confessions "not by stretching them on the rack ... but by beating them with rods" (Letter 133) — clemency that still takes flogging for granted. **A Father's opinion, never a definition.** The Church's teaching on religious freedom is *Dignitatis Humanae* (1965), owned by [`catholic-social-teaching`](../catholic-social-teaching/syllabus.md) 5.2. **Boss 4(d) evaluates it; this entry reports it.**

*Read at:* [4.4](lessons/04-04-augustine-against-the-donatists.md)

### The Twelve Anathemas

Cyril's ultimatum appended to his Third Letter to Nestorius, sent from an Alexandrian synod in 430.

**What carries which weight.** The **Second** Letter was read at the first session of Ephesus in 431 and approved by the bishops one by one, and read again at Chalcedon in 451 and acclaimed together with Cyril's peace letter to John of Antioch. The **Third** Letter with the Twelve Anathemas was read into the acts at Ephesus, but the acts record no discussion or vote on the anathemas — Percival's NPNF note observes that any discussion appears to have dropped out of the record — and Chalcedon did not read it. **So citing the anathemas as though a council had defined them overstates their level.** They were also read as Apollinarian by John of Antioch's party at the time, a charge Cyril spent years answering. Levels: [`fundamental-theology` 4.4](../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-04-the-ladder-of-doctrinal-authority.md); the doctrine as defined is [`christology`](../christology/syllabus.md)'s.

### Formula of Reunion

The Antiochene confession of **433** that Cyril accepted: perfect God and perfect man, "a union of two natures," one Christ, one Son, one Lord, and *Theotokos* with the Antiochene gloss attached.

Its evidential value is exactly that Cyril signed it: **he could say what Chalcedon would later say.** Which is why the churches that refused Chalcedon had to take "one incarnate nature" rather than this as the test of his real mind.

*Read at:* [5.1](lessons/05-01-cyril-of-alexandria.md)

### Tome of Leo

Leo's **Letter 28 to Flavian, 449**, written after Flavian had condemned Eutyches and Eutyches had appealed to Rome.

The argument (chapter numbers as printed in NPNF 2nd ser. vol. 12, Feltoe): start from the creed, not a technical term — Eutyches has not grasped the rudiments every candidate for baptism professes, which already names two births (ch. 1); the eternal birth and the birth in time are two, and the second adds nothing to the first (ch. 2); the humanity is really ours, taken from Mary's body, against the idea of the form of a man without the reality of his mother's body (ch. 2); one person, and neither nature loses what makes it that nature (ch. 3); so the acts fall into two kinds though one person does them all — "each form does what is proper to it with the co-operation of the other" (ch. 4), Latin *agit enim utraque forma cum alterius communione quod proprium est*; and salvation requires a death that is real and a death that cannot hold (ch. 3). Chapter 6 adds the terms: if Eutyches recants in his own hand he is received back. **It is a pastoral judgment on a case, not a treatise — and it is not the Chalcedonian definition.** Only the definition is defined dogma; the *Tome*'s authority in Catholic theology comes from the council's reception of it, not the other way round. The definition does borrow Leo's clause almost word for word: "the peculiar property of each nature being preserved" is *salva proprietate utriusque naturae*.

*Read at:* [5.2](lessons/05-02-leo-the-great.md)

### Reception of the Tome at Chalcedon

What the acts of the **second session, October 451**, actually record.

**Cyril's letters are read first, then the *Tome*.** The bishops acclaim it — "Peter has spoken thus through Leo" — and in the same breath that "Leo and Cyril taught the same thing". The Illyrian bishops ask for explanations, a delay of some days is requested, and the session breaks up. **Individual subscription comes at a later session, with a committee under Anatolius checking the *Tome* against Cyril's writings in between.** So the record shows the *Tome* received **after being measured against Cyril**. On what ground it was received — because Peter had spoken, or because it matched Cyril — the acts support both readings, and arguing that out belongs to [`apologetics-catholic-claims`](../apologetics-catholic-claims/syllabus.md). **Acclamations are not doctrinal acts:** they are rhythmic shouts recorded by notaries in an edited record.

*Read at:* [5.2](lessons/05-02-leo-the-great.md); the council as an event is [`church-history` 2.2](../church-history/lessons/02-02-ephesus-chalcedon-and-leos-rome.md)

### Pastoral Rule

Gregory the Great's handbook for bishops, c. 591, addressed to John of Ravenna (NPNF 2nd ser., Barmby).

**Book I:** the care of souls as "the art of arts", harder than medicine, because "the sores of the thoughts of men are more occult than the sores of the bowels" — nobody untrained dares practise on bodies, yet men with no knowledge of spiritual precepts cheerfully call themselves physicians of the heart. **Book II:** the pastor's own life — pure in thought, discreet in silence, useful in speech, neighbour to all in compassion and above all in contemplation — because character is the diagnostic instrument. **Book III:** thirty-six paired opposites, each with its remedy, usually crossed (the joyful hear about judgement, the sad about the kingdom), and then **the vice the remedy risks feeding** named — "the joyful have lechery close at hand, and the sad wrath." **Book IV:** the preacher's humility. Gregory names his source in the prologue to Book III: Gregory Nazianzen, "one and the same exhortation does not suit all". The four images that follow are all **dosage** images: nothing says the truth varies, only the quantity and the manner. A citation trap: Barmby's list at the head of III.1 prints thirty-five pairs while the body treats thirty-six (young and old, Admonition 2, is in the body only). The same instinct scaled to a nation in the letter to Mellitus of July 601 — do not destroy the English temples, only the idols in them — "it is undoubtedly impossible to cut away everything at once from hard hearts, since one who strives to ascend to the highest place must needs rise by steps or paces, and not by leaps" (Letter XI.76 in Barmby; also in Bede, *HE* I.30). **The strain:** a month earlier he had written to King Æthelberht urging him to overthrow the temple structures (Bede I.32). Either he changed his mind within weeks, or he was telling a king to lead from the front while telling missionaries to go gently, which is what "according to the quality of the hearers" would prescribe. **The letters do not say.**

*Read at:* [5.3](lessons/05-03-gregory-the-great.md)

## Contested passages

The passages Catholic, Orthodox and Protestant readers each claim. **Each entry gives every tradition's reading and the words it leans on, and stops there.** No adjudication: what these texts *prove* is argued in [`apologetics-catholic-claims`](../apologetics-catholic-claims/syllabus.md), and the doctrines belong to [`ecclesiology-and-mariology`](../ecclesiology-and-mariology/syllabus.md) and [`sacraments-and-liturgy`](../sacraments-and-liturgy/syllabus.md). ### The Didache's thanksgiving prayers

Are Didache 9-10 the Eucharist? **The course's first genuinely open question**, and the way to run it is to sort the text into present and absent.

*Present:* thanksgiving, *eucharistia*, as the name of the thing; a fixed order of cup and bread; a rule excluding the unbaptized, hard to explain for an ordinary supper; and in ch. 14 bread broken with thanksgiving on the Lord's day, after confession, called a sacrifice. *Absent:* any institution narrative, any mention of the body and blood or of the Lord's death, any word said over the elements; the order is cup before bread, unlike 1 Corinthians 11 and the Synoptics; and ch. 10 opens after the participants are filled, which sounds like a full meal.

**Catholic and Orthodox readers** usually take 9-10 with 14 and read the whole as eucharistic, leaning on the sacrifice language, the confession and the exclusion of the unbaptized. **Protestant readers** more often keep 9-10 as table prayers, leaning on the missing institution words and ch. 10's full meal. A third reading: meal and Eucharist were not yet separated. **The argument is less about what the words mean than about which chapter interprets which** — naming that is the exegesis.

*Read at:* [1.2](lessons/01-02-first-clement-and-the-didache.md)

### Ignatius on the bishop

The same thing said to three churches: *Magnesians* 6, *Trallians* 3, *Smyrnaeans* 8.

The words: the bishop "presides in the place of God" and the presbyters "in the place of the assembly of the apostles"; reverence the deacons as an appointment of Jesus Christ, and apart from these there is no Church; follow the bishop as Jesus Christ follows the Father, do nothing connected with the Church without the bishop, and a proper Eucharist is one the bishop holds or entrusts. **The limits are as much part of the reading as the passages.** He writes to churches in western Asia Minor; his letter to Rome greets no bishop there and names none; his warrant is theological analogy, not an apostolic command he can cite; and every passage is an imperative — **nobody exhorts at this pitch about what everyone already does.** *Smyrnaeans* 8 also carries the first "Catholic Church": the adjective means whole or universal, set against the local assembly round its bishop in the clause before, and reading the later denominational sense back into 110 is a plain anachronism. **Before citing the passage you must establish which recension you are quoting**, because the fourth-century interpolator worked precisely on sentences about the bishop, the Trinity and Christ's person. **Boss 1 territory.**

*Read at:* [1.3](lessons/01-03-ignatius-of-antioch.md)

### The Romans preface

Ignatius greets the Roman church with two clauses built on the same verb: it "presides in the place of the region of the Romans", and it is the church "which presides over love" (ANF; Lightfoot renders both with "presidency").

The Greek behind the second is a participle governing the genitive *agape*. Three readings, with the words each leans on.

| Reading | Leans on |
|---|---|
| A primacy over the whole Church: *agape* names the communion of churches joined in love, so the second clause says something the first does not | The contrast between the clauses — why say it twice, unless the second is wider? |
| A primacy of love and witness, not of jurisdiction (the Orthodox theologian **Nicholas Afanasiev**): *agape* is the eucharistic assembly, and Rome presides among equal churches as the one whose charity and martyrs are exemplary | Ignatius's eucharistic use of the word, and the letter's contents, which beg Rome for prayers and for silence and ask for no ruling |
| Preeminence in charity, or in the love-feast | The ordinary sense of *agape* in the period, and the first clause, which localizes the presidency geographically and so fixes the scope of both |

**The passage will not decide between them:** a genitive carries the weight, with little context to steady it.

*Read at:* [1.3](lessons/01-03-ignatius-of-antioch.md)

### The Rome sentence

*Against Heresies* 3.3.2. **The Greek of Book 3 is lost**; what survives is an early, painfully literal Latin: *Ad hanc enim ecclesiam propter potiorem principalitatem necesse est omnem convenire ecclesiam*.

ANF renders it "For it is a matter of necessity that every Church should agree with this Church, on account of its pre-eminent authority" — and admits in a footnote it is far from sure. The same edition prints a Catholic alternative from Berington and Kirk: "For to this Church, on account of more potent principality, it is necessary that every Church ... resort." **Three words carry the difference.** *Convenire*: "agree with" or "come together at, resort to". *Potiorem principalitatem*: pre-eminent authority or a more powerful origin, and it is not certain whose — Rome's, or the apostles' who founded it. And the following "in which" may locate the preserved tradition in Rome or in the churches that resort there. **Catholic readers** take it as an early witness that communion with the Roman church is the test of apostolic faith; **Orthodox and Protestant readers** as a statement about Rome's prestige as a meeting-point, a mirror gathering everyone's witness rather than a source of its own — how the ANF's Episcopalian editor read it in a bracketed note. **Nothing here is decided.** When the original is lost and the translation ambiguous, the honest report is the ambiguity, stated precisely, with the words named. **Boss 2(b) territory.**

*Read at:* [2.1](lessons/02-01-irenaeus-against-the-gnostics.md)

### Irenaeus on the Eucharist

*Against Heresies* 4.18.5 and 5.2.3, argued from a doctrine of the flesh rather than from a text.

The bread, when it receives the invocation of God, "is no longer common bread, but the Eucharist, consisting of two realities, earthly and heavenly" — and then our bodies, nourished by it, are no longer corruptible; the same inference runs the other way at 5.2.3, bodies "deposited in the earth, and suffering decomposition there, shall rise at their appointed time". **The logic is a loop and he says so:** "Our opinion is in accordance with the Eucharist, and the Eucharist in turn establishes our opinion" (4.18.5). **Catholic and Orthodox readers** find an early witness to a real change in the elements and to the Eucharist as offering, leaning on "no longer common bread". **Many Protestant readers** grant a strong realism of presence but deny that "two realities, earthly and heavenly" settles anything about substance or sacrifice, and note that Irenaeus's interest is the destiny of the body, not a theory of the elements.

*Read at:* [2.2](lessons/02-02-irenaeus-recapitulation.md)

### Cyprian's chapter 4 in two versions

*On the Unity of the Catholic Church* ch. 4, the Peter passage, **survives in two versions.**

One — the **Primacy Text** — speaks of a primacy given to Peter and of "the chair of Peter" on which the Church is founded. The **received text**, which Schaff's ANF prints, has neither phrase and speaks of resisting "the Church" where the first has deserting Peter's chair. **Both contain the sentence about the other apostles being Peter's equals** — "the rest of the apostles were also the same as was Peter, endowed with a like partnership both of honour and power" (ANF, Wallis). The leading account is **Maurice Bévenot**'s (*St Cyprian's De Unitate Chap. 4 in the Light of the Manuscripts*, 1938; critical edition 1971): both are Cyprian's own, the Primacy Text is the earlier, and he revised it when its wording proved open to a use he had not intended. Scholars differ over when and why, and the reconstruction has never been unanimous. **The rule that is settled enough to act on: when you cite chapter 4, say which version you are quoting and in whose edition.** You cannot drop "the chair of Peter" into an argument unannotated. **Boss 2(c) sets the full comparison; this entry does not make it.** Note also that Cyprian's letters are quoted on both sides of the primacy question, that "bishop of bishops" names nobody, and that the two texts of ch. 4 sit on opposite sides.

*Read at:* [2.4](lessons/02-04-cyprian-of-carthage.md)

### Unwritten tradition in Basil

*On the Holy Spirit* ch. 27, written because opponents demanded a written warrant for "with".

Basil inventories what every church did that no text commands: signing with the cross, turning east to pray, the words of invocation over the bread and the cup, blessing the font and the chrism, triple immersion, renouncing Satan, standing to pray on Sundays and through Pentecost. Of beliefs and practices, he says, some come from written teaching and others "we have received delivered to us in a mystery by the tradition of the apostles; and both of these in relation to true religion have the same force." **Catholic and Orthodox readers** take that at face value: Tradition transmits content alongside Scripture. **Protestant readers** answer that every example is a rite, never a doctrine, and that his case for the Spirit runs from Scripture — so the chapter defends a liturgical word, not a second source of revelation. **Both must reckon with the *dogma*/*kerygma* distinction in the same chapter**, where *dogma* is the unspoken content of the rites, nearly the opposite of the modern sense. Tradition as a doctrine is [`fundamental-theology` 3.4](../fundamental-theology/lessons/03-04-tradition.md). **Boss 3(b) and 3(c) territory** — the list is here, the reading is not worked.

*Read at:* [3.2](lessons/03-02-basil-the-great.md)

### Ambrose on the change of the elements

*On the Mysteries* ch. 9 (NPNF 2nd ser. vol. 10, de Romestin, de Romestin and Duckworth): an *a fortiori* from scriptural wonders worked by a prophet's blessing to the word of Christ.

The argument: the objection is granted in the objector's own terms, "I see something else" (9.50); Scripture records blessings that changed what things were — Moses' rod, the Nile, the sea, Jordan, the rock, bitter Marah, floating iron (9.51); all of those were a man's blessing, so they are the weaker cases; therefore the word of Christ, "which was able to make out of nothing that which was not," can change "things which already are into what they were not" (9.52); the words are Christ's own (9.54); **and the case that controls the analogy is the Incarnation** — "this body which we make is that which was born of the Virgin" (9.53). **9.53 is the load-bearing section**, because every miracle in the list is a change you can *see*, and on the altar nothing visible happens: the virgin birth gives him a precedent for a real change under an unchanged appearance.

| Reader | Takes ch. 9 to say | Leans on |
|---|---|---|
| **Catholic** | Christ's words change what the elements are; the change is real and hidden, as in the Incarnation. A pre-technical witness to what Trent (Session XIII, 1551) defined as transubstantiation — **dogma because Trent defined it, not because Ambrose wrote it** | "by blessing nature itself is changed"; "change things which already are into what they were not"; 9.53 |
| **Lutheran** | The word of Christ makes his body truly present with the bread; Ambrose's own analogies leave the creature standing | "made what it is by the word of Christ"; the rod, the river and the iron, none of which are annihilated |
| **Reformed** | A change of signification and use, received spiritually by faith | 9.54, "after the consecration the Body is signified"; 9.58, "not bodily food but spiritual" |

**There is no substance/appearance vocabulary in the chapter at all** — Ambrose's operative word is "nature", and he uses it of rods, rivers and iron. **The honest description is that the chapter will not settle the question in its own terms, because he has no terms for it:** he asserts a change and offers no account of what survives it. Reading him as an anticipation of a thirteenth-century distinction is an anachronism; reading him as a mere symbolist flattens "nature itself is changed."

*Read at:* [4.1](lessons/04-01-ambrose-of-milan.md)

### Leo's Petrine sermons

Sermons 1-5, preached on the anniversary of Leo's consecration (29 September 440), translated by Charles Lett Feltoe (NPNF 2nd ser. vol. 12, 1895).

From Sermon 3, sections 3-4: "if anything is rightly done and rightly decreed by us ... it is of his work and merits whose power lives and whose authority prevails in his See"; "in my humble person he may be recognized and honoured, in whom abides the care of all the shepherds ... whose dignity is not abated even in so unworthy an heir"; "him whom they know to be not only the patron of this see, but also the primate of all bishops"; "believe that he is speaking whose representative we are." **The "he" throughout is Peter, and the redirection is what carries the claim:** every honour offered to Leo is sent on to Peter. **Two facts about the genre matter to any use of them:** the occasion is a liturgy in Leo's own honour, and the genre is a bishop's *natalis* sermon, **not a decree**. Catholic and Orthodox readings are both named in 5.2; a pope's sermon is a Father's teaching, and Leo's Doctorate endorses no particular opinion of his.

*Read at:* [5.2](lessons/05-02-leo-the-great.md)

### The title universal bishop

Gregory the Great's letters of 595-602 against John the Faster's title "ecumenical" or "universal" (NPNF Letters V.18, V.20, V.43, VII.33).

| | Catholic reading | Orthodox and Protestant reading |
|---|---|---|
| **What Gregory refuses** | A title that would make one bishop *the* bishop and so unchurch the rest — a claim about the episcopate, not about Rome's authority | Any claim by any see, Rome included, to be bishop of the whole Church |
| **Words it leans on** | Chalcedon "offered" the name to the popes and none "wished to be called by such a title" (V.18): a title Rome could have had and declined. In V.43 he approves his predecessor Pelagius II annulling John's synod "by the authority of the holy apostle Peter" — in the same letter that condemns the title | Peter himself "is not called the universal apostle" though "the care and principality of the whole Church is committed to him" (V.20). Whoever "desires to be called, Universal Priest, is in his elation the precursor of Antichrist" (VII.33) — a sentence later Protestant polemic turned back on Rome |
| **What it must explain** | Why Gregory says the honour was *offered* to Rome and refused, rather than that it belongs to Rome | Why a pope who denies that any bishop is universal annuls an Eastern synod by Peter's authority, confirms and rebukes patriarchs, and is obeyed |

**Both read the same letters honestly; the disagreement is over which sentence is load-bearing, and re-reading alone does not settle that** — which is the lesson. They are letters of one pope in one quarrel, not a definition. **Boss 5(c) territory.**

*Read at:* [5.3](lessons/05-03-gregory-the-great.md)

### The rock of Matthew 16:18

**Boss 5(d)'s question, and the card holds only its shape.**

The question is whether there is a "unanimous consent of the Fathers", in the sense of Trent Session IV, on the meaning of "rock". Working it means gathering patristic readings from across the course, classifying them, saying what "unanimous" must mean for the criterion to be usable — numerical, moral, on the literal sense, or on the doctrine — and stating what follows if the readings diverge while all are compatible with one doctrine. One datum the syllabus names: Augustine's own remark in the *Retractations* that he had explained the verse two ways and left the reader to choose. **The card deliberately does not assemble the readings; that is the problem.** The tools are [Unanimous consent of the Fathers](#unanimous-consent-of-the-fathers) and [How to check a patristic quotation](#how-to-check-a-patristic-quotation), and the standing observation that the record's normal state is divergence on the letter with convergence on the doctrine. The argument over primacy itself is [`apologetics-catholic-claims`](../apologetics-catholic-claims/syllabus.md)'s.

## Spurious and garbled quotations

Two kinds of trouble: **whole works under the wrong name**, and **real sentences that circulate altered**. Both are step-two and step-four failures of [the check](#how-to-check-a-patristic-quotation). A spurious work is not worthless — 2 Clement is first-rate evidence for mid-second-century preaching — and the error is not reading it but **citing it under a name that buys authority it never earned.**

|---|---|---|

| "The blood of the martyrs is the seed of the Church" | Tertullian | *Apology* 50: "the blood of Christians is seed". See [The blood of the martyrs](#the-blood-of-the-martyrs) |

### The absurdity tag

**Tertullian did not write "credo quia absurdum."**

What he wrote is *On the Flesh of Christ* 5 (ANF vol. 3, Holmes), **two impersonal clauses, neither in the first person**: of the death, "it is by all means to be believed, because it is absurd" (*prorsus credibile est, quia ineptum est*); of the burial and rising, "the fact is certain, because it is impossible" (*certum est, quia impossibile*). The verbs matter: *credibile est* is "it is to be believed", not *credo*; *ineptum* is "unfitting, silly", and Holmes's "absurd" is most of the tag's English afterlife. **The context is an argument against Marcion's docetism**: a phantom Christ empties the crucifixion, death and resurrection — "falsely, therefore, is our faith also" — and three sentences later Tertullian asks how any of it could be true "in Him, if He was not Himself true." The paradox is a move inside an argument, not a substitute for one. **Why the change matters:** the tag is almost always produced as evidence that Christian faith is proudly irrational, the position called fideism, which the Church has condemned ([`fundamental-theology` 2.3](../fundamental-theology/lessons/02-03-fideism-and-rationalism.md)). A misquotation is being used to saddle the tradition with a doctrine it rejects.

*Read at:* [2.3](lessons/02-03-tertullian.md)

### The blood of the martyrs

*Apology* 50 (ANF, Thelwall) reads: "The oftener we are mown down by you, the more in number we grow; the blood of Christians is seed" (*semen est sanguis Christianorum*).

**A paraphrase, not a quotation.** The original has neither "martyrs" nor "Church": the subject is Christians under judicial killing, and what grows is more of them.

*Read at:* [2.3](lessons/02-03-tertullian.md)

### Athens and Jerusalem

*Prescription* 7 (ANF, Holmes). The question closes a **list of parentages**: the aeons from Plato's school through Valentinus, Marcion's better god from the Stoics, the soul's death from Epicurus.

Quoted as a general ban on thinking, it converts a genealogy of specific heresies into something the next four chapters refute: in chs. 8-12 Tertullian takes the heretics' favourite verse, "seek and you shall find," and analyses it under three heads — the thing sought, the time, the manner — to show that seeking has a term. **He is hostile to *curiositas*, speculation without a stopping rule, not to argument.** This is the row that turns him into a cartoon.

*Read at:* [2.3](lessons/02-03-tertullian.md)

### Roma locuta, causa finita

**Augustine never says "Rome has spoken."**

The tag is fastened to the close of **Sermon 131.10**, preached at Carthage and usually dated 23 September 417 (PL 38; not in Schaff's sermon selection, so 4.5 gives its own translation): *Iam enim de hac causa duo concilia missa sunt ad Sedem Apostolicam: inde etiam rescripta venerunt. Causa finita est: utinam aliquando finiatur error!* — "For on this matter two councils have now been sent to the Apostolic See; and from there replies have come back too. The case is finished — would that the error were finished some day!" **What the slogan drops:** the two African councils (Carthage and Milevis, 416), the exchange of documents with Innocent I, and the closing prayer. **And it hides that he was wrong:** Zosimus reopened the case within weeks, and it closed only with the council of Carthage of 1 May 418 and Zosimus's *Epistula Tractoria*. **Boss 4(c) reads the passage; this entry supplies the text and stops.**

*Read at:* [4.5](lessons/04-05-augustine-against-the-pelagians.md)

### Ambrosiaster

An anonymous Latin commentary on the thirteen Pauline epistles, written at Rome in the time of Damasus (366-384), **transmitted inside Ambrose's works and read as his for a thousand years.**

The 1907 *Catholic Encyclopedia* states flatly that Ambrose "is not the author." The name — the would-be Ambrose — goes back to **Erasmus**, who rejected the attribution; the author remains unidentified. **Worse for anyone who trusts old attributions:** Augustine quotes its comment on Romans 5:12, "wherein, that is, in Adam, all have sinned", and ascribes it to "the sainted Hilary" (*Against Two Letters of the Pelagians* 4.7, NPNF 1st ser. vol. 5). So a sentence that matters enormously for the Pelagian controversy has circulated under **two** wrong names.

*Read at:* [4.1](lessons/04-01-ambrose-of-milan.md); it bears on [4.5](lessons/04-05-augustine-against-the-pelagians.md)

### The Greek Ephrem

A very large corpus circulated under Ephrem's name **in Greek**, most of it not translated from his Syriac and most of it not his.

Before quoting, ask three questions: Syriac or Greek in origin; which edition and translation; does the hymn number match that edition? **Numbering differs** between the Roman edition of 1732-46, the nineteenth-century editions of Morris, Gwynn and Lamy, and the modern critical Syriac editions — so a bare "Hymn 4" is not a citation. Benedict XV's 1920 encyclical conferring the Doctorate quotes works of varying authenticity in its own footnotes.

*Read at:* [3.4](lessons/03-04-ephrem-and-syriac-christianity.md)

### Liturgy of St John Chrysostom

The standard Byzantine eucharistic liturgy, **named for Chrysostom by tradition.**

The received text is later; he at most modified an older rite (*Catholic Encyclopedia*, 1910). Quoting "the Liturgy of St John Chrysostom" as evidence of *his* eucharistic theology is the attribution error in a very common disguise.

## Authenticity and transmission

Recensions, translations that adjust their originals, single-manuscript texts and disputed attributions — and what each does to citing the text.

### Migne and Schaff

How the corpus reaches you, and why the edition has to be named.

**Migne's** *Patrologia Latina* runs to 217 volumes (1844-55), covering Latin writers down to Innocent III; his *Patrologia Graeca*, Greek with facing Latin, to 166 (1857-66). He was fast and cheap, reprinting whatever earlier edition came to hand, **misattributions included**. **Schaff's** English sets are this course's default and what you will actually read: the *Ante-Nicene Fathers* (ten volumes) and the *Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers* (two series of fourteen), all public domain. Since then the critical series — **CSEL, GCS, CCSL, *Sources Chretiennes*** — have re-edited most of the corpus from the manuscripts, often differing from Migne. Two consequences: when a single word decides your reading, name the edition and check whether a critical text has moved it; and **Migne prints spurious works beside genuine ones under the same name.** One further caution: Schaff's *text* is the default translation here, and the nineteenth-century apparatus around it is not — the ANF prooemium to the Seventh Council of Carthage dates it to 258, and it met on 1 September **256**.

*Introduced:* [1.1](lessons/01-01-how-to-read-a-church-father.md); the apparatus warning is [2.4](lessons/02-04-cyprian-of-carthage.md)

### The Jerusalem Codex

The eleventh-century Greek manuscript found in 1873 by **Philotheos Bryennios**, a Greek Orthodox metropolitan, in the patriarchal library at Constantinople.

**The sole complete witness to the Didache** (published 1883) and the source of the complete 1 Clement (published 1875). Until then the Didache was a title in ancient book-lists and 1 Clement a text with holes in it, read from the damaged fifth-century Codex Alexandrinus alone. **The standing example of single-witness transmission.**

*Read at:* [1.2](lessons/01-02-first-clement-and-the-didache.md)

### Paris gr. 450

The single manuscript, finished **11 September 1364**, carrying all three genuine works of Justin; the only other witness, Claromontanus 82 (1571), is a copy of it.

It has real gaps: most of *Second Apology* 2, restored from Eusebius (*HE* IV.17); the *Dialogue*'s dedicatory letter to Marcus Pompeius; a large stretch at *Dialogue* 74 (*Catholic Encyclopedia*, 1910). Its order is *Second Apology*, *First Apology*, *Dialogue*; **Dom Maran reordered them in 1742** and everyone has followed, and Eusebius reports two Apologies to two emperors, with many scholars reading our "Second" as an appendix to the First. **So "Second Apology 13" cites a numbering imposed by an eighteenth-century Maurist on a single fourteenth-century copy.** That does not make the text unreliable; it means your certainty is the certainty of one line of transmission, and you should say so. **Justin is the course's cleanest illustration that a text can be securely authentic and still hang by one thread.**

*Read at:* [1.4](lessons/01-04-justin-martyr.md)

### The three recensions of Ignatius

| Recension | Contents | Date of the text | How it reaches us | Good for |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Short (Syriac)** | 3 letters, abridged: Ephesians, Romans, Polycarp | an abridgement, made later | Cureton's Nitrian manuscripts, 1843 | a Syriac abridgement; not the original |
| **Middle** | the 7 Eusebius names | the letters themselves; early second century on the majority view | Greek from the Medicean codex, which lacks *Romans*; *Romans* separately, attached to an account of the martyrdom; Latin in Grosseteste's version | **the text to quote** |
| **Long** | the 7, interpolated, plus 6 forged letters | late fourth century; often identified with the compiler of the *Apostolic Constitutions* | Greek and Latin manuscripts, and the old printed editions | fourth-century Syria, never Ignatius |

Untangling it took two centuries: **Ussher** (1644) found and published a Latin version made about 1250 by Robert Grosseteste of a shorter, unexpanded text; **Isaac Voss** (1646) printed the Greek of that same short form from the Medicean codex at Florence; Pearson's *Vindiciae Ignatianae* (1672) defended it; **Cureton** (1843) then found Syriac manuscripts with only three letters and argued *these* were original, and by the end of the century **Lightfoot**, Zahn and Harnack had shown the Syriac to be an abridgement. Two external checks: Polycarp, writing to Philippi soon after, says he is forwarding the letters of Ignatius he has (Eusebius, *HE* III.36.13-15), and Irenaeus about 180 quotes the wheat-and-bread sentence as a saying of one of their own, without the name (*AH* V.28.4). **Two traps in the long recension.** The six extra letters are easy to catch — they are not on Eusebius's list. **The interpolations inside the genuine seven are not**, since they sit in a letter with the right name on it, and they cluster on the bishop, the Trinity and Christ's person: exactly the sentences later readers want. Older editions and much devotional material print the long recension. **Boss 1(b) asks why the recension question bites harder on this topic than on most.**

*Read at:* [1.3](lessons/01-03-ignatius-of-antioch.md)

### The transmission stack

Four pipes carry *On First Principles* to a modern reader, and **each bends in a known direction.**

| Pipe | Date | Bends |
|---|---|---|
| **Rufinus's Latin** — the only complete text | 398 | Toward **orthodoxy**. His own prologue: "If, therefore, we have found anywhere in his writings, any statement opposed to that view, which elsewhere in his works he had himself piously laid down regarding the Trinity, we have either omitted it, as being corrupt, and not the composition of Origen" (ANF vol. 4) |
| **Jerome's literal Latin** — lost, excerpted at length in Letter 124 to Avitus (NPNF 2nd ser. vol. 6) | c. 399 | Toward **heresy**: made to expose Rufinus, and summarizing rather than transcribing |
| **The *Philocalia*** — the only real Greek, covering III.1 and parts of IV | c. 360s | Selected as **edifying**, by two admirers (traditionally Basil and Gregory of Nazianzus) |
| **Sixth-century hostile dossiers**, e.g. Justinian's letter to Menas | 543 | Selected **to convict** |

Rufinus is not confessing a fraud: he believed, and argued in a separate tract, that heretics had corrupted Origen's books, so removing a heterodox sentence was restoring the text — and he notes that Jerome had already "smoothed and corrected" Origen's homilies. Jerome then spent years attacking him for doing it. **The rule: name the pipe before you quote Origen.** "Origen says" is a claim about a manuscript tradition, not just about a man. What this costs is almost nothing doctrinally and everything rhetorically: *that* Origen taught eternal generation is not in doubt, since defenders and accusers agree he did, but **no particular Latin sentence of Book I can be quoted as his wording.** Say "Origen, as Rufinus gives him" or "as Jerome reports him", and name which way that witness leans.

*Read at:* [2.5](lessons/02-05-clement-of-alexandria-and-origen.md); the Book IV Greek is used at [3.5](lessons/03-05-allegory-and-history-alexandria-and-antioch.md)

### Letter 38 attribution

The fullest statement of the general-and-particular distinction stands among **Basil's** letters, addressed "to his brother Gregory", and is widely argued to be **Gregory of Nyssa's**.

Since Reinhard Hübner's 1972 study much scholarship has taken that view, on the ground that its metaphysics is more worked out than anything securely Basil's; others defend the traditional ascription. **The question is open, so cite it as "Letter 38, attributed to Basil, possibly by Gregory of Nyssa" — never a flat "Basil says".**

*Read at:* [3.2](lessons/03-02-basil-the-great.md)

### On the Sacraments

*De Sacramentis*: six books, plainer than *On the Mysteries*, apparently taken down as spoken, on the same Milanese rites — and **containing the earliest Latin witness to a form of the Roman Canon of the Mass.**

Authenticity doubted from the sixteenth century; the 1907 *Catholic Encyclopedia* still reports Bardenhewer's view that it is *On the Mysteries* "indiscreetly published by some hearer of Ambrose." **The twentieth-century majority, after Faller and Botte, accepts it as a stenographic record of Ambrose's own catechesis.** Say which view you assume before citing it: whether it is evidence for Ambrose or for a listener turns on it.

*Read at:* [4.1](lessons/04-01-ambrose-of-milan.md)

### Ambrosian hymn

Iambic dimeter, eight four-line stanzas, sung antiphonally by a congregation split in two — **a genre before it is a corpus.**

Deployed during the Milan basilica crisis of 385-386 with the empress Justina; Augustine was there and saw it, recording that at that time, "after the manner of the Eastern Church, hymns and psalms should be sung" was instituted (*Confessions* 9.7.15, NPNF 1st ser. vol. 1). **"Ambrosian" soon named the metre**, so most hymns under the name are imitations: the 1907 *Catholic Encyclopedia* (Ambrosian Chant) reports fourteen judged certainly his and four more probable. *Aeterne rerum conditor* is normally counted genuine; the certainty is *Deus creator omnium*, which Augustine quotes from memory (*Confessions* 9.12.32).

*Read at:* [4.1](lessons/04-01-ambrose-of-milan.md)

### The Dialogues of Gregory

Four books of miracle stories, c. 593-594. **Book II is our principal life of St Benedict**; Book IV is an early Latin witness to prayer for the dead and purifying fire (doctrine: [`creation-grace-last-things`](../creation-grace-last-things/syllabus.md)).

**Francis Clark** argued in 1987 that the work is a late-seventh-century compilation built around genuine Gregorian fragments. **This is a minority view**; Paul Meyvaert, Adalbert de Vogüé and others answered it, and authenticity remains the majority position. **Cite it as "disputed by Clark, majority against him" — not "spurious", and not "undisputed".**

*Read at:* [5.3](lessons/05-03-gregory-the-great.md)

## Pitfalls

One line each, grouped by theme rather than by lesson. If two lessons warn about the same thing, it is one entry.

### Pitfalls of attribution

- **A sentence with a Father's name under it is not that Father's.** Step two of the check fails more often than beginners expect, and Migne prints the spurious beside the genuine under one name. *([1.1](lessons/01-01-how-to-read-a-church-father.md), [4.1](lessons/04-01-ambrose-of-milan.md))*
- **A spurious work is not worthless.** The error is not reading 2 Clement but citing it under a name that buys authority it never earned. *([1.1](lessons/01-01-how-to-read-a-church-father.md))*
- **"Everyone has always attributed this to X" is weak evidence about authorship** — Aphrahat's whole corpus lost its author's name for five centuries. *([3.4](lessons/03-04-ephrem-and-syriac-christianity.md))*
- **A striking sentence is not safe because everyone repeats it.** All three Tertullian tags are repeated constantly, by careful writers. Frequency is not verification. *([2.3](lessons/02-03-tertullian.md))*
- **"The Fathers say" is never a citation.** Work, book, chapter, section, recension, translation — or the sentence does no work. *([1.1](lessons/01-01-how-to-read-a-church-father.md))*
- **Quoting a treatise for a sentence it does not contain** is the same error in disguise: *On the Holy Spirit* never says "the Spirit is God", and *Against Praxeas* never says *tres personae*. *([3.2](lessons/03-02-basil-the-great.md), [2.3](lessons/02-03-tertullian.md))*
- **Even the commentary can have an attribution problem:** the scholia on Pseudo-Dionysius printed under Maximus's name are now largely assigned to John of Scythopolis. *([5.4](lessons/05-04-maximus-and-john-of-damascus.md))*

### Pitfalls of level and authority

- **A Father's opinion is never Church teaching**, and the ladder has no rung for it: rungs 1-3 record magisterial acts, rungs 4-5 grade theological inferences. Placing "Irenaeus taught it" anywhere on that ladder is an exegetical error. *([2.2](lessons/02-02-irenaeus-recapitulation.md), [`fundamental-theology` 4.4](../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-04-the-ladder-of-doctrinal-authority.md))*
- **A Doctor's title honours the man, not his opinions one at a time.** Irenaeus's millenarianism and Basil's twenty-four-hour days are exactly as binding after the declaration as before: not at all. *([1.1](lessons/01-01-how-to-read-a-church-father.md), [2.2](lessons/02-02-irenaeus-recapitulation.md), [3.4](lessons/03-04-ephrem-and-syriac-christianity.md), [4.1](lessons/04-01-ambrose-of-milan.md), [4.5](lessons/04-05-augustine-against-the-pelagians.md))*
- **Trent does not oblige you to find a patristic consensus; it forbids reading against one.** Promoting the restraint into a method is the commonest misuse of the clause. *([1.1](lessons/01-01-how-to-read-a-church-father.md))*
- **A conciliar acclamation is not a decree**, and an ultimatum read into the acts is not a definition. "Peter has spoken thus through Leo" is a shout in an edited record; the Twelve Anathemas were never voted on. *([5.2](lessons/05-02-leo-the-great.md), [5.1](lessons/05-01-cyril-of-alexandria.md))*
- **A letter of one pope in one quarrel is not a definition** — Gregory on "universal", and Leo's *natalis* sermons. *([5.3](lessons/05-03-gregory-the-great.md), [5.2](lessons/05-02-leo-the-great.md))*
- **A ruling received by a council is not the council's definition.** The *Tome* is Leo's judgment on the case of Eutyches; only Chalcedon's definition is defined dogma. *([5.2](lessons/05-02-leo-the-great.md))*
- **A Father's later standing does not automatically void his earlier testimony**, and sanctity does not validate a page. Origen died in communion; Cyprian was never condemned; Chrysostom is a saint and a Doctor. *([2.5](lessons/02-05-clement-of-alexandria-and-origen.md), [2.4](lessons/02-04-cyprian-of-carthage.md), [3.6](lessons/03-06-john-chrysostom.md))*

### Pitfalls of translation and text

- **Name the translation before you hang a claim on the English.** *Cum alterius communione* is "with the co-operation of the other" in Feltoe and "in communion with the other" elsewhere, and how strongly the two operations are bound is exactly what is at issue. *([5.2](lessons/05-02-leo-the-great.md), [1.1](lessons/01-01-how-to-read-a-church-father.md))*
- **"Origen says" is a claim about a manuscript tradition.** Name the pipe. *([2.5](lessons/02-05-clement-of-alexandria-and-origen.md))*
- **Where the original is lost, the honest report is the ambiguity, stated precisely, with the words named** — not a choice of rendering presented as the text. *([2.1](lessons/02-01-irenaeus-against-the-gnostics.md))*
- **A bare hymn or section number is not a citation** when the editions number differently: the Greek Ephrem, and ANF's straight-through numbering of *On First Principles* Book IV. *([3.4](lessons/03-04-ephrem-and-syriac-christianity.md), [3.5](lessons/03-05-allegory-and-history-alexandria-and-antioch.md))*
- **Do not trust nineteenth-century editorial apparatus.** Schaff's text is the default; his prooemia are not — the Seventh Council of Carthage met in 256, not 258, and Barmby's own list at *Pastoral Rule* III.1 miscounts the admonitions. *([2.4](lessons/02-04-cyprian-of-carthage.md), [5.3](lessons/05-03-gregory-the-great.md))*
- **Say "disputed" when it is disputed, and give the majority view as a majority view** — the date of *On the Incarnation*, Basil's Letter 38, Gregory's *Dialogues*, the middle recension of Ignatius, *On the Sacraments*. *([3.1](lessons/03-01-athanasius.md), [3.2](lessons/03-02-basil-the-great.md), [5.3](lessons/05-03-gregory-the-great.md), [1.3](lessons/01-03-ignatius-of-antioch.md), [4.1](lessons/04-01-ambrose-of-milan.md))*

### Pitfalls of anachronism

- **Date your vocabulary.** Before about 150, check what *episkopos* does in the text in front of you; reading Basil's *hypostasis* back into the 325 anathema makes Nicaea forbid what Basil taught; judging Justin by Nicene standards he did not have is this course's standing anachronism. *([1.2](lessons/01-02-first-clement-and-the-didache.md), [3.2](lessons/03-02-basil-the-great.md), [1.4](lessons/01-04-justin-martyr.md))*
- **"The Catholic Church" in *Smyrnaeans* 8 means the whole Church against the local one.** The later denominational sense is a plain anachronism, and a common one. *([1.3](lessons/01-03-ignatius-of-antioch.md))*
- **Do not import a later frame into an earlier argument.** Athanasius's picture of sin is corruption of nature, not guilt in a courtroom; Ambrose has no substance/appearance vocabulary, and reading ch. 9 as anticipating a thirteenth-century distinction is anachronism while reading it as mere symbolism flattens "nature itself is changed". *([3.1](lessons/03-01-athanasius.md), [4.1](lessons/04-01-ambrose-of-milan.md))*
- **Read the Eve-Mary parallel as it was written** — obedience undoing disobedience — rather than back-reading later Marian doctrine into it. *([2.2](lessons/02-02-irenaeus-recapitulation.md))*
- **"Semi-Pelagian" is a later label and a verdict.** The ancient name was "remnants of the Pelagians", Orange's canons name nobody, and Cassian is venerated as a saint. *([4.5](lessons/04-05-augustine-against-the-pelagians.md))*

### Pitfalls of reading an argument

- **An imperative is evidence that something was being urged, not proof that it was universal.** Ignatius exhorts; he does not describe. *([1.3](lessons/01-03-ignatius-of-antioch.md))*
- **A polemicist's summary of his opponents is evidence about the polemicist first** — Arius through Athanasius, "Nestorius taught two persons", the Valentinians through Irenaeus, Pelagius through Augustine. *([3.1](lessons/03-01-athanasius.md), [5.1](lessons/05-01-cyril-of-alexandria.md), [2.1](lessons/02-01-irenaeus-against-the-gnostics.md), [4.5](lessons/04-05-augustine-against-the-pelagians.md))*
- **A silence usually proves nothing**, since these documents are short and occasional; a silence exactly where a later writer would certainly have spoken is worth noticing and still weaker than a statement. *([1.2](lessons/01-02-first-clement-and-the-didache.md))*
- **A bad argument can be excellent evidence.** Irenaeus's four winds prove nothing and show that four gospels were already settled. *([2.1](lessons/02-01-irenaeus-against-the-gnostics.md))*
- **Read the whole sentence.** "The glory of God is a living man" has a second half; "the whole world judges securely" has an object clause; Leo's "each form" clause has "with the co-operation of the other" attached. *([2.2](lessons/02-02-irenaeus-recapitulation.md), [4.4](lessons/04-04-augustine-against-the-donatists.md), [5.2](lessons/05-02-leo-the-great.md))*
- **A criterion is not self-executing.** The rule of faith rules arrangements out, not in; succession works by convergence, not by any single line; and what happens when apostolic churches diverge is Vincent's question, three centuries later. *([2.1](lessons/02-01-irenaeus-against-the-gnostics.md))*
- **Do not let one half of a theology license or discredit the other.** Augustine's mixed-body picture is a reason for patience with bad men inside; the coercion letters add a claim about those outside. *([4.4](lessons/04-04-augustine-against-the-donatists.md))*
- **Do not turn preaching into a programme.** Chrysostom is moving a congregation, not drafting law. *([3.6](lessons/03-06-john-chrysostom.md))*

### Pitfalls of reading polemic and hard pages

- **The genre argument answers a different question from the reception argument.** *Psogos* is about what the words were doing in 386; the reception history is about what the words do when read. Answering the first and stopping is not an answer to the second. *([3.6](lessons/03-06-john-chrysostom.md))*
- **An explanation of why a text sounds like that is not a defence of what it says**, and the setting does not touch the fact that the Judaizing homilies generalize far beyond the Judaizers. *([3.6](lessons/03-06-john-chrysostom.md))*
- **The honesty rule: say what the page says, and name its later use, before offering any context.** Chrysostom's homilies, Justin's *Dialogue* 16, Augustine on coercion. *([3.6](lessons/03-06-john-chrysostom.md), [1.4](lessons/01-04-justin-martyr.md), [4.4](lessons/04-04-augustine-against-the-donatists.md))*

### Pitfalls of scope

- **Stating the readings is this course's job; deciding them is not.** Every contested passage above is presented with each tradition's reading and the words it leans on, and nothing more. *([syllabus](syllabus.md), [2.1](lessons/02-01-irenaeus-against-the-gnostics.md), [5.3](lessons/05-03-gregory-the-great.md))*
- **A Father's text is not the doctrine drawn from it.** Cyprian's "no salvation outside the Church" and Ambrose's chapter 9 are read here as arguments; what the Church teaches is fixed elsewhere. *([2.4](lessons/02-04-cyprian-of-carthage.md), [4.1](lessons/04-01-ambrose-of-milan.md))*
- **A method is not settled by a condemnation or a title.** Theodore was condemned in 553 for Christological reasons, and neither that nor Origen's ecclesiastical-writer status settles how to read Scripture. *([3.5](lessons/03-05-allegory-and-history-alexandria-and-antioch.md))*
- **"Antioch read the Bible literally" and "Origen thought the Bible was not historical" are both false as stated.** Chrysostom allegorizes constantly; Origen argues that most of Scripture's history stands. *([3.5](lessons/03-05-allegory-and-history-alexandria-and-antioch.md))*
- **Boss problems are not on this card.** Where an entry touches one — Clement/Didache/Ignatius on the offices, Irenaeus 3.3.1-3, Cyprian ch. 4, Athanasius 54 with Letter 101, Basil ch. 27, Origen on Genesis 2-3, the Pelagius exchange and *Confessions* 10.29, *On Baptism*, Sermon 131, Letter 93, Cyril and Leo, Gregory on "universal", Matthew 16:18 — it names the text and the positions and stops.

## Conventions

- **One card per course**, covering every lesson. The linter checks that every lesson file is cited somewhere here.
- **Intuition first**, same as lessons: a plain-English line before any technical statement.
- **Headings are anchors.** Renaming a `###` breaks inbound lesson links; the linter catches it, but prefer not to rename.
- **No prose dollar signs** — write "ten dollars", not the symbol (see CLAUDE.md).
- **Contested passages carry no verdict.** Each gives every tradition's reading and the words it leans on; arguing them out is [`apologetics-catholic-claims`](../apologetics-catholic-claims/syllabus.md)'s job, and this card will not be edited to take sides.
- **Authority discipline.** An individual Father's opinion is never presented as Church teaching, and a Doctor's title is not an endorsement of any opinion. Doctrinal levels are given only by citing [`fundamental-theology` 4.4](../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-04-the-ladder-of-doctrinal-authority.md) and the owning dogmatic course.
- **Attribution discipline.** Every quotation carries its work and chapter and its named translation. Where authenticity is disputed among scholars rather than settled, the card says so and gives the majority view as a majority view. Dates for 1 Clement, the Didache and Ignatius are ranges.
- **Boss problems are reserved.** Entries that touch one give the text and the names of the positions, never the worked reading.
- Length is not capped the way a lesson's is: this is a lookup surface, not a read-through. But every line still has to earn its place.
