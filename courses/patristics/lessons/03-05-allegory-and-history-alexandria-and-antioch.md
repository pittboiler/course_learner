# The Church Fathers · Lesson 3.5: Allegory and history: Alexandria and Antioch

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 3: The fourth-century East · Builds on: [2.5 Clement of Alexandria and Origen](02-05-clement-of-alexandria-and-origen.md), [3.4 Ephrem and Syriac Christianity](03-04-ephrem-and-syriac-christianity.md) · Unlocks: [3.6 John Chrysostom](03-06-john-chrysostom.md), [5.3 Gregory the Great](05-03-gregory-the-great.md)

## Why this matters

Every Father you have read argues from the Bible, and none gets his conclusions out of it the way a modern reader would; Ephrem finds the Son in a pearl ([3.4](03-04-ephrem-and-syriac-christianity.md)). So the deferred question is due: what *rule* were they following? There is one, written down by Origen around 229 in Book IV of *On First Principles* — the most influential theory of reading in Christian history, and the one that drew the Fathers' sharpest reply to one another, from the schoolmen of Antioch. The textbook summary of that quarrel — Alexandria allegorized, Antioch read literally — is the most quoted sentence about patristic exegesis, and it is about half true. This lesson gives you the texts and the reason to distrust the summary.

## The idea

Origen's theory is not a licence to invent meanings but a conclusion drawn from a doctrine about the Bible, and the doctrine comes first. Grant that the Holy Spirit wrote every part of Scripture to save its readers. Two things follow. First, **no passage can be idle** — a genealogy, a list of clean animals, a march through the wilderness, all must profit somebody, or the premise is false. Second, **no passage can say anything unworthy of God**, since the Spirit is the author throughout.

Now find passages that appear to do both: a command nobody could obey, a rule about a nonexistent animal. The literalist swallows these; the Gnostic reads them as proof that the Old Testament's God is a lesser being ([2.1](02-01-irenaeus-against-the-gnostics.md)). Origen takes a third option, the move that makes him Origen: **the difficulty was put there on purpose** — a bolt across the obvious road, laid by the Spirit so that a reader who reaches it stops, turns, and takes the narrow path to a higher meaning.

That converts embarrassments into signals, and hands the reader a rule with an entrance condition, which is what makes it a method: *where the letter is impossible, unworthy or useless, a spiritual sense is not optional but commanded*. Antioch's masters did not deny that Scripture carries more than its surface. They denied the entrance condition, and what Origen does with the letter once he has passed it.

## Source

Origen, *On First Principles*, Book IV; the Greek text, section 15 in the *Ante-Nicene Fathers*, vol. 4 (Frederick Crombie's translation; modern editions call this IV.2.9, since ANF numbers Book IV straight through):

> the word of God has arranged that certain stumbling-blocks, as it were, and offenses, and impossibilities, should be introduced into the midst of the law and the history, in order that we may not, through being drawn away in all directions by the merely attractive nature of the language, either altogether fall away from the (true) doctrines, as learning nothing worthy of God, or, by not departing from the letter, come to the knowledge of nothing more divine.

Read the grammar. The subject is **the word of God**: the [stumbling blocks](../reference.md#stumbling-blocks) are not defects in the text, or failures of its human authors, but deliberate acts of its divine one. And notice the *two* dangers guarded against. A reader seduced by the surface learns "nothing worthy of God"; one who will not leave it comes "to the knowledge of nothing more divine". This is a theory of inspiration before it is a theory of reading.

A transmission note ([1.1](01-01-how-to-read-a-church-father.md)). *On First Principles* survives whole only in Rufinus's Latin, which he admitted adjusting ([2.5](02-05-clement-of-alexandria-and-origen.md)); most of Book IV survives in Greek too, excerpted into the *Philocalia*, an anthology traditionally ascribed to Basil and Gregory of Nazianzus ([3.2](03-02-basil-the-great.md), [3.3](03-03-the-two-gregories.md)). ANF prints both; prefer the Greek.

## The argument

### Origen's rule

1. **Scripture is inspired throughout and given for salvation.** *In words:* the Spirit is the author of every line, and wrote to save the reader.
2. **Readers come in three capacities, and the text is built for all three.** Origen takes the pattern from Proverbs 22:20, read in his Greek as a command to write "in a threefold manner", and from the body–soul–spirit division of a human being: a bodily sense for the simple, a soul-sense for those progressing, a spiritual sense for the perfect. *In words:* the same text feeds a beginner and a contemplative at different depths. These are [the three senses of Scripture](../reference.md#the-three-senses-of-scripture).
3. **The spiritual sense is universal; the bodily sense is not.** "there are certain passages of Scripture which do not at all contain the corporeal sense" (ANF Book IV, Greek, section 12). *In words:* every passage has a higher meaning, but not every passage is true as it stands.
4. **Where the letter fails, the failure is deliberate** (the Source). *In words:* impossibility in the letter is the Spirit's signal, not the text's flaw.
5. **Therefore the reader who hits an impossibility must climb.** *In words:* the higher reading is obedience to the text, not liberty with it.

Two guards, often forgotten. The "soul" sense is rarely used in Origen's own commentaries, which mostly run on two levels, letter and spirit. And he insists that the historical sense of most of Scripture stands, the stumbling blocks being scattered among narratives and laws true as they are. He is not saying the Bible's history did not happen, but that *at certain marked points* it did not, and that the marks are legible.

### The Antiochene reply

The reply came from a school, not a man. **Diodore of Tarsus** (bishop from 378, died about 390) ran an ascetic study house at Antioch and taught the next two names; he wrote a treatise on the difference between *theoria* and allegory, now lost, and a Psalms commentary whose surviving preface states his preference for the historical reading plainly. **Theodore of Mopsuestia** (about 350–428) read each book against its own historical setting, cut the directly messianic psalms to a handful, and treated the Song of Songs as a marriage poem. **John Chrysostom** ([3.6](03-06-john-chrysostom.md)) is that school in the pulpit.

Their objection is not that figurative reading is illegitimate. It is that the figure must be found *in* the history, not *instead of* it. Their word for the right procedure is **[theoria](../reference.md#theoria)**: an act of vision in which the prophet, and after him the reader, sees the near historical referent and a further one *through* it, both standing. Hezekiah's deliverance really happened and really shows Christ's. Alexandrian allegory, as they saw it, cuts the second reference loose and leaves the first idle.

So the entrance condition is denied. Where Origen finds an impossibility, an Antiochene finds an idiom, a figure or a translator's artefact — solvable at the level of the letter, so the bolt never drops.

## Map of positions

| | Alexandria (Origen and his heirs) | Antioch (Diodore, Theodore, Chrysostom) | Young's re-description |
|---|---|---|---|
| What licenses a further sense | Inspiration: the Spirit means more everywhere, and impossibilities command the climb | The text: prophecy and the shape of the narrative point beyond themselves | Both; the disagreement is about *what kind* of pointing counts |
| Status of the *historia* | True in most places, suspended at marked points | Never suspended; the further sense rests on it | The real fault line, and the one worth keeping |

**[Typology and allegory](../reference.md#typology-and-allegory)** is how the textbooks draw the line: a *type* is a real person or event prefiguring another real one (Jonah in the fish); an *allegory* treats the narrative as a coded surface whose elements stand for truths of another order. Typology keeps the history; allegory can spend it.

**Frances Young** (*Biblical Exegesis and the Formation of Christian Culture*, 1997) argues that the line is drawn in the wrong place, by modern anxieties about historicity rather than by the Fathers' own categories. Her case, compressed: both schools allegorize and both care about the letter, and both were trained in the same grammatical and rhetorical curriculum — Alexandrian allegory grew out of the philological commentary practised on Homer, Antiochene reading out of rhetoric — so their techniques of construing a text are the same techniques. What differs is the mode of reference — Alexandrian reading treats words as tokens pointing into a symbolic order, Antiochene reading insists that the text's governing subject, its *[hypothesis](../reference.md#hypothesis-and-akolouthia)*, and its narrative sequence control the further meaning. The Antiochene complaint is then not "too figurative" but "you have broken the sequence". Call this [the Young thesis](../reference.md#the-young-thesis); it is widely adopted and not unanimous — readers who live in the commentaries still report a real difference in practice.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean: the criterion at work).** Exodus 16:29, in Origen's Greek: "You shall sit each one in your dwellings: let no one go out from his place on the seventh day." Run the rule. Step 3 asks whether the letter stands; Origen says it cannot: "no living being is able to sit throughout a whole day, and remain without moving from a sitting position" (ANF Book IV, Greek, section 17). He names the literalists' shifts — a two-thousand-cubit limit, or Dositheus the Samaritan's rule that you stay in the posture you were found in — as evidence that the letter is broken. Step 4 makes that brokenness deliberate; step 5 sends him upward, to the true sabbath rest. The same section adds two cases from the law: it lists among clean animals a goat-stag, which does not exist, and forbids the griffin, never caught by anyone.

Now watch the Antiochene instinct dissolve the case. "Sit" renders an idiom meaning *stay*, not *remain seated*; the goat-stag and the griffin are the Greek translator's guesses at rare Hebrew animal names. Handled at the level of the letter, by idiom and translation history, the impossibility vanishes — and with it the summons to climb. The method has not been refuted; its entrance condition has been closed.

**Example 2 (where it strains: Galatians 4).** Paul reads Hagar and Sarah as the two covenants and says the things "are an allegory" — and Origen quotes that very verse as apostolic warrant for the whole procedure (ANF Book IV, Greek, section 13). It is the strongest card in the Alexandrian hand: the method is not imported from Homer's commentators, it is used in the New Testament.

The Antiochene cannot deny the verse, so he must manage the word. Chrysostom, preaching on the same text, says Paul uses "allegory" loosely, for what is properly a *type* — the history stands, and the further reference is announced in it. P1 asks you to read that sentence. Two things are visible. The dispute is about the kind of reference, not about whether Scripture may be read figuratively, which is Young's point in miniature. And here it is the Antiochene doing the harder exegetical work, explaining away the apostle's own word.

## Watch out

- **"Antioch read the Bible literally" is false as stated.** Chrysostom allegorizes constantly and Theodoret's commentaries are full of figures. What Antioch refuses is a reading that *replaces* the narrative rather than resting on it.
- **"Origen thought the Bible was not historical" is also false.** He argues the opposite about most of it. His claim is narrower and stranger: at particular marked places the Spirit inserted what did not happen, so that the reader would notice.
- **Do not promote either school into Church teaching.** Origen is an ecclesiastical writer, not a Father in the strict sense ([1.1](01-01-how-to-read-a-church-father.md)); Theodore's person and works were condemned posthumously in 553 as one of the Three Chapters, for Christological reasons, with his exegesis read in that light — the affair as an event is [`church-history` 2.2](../../church-history/lessons/02-02-ephesus-chalcedon-and-leos-rome.md). No condemnation and no Doctor's title settles a method; levels of authority are owned by [`fundamental-theology` 4.4](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-04-the-ladder-of-doctrinal-authority.md).
- **The four senses are not Origen's three.** The Latin scheme — literal, allegorical, moral, anagogical — grows out of this material through Cassian, Gregory the Great ([5.3](05-03-gregory-the-great.md)) and the medievals, and the Catechism sets it out with the literal sense as the basis of the others (CCC 115–118). That teaching belongs to [`old-testament`](../../old-testament/syllabus.md) 1.2; here you read the Fathers' practice, which is untidier.

## One-liner

> Origen says a broken letter is a door the Spirit built; Antioch says look again, it is not broken — and the quarrel is less about allegory than about whether the story has to survive.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢) *(Exegetical.)*** John Chrysostom, preaching on Galatians 4:22–24 (*Homilies on Galatians*, NPNF first series, vol. 13, translated by Gross Alexander):

> **Ver. 22.** For it is written, that Abraham had two sons, one by the hand-maid and the other by the freewoman. …
>
> **Ver. 24.** Which things contain an allegory.
>
> Contrary to usage, he calls a type an allegory; his meaning is as follows; this history not only declares that which appears on the face of it, but announces somewhat farther, whence it is called an allegory. And what has it announced? No less than all the things now present.

(a) State the distinction Chrysostom is drawing, and quote the words that carry it. (b) What does he concede to the figurative reading, and what does he keep back from it? Two sentences per part.

**P2 (🟡) *(Exegetical.)*** An invented parish-bulletin note, written for this problem:

> **From the Pastor's Desk.** Some of you were troubled by last week's reading. Relax! Origen, the greatest scholar of the early Church, taught that the Old Testament stories are "impossibilities" that never happened, and that the Spirit hid a meaning underneath for the mature. So let us not get stuck on whether these things took place. Each of us should ask what the passage means for me, today.

Name the two distinct moves by which the note misrepresents Origen's actual position as this lesson states it, and for each, name the step of his rule (1–5) that it breaks. 150 words or fewer.

**P3 (🔴, optional) *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)*** Origen's rule tells you *when* to look for a spiritual sense. It does not, by itself, tell you *which* spiritual sense is the right one. (a) From the lesson and from [2.1](02-01-irenaeus-against-the-gnostics.md), name two constraints Origen and his tradition would say limit the answer. (b) Is the constraint strong enough to stop the method producing whatever the interpreter already believes? Any verdict; 150 words or fewer for (b).

<details>
<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical.)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** the distinction is between a **type** and an **allegory**, and Chrysostom's claim is that Paul has used the second word for the first thing. The deciding words are "**Contrary to usage, he calls a type an allegory**" — "contrary to usage" is doing all the work, conceding that the term is Paul's while denying that Paul's technical sense is the usual one.

**Must hit, strict (b):** he concedes that the passage "announces somewhat farther" than its surface, and that the further reference is enormous ("all the things now present"). He keeps back the dissolution of the narrative: the sentence says "this history **not only** declares that which appears on the face of it" — the history still declares what it appears to, and the further announcement is added to it, not substituted for it.

**Wrong turns:** reading Chrysostom as rejecting figurative interpretation (he affirms it in the same breath); treating "contrary to usage" as a claim that Paul made a mistake, rather than that Paul used a word loosely; missing "not only", which is the hinge of part (b).

**Model answer:** (a) He distinguishes a type, a real history that prefigures, from an allegory, a coded surface; the words "Contrary to usage, he calls a type an allegory" state that Paul's term is not being used in its ordinary sense. (b) He concedes a further reference, and a very large one, "all the things now present". He withholds any suspension of the story: "not only declares that which appears on the face of it" leaves the face-value declaration standing.

---

**P2** *(Exegetical.)*

**Must hit, strict:** two moves, each tied to a step.

1. **Generalizing the impossibilities.** Origen holds that a *particular, marked* set of passages is impossible in the letter, while the historical sense of most of Scripture stands and is useful. The note turns his narrow claim into a claim about "the Old Testament stories". This breaks **step 3** (the bodily sense is absent only in certain passages), and with it step 4, which needs the failures to be exceptional in order to be signals at all.
2. **Making the higher sense private.** Origen's spiritual sense is the Spirit's, objective, and reached by a disciplined reading; the note replaces it with "what the passage means for me, today". This breaks **step 5**: for Origen the climb is obedience to the text, not liberty with it. Credit also for noting that this abandons step 1 — the sense is put there by the Spirit for salvation, so it is not the reader's to supply.

**Wrong turns:** objecting that Origen was condemned (irrelevant here, and the later anathemas are not about this rule); objecting that the note quotes a real word of Origen's ("impossibilities") — it does, which is what makes the misuse worth diagnosing; treating "for me, today" as Origen's "soul" sense, which is a sense of the text, not of the reader.

**Model answer:** First, the note generalizes. Origen says certain marked passages have no bodily sense, while most of the narrative is true and profitable as it stands; the note rewrites this as a verdict on the Old Testament as such, which destroys step 3 and leaves step 4 nothing to signal, since a stumbling block only works against a background of passable road. Second, it privatizes the higher sense. For Origen the spiritual meaning is the Spirit's own, sought under discipline; step 5 makes the climb obedience to the text. "What the passage means for me, today" makes the reader the author of the sense, which is the opposite of the doctrine of inspiration the whole rule rests on.

---

**P3** *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** any two of — the **rule of faith** received from the apostles, which fixes in advance what a reading may conclude and is exactly what Irenaeus deploys against Gnostic exegesis ([2.1](02-01-irenaeus-against-the-gnostics.md)); the **apostolic examples**, since Origen reads his own procedure off Paul (Galatians 4) and so is bound to the kind of reading Paul performs; **Scripture interpreted by Scripture**, the collection of parallel texts that supplies the terms of the higher sense; and the criterion that a proposed sense must be **worthy of God and useful for salvation**, which rules out readings that are merely ingenious.

**Must hit, any verdict (b):** state the constraint precisely rather than gesturing at "tradition"; then test it in one direction. If you say it is strong enough, say what it excludes concretely — the rule of faith does in fact block the Gnostic readings, which is a real result, and the *worthy of God* test is a filter with teeth. If you say it is not, say why: the rule of faith fixes conclusions, not meanings, so any reading compatible with the creed passes, and the space of such readings is very large; and a criterion that authorizes the climb without directing it leaves the landing point to the reader's prior commitments. Either way, name what would settle it: a case where two orthodox readers derive incompatible spiritual senses from one passage and the constraints cannot adjudicate.

**Wrong turns:** answering (b) by saying Origen was later condemned, which is a fact about his metaphysics and afterlife, not a test of the method; treating the Antiochene objection as if it were the same objection (theirs is about the letter's survival, not about under-determination); giving (a) as "the Church" without naming a mechanism.

**Model answer (b), one of several:** Not strong enough on its own, though not nothing. The rule of faith is a real filter: it is what stops the Valentinian reading, and Irenaeus shows it doing that work. But it constrains conclusions, not routes, and the set of spiritual senses compatible with the creed is enormous — which is why two orthodox Fathers can read the same psalm to opposite pastoral effect with neither in error. The *worthy of God* criterion has the same shape, ruling out the crude rather than selecting among the plausible. What actually disciplined the tradition was slower and more social: accumulated liturgical use, the preference for readings already made by earlier teachers, and the weight of a consensus rather than of any one reader. That is a constraint on the community, not a rule the individual exegete can apply.

## Flashback

**From Lesson [3.3](03-03-the-two-gregories.md) (Gregory of Nazianzus and Gregory of Nyssa):** *(Exegetical.)* In the *Life of Moses* Gregory of Nyssa ends the soul's approach to God not in vision but in the darkness of Sinai, and, because God is infinite, the soul never arrives but stretches forward forever. (a) Say in one sentence what the darkness asserts about knowing God, then name the Greek term for that endless stretching and what it makes of desire. (b) A writer concludes that Nyssa's blessed are therefore permanently frustrated, and that this is the Christian doctrine of heaven. Correct the first claim in one sentence, and say what is wrong with the second as a claim about authority. **Two sentences per part.**

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):** the darkness says God is known **as unknowable** — the ascent ends in a knowing that does not comprehend its object, not in the failure of theology. The term is ***epektasis***, the endless ascent, and it makes unending desire a **permanent feature of blessedness rather than a defect in it**.

**Must hit, strict (b):** **The first claim inverts him.** Frustration is precisely the reading the account is built to exclude: the soul is always receiving and there is always more to receive because the object is infinite, so the absence of a terminus is the form that having an infinite God takes, not a lack of him. **The second is an authority error.** This is one Father's speculation, and a Father's opinion never carries magisterial weight by itself, whatever his standing; the levels are [`fundamental-theology` 4.4](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-04-the-ladder-of-doctrinal-authority.md)'s and what the Church teaches about the last things belongs to `creation-grace-last-things`.

**Wrong turns:** confusing *epektasis* with the restoration of all things, which is a different teaching of Gregory's and one whose extent is disputed among scholars; reading the darkness as a claim that nothing whatever may be said of God, when Gregory goes on arguing about him at length and holds that our words reach God's surroundings rather than his essence; answering the authority point by saying Gregory was condemned — he was not, and the sixth-century anathemas were aimed at Origenism.

**Model answer:** (a) That God is reached as incomprehensible: the summit of the ascent is darkness, a knowledge that does not enclose its object. The stretching is *epektasis*, and it makes never-ending desire part of what blessedness is rather than evidence that something is missing. (b) On Gregory's own terms the soul is continually filled and continually has more to receive, so "frustration" describes a finite object running out, which is exactly what an infinite one cannot do. And the writer has promoted one Father's speculation to Church teaching, which no Father's standing can do; the last things are taught elsewhere, with their levels marked.

</details>
