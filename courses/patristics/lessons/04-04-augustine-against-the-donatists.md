# The Church Fathers · Lesson 4.4: Augustine against the Donatists

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 4: The Latin Doctors · Builds on: [4.3 Augustine: Confessions](04-03-augustine-confessions.md), [2.4 Cyprian](02-04-cyprian-of-carthage.md) · Unlocks: [4.5 Augustine against the Pelagians](04-05-augustine-against-the-pelagians.md)

## Why this matters

Two things came out of Augustine's thirty-year argument with the African schismatics, and a serious reader has to carry both. The first is the Latin West's theology of the Church and her sacraments: the reason your baptism does not depend on whether the man who performed it was holy, and the reason the Church has never claimed to be a society of the pure. The second is the first sustained Christian argument that the state should coerce religious dissenters, written by a bishop who had held the opposite view and says so in the letter. Canon law collected those letters; Aquinas quoted them.

## The idea

In the persecution under Diocletian some African clergy handed over the scriptures to be burned. In 311–312 a party at Carthage held that one of the bishops who consecrated Caecilian had been such a *traditor*, set up a rival, and never came back; the events, the appeals to Constantine and the councils that judged them belong to [`church-history` 1.4](../../church-history/lessons/01-04-constantine.md). The premise underneath is what concerns us. [Donatism](../reference.md#donatism) held that **a sacrament carries the holiness of the man who gives it**. A traitor's hands are empty, so his ordinations are empty, and so is everything flowing from them. Hence rebaptism: anyone coming from the Catholic side had never really been baptized.

The premise is not silly, and it is not new. Cyprian had argued that baptism outside the Church is no baptism ([2.4](02-04-cyprian-of-carthage.md)), and the Donatists claimed him. Augustine's strategy was not to attack Cyprian but to divide him: concede the holiness and the martyrdom, deny the conclusion, and show that Cyprian himself had forbidden the move the Donatists were making.

The reply has three moves.

1. **Change the minister.** The one who baptizes is Christ; the human minister is a channel. What matters is what is given, not who gives it.
2. **Split having from having usefully.** A schismatic really has baptism; it does him no good while he stays outside. So do not rebaptize — reconcile.
3. **Change the picture of the Church.** Not a remnant of the clean but a field with tares in it, sorted at the harvest and not before.

## Source

*On Baptism, against the Donatists* IV.17 (NPNF, first series, J. R. King's translation). Augustine answers a letter of Cyprian sentence by sentence; the words quoted inside the passage are Cyprian's.

> "Salvation," he says, "is not without the Church." Who says that it is? And therefore, whatever men have that belongs to the Church, it profits them nothing towards salvation outside the Church. But it is one thing not to have, another to have so as to be of no use. He who has not must be baptized that he may have; but he who has to no avail must be corrected, that what he has may profit him.

Two verbs carry the theology: *have* and *profit*. Augustine grants Cyprian's slogan whole, then drives a distinction through the middle of it.

## The argument

**The Donatist case, as Augustine states it in order to answer it.**

1. A sacrament is holy with the holiness of its minister. *In words: what a bad man hands you, he hands you spoiled.*
2. Caecilian's consecrators were traitors, and those in communion with traitors share the guilt. *In words: the stain travels along the chain of communion.*
3. So the Catholic communion has no sacraments, and those who come from it must be baptized afresh. *In words: we are not repeating your baptism; you never had one.*

**Augustine's reply.**

4. Baptism is Christ's, not the minister's: of Christ the gospel says "the same is He which baptizes" (Jn 1:33, cited at *On Baptism* IV.11). *In words: the priest is the pipe, not the spring.*
5. So we consider not who gives but what he gives, not who has but what he has (IV.11). *In words: look at the gift, not the hands.*
6. Baptism given in the words of the gospel is therefore **real even outside the Church**, and is not repeated when a schismatic is reconciled — later theology's [valid but not fruitful](../reference.md#valid-but-not-fruitful). *In words: he has it; it is doing nothing for him.*
7. What it lacks is charity, which only unity supplies, so the remedy is correction, not repetition (IV.17). *In words: mend the man, not the sacrament.*
8. And the guilt in premise 2 does not travel, because the Church is [a mixed body](../reference.md#the-church-as-a-mixed-body) until the harvest (Mt 13:24–30). *In words: nobody is damned by his neighbour's sin.*

Premise 8 is load-bearing. If the Church were the society of the pure, the Donatists would be right that one traitor bishop is fatal; Augustine answers that no visible society has ever been that, and the Lord told the servants to let both grow together.

### The sentence Newman heard

There is a fourth move, quieter and more famous. A party in one African province was condemning Christians in Gaul, Egypt and Syria whose lives it could not know. Augustine turns that round: what the world *cannot* know is whether a distant man is wicked; what it *can* know is that a group has cut itself off. At the end of that argument stands one sentence, in *Against the Letter of Parmenianus* III.4.24 (often cited III.24; the work is not in Schaff, so this is the lesson's own translation of the Latin):

> Therefore the whole world judges securely that those are not good who divide themselves from the whole world, in whatever part of the world.

In the summer of 1839 John Henry Newman, then an Anglican, met three words of it — *securus iudicat orbis terrarum* — quoted in a review article on the Donatists, and wrote later in the *Apologia pro Vita Sua* (ch. 3) that they "went beyond the occasion of the Donatists" and applied to the Monophysites too, so that "the theory of the Via Media was absolutely pulverized." Note what happened on the way. Augustine's sentence has an object: the world judges securely *that separatists are not good*. The three-word version has none, and so reads as a general rule for settling controversies. Only one of them is what [Augustine wrote](../reference.md#the-whole-world-judges-securely).

## Map of positions

| Question | Donatist answer | Augustine's answer |
|---|---|---|
| Who is the Church? | the untainted, in Africa | the body spread through the world, mixed until the harvest |
| What makes a sacrament real? | the holiness of the minister | Christ, who is the true minister |
| A convert from the other side? | baptize him; he had nothing | receive him; he had it, it did him no good |
| The wicked inside? | purge them, or leave | tolerate them; sorting is the Lord's work at the end |
| May the emperor act? | never against us; we are the martyrs' church | yes, and Augustine came to urge it |

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean).** A Donatist bishop and his congregation reconcile. Run the premises. Their baptisms were given in the words of the gospel, so premise 6 applies: nothing is repeated, and re-baptizing would treat the sacrament as the minister's property. Their ordinations stand too, which is why Augustine's party could offer a returning Donatist bishop a share of the see. Reconciliation supplies premise 7, the charity that unity gives, so that what was inert begins to profit them. Notice what Augustine does *not* say — that their years outside were fine. "Not without the Church" is granted, not softened.

**Example 2 (where it strains): the coercion letters.**

Read plainly, and in his own order. Augustine tells Vincentius in 408 that he has changed his mind, and why:

> originally my opinion was, that no one should be coerced into the unity of Christ, that we must act only by words, fight only by arguments … But this opinion of mine was overcome not by words … but by the conclusive instances to which they could point. (Letter 93, ch. 5 §17, NPNF, J. G. Cunningham's translation)

The instances are results. His own town came over under fear of the imperial edicts and now detests the schism; men tell him they were held by custom and by fear of Donatist violence. Fear, he argues, does not produce faith, but it breaks the grip of habit so that a man will examine what he had not bothered to examine. The measures in view are fines, confiscation of meeting places, exile for clergy and the lash.

In Letter 185 to Boniface, circulated as *On the Correction of the Donatists*, the scriptural case is set out. Luke 14:23 — "Go out into the highways and hedges, and compel them to come in" — is read as authorizing compulsion (§24). Suffering settles nothing by itself: Hagar suffered from Sarah, David from Saul, two thieves were crucified beside Christ. So "the punishment of the impious may be the same; but the cause of the martyrs is always different" (§9). The conclusion (§11):

> there is a persecution of unrighteousness, which the impious inflict upon the Church of Christ; and there is a righteous persecution, which the Church of Christ inflicts upon the impious … she persecutes in the spirit of love, they in the spirit of wrath. (Letter 185 §11, NPNF, J. R. King's translation)

That is the argument. Now its use. Gratian's *Decretum* gathered these texts, and Thomas Aquinas, asking whether unbelievers ought to be compelled to the faith, answers with Luke 14:23, rules that Jews and pagans may not be compelled to believe, then adds that heretics and apostates — who once professed the faith — "should be submitted even to bodily compulsion"; his first reply quotes Letter 93's change of mind almost word for word and rereads the parable of the tares to allow what Augustine had used it to forbid (*ST* II-II q.10 a.8 and ad 1). Through that channel the argument became the standing authority for coercing heretics in the Latin West, and it is what Pierre Bayle set out to demolish in his *Philosophical Commentary* of 1686 on "compel them to come in".

Only now, the rest of the record. Augustine fought against killing. In 412, writing about Circumcellions who had confessed to murdering one Catholic priest and maiming another, he begged the imperial commissioner not to execute or mutilate them, praising him for extracting confessions "not by stretching them on the rack … but by beating them with rods" (Letter 133) — clemency that still takes flogging for granted. And on [the coercion letters](../reference.md#the-coercion-letters) as authority, in the sense of [`fundamental-theology` 4.4](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-04-the-ladder-of-doctrinal-authority.md): this is a Father's opinion, never a definition, and a Doctor's title endorses no particular opinion of his. The Church's teaching on religious freedom is *Dignitatis Humanae* (1965), that no one may be forced to embrace the faith — conciliar teaching owed at least religious submission, read by many theologians as definitive, and owned as doctrine by [`catholic-social-teaching`](../../catholic-social-teaching/syllabus.md) 5.2.

## Watch out

- **"Valid but not fruitful" is not "sacraments work automatically."** The sacrament is Christ's and therefore intact, but Augustine is emphatic that what it does for the recipient depends on charity and unity. Reading premise 6 without premise 7 turns him into a magician.
- **Do not let the ecclesiology license the coercion, or the coercion discredit the ecclesiology.** The mixed-body picture is a reason for patience with bad men *inside*; the letters add a claim about what may be done to those *outside*, and the letters are where he argues for it.
- **The three-word tag is a compression, not a forgery.** The sentence is genuinely Augustine's; the free-standing maxim is not. Reading it as "the majority is always right" imports a rule about counting that his context — rash condemnation of people you cannot know — never supplies.
- **Cyprian is not a Donatist.** He held the rebaptism position they used, and also forbade separating from a Church with bad men in it. They took half of him; Augustine takes the other half.

## One-liner

> Christ is the one who baptizes, so the sacrament survives the schism and the Church survives her sinners — and the bishop who proved that no man is defiled by his neighbour's sin also argued that the state should make his neighbours come in.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢) *(Exegetical.)*** In *On Baptism* IV.12 Augustine quotes Cyprian's letter to Antonianus, written in the controversy with Novatian's rigorist party (NPNF, first series, King's translation):

> "What swelling of arrogance it is, what forgetfulness of humility and gentleness, that any one should dare or believe that he can do what the Lord did not grant even to the apostles — to think that he can distinguish the tares from the wheat, or, as if it were granted to him to carry the fan and purge the floor, to endeavor to separate the chaff from the grain!"

Augustine's gloss follows: by this argument, against those who "under the pretext of avoiding the society of wicked men, had severed themselves from the unity of the Church," Cyprian shows that the apostle's "great house" is the Church, "in which there should be good and bad, till at the last day it should be cleansed as a threshing-floor by the winnowing-fan."

(a) State in one sentence the conclusion the quotation is being used to establish, and give the three or four words that carry it.
(b) Augustine could have made this argument in his own voice. Say what he gains by making it in Cyprian's. Two sentences per part.

**P2 (🟡) *(Exegetical.)*** An invented parish-bulletin note:

> "St Augustine settled arguments like this one in three words: *securus iudicat orbis terrarum*, the whole world judges securely. When a small group breaks away claiming to be purer, remember that the verdict of the worldwide majority of Christians is the safe one. Count heads and follow the crowd."

Augustine's own sentence, from *Against the Letter of Parmenianus* III.4.24, is quoted above under "The sentence Newman heard".

(a) Name what the bulletin has dropped from the sentence, and say what Augustine actually claims the world judges securely.
(b) Newman reported that the words decided ecclesiastical questions "on a simpler rule than that of Antiquity" and applied beyond the Donatists. State what Newman had to add to Augustine's sentence to get a rule of that scope.

120 words or fewer in total.

**P3 (🔴, optional) *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)*** Use Letter 185 §§9 and 11, quoted in Example 2, together with the sentence that follows there (same translation):

> she that she may correct, they that they may overthrow: she that she may recall from error, they that they may drive headlong into error.

(a) Reconstruct in numbered premises the argument by which Augustine denies that the Donatists' sufferings make them martyrs. Name the premise that carries the inference, and the different premise a Donatist must deny.
(b) Taking the §9 and §11 test exactly as Augustine states it, does it place any limit on what the coercer may do? Name whatever in the two sections supplies a limit and whatever does not, and say who is in a position to apply it. 120 words, any verdict.

<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical.)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** the conclusion is that no one may separate from the Church because wicked men are mixed into it, since sorting the good from the bad before the end was not granted even to the apostles. Carrying words: "did not grant even to the apostles"; "distinguish the tares from the wheat"; "separate the chaff from the grain"; or, from the gloss, "in which there should be good and bad."
**Must hit, strict (b):** Cyprian is the authority the Donatists claim as their own — the African martyr-bishop whose rebaptism position is the root of their practice ([2.4](02-04-cyprian-of-carthage.md)). An argument in Cyprian's words cannot be met by appealing to Cyprian, and it concedes his holiness while denying the inference the Donatists draw from him. Credit for noting that Cyprian wrote this against a *different* separatist party, so the principle is his own and not framed for this quarrel.
**Wrong turns:** reading the passage as being about sacramental validity — it is about who may separate, not about whose baptism is real; treating the first paragraph as Augustine's own words; saying Cyprian condemned the Donatists, who did not exist in his lifetime.
**Model answer:** (a) Cyprian's words establish that separating from a Church containing wicked men usurps a judgment reserved to the Lord at the harvest; the phrase "the Lord did not grant even to the apostles" carries it, with "distinguish the tares from the wheat." (b) Making it in Cyprian's voice takes the Donatists' own patron away from them, since they cannot answer Cyprian by citing Cyprian. It also lets Augustine grant everything they revere in him — holiness, martyrdom — while denying the conclusion they draw.

---

**P2** *(Exegetical.)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** the bulletin drops the object clause. Augustine does not write "the whole world judges securely" and stop; he writes that the world judges securely **that those are not good who divide themselves from the whole world**. The claim is narrow — about separation, not about controversies generally — and its stated ground is what can be known: a local party cannot know the lives of distant Christians it condemns, whereas the fact of the division is open to everyone. "Count heads" is not his ground.
**Must hit, strict (b):** Newman generalized twice. He moved the sentence from schism to doctrinal controversy (he applied it to the Monophysites), and he turned "the world judges securely against those who divide" into a rule that the settled judgment of the whole Church is a final sentence on any portion that secedes — which also required treating the present judgment of the whole as outranking the appeal to antiquity.
**Wrong turns:** arguing whether Newman's use is legitimate, which belongs to `apologetics-catholic-claims`; calling the tag spurious (the sentence is genuine and the tag is a compression of it); answering only that Augustine meant the Donatists, without naming the dropped clause.
**Model answer:** The bulletin drops Augustine's object clause. He says the world judges securely *that those who divide themselves from it are not good* — a verdict about separating, grounded on what is knowable, since a local party condemning distant Christians judges what it cannot see. Newman had to add scope and force: extend the sentence from schism to doctrinal disputes such as the Monophysite one, and read it as making the Church's settled judgment a final sentence against any seceding part, outranking antiquity.

---

**P3** *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** premises of the form —
P1. Suffering by itself does not show that one suffers for righteousness (Hagar under Sarah, David under Saul, the thieves beside Christ).
P2. What makes a martyr is the cause, not the punishment ("the punishment of the impious may be the same; but the cause of the martyrs is always different").
P3. The Donatists' cause is schism, not righteousness.
∴ C. Their sufferings under the laws do not make them martyrs; and since the Church acts to correct rather than destroy, the persecution she inflicts is righteous.
The premise carrying the inference is **P2** — P1 alone only shows that suffering is inconclusive. The premise a Donatist must deny is **P3**, which is the whole question at issue, so the argument is worth exactly as much as the ecclesiology behind it.
**Must hit, any verdict (b):** identify what §11 does supply — the end is correction, not destruction ("she that she may correct, they that they may overthrow"; "recall from error" rather than "drive headlong"), which rules out measures that destroy rather than reclaim, and matches Augustine's refusals elsewhere (Letter 133). Identify what it does not supply: any procedure, any external check, and any test of the "spirit of love" or of the rightness of the cause that is not applied by the coercer to himself. Then say whether a self-applied criterion counts as a limit. Either verdict passes.
**Wrong turns:** arguing that coercion is wrong without engaging Augustine's own criteria; treating "spirit of love" as though Augustine gives it a procedural content; using *Dignitatis Humanae* as a premise available to Augustine rather than as the Church's later teaching; answering (b) as though (a) settled it.
**Model answer (b), one of several:** The sections do supply one limit with teeth: the stated end is correction, so anything that destroys rather than reclaims is excluded, which is why Augustine can consistently beg against executions and mutilation. But everything else in the test is internal. Whether the cause is righteous and whether the spirit is love are judgments the persecutor makes about himself, and §9 has already taught that the sufferer's experience is no evidence either way. On my reading that leaves the criterion unable to restrain anyone who is sincere, which is the condition most persecutors satisfy.

</details>

## Flashback

**From Lesson [4.2](04-02-jerome.md) (Jerome):** *(Exegetical.)* About 383 a Roman named Helvidius argued from Matthew 1 that Mary had other children, resting the case on three things in the text: that Joseph "knew her not till she brought forth", that Jesus is called her "first-born", and that the gospels name "brethren" of the Lord. Jerome's reply is lexical on each of the three. (a) Reconstruct it as three numbered premises and a conclusion, one line each. (b) In two sentences, say exactly what the argument establishes, what it does not, and on what the belief itself rests.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):**

- **P1.** In scriptural usage *till* often marks a limit without implying that what it limits was reversed afterwards, so "knew her not till she brought forth" settles nothing about what came after.
- **P2.** "First-born" is a legal and cultic term for the one who opens the womb, not a statement that others followed.
- **P3.** Scripture uses "brother" in four ways — by nature, by race, by kindred and by love (*Against Helvidius* 16) — so the word cannot by itself decide who the Lord's "brethren" were; Jerome takes them to be cousins.
- **∴ C.** None of the three words carries Helvidius's inference: his case fails on the vocabulary he rests it on.

Credit for noting that all three premises are about usage, which is why the lesson calls the reply lexical throughout.

**Must hit, strict (b):** it establishes that the three texts do not **entail** what Helvidius drew from them — it removes an inference, which is not the same as proving the contrary, since words consistent with a reading do not establish it. The belief itself does not rest on the philology: that Mary remained ever-virgin is Catholic dogma, and Jerome did not invent it but supplied the exegetical defence the West then used; the acts that carry it and the assent it requires belong to `ecclesiology-and-mariology`, read with the ladder in [`fundamental-theology` 4.4](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-04-the-ladder-of-doctrinal-authority.md).

**Wrong turns:** arguing out which reading of "brethren" is right, or weighing the traditions against each other — this course reads the argument, and what the texts prove belongs to `apologetics-catholic-claims`; giving P3 as "the brethren were cousins" without the premise about the four usages, which is the part doing the work; presenting the argument as a proof of the dogma, or the dogma as resting on Jerome; adding his claim that virginity is the higher state, which is in the treatise but is no part of the lexical case.

**Model answer:** (a) P1: *till* in Scripture often marks a limit without implying a change beyond it. P2: "first-born" names the one who opens the womb, a legal and cultic term, not a claim that others came later. P3: Scripture calls men brothers by nature, race, kindred and love, so the word alone decides nothing; Jerome reads these as cousins. ∴ Helvidius's three words do not carry his conclusion. (b) The argument blocks an inference: it shows the texts do not entail other children, not that there were none. The belief is dogma held on the Church's authority, for which Jerome supplied the exegetical defence rather than the ground.

</details>
