# The Church Fathers · Lesson 4.3: Augustine: *Confessions*

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 4: The Latin Doctors · Builds on: [4.1 Ambrose of Milan](04-01-ambrose-of-milan.md), [4.2 Jerome](04-02-jerome.md) · Unlocks: [4.4 Augustine against the Donatists](04-04-augustine-against-the-donatists.md), [4.5 Augustine against the Pelagians](04-05-augustine-against-the-pelagians.md)

## Why this matters

Everyone reads the *Confessions* as the first autobiography and stops at Book 9, where the story ends. That reading misses what the book is for. Augustine wrote it around 397–401, a decade after his conversion and a few years into his episcopate, and he wrote it as a prayer: every sentence is addressed to God, and the reader is overhearing. That formal choice is an argument. It says that the man in the story did not save himself. Twenty years later Pelagius heard one sentence of it read aloud in Rome and could not sit still — the book already contained, in the form of a prayer, the position Augustine would spend his last two decades defending.

## The idea

Augustine of Hippo (354–430) was born at Thagaste in Roman Africa, taught rhetoric at Carthage, Rome and Milan, was a Manichaean hearer for about nine years, was converted in a Milan garden in 386, baptized by Ambrose ([4.1](04-01-ambrose-of-milan.md)) at Easter 387, and made bishop of Hippo about 395. He wrote the *Confessions* as bishop, looking back.

Start with the title, because it is the whole method. Latin *confiteri* carries three senses at once, and Augustine uses all three: to **acknowledge** a fault, to **praise**, and to **profess** a faith. A confession of sin that is not also praise would be a memoir with a guilty conscience; praise that skipped the sin would be a lie. Holding the [two senses of confession](../reference.md#the-two-senses-of-confession) together forces the retrospective structure of the book: each episode is told twice, once as what he did and once as what God was doing in it while he could not see it.

That is why this course reads the book as a theology of grace, not a life. And it is why the last four books are not an appendix. A memoir ends when the story does. A prayer addressed to God does not: having confessed his past (1–9), Augustine has to confess what he still is (10), and then what God is and what the Scripture says that made him (11–13). The philosophy in Books 10 and 11 — memory as the place where the mind meets itself, and time as a distension of the soul — is genuinely first-rate and belongs to [`ancient-medieval-philosophy` 5.1](../../ancient-medieval-philosophy/lessons/05-01-augustine-certainty-and-illumination.md) and [5.3](../../ancient-medieval-philosophy/lessons/05-03-time-eternity-and-foreknowledge.md). Take from it here only the structural point: they are in the same act of prayer as the pears.

## Source

*Confessions* 8.12 (29), in E. B. Pusey's translation (Oxford, 1838; public domain), abridged:

> So was I speaking and weeping in the most bitter contrition of my heart, when, lo! I heard from a neighbouring house a voice, as of boy or girl, I know not, chanting, and oft repeating, "Take up and read; Take up and read." … So checking the torrent of my tears, I arose; interpreting it to be no other than a command from God to open the book, and read the first chapter I should find. … I seized, opened, and in silence read that section on which my eyes first fell: Not in rioting and drunkenness, not in chambering and wantonness, not in strife and envying; but put ye on the Lord Jesus Christ, and make not provision for the flesh, in concupiscence. No further would I read; nor needed I: for instantly at the end of this sentence, by a light as it were of serenity infused into my heart, all the darkness of doubt vanished away.

Notice what is *not* in the passage. No new doctrine arrives. Augustine has been a catechumen's son, a listener to Ambrose's sermons, and a reader of Paul; the Romans verses are verses he already had. What arrives is the ability to obey them.

## The argument

Laid out as premises, the narrative is a single case for one conclusion about the will.

1. **The heart is made for God and cannot settle short of him.** "Thou madest us for Thyself, and our heart is restless, until it repose in Thee" (1.1 (1), Pusey). *In words: the [restless heart](../reference.md#the-restless-heart) is the book's diagnosis, stated on page one — not its cure.*
2. **Sin at its purest has no motive outside itself.** Augustine picks the pear theft of his sixteenth year (2.4 (9)) precisely because nothing about the pears explains it. *In words: to see a bent will, take a case where there was nothing to gain.*
3. **Every sin is a botched imitation of God** (2.6 (13–14)): pride imitates height, cruelty imitates the one who should be feared, and a boy breaking a rule for the sake of breaking it imitates omnipotence with, in Augustine's phrase, an imperfect liberty. *In words: the will does not want evil as such; it wants a divine thing on the cheap.*
4. **The Manichees had offered him a way to say none of this was his.** Evil was a foreign dark substance lodged in him. Giving that up is what made a confession possible at all; the philosophical route out — evil as privation, the will as its own deficient cause — is [`ancient-medieval-philosophy` 5.2](../../ancient-medieval-philosophy/lessons/05-02-augustine-evil-and-the-will.md)'s. *In words: before he could confess the theft he had to stop blaming a substance for it.*
5. **The Platonist books took him a long way and stopped one verse short.** He found in them the Word's eternity and divinity, and then: "But that the Word was made flesh, and dwelt among us, I read not there" (7.9 (14), Pusey). *In words: they could lift the mind and could not bend the will, because [what the Platonists lacked](../reference.md#what-the-platonists-lacked) was a God who came down.*
6. **Knowing the good does not produce the will to do it.** Book 8 is the anatomy: the mind commands the body and is obeyed at once, commands itself and is resisted (8.9 (21)); years before, he had asked God for chastity — but not yet (8.7 (17)). *In words: the [divided will](../reference.md#the-divided-will) is one will willing incompletely, not two souls fighting.*
7. **So what changed in [the garden at Milan](../reference.md#the-garden-at-milan) was the will, not the evidence.** *In words: nothing was learned that was not already known; something was wanted that had not been wanted.*
8. **Therefore the book has to be a prayer.** If the decisive move had been Augustine's, the honest form would be a memoir. *In words: the genre is the conclusion of the argument.*
9. **Hence the request in 10.29 (40):** "Give what Thou enjoinest, and enjoin what Thou wilt" (Pusey), more familiar in the rendering "[give what you command](../reference.md#give-what-you-command), and command what you will" (*da quod iubes et iube quod vis*). *In words: if 6 and 7 hold, the only coherent thing to ask of a commandment is that God supply the obedience it demands.*

That last sentence is the hinge of Module 4. In *On the Gift of Perseverance* 20 (53), written around 428–429, Augustine reports that a brother bishop quoted it in Pelagius's hearing at Rome and that Pelagius could not bear it, nearly coming to a quarrel over it — and Augustine's point in retelling the story is that he had written the words before the controversy existed. What the objection was and how Augustine answered it is [4.5](04-05-augustine-against-the-pelagians.md)'s work and the module's boss problem.

## The shape of thirteen books

| Books | What they hold | The confession being made |
|---|---|---|
| 1–9 | Infancy to Monica's death at Ostia in 387, at 56, Augustine being 33 (9.10–9.11) | Of sin, and of the grace that was at work inside the sin |
| 10 | What he is *now*, at about 43: memory, and temptations still live in a bishop | Of the present self — so the story cannot be read as a finished cure |
| 11 | Time and eternity, opening the first verse of Genesis | Of the creature's footing before a God who does not have a past |
| 12–13 | Genesis 1 read for its spiritual senses, ending in the sabbath rest | Of the Scripture that made the man in Books 1–9 |

Read the right-hand column downwards and the book is continuous. Read the left-hand column only, and Books 10–13 look like a different work bolted on, which is how they are usually described.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean: why a whole book about pears).** A modern reader finds Book 2 disproportionate — a page of theology on stolen fruit. Run premise 2 and the proportion is exact. Augustine eliminates motives one at a time, and the eliminations are the argument. Hunger: ruled out, he had better fruit at home. The fruit's quality: ruled out, the tree was "tempting neither for colour nor taste." Appetite: ruled out, they threw the pears to the pigs. What remains is that the act was forbidden, plus one more thing he is careful to record — he would not have done it alone (2.8 (16)). So the episode is a controlled experiment on the will: strip away every good that might have been sought, and something is still sought. Premise 3 names it as the counterfeit of a divine attribute, and premise 6 then explains why knowing all this did not stop him doing worse for another sixteen years.

**Example 2 (where it strains: how good is the garden as evidence?).** Augustine is telling, at about 43 and as a bishop, a story about himself at 31, and the telling is saturated with Scripture and with the *Life of Antony*, which he has just had described to him in the previous chapter. Pierre Courcelle, in *Recherches sur les Confessions de saint Augustin* (1950), pressed this hard and argued that the garden scene is a literary composition — the child's voice a device, the fig tree an allusion — rather than a report. The reading has been influential and is not the majority view; most scholars hold that the narrative is heavily shaped by Scripture and by the purpose of the book without being invented. The comparison that keeps the question honest is with the *Cassiciacum* dialogues Augustine actually wrote in the winter of 386–387, whose convert sounds much more like a philosopher on retreat than like Paul's wretched man.

Two lessons, and they point in different directions. As evidence for **what happened in a Milan garden in August 386**, the *Confessions* is a shaped retrospect and should be used with the caution [1.1](01-01-how-to-read-a-church-father.md) asks for. As evidence for **what Augustine held about grace in 397–401**, it is nearly ideal, because the shaping *is* the doctrine: a man who thought the decision had been his would not have shaped it this way.

## Watch out

- **It is not an autobiography, and the gaps prove it.** Augustine never gives the name of the woman he lived with for some fifteen years and who bore his son Adeodatus, though he records the parting as the tearing of a wound. A memoirist supplies the name; a man confessing to God does not need to. Judging the book by what a life of Augustine ought to contain will get its silences wrong.
- **"Our heart is restless" is not an argument for God's existence.** It is the opening move of a prayer to a God already addressed in the previous clause. Used as a premise in natural theology it does work Augustine does not ask of it — and that work belongs to `philosophy-of-religion`. Note also that the English varies by translator (Pusey's "until it repose in Thee"; others "until it rests in you"), so quote a named translation, as [1.1](01-01-how-to-read-a-church-father.md) requires.
- **The book is not Church teaching, and neither is Augustine.** He is a Doctor of the Church, which endorses no particular opinion of his; what the Church holds about grace, and at what level, is given by [`fundamental-theology` 4.4](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-04-the-ladder-of-doctrinal-authority.md) and owned by `creation-grace-last-things`. Late Augustine on predestination goes beyond what the Church later received at Orange II — a point [4.5](04-05-augustine-against-the-pelagians.md) has to make carefully.
- **Don't import the two cities.** *City of God* is a later work with a different question, and Augustine as a political theorist is [`history-of-political-thought` 2.2](../../history-of-political-thought/lessons/02-02-augustine-the-two-cities.md). Nothing in the *Confessions* is about the state.

## One-liner

> The *Confessions* is not a story about how Augustine found God; it is a prayer arguing, by its own form, that the finding ran the other way.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢) *(Exegetical.)*** Close reading. *Confessions* 2.4 (9) and 2.8 (16), Pusey (public domain):

> For I stole that, of which I had enough, and much better. Nor cared I to enjoy what I stole, but joyed in the theft and sin itself. A pear tree there was near our vineyard, laden with fruit, tempting neither for colour nor taste. To shake and rob this, some lewd young fellows of us went, late one night (having according to our pestilent custom prolonged our sports in the streets till then), and took huge loads, not for our eating, but to fling to the very hogs, having only tasted them. And this, but to do what we liked only, because it was misliked. … For had I then loved the pears I stole, and wished to enjoy them, I might have done it alone. … But since my pleasure was not in those pears, it was in the offence itself, which the company of fellow-sinners occasioned.

(a) List the candidate motives the passage rules out, and quote the words that rule out each. (b) The second extract adds a motive Augustine does *not* rule out. Name it, and say what it shows about the theft that acting alone would not have shown. Two sentences per part.

**P2 (🟡) *(Exegetical.)*** An invented parish-bulletin note (not a quotation from any real writer; the *Confessions* material in it is accurately reported):

> "Augustine's own account should settle the matter for us. He heard a child chanting 'take up and read', he opened Paul at Romans 13, he read the verse — and he changed. That is the pattern: get the word of God in front of a person, let them weigh it honestly, and the decision follows. Conversion is a choice, and Augustine made his that afternoon in the garden."

(a) The note's summary of 8.12 is factually right. Name the single premise of the lesson's reconstruction that it nonetheless denies, and state that premise. (b) Give one detail *from the Source passage itself* and one from elsewhere in Book 8 that the note has to explain away. 150 words or fewer in total.

**P3 (🔴, optional) *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)*** Courcelle argued that the garden scene is a literary composition rather than a report.

(a) State what that thesis does and does not claim, and name the two features of the narrative it leans on. One or two sentences.
(b) A writer wants to cite the garden scene to establish that Augustine, in 397–401, held that conversion is God's work rather than the convert's. In 150 words, any verdict: say what the literary-composition thesis does to that use of the passage, and what it would take to defeat the citation.

<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical — strict.)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** at least three, each with its words. (i) **Want or hunger** — "I stole that, of which I had enough, and much better." (ii) **The fruit's attractiveness** — the tree was "tempting neither for colour nor taste." (iii) **Appetite, or any use of the loot** — "not for our eating, but to fling to the very hogs, having only tasted them," and "Nor cared I to enjoy what I stole." Credit for naming the residue Augustine leaves standing: the act was done "only, because it was misliked."

**Must hit, strict (b):** **the company of the accomplices** — "since my pleasure was not in those pears, it was in the offence itself, which the company of fellow-sinners occasioned." It shows that the pleasure was not in possessing anything but in the shared transgression: Augustine's own test is counterfactual — had the fruit been the point, "I might have done it alone" — so the fact that he would not have done it alone locates the attraction in the offence rather than in the object.

**Wrong turns:** listing "peer pressure" as a motive Augustine *rules out* (he does the opposite: he keeps it, as a motive that is not a motive for the fruit); treating "tempting neither for colour nor taste" as a claim that the pears were bad fruit, when the point is that their quality is irrelevant either way; supplying a psychological diagnosis (adolescence, boredom) that the text does not state.

**Model answer:** (a) He rules out need — he had "enough, and much better"; the fruit's appeal — "tempting neither for colour nor taste"; and any enjoyment of the takings — they were thrown "to the very hogs," and he "cared not to enjoy" what he stole. What is left is the forbiddenness: he did it "only, because it was misliked." (b) The company of fellow-sinners, which he keeps rather than eliminates. Since he says he "might have done it alone" had the pears been the point, his refusal to do it alone shows that what attracted him was the shared offence itself, not anything the theft got him.

---

**P2** *(Exegetical — strict.)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** premise 7 — that what changed in the garden was the *will*, not the evidence — or, equivalently, premise 6, that knowing the good does not by itself produce the will to do it. The note reconstructs the scene as information plus honest weighing yielding a decision, which is precisely the model Book 8 is written to refute. Credit for adding premise 8: if the note's account were right, the book's form as a prayer of thanks would be unmotivated.

**Must hit, strict (b):** from the Source, any one of — he was already "speaking and weeping in the most bitter contrition," so the deliberation had been going on and had not produced a decision; he took the voice as "a command from God," not as evidence to be weighed; the change is described as something infused ("by a light as it were of serenity infused into my heart"), and the doubt "vanished," not was resolved. From elsewhere in Book 8, any one of — the divided will of 8.9 (21), where the mind commands itself and is not obeyed; the earlier prayer of 8.7 (17) for chastity, but not yet; the fact that the Romans text was already familiar, so no new information arrived.

**Wrong turns:** answering that the note misreports the facts — it does not, which is the point of the problem; naming premise 1 (the restless heart), which the note can accept; arguing the theological question of whether conversion is a choice, which is `creation-grace-last-things`'s and not asked here.

**Model answer:** (a) It denies premise 7: that what changed in the garden was the will and not the evidence. On the note's account the scene is information honestly weighed, and the decision follows — exactly the model Book 8 exists to deny, since by premise 6 knowing the good does not by itself produce willing it. (b) From the Source: Augustine is already weeping in "bitter contrition" before the voice comes, so weighing had been happening without producing a decision; and he treats the chant as "a command from God," reading a text he already knew. From elsewhere in Book 8: at 8.9 (21) the mind commands itself and is resisted, and at 8.7 (17) he asks for chastity — but not yet. Both describe a man who has the information and cannot will.

---

**P3** *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** the thesis claims that the scene as narrated is a literary construction — composed, shaped by Scripture and by the *Life of Antony*, its details (the chanting voice, the tree) functioning as devices — rather than a report of events in that form. It does **not** claim that Augustine was not converted, that he is lying, or that nothing happened in 386. The two features it leans on: the density of scriptural and literary allusion in the narrative, and the distance between this account and Augustine's own contemporary writings from Cassiciacum, where the convert sounds like a philosopher on retreat. Credit for naming the date gap: the narrator is about 43 writing about himself at 31.

**Must hit, any verdict (b):** separate the two things a citation of the scene might establish — what happened in 386, and what Augustine held when he wrote. Note that the literary-composition thesis attacks only the first, and that shaping is evidence *for* the second, since a text's shape reveals its author's commitments. Then say what would actually defeat the citation: a showing that the theology of Book 8 is peculiar to the narrative and absent from what Augustine was writing around it — *To Simplician* (396) being the obvious place to test — or that the passage does not bear the reading given it, or that a later hand altered it. Either verdict passes provided the two claims are kept apart and the test named is one that could be run.

**Wrong turns:** treating Courcelle's thesis as showing the *Confessions* unreliable about Augustine's theology — the opposite follows; treating the thesis as settled, or as refuted, when it is neither; answering with whether the conversion "really happened," which no party here disputes; arguing the doctrine of grace itself.

**Model answer (b), one of several:** The thesis bites on the wrong claim. It says the garden scene is composed rather than reported, which bears on what occurred in August 386; the citation is about what Augustine held in 397–401, and for that the composition is the evidence, not the obstacle. A man who thought the decisive move had been his own would not have built the scene out of a command from God, an already-familiar text and a light infused into the heart, nor set it inside a book addressed to God throughout. To defeat the citation you would have to show that this theology is peculiar to the narrative — absent from the works Augustine was writing at the same time, such as *To Simplician* — or that the passage will not carry the reading. Showing that the scene is stylized does neither.

## Flashback

**From Lesson [4.1](04-01-ambrose-of-milan.md) (Ambrose of Milan):** *(Exegetical.)* A parish music director's programme note prints two texts side by side and labels both "words of St Ambrose": *Deus creator omnium*, and an anonymous office hymn in iambic dimeter, eight four-line stanzas, known from a medieval manuscript that calls it "Ambrosian". (a) Say what separates the two cases, and name the evidence that makes the first the secure one. (b) The same note cites *On the Sacraments* for "Ambrose's own words about the Roman Canon". Name what the writer must declare before that citation can do any work, and give the two views on offer. **Two sentences per part.**

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):** "Ambrosian" became the name of the **metre** — iambic dimeter in eight four-line stanzas, sung antiphonally — rather than a claim about the author, and the imitators are numerous: of the many hymns ascribed to him the 1907 *Catholic Encyclopedia* reports only fourteen judged certainly his and four more probable. So a manuscript label is a genre tag, not a warrant. *Deus creator omnium* is the secure case because Augustine, who was at Milan and heard him, quotes it from memory in *Confessions* 9.12.32 — a datable contemporary witness independent of the hymn manuscripts. (*Aeterne rerum conditor* is normally counted among the genuine, but it is *Deus creator omnium* that the lesson calls the certainty.)

**Must hit, strict (b):** he must declare **which view of the work's authorship he is assuming**, because whether it is evidence for Ambrose or for a listener turns on it. The two views: the older doubt, reported in the 1907 *Catholic Encyclopedia* from Bardenhewer, that the six books are *On the Mysteries* indiscreetly published by some hearer of Ambrose; and the twentieth-century majority after Faller and Botte, that it is a stenographic record of Ambrose's own catechesis. Credit for the stake: either way it is the earliest Latin witness to a form of the Roman Canon, but only on the second view is it Ambrose's own wording.

**Wrong turns:** calling the anonymous hymn a forgery — nobody claims one, and "Ambrosian" names a metre; treating the *Catholic Encyclopedia*'s count of fourteen as a ruling of the Church rather than a scholarly estimate; concluding that *On the Sacraments* may not be cited, when the requirement is a declared assumption, not silence; answering (a) with the hymns' role in the basilica crisis of 385–386, which shows what the singing was for and nothing about who wrote which text.

**Model answer:** (a) The manuscript's "Ambrosian" describes the metre — iambic dimeter, eight four-line stanzas — not the author, and imitators abound; only fourteen of the ascribed hymns are judged certainly his. *Deus creator omnium* is secure because Augustine, who heard Ambrose at Milan, quotes it from memory at *Confessions* 9.12.32. (b) He must say which view of the authorship of *On the Sacraments* he assumes. Either it is *On the Mysteries* published indiscreetly by a hearer, as Bardenhewer held, or it is a stenographic record of Ambrose's own catechesis, the twentieth-century majority after Faller and Botte — and the citation is evidence for Ambrose only on the second.
