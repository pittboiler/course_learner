# The Church Fathers · Lesson 1.2: 1 Clement and the Didache

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 1: Reading the Fathers, and the first witnesses · Builds on: [1.1 How to read a Church Father](01-01-how-to-read-a-church-father.md) · Unlocks: [1.3 Ignatius of Antioch](01-03-ignatius-of-antioch.md)

## Why this matters

Nearly every sentence anyone writes about "the early Church around the year 100" runs through two documents: a letter from Rome to the church at Corinth, and a short manual telling a congregation how to baptize, fast, pray and eat together. They are our earliest non-canonical evidence for how Christians organized themselves and what they did on Sunday, and both get quoted hard in every direction. They are also the cleanest case of the gap [1.1](01-01-how-to-read-a-church-father.md) was about: what a text shows, and what it is made to show.

## The idea

### Two documents and one manuscript

[1 Clement](../reference.md#1-clement) is a letter of sixty-five short chapters, sent from the church of God sojourning at Rome to the church of God sojourning at Corinth. Its occasion is stated plainly: the Corinthians have deposed presbyters who had served blamelessly, and Rome writes to get them restored. The letter names no author in its text. The manuscripts head it with Clement's name and the ancient witnesses agree — Dionysius of Corinth about 170 (in Eusebius, *Church History* 4.23.11), then Hegesippus, Irenaeus and Origen — but notice how Irenaeus puts it: "the Church in Rome dispatched a most powerful letter to the Corinthians" (*Against Heresies* 3.3.3, ANF, Roberts–Donaldson). The letter is the act of a church; Clement is the hand. Cite it as 1 Clement, and say, when it matters, that the attribution is early and traditional rather than internal.

[The Didache](../reference.md#the-didache) — "The Lord's Teaching Through the Twelve Apostles to the Nations" — is sixteen short chapters and anonymous: [the Two Ways](../reference.md#the-two-ways) of life and death (1–6), baptism, fasting, prayer and the thanksgiving prayers (7–10), travelling apostles and prophets (11–13), the Sunday assembly (14), local officers (15), the last things (16). It is composite — a church order assembled from older material, including a Two Ways tract that circulated on its own.

The two reach us together. Until 1873 the Didache was a title in ancient book-lists, and 1 Clement a text with holes in it, read from the damaged fifth-century Codex Alexandrinus alone. In that year Philotheos Bryennios, a Greek Orthodox metropolitan, found an eleventh-century Greek manuscript in the patriarchal library at Constantinople — [the Jerusalem Codex](../reference.md#the-jerusalem-codex) — with both complete. He published the full 1 Clement in 1875 and the Didache in 1883.

### Dates, given as ranges

**1 Clement: conventionally about 95–97.** That date is an inference, not a datum: it rests mainly on reading the sudden and successive calamities of chapter 1 as troubles at Rome late in Domitian's reign, helped by the letter treating the deaths of Peter and Paul as its own generation's (ch. 5). Thomas Herron argued for about 70; L. L. Welborn allows anywhere from 80 to 140. The 90s is the majority view; say so.

**The Didache: roughly 50 to 150.** The work is composite, so the real question is a date per layer; most scholars place the core early, in the first century. An argument needing the earliest edge of that range stands on a contested reading.

## Source

1 Clement 44, in John Keith's translation (*Ante-Nicene Fathers*, vol. 9):

> Our apostles also knew, through our Lord Jesus Christ, that there would be strife on account of the office of the episcopate. For this reason … they appointed those [ministers] already mentioned, and afterwards gave instructions, that when these should fall asleep, other approved men should succeed them in their ministry. We are of opinion, therefore, that those appointed by them, or afterwards by other eminent men, with the consent of the whole church, and who have blamelessly served the flock of Christ … cannot be justly dismissed from the ministry.

## The argument

Chapters 42–44, in premises:

1. **The sending runs in a chain: God sends Christ, Christ sends the apostles, both missions in order and by God's will (ch. 42).** *In words: the ministry is not an arrangement the Corinthians made, so it is not theirs to unmake.*
2. **Preaching through country and town, the apostles appointed their first converts, tested by the Spirit, as bishops and deacons for later believers (ch. 42).** *In words: the first local officers were apostolic appointments, and the words for them are bishops and deacons.*
3. **Foreseeing strife over the episcopate, the apostles provided that when these men died, other approved men should succeed them (ch. 44).** *In words: succession was built in against exactly this quarrel.*
4. **So men appointed by the apostles, or later by other eminent men with the consent of the whole church, who have served blamelessly, cannot justly be dismissed (ch. 44).** *In words: lawful appointment plus blameless service is the test, and a vote does not override it.*
5. **Therefore the Corinthian deposition is unjust.** The rebels are told to submit to the presbyters, and whoever caused the trouble is invited to leave the city rather than prolong the quarrel (chs. 54, 57).

Two features matter more than the conclusion.

**The vocabulary has not settled.** Chapter 42 names two offices, bishops and deacons. Chapter 44 opens on the office of the episcopate and closes by calling the same men presbyters; chapters 47 and 57 speak of presbyters only. Nowhere is there a bishop of Corinth, and the leadership is always plural.

**The argument is built from Scripture and order, not from Rome's office.** Chapter 40 sets out high priest, priests, Levites and layman, each with his own place; chapter 43 retells the blossoming of Aaron's rod as God settling a quarrel over priesthood in advance. The warrant is the pattern in Scripture. Only near the end does the letter speak with weight of its own: it warns that whoever disobeys what God has said through it stands in danger (ch. 59), and it sends named messengers to bring word back (chs. 63, 65). What that establishes about Roman authority is argued in `apologetics-catholic-claims`; the skill here is seeing which sentences carry the weight.

## The offices at a glance

[Three texts, three vocabularies](../reference.md#bishops-presbyters-and-deacons-around-100):

| Text | Officers named | Words to look at | What it does not say |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1 Clement 42, 44, 47** (Rome to Corinth, c. 95–97) | bishops and deacons; the same men called presbyters | "the office of the episcopate" and "presbyters" inside one chapter (44) | that anyone presides alone at Corinth or at Rome |
| **Didache 15** (a church order, c. 50–150) | bishops and deacons, beside prophets and teachers | "Therefore, appoint for yourselves bishops and deacons worthy of the Lord … for they also render to you the service of prophets and teachers" | that presbyters exist, or that one bishop presides |
| **Ignatius, *Smyrnaeans* 8** (to Smyrna, c. 107–140) | one bishop, a presbytery, deacons | "See that you all follow the bishop, even as Jesus Christ does the Father, and the presbytery as you would the apostles; and reverence the deacons" | how old the arrangement is, or how widely it held |

Between the first two rows and the third something changes: two offices, with bishop and presbyter interchangeable, become three, with one bishop over the others. Two readings are live. On the first, the threefold order is apostolic and only becomes *visible* in Ignatius, since a text argues for what is challenged and stays silent about what nobody disputes. On the second, it is a post-apostolic development, first in Syria and Asia, with presbyter-bishops as the original form and one of them coming to preside. Catholic and Orthodox readers generally take the first, many Protestant historians the second, and scholars in all three traditions hold positions in between. Both must explain the same evidence: the Didache telling a congregation to appoint its own officers, 1 Clement's plural leadership and its insistence on lawful succession, and Ignatius's vehemence, itself a sign that someone needed persuading. Finding the crux — after settling which recension of Ignatius you are quoting, a prior question taken up in [1.3](01-03-ignatius-of-antioch.md) — is this module's boss problem. The same period read as history is [`church-history` 1.2](../../church-history/lessons/01-02-bishops-canon-and-the-parting-of-the-ways.md).

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean: a Sunday from the Didache).** Read chapters 7 through 14 and the text hands you a church. (Quotations: M. B. Riddle, *Ante-Nicene Fathers*, vol. 7.)

*Baptism* (7): in the name of the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit, in living — that is, running — water if there is any; in other water if not; in warm if cold is unavailable; failing that, pour water three times on the head. The formula is fixed; the mode is ranked by preference, and the fallback is given without apology. Baptizer and candidate fast beforehand.

*Fasting and prayer* (8): not Mondays and Thursdays, as the hypocrites do, but Wednesdays and Fridays — a discipline defined against the synagogue's — and the Lord's Prayer three times a day, close to Matthew's, with a doxology giving God the power and the glory.

*Thanksgiving* (9–10): prayers over the cup first and then the broken bread — "We thank you, our Father, for the holy vine of David Your servant, which You made known to us through Jesus Your Servant" — with the scattered grain gathered into one loaf as a figure of the Church, and only the baptized admitted.

*The Lord's day* (14): gather, break bread and give thanks, having first confessed your transgressions so that the sacrifice may be pure, with those at odds reconciled beforehand, and Malachi's pure offering applied to it.

**Example 2 (where it strains: are chapters 9 and 10 the Eucharist?).** The course's first genuinely open question, so run it properly: sort the text into present and absent.

*Present:* thanksgiving, *eucharistia*, as the name of the thing; a fixed order of cup and bread; a rule excluding the unbaptized, hard to explain for an ordinary supper; and in chapter 14 bread broken with thanksgiving on the Lord's day, after confession, called a sacrifice.

*Absent:* any institution narrative, any mention of the body and blood or of the Lord's death, any word said over the elements. The order is cup before bread, unlike 1 Corinthians 11 and the Synoptics. Chapter 10 opens after the participants are filled, which sounds like a full meal.

Three readings survive that list: these are eucharistic prayers whose theology is carried by thanksgiving and gathering rather than by narrative, with chapter 14 showing how the community understood it; or they are the prayers of an *agape*, a community meal, with the Eucharist in chapter 14 or not described at all; or they come from a stage at which meal and Eucharist were not yet separated. Catholic and Orthodox readers usually take 9–10 with 14 and read the whole as eucharistic, leaning on the sacrifice language, the confession and the exclusion of the unbaptized. Protestant readers more often keep 9–10 as table prayers, leaning on the missing institution words and chapter 10's full meal. The argument is less about what the words mean than about which chapter interprets which — naming that is the exegesis; deciding it belongs to `apologetics-catholic-claims`, and the doctrine to `sacraments-and-liturgy`.

One authority note, since these texts attract the mistake: neither is Scripture. 1 Clement was read aloud in the assembly at Corinth in the 170s and stands in Codex Alexandrinus after Revelation, and the Church still did not receive it into the canon ([`fundamental-theology` 3.3](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/03-03-the-canon-as-defined.md)). Both are witnesses — a great deal, and not teaching.

## Watch out

- **You might think 1 Clement 42 shows bishops in the later sense.** Same word, different job. Before about 150, check what *episkopos* means in the text in front of you.
- **You might think a silence settles something.** These documents are short and occasional, so most silences prove nothing. A silence exactly where a later writer would certainly have spoken — 1 Clement on a bishop of Corinth, in a letter about who may be deposed — is worth noticing, and still weaker than a statement.
- **You might think either text licenses "the earliest Christians did X".** Each shows one community at one moment: Rome writing to Corinth, and a manual probably Syrian.

## One-liner

> Around the year 100 the offices had not yet fixed their names: read these two texts for what their own words carry, and let the arguments about what they prove come afterwards.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢) *(Exegetical.)*** 1 Clement 24–26 argues for the resurrection from nature. Chapter 24 has already said that God shows a coming resurrection in things that recur: night giving way to day, and the sown grain that dissolves in the ground and rises as many. Then comes this, with the sentence that opens chapter 26 (John Keith, *Ante-Nicene Fathers*, vol. 9):

> Let us consider that wonderful sign [of the resurrection] which takes place in eastern lands, that is, in Arabia and the countries round about. There is a certain bird which is called a phœnix. This is the only one of its kind, and lives five hundred years. And when the time of its dissolution draws near that it must die, it builds itself a nest of frankincense, and myrrh, and other spices, into which, when the time is fulfilled, it enters and dies. … Do we then deem it any great and wonderful thing for the Maker of all things to raise up again those that have piously served Him in the assurance of a good faith, when even by a bird He shows us the mightiness of His power to fulfil His promise?

(a) Set out the argument of chapters 24–26 in numbered premises, and say what job the phoenix does in it: a proof, an illustration inside a larger step, or a premise the conclusion cannot do without. Point to the words that decide.
(b) There is no phoenix. In two sentences, say what that does to the argument as Clement frames it, and which test from [1.1](01-01-how-to-read-a-church-father.md) tells you whether it touches his standing as a witness.

**P2 (🟡) *(Exegetical.)*** A note invented for this problem, from a parish bulletin:

> "This week's adult formation takes up the *Didache*, written around AD 50 — earlier than most of the New Testament. It proves that from the very beginning Christians celebrated the Mass on Sunday exactly as we do, and that the apostles left the churches in the hands of bishops and deacons who were already what our bishops and deacons are."

Take the three claims — the date, the Mass, the officers — one at a time. For each, say what the Didache does support and what it does not, pointing at chapters. Do not argue about whether the Catholic reading of the text is correct. 150 words or fewer in total.

**P3 (🔴, optional) *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)*** Until 1873 no known manuscript carried the Didache complete, and 1 Clement was read from one damaged fifth-century Bible. Bryennios's eleventh-century codex supplied both.

(a) 1 Clement's text names no author and speaks in the name of the Roman church. State what the attribution to Clement actually rests on, and what follows for how you cite the letter.
(b) Someone builds a doctrinal argument on a single word of the Didache. In two sentences, any verdict: what does the transmission history oblige him to do before the argument runs, and what should he do if the Jerusalem Codex turns out to be the only Greek witness for that word?

<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical.)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** the premises, in any equivalent wording — P1: God continually shows a coming resurrection in things that happen all the time (ch. 24: night giving way to day, the sown grain dissolving and rising as many). P2: the phoenix is another such sign, and a rarer one (ch. 25). P3: if God shows his power to keep his promise even by a bird, raising those who have served him faithfully is no great thing for the Maker of all (ch. 26, the argument running from the lesser case to the greater). P4: Scripture says the same, and chapter 26 turns at once to it. ∴ C: we shall rise. The phoenix is an **illustration inside the lesser-to-greater step**, not a proof and not load-bearing. Deciding words: it is introduced as a *sign*, one item in the class opened at chapter 24, where day and night are said to declare a resurrection, and chapter 26's "when even by a bird" marks it as the lesser term; the conclusion is then carried by "the Maker of all things" and by the scriptural texts quoted immediately after.

**Must hit, strict (b):** the falsehood removes one illustration from a list whose other members (day and night, the sown seed) are untouched, and leaves the conclusion resting on God's power plus the scriptural promise — so the argument is weakened as rhetoric, not refuted. On the standing question: the truth of the phoenix is not a matter of faith and morals, so it falls outside the scope of the consent clause read in [1.1](01-01-how-to-read-a-church-father.md), and in any case a Father's opinion is a witness and never teaching. Credit for adding that Clement is repeating a commonplace of ancient natural history that Herodotus and Pliny also report, which is what you expect of an ancient writer.

**Wrong turns:** treating the phoenix as Clement's *proof*, so that a false bird refutes the chapter — the text has already given two other signs and is about to quote Scripture; reading chapter 24's day-and-night as decoration rather than as the first member of the class; sliding into whether the resurrection is true, which is not asked.

**Model answer:** (a) P1 God shows a future resurrection in ordinary recurrences — night and day, the buried seed (ch. 24). P2 The phoenix is a further, rarer sign of the same power (ch. 25). P3 If God shows that power even by a bird, then raising those who served him faithfully is nothing great for the Maker of all (ch. 26). P4 Scripture promises exactly this (ch. 26). ∴ C We shall rise. The phoenix is an illustration inside P3's lesser-to-greater move; "sign" and "even by a bird" decide it. (b) It costs Clement one illustration and leaves P1's other signs and P4's Scripture standing, so his conclusion is untouched by it. It says nothing about his standing, because the natural history of birds is not a matter of faith and morals, and his own opinion was never teaching in the first place.

---

**P2** *(Exegetical.)*

**Must hit, strict:** all three claims, each with chapters.
*Date:* the range is roughly 50–150 for a composite text, so "around AD 50" states the earliest edge of a contested range as a fact; most scholars place the core early, which is not the same claim. Credit for adding that the complete text was not published until 1883.
*The Mass:* chapter 14 does give a Lord's-day assembly with breaking of bread, thanksgiving, prior confession and sacrifice language; chapters 9–10 give prayers with no institution narrative, no words over the elements, cup before bread, and a meal at which people are filled. Whether 9–10 are eucharistic in the later sense is precisely what is disputed, so "exactly as we do" outruns the text.
*The officers:* chapter 15 tells the congregation to appoint bishops and deacons *for itself*; it does not say the apostles appointed them, names no presbyters and no presiding bishop, and defines the officers by the service of prophets and teachers whom they are coming to replace. (1 Clement, not the Didache, is the text that argues from apostolic appointment.)

**Wrong turns:** deciding whether the Catholic reading is right, which the problem forbids and which belongs to `apologetics-catholic-claims`; saying the Didache "disproves" the Mass, which is the same overreach with the sign flipped; reading chapter 14 back into 9–10 as though it described the same rite without argument; importing Ignatius's threefold order into Didache 15.

**Model answer:** The date is the earliest edge of a 50–150 range for a composite work, given as fact; most place the core early, and the complete text was not published until 1883. On the Mass, ch. 14 supports a Sunday assembly with breaking of bread, thanksgiving, confession and sacrifice language; chs. 9–10 lack any institution narrative or words over the elements, put cup before bread, and follow a meal, so whether they are the Eucharist in the later sense is exactly what scholars dispute — "exactly as we do" is not supported either way. On the officers, ch. 15 has the congregation appoint bishops and deacons who take over from prophets and teachers; it says nothing of apostolic appointment, presbyters, or one presiding bishop.

---

**P3** *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** the attribution is **external and early, not internal** — manuscript headings plus a chain of witnesses beginning with Dionysius of Corinth about 170 (in Eusebius, *Church History* 4.23.11), then Hegesippus, Irenaeus and Origen — and Irenaeus in particular credits the *church* of Rome with sending it. So cite it as "1 Clement" with chapter and translation, note that the letter is written in the name of the Roman church, and do not build an argument on Clement's personal office that the text never claims.

**Must hit, any verdict (b):** two moves, whatever he concludes. First, name what a single complete witness costs him: with no second Greek manuscript there is nothing to collate against, so a scribal slip or a copyist's normalization cannot be excluded on textual grounds, and an eleventh-century copy sits a thousand years from the composition. Second, say what independent checks remain and use them — the fragmentary old Latin of the Two Ways, the ancient versions, and the later church orders that reuse the Didache (*Apostolic Constitutions* VII) — and then either scale the claim to what one witness can carry or drop it. Consistent with [1.1](01-01-how-to-read-a-church-father.md): name the edition, and check a critical text whenever one word decides the reading.

**Wrong turns:** treating a single witness as worthless, which would throw out most of antiquity; treating it as equivalent to a well-attested text because "it is the Didache"; answering the textual question by how congenial the resulting doctrine is.

**Model answer, one of several (b):** Before the argument runs he has to establish the reading itself: one eleventh-century manuscript admits of no collation, so he must report the word as the Jerusalem Codex's and check whether any version, the old Latin of the Two Ways, or a later church order that reuses the passage supports it. If it is the only Greek witness, I would let the argument stand but demote it to a probable reading offered as corroboration rather than as a load-bearing premise, and say so in the citation.
