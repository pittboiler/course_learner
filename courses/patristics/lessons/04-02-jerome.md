# The Church Fathers · Lesson 4.2: Jerome

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 4: The Latin Doctors · Builds on: [4.1 Ambrose of Milan](04-01-ambrose-of-milan.md) · Unlocks: [4.3 Augustine: Confessions](04-03-augustine-confessions.md)

## Why this matters

Ambrose taught Milan its sacraments; Augustine will give the West its theology of grace. Jerome did something narrower whose effect outlasted both: he fixed the words the Latin West would hear when Scripture was read aloud, and they held for a thousand years. He also started two arguments that never closed — what makes a translation faithful, and which books belong. On the second the Church did not adopt his answer, which makes him the course's cleanest test of a rule you have from [1.1](01-01-how-to-read-a-church-father.md): a Father's opinion, however learned, is not the Church's teaching.

## The idea

Jerome was born at Stridon on the Dalmatian frontier around 347 — the older reference books say 340–342, and the date is not fixed — and died at Bethlehem in 420. Schooling at Rome; ascetic years in the Syrian desert, where he began Hebrew; ordination at Antioch; then three years at Rome (382–385) as scholar and secretary to **Pope Damasus**, in the imperial Church of [`church-history` 2.1](../../church-history/lessons/02-01-nicaea-and-the-imperial-church.md). There he gathered the circle that shaped the rest of his life: the widow **Paula** and her daughter **Eustochium**, Roman aristocrats who took up asceticism under his direction and later paid for his secretaries and copyists. When Damasus died in December 384 Jerome's position in Rome collapsed under the hostility his satire had earned him. He left, settled at Bethlehem in 386 beside the monastery Paula founded, and stayed thirty-four years. Paula died in 404; his biblical prefaces are addressed to the two women, the last of them to Eustochium alone.

He did two different jobs on the Bible, and running them together is the commonest mistake made about him.

1. **A revision of the Latin New Testament**, at Rome between about 382 and 385, at Damasus's request: the circulating Latin gospels corrected against the Greek. Not a new version. When Augustine thanked him for his "translation of the Gospels," Jerome restated it as *revising* the New Testament (Letter 75.20; his own Letter 112).
2. **A translation of the Old Testament out of the Hebrew**, at Bethlehem from about 391 to about 404, with no ecclesiastical commission at all — at friends' requests and on his own judgement.

[The Vulgate](../reference.md#the-vulgate) — *vulgata*, the common edition — is a later name for a later composite, and not all of it is his. The books he refused to translate came in from the Old Latin, and the Psalter the Church sang was his earlier correction of the Latin against the Greek, not his version from the Hebrew, which never entered liturgical use. What he argues for in almost every preface is the principle behind job 2: [*Hebraica veritas*](../reference.md#hebraica-veritas), the Hebrew truth — for the Old Testament the Hebrew, not the Greek of the Septuagint, is what a Latin translation should be a translation *of*.

## Source

Jerome, Letter 57 (*To Pammachius, on the best method of translating*), section 5, in the *Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers*, second series, vol. 6 (Fremantle, Lewis and Martley; public domain). He is defending himself against the charge of falsifying a letter of Epiphanius that he had translated for a friend:

> For I myself not only admit but freely proclaim that in translating from the Greek (except in the case of the holy scriptures where even the order of the words is a mystery) I render sense for sense and not word for word.

Two things are in that sentence: a [rule](../reference.md#sense-for-sense-translation), and an exception carved out for Scripture. Hold both — he spends the rest of the letter quietly eroding the exception.

## The argument

Jerome's programme, reconstructed from Letter 57 and the prefaces:

1. **The Latin copies in use disagree with each other, and not in small ways.** *In words:* there is no single Latin Bible to defend; there are manuscripts, and they differ.
2. **For the New Testament the remedy is obvious: correct the Latin against the Greek original.** *In words:* the original exists, so use it.
3. **For the Old Testament the Greek is not the original.** The Septuagint is itself a translation, and Jerome rejects the legend that its translators worked in sealed cells and produced identical texts, and with it the claim that they were inspired (preface to Genesis). *In words:* correcting Latin by Greek here corrects one translation by another.
4. **The apostles themselves sometimes quote the Hebrew against the Septuagint.** Jerome's example is Paul at Romans 9:33, whose "stumbling-stone and rock of offense" follows the Hebrew and not the Greek (Letter 57.9). *In words:* the New Testament's own practice licenses going behind the Greek.
5. **Therefore translate the Old Testament from the Hebrew.** *In words:* [*Hebraica veritas*](../reference.md#hebraica-veritas).
6. **A translation renders the sense, not the words** — with holy Scripture excepted, where even word order is a mystery (the Source). *In words:* the rule of Cicero, but not inside the Bible.
7. **Yet the evangelists and apostles themselves rendered sense for sense when they quoted the Old Testament.** Jerome piles up examples across sections 7–11 and concludes that in dealing with the Scriptures it is the sense we have to look to and not the words. *In words:* he has just handed the exception back with one hand while holding it with the other. See [Watch out](#watch-out).
8. **What the Hebrew list does not contain is not canonical.** In the "helmeted preface" to Samuel and Kings (about 391) he gives the twenty-two books and says that "what is not found in our list must be placed amongst the Apocryphal writings," naming Wisdom, Sirach, Judith, Tobit and the Shepherd. In the preface to the books of Solomon (393) he gives such books a real but lesser use: the Church may read them "for the edification of the people, not to give authority to doctrines of the Church." *In words:* two shelves, one canonical and one edifying.

Premise 8 is where he parts company with the Latin Church around him and with Augustine. **The Church did not receive it.** The canon as defined, with its authority level, is [`fundamental-theology` 3.3](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/03-03-the-canon-as-defined.md); the history of the dispute is [`old-testament`](../../old-testament/syllabus.md) 6.1. Neither is re-argued here. The reading skill is: Jerome held 8 as a scholar's judgement, so citing him for it cites a Father, not the Church. It is [an opinion the Church did not receive](../reference.md#opinions-the-church-did-not-receive), like Irenaeus's millenarianism in [2.2](02-02-irenaeus-recapitulation.md) — and it did not stop him being made a Doctor.

### Against Helvidius and the four kinds of brethren

The same philology, turned on a doctrinal question. About 383 a Roman named Helvidius argued from Matthew 1 that Mary had other children. [Jerome's reply](../reference.md#against-helvidius) holds three things: Joseph was Mary's husband putatively, the Lord's "brethren" were his cousins, and virginity is the higher state. The argument is lexical throughout. "Knew her not *till* she brought forth" settles nothing about what came after, because *till* in scriptural usage often marks a limit without implying a reversal beyond it; "first-born" is a legal and cultic term for the one who opens the womb, not a claim that others followed; and in Scripture there are four kinds of brethren — by nature, race, kindred and love (*Against Helvidius* 16), so "brother" cannot decide the question by itself.

Read it as an argument, which is all this course does with it. That Mary remained ever-virgin is Catholic dogma; the acts that carry it and the assent it requires belong to `ecclesiology-and-mariology`, read with the ladder in [`fundamental-theology` 4.4](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-04-the-ladder-of-doctrinal-authority.md). Jerome did not invent the belief; he supplied the exegetical defence the West then used.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean: [a riot at Oea](../reference.md#the-oea-incident)).** A North African bishop had Jerome's new version of Jonah read in church. At Jonah 4:6 the plant that shaded the prophet was no longer the gourd the congregation had heard all their lives. Augustine reports the result to Jerome: an uproar, loudest among the Greek-speakers; the bishop forced to consult the town's Jewish residents, who said the Greek and the old Latin were right; the new reading corrected back, to keep the congregation. Augustine's moral is that Jerome may be mistaken, and that where no living language can check him the danger is greater (Letter 71.5).

Jerome's reply is pure philology (Letter 75.7). The Hebrew word is *ciceion*; the Septuagint made it a gourd, Aquila and the others ivy; the plant is neither, but a shrub that shoots up to the height of a small tree on its own stem. Transliterating would tell a Latin reader nothing; ivy at least says something true of it. Neither man is wrong about his own subject. Jerome is right about the Hebrew. Augustine is right that a Bible is a public text and that changing what a congregation prays is a pastoral act — which is why, in the same letter, he asks Jerome to give the Latins an accurate version of *the Septuagint* instead.

**Example 2 (where it strains: [two Fathers on Galatians 2](../reference.md#jerome-and-augustine-on-galatians-2)).** Paul says he withstood Peter to his face at Antioch. Jerome, following Origen and, he says, every commentator after him, read the scene as a staged rebuke: Peter's withdrawal and Paul's protest were both *dispensatio*, useful play-acting for the audience. The motive was to silence the pagan critic Porphyry, who used the passage to show the apostles squabbling. This defends no lie, Jerome insists; it teaches the honourable exercise of a wise discretion (Letter 75.6, 75.11).

Augustine will not have it. If Paul reported as real a rebuke that was not real, Scripture contains a useful falsehood, and once you admit one there is no principled place to stop: the same move excuses the gospel history about Peter's denial, or David's adultery (Letter 82.5). His reading is that Peter genuinely erred and Paul genuinely corrected him. The exchange ran for over a decade, in letters that went astray or circulated before they arrived, and it hurt. Two Fathers, both later Doctors, publicly and sharply at odds — and it is Augustine's reading the tradition followed. A consensus of the Fathers is something you test, not something you assume ([1.1](01-01-how-to-read-a-church-father.md)).

## Watch out

- **You might think the exemption in the Source settles Jerome's practice. It does not.** He exempts Scripture from sense-for-sense in section 5, then argues for six sections that the evangelists quoted sense for sense. The consistent reading: the exemption governs *his* translating, where he will not risk the mystery of the order, while the apostles' freedom shows that fidelity lives in the sense. Do not quote section 5 as if he were a literalist, or section 10 as if he had no exception.
- **You might think "Vulgate" means "Jerome's Bible."** It is a mixed text: his revision of the gospels, his Hebrew Old Testament, the Old Latin for the books he declined, and his earlier Greek-based Psalter. Trent's decree that the Vulgate is "authentic" is a juridical ruling about which Latin edition to argue from, not a claim that Jerome's Latin is inspired ([`fundamental-theology` 3.3](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/03-03-the-canon-as-defined.md)).
- **You might think a famous tag licenses whatever it is quoted for.** *Ignoratio enim Scripturarum ignoratio Christi est* — "ignorance of the Scriptures is ignorance of Christ" — is [Jerome's](../reference.md#ignorance-of-the-scriptures), from the prologue to his *Commentary on Isaiah* (the lesson's own translation of the Latin; the Latin is quoted at Vatican II, *Dei Verbum* 25, citing PL 24, 17). Where it stands it introduces a commentary; it is no ruling on canon, translation, or who may read what.

## One-liner

> Jerome gave the West its Bible and two of its longest arguments: whether a translator serves the words or the sense, and whether a scholar's canon can outrank the Church's.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢) *(Exegetical.)*** Augustine to Jerome, Letter 71, ch. 3, section 5 (NPNF, first series, vol. 1, Cunningham; public domain):

> A certain bishop, one of our brethren, having introduced in the church over which he presides the reading of your version, came upon a word in the book of the prophet Jonah, of which you have given a very different rendering from that which had been of old familiar to the senses and memory of all the worshippers, and had been chanted for so many generations in the church. Thereupon arose such a tumult in the congregation, especially among the Greeks, correcting what had been read, and denouncing the translation as false, that the bishop was compelled to ask the testimony of the Jewish residents (it was in the town of Oea). These, whether from ignorance or from spite, answered that the words in the Hebrew manuscripts were correctly rendered in the Greek version, and in the Latin one taken from it.

(a) State the objection Augustine is actually pressing, and say whether the passage claims that Jerome's rendering is philologically wrong. (b) Quote the words in the passage that show Augustine does not treat the Jewish residents' testimony as settling the philological question. Two sentences per part.

**P2 (🟡) *(Exegetical.)*** An invented parish-bulletin note, written for this problem:

> "St Jerome, Doctor of the Church, taught that ignorance of the Scriptures is ignorance of Christ. He also taught that Wisdom, Sirach, Tobit, Judith and Maccabees are apocryphal and may not be used to establish doctrine. So the Church's own Doctor tells us these books do not belong in a Catholic Bible, and a Catholic who leaves them out is following the tradition."

Name the **two distinct errors**, one about what the first quotation is doing in its source and one about authority, and in each case say what is actually the case. For the second, name the level the bulletin has misassigned and the lesson that owns it. 150 words or fewer.

**P3 (🔴, optional) *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)*** Take as given the summary in Example 2. (a) Name the crux: the single premise Augustine must deny for his reading to stand, and the single premise Jerome must deny for his. One sentence each. (b) Jerome's chief support is that Origen first gave this reading and that the commentators after him, Chrysostom included, all followed it. Augustine answers (Letter 82.23–24) that the names are six or seven in all, that Jerome has himself elsewhere attacked four of them, and that Ambrose and Cyprian stand the other way. Using the criteria of [1.1](01-01-how-to-read-a-church-father.md), assess in 150 words or fewer how much weight the lineage should carry here. Any verdict passes, provided you say what would have to be true of it to count as a consensus rather than a chain of copying, and name one thing in the case that bears on that.

<details>
<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical.)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** the objection is **pastoral and ecclesial, not philological** — a Bible read aloud is a public, memorized, sung text, and changing it in church is an act with consequences for the congregation and for the bishop's standing. The passage makes no claim that Jerome is wrong about the Hebrew; the only testimony it reports against him is the townsmen's, and Augustine immediately marks that testimony as unreliable. Credit for noting that Augustine's own inference, which follows this paragraph, is the weaker one that Jerome "may be occasionally mistaken," not that he is mistaken here.

**Must hit, strict (b):** "**whether from ignorance or from spite**" — the disjunction concedes that the answer may be worthless. Also creditable: "as if it had been falsely translated" in the sentence that follows the quoted passage, where Augustine marks the correction as a forced appearance rather than a finding; and "denouncing the translation as false" as reported speech of the crowd, not Augustine's own verdict.

**Wrong turns:** reading the passage as Augustine's judgement that "ivy" is a mistranslation (he never says so, and Jerome's reply in Letter 75.7 answers the philology unopposed); treating the congregation's reaction as evidence about the Hebrew; missing that "especially among the Greeks" points to loyalty to the Septuagint, which is the real issue between the two men.

**Model answer:** (a) Augustine's point is that a version read in church is public property: the people know it by heart, and a new reading provoked a riot the bishop could not survive. He nowhere says the rendering is wrong about the Hebrew. (b) "These, whether from ignorance or from spite, answered..." — the concession that the informants may be ignorant or malicious withdraws any evidential force from their answer, which is why Augustine's conclusion is only that Jerome might sometimes err.

---

**P2** *(Exegetical.)*

**Must hit, strict, error 1:** the tag is from the **prologue to Jerome's *Commentary on Isaiah***, where it urges the study of Scripture and introduces a commentary. It says nothing about the extent of the canon, and quoting it as if it supported the bulletin's second claim is using a sentence to do work its context does not support. (It is genuine, and it is Jerome's; the fault is the transplant, not the attribution.)

**Must hit, strict, error 2:** the bulletin promotes **a Father's private opinion to Church teaching**, and in a case where the Church decided otherwise. Jerome's canon position is his scholarly judgement, stated in the helmeted preface and the preface to Solomon. The Church's canon is **defined dogma**, enumerated at Trent, Session IV, in one undifferentiated list — [`fundamental-theology` 3.3](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/03-03-the-canon-as-defined.md), with the ladder of levels in [4.4](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-04-the-ladder-of-doctrinal-authority.md). A Doctor's title endorses no particular opinion of his. A Catholic Bible contains those books.

**Wrong turns:** saying Jerome did not hold the canon position (he did, and the course does not pretend otherwise); arguing the merits of the canon dispute, which belongs to [`old-testament`](../../old-testament/syllabus.md) 6.1; calling the Isaiah quotation spurious.

**Model answer:** First, the tag comes from the prologue of the *Commentary on Isaiah* and is an exhortation to study Scripture; it carries no view about which books are Scripture, so it cannot support the sentence it is attached to. Second, the note treats a Father's opinion as the Church's teaching. Jerome's shorter list is his own judgement in the prefaces; the Church's canon is defined dogma, listed without ranks at Trent Session IV. Being a Doctor makes none of his opinions binding, and on this one the Church went the other way. The books belong in a Catholic Bible.

---

**P3** *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** **Augustine must deny** that Paul's report can be true while Peter's conduct was right — i.e. he denies Jerome's premise that the rebuke was staged, because a staged rebuke makes Paul's written statement that Peter "walked not uprightly" false, and a single admitted falsehood in Scripture has no stopping point. **Jerome must deny** that Peter genuinely erred — his premise is that Peter knew the law was abrogated (Acts 10) and so could only have been dissembling for the sake of the Jewish converts present. Equivalent formulations pass; naming the crux as "was the rebuke real?" without saying which premise each man denies does not.

**Must hit, any verdict (b):** (i) state the lineage claim precisely — one reading, first advanced by Origen, taken up by the later Greek commentators including Chrysostom; (ii) give the condition that separates independent witnesses from transmission, e.g. whether any of them reached the reading without Origen in hand, whether it is attested where Origen's influence is absent, or whether one shared polemical motive (answering Porphyry) explains the agreement all at once; (iii) name one feature of this case that bears on the condition — the narrowness of the list, Jerome's own attacks on four of the names, the Latin witnesses on the other side, or the common motive. Any verdict on the weight is acceptable.

**Wrong turns:** deciding the question by counting names, which is the thing being tested; treating Origen's later condemnation as by itself voiding his exegesis (a separate question, [2.5](02-05-clement-of-alexandria-and-origen.md)); answering (b) with a verdict and no condition; arguing which reading is true, which is (a)'s business and not (b)'s.

**Model answer (b), one of several:** The lineage is real but not yet a consensus. The witnesses are Greek and downstream of Origen, Chrysostom included, so they may be one opinion transmitted rather than several arrived at — and they share a motive, since the reading was framed to answer Porphyry's charge that the apostles quarrelled, which explains agreement without independent judgement. For 1.1's purposes it would have to be attested outside that dependency. Two things in the case tell against it: Augustine notes the list runs to six or seven names, four of which Jerome himself has attacked elsewhere, and he can put Ambrose and Cyprian on the other side. So the lineage shifts the burden onto Augustine and no further.

## Flashback

**From Lesson [3.6](03-06-john-chrysostom.md) (John Chrysostom):** *(Exegetical.)* An editor assembling an anthology of patristic preaching wants to include selections from the eight homilies John Chrysostom preached at Antioch in 386 and 387 against Christians who were attending synagogue — sustained invective against the synagogue and against the Jews, excerpted and reprinted in the twentieth century by the Nazi party to enlist German and Austrian Christians in antisemitism. His draft headnote says three things, all of them true: the register belongs to the recognized ancient genre of formal invective; Chrysostom used comparable abuse on heretics and lax Christians; the audience was his own congregation, not Jews.

(a) Name the question those three statements answer and the different question they leave unanswered, and say what it is about the homilies that makes the second question live. (b) This course fixes an order for presenting such a text. State it, and say what the ordering prevents that a true account of the setting, given first, does not. **Two sentences per part.**

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):** the headnote answers what the words were doing when they were preached in 386 — the register, the convention, the target, the occasion. It leaves the reception question unanswered: what the words do when read. That question is live because the homilies generalize well past the Judaizers to the Jews as such, presenting their covenant as finished and their sufferings after 70 as punishment — and it is that content, not the volume, that later writers could lift intact. Credit for the general form: an explanation of why a text sounds like that is not a defence of what it says.

**Must hit, strict (b):** the rule is that the homilies are read as they stand and their later use is named **first**, before any context, genre or defence. What the ordering prevents is the setting arriving first and absorbing the sentences, so that a true statement about the occasion does the work of an excuse; the context explains the register and softens no sentence. Credit for placing the churches' later repudiation, including *Nostra Aetate* 4 (1965) on the charge of collective guilt, with the reception rather than with the mitigation.

**Wrong turns:** treating the genre argument as false — it is true, and it answers a real question; using "he was addressing his own congregation" to excuse what the sentences say, which is exactly the move the ordering is built to stop; inferring from the Nazi reuse that Chrysostom intended it, which reads history backwards; settling anything by his sanctity or his Doctor's title, neither of which is a claim about these pages.

**Model answer:** (a) The three statements answer what kind of speech act this was in 386, and they answer it correctly. They say nothing about what the pages do when read, which is the live question because the homilies argue past the Judaizers to the Jews as such, so that later writers could quarry the claims rather than the style. (b) Read the text as it stands and name its later use first, before context, genre or defence. Put the setting first and it absorbs the sentences before they have been heard, which is how a true explanation ends up functioning as a defence.

</details>
