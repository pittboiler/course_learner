# The Church Fathers · Lesson 1.4: Justin Martyr

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 1: Reading the Fathers, and the first witnesses · Builds on: [1.1](01-01-how-to-read-a-church-father.md), [1.3](01-03-ignatius-of-antioch.md) · Unlocks: [2.1](02-01-irenaeus-against-the-gnostics.md), [2.2](02-02-irenaeus-recapitulation.md)

## Why this matters

Ignatius wrote to churches. Justin wrote to the emperor. He is the first Christian we can read who argues with the outside world in its own idiom, and the first who tells that world what Christians do on a Sunday morning — the earliest full description of Christian worship we possess, Rome around 155. He also invented the move every later Christian thinker has had to accept, refuse or qualify: Greek philosophy was not simply darkness, because the Word who became Jesus had sown seed in Socrates. And the same man, turning to a Jewish interlocutor, wrote pages later Christians quarried for something uglier. Both belong to him.

## The idea

Justin was born early in the second century at Flavia Neapolis in Samaria (modern Nablus), of a pagan family. He tried a Stoic, a Peripatetic who wanted his fee up front, a Pythagorean who demanded music and geometry first, and finally a Platonist — until, in the opening scene of the *Dialogue with Trypho* (chs. 2–8), an old man by the sea unpicked his Platonism and pointed him to the prophets. "Straightway a flame was kindled in my soul," he says; "thus, and for this reason, I am a philosopher." He kept the philosopher's cloak after baptism, taught in a rented room above a Roman bathhouse, and went on arguing.

That autobiography is also his argument: Christianity is not a new eastern superstition competing with philosophy but **the philosophy the philosophers were reaching for**. Two engines, two audiences:

- **[The argument from prophecy](../reference.md#argument-from-prophecy)**, for the emperor and for Trypho: the Hebrew prophets described a man's life centuries before he lived it, and you can check the books.
- **[The seeds of the Word](../reference.md#seeds-of-the-word)** (*logos spermatikos*), for anyone who reveres Socrates: reason itself is a share in the Word who is Christ, so whatever anyone reasoned rightly already belonged to him.

Both still run in every apologetic tradition since; both, in Justin's hands, show where they strain.

## Source

*First Apology* 66–67 (ANF, Dods and Reith), the [earliest description of the Christian Sunday](../reference.md#justins-sunday-liturgy):

> For not as common bread and common drink do we receive these; but in like manner as Jesus Christ our Saviour, having been made flesh by the Word of God, had both flesh and blood for our salvation, so likewise have we been taught that the food which is blessed by the prayer of His word, and from which our blood and flesh by transmutation are nourished, is the flesh and blood of that Jesus who was made flesh. …
>
> And on the day called Sunday, all who live in cities or in the country gather together to one place, and the memoirs of the apostles or the writings of the prophets are read, as long as time permits; then, when the reader has ceased, the president verbally instructs, and exhorts to the imitation of these good things.

Chapter 65 gives the rest of the shape: prayers, a kiss, bread and a cup of wine mixed with water brought to "the president of the brethren," a long thanksgiving, the people's *Amen*, deacons distributing and carrying a portion to the absent. Chapter 61 gives baptism — instruction, fasting, prayer, then washing "in the name of God, the Father and Lord of the universe, and of our Saviour Jesus Christ, and of the Holy Spirit" — and 67 the collection for widows, orphans, the sick and strangers.

## The argument

The *First Apology* is a legal petition before anything else, and it moves in steps.

1. **A name settles nothing.** "By the mere application of a name, nothing is decided, either good or evil, apart from the actions implied in the name" (ch. 4). *In words:* you acquit the man who denies he is a Christian and kill the man who admits it — not a trial, a spelling test.
2. **So investigate us; here is the evidence.** Chapters 61–67: this is our initiation, this is our meal, this is our collection. *In words:* he opens the doors because the charges are about what happens behind them.
3. **But conduct proves only innocence, not truth — so, proof.** "We will now offer proof, not trusting mere assertions, but being of necessity persuaded by those who prophesied [of Him] before these things came to pass" (ch. 30). *In words:* anyone can be well behaved; only a fulfilled prediction can be checked against a book you already hold.
4. **Where the Greeks agree with us, they borrowed.** Plato took the cross in the *Timaeus* from Moses' bronze serpent, badly understood (ch. 60). *In words:* the resemblances are not coincidence, and Moses is older.
5. **And where they reasoned well without Moses, the Word was already in them.** "Those who lived reasonably are Christians, even though they have been thought atheists; as, among the Greeks, Socrates and Heraclitus" (ch. 46); "each man spoke well in proportion to the share he had of the spermatic word" (*Second Apology* 13). *In words:* reason is not a rival to Christ but a fragment of him.
6. ∴ **Punishing Christians punishes reason.** "Whatever things were rightly said among all men, are the property of us Christians" (*Second Apology* 13).

Premises 4 and 5 explain the same data two different ways, and Justin uses both without reconciling them.

## The corpus, and how sure we are

Lesson [1.1](01-01-how-to-read-a-church-father.md) asked first whether a text is his and how it reached us. Justin is a clean case with one alarming feature.

| Class | Works | Status |
|---|---|---|
| Certainly Justin's | *First Apology*, *Second Apology*, *Dialogue with Trypho* | Not seriously disputed; the *Dialogue* was once questioned, no longer |
| Ascribed by some | *Address to the Greeks*, *Hortatory Address to the Greeks*, *On the Sole Government of God*, *On the Resurrection* (fragments), *Epistle to Diognetus* | ANF listed them as disputed; the consensus is that none is Justin's, though all are early |
| Certainly not his | *Exposition of the True Faith*, *Replies to the Orthodox*, two sets of *Questions*, *Epistle to Zenas and Serenus*, *Refutation of Aristotle* | Post-Nicene. Calvin and others once cited the first as genuine Justin |
| Lost | *Against All Heresies*, a work against Marcion | Known only from Justin's reference and from Irenaeus |

The alarming feature: all three genuine works survive in **one manuscript**, [Paris gr. 450](../reference.md#paris-gr-450), finished 11 September 1364; the only other witness, Claromontanus 82 (1571), is a copy of it. It has real gaps — most of *Second Apology* 2, restored from Eusebius (*Church History* IV.17); the *Dialogue*'s dedicatory letter to Marcus Pompeius; a large stretch at *Dialogue* 74 (Catholic Encyclopedia, 1910, art. "St. Justin Martyr"). There they stand as *Second Apology*, *First Apology*, *Dialogue*; Dom Maran reordered them in 1742 and everyone has followed. Eusebius reports two Apologies to two emperors; many scholars read our "Second" as an appendix to the First. **So "Second Apology 13" cites a numbering imposed by an eighteenth-century Maurist on a single fourteenth-century copy.** That does not make the text unreliable; it means your certainty is the certainty of one line of transmission, and you should say so.

Dating: *First Apology* 46 says Christ was born "one hundred and fifty years ago under Cyrenius," putting the work near 155 (roughly 150–160). It is addressed to Antoninus Pius and his sons, including the young Marcus Aurelius, whom Justin calls "Verissimus the Philosopher" (ch. 1). Around 165, under the prefect Q. Junius Rusticus, Justin and six companions were tried and beheaded at Rome; the *Acts of Justin* records his flat answer to the question where Christians met — above one Martinus, by the Timiotinian Bath.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 — the seeds of the Word at its cleanest: Socrates.** Socrates was charged with atheism and with introducing new divinities (*First Apology* 5); so are we. He was killed for using reason against the demons behind the civic gods; so are we. He "cast out Homer," taught men to seek the God unknown to them, and said it is neither easy to find the Father and Maker of all nor safe to declare him (*Second Apology* 10) — not a pagan sentiment but an incomplete Christian one. Therefore Socrates had a share of the Word; therefore he "was a Christian"; therefore the emperor who honours Socrates and kills Christians is incoherent.

Notice how carefully he bounds it: the philosophers "did not know the whole of the Word, which is Christ, they often contradicted themselves"; "no one trusted in Socrates so as to die for this doctrine" (*Second Apology* 10). Seed and harvest, part and whole — those distinctions do the limiting work. Notice the level too: a second-century construction, never defined; treating "the virtuous pagan is a Christian" as Church teaching is the overstatement [`fundamental-theology` 4.4](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-04-the-ladder-of-doctrinal-authority.md) grades strictly. And he writes before Nicaea had a vocabulary: he "holds [the Son] in the second place, and the prophetic Spirit in the third" (*First Apology* 13), which a fourth-century reader would hear as ranking. Judging him by Nicene standards he did not have is this course's standing anachronism; how the vocabulary got fixed is [3.1](03-01-athanasius.md)'s story, the doctrine `trinity-and-god`'s.

**Example 2 — where the engine strains: the *Dialogue with Trypho*.** What the text says, plainly, first. The argument from prophecy needs the Hebrew Scriptures to be about Christ, which forces Justin to explain why the people who hold those books do not read them so. His answer in ch. 16: circumcision was given "for a sign; that you may be separated from other nations, and from us; and that you alone may suffer that which you now justly suffer" — Judaea's desolation and the exclusion from Jerusalem after 135 read as deserved punishment, "for you have slain the Just One." Chapter 11 makes the law of Horeb "now old, and belongs to yourselves alone," abrogated by a final law, Christ. This is supersessionism with a punitive edge, and it was quarried: the *Adversus Iudaeos* tradition, and later writers with far worse ends, took over both the divine-sentence reading of 70 and 135 and the habit of addressing Jews as a convicted party.

Only now the rest. Trypho is no straw man: courteous, refusing the argument at points, pressing the real objections (a crucified Messiah, the plain sense of Isaiah 7:14), and at the close "particularly pleased with the conference," asking to be remembered "as friends" (ch. 142). Still, the genre is a set-piece dialogue and the Jewish voice is Justin's own. Boyarin's argument that such texts *drew* the border between two religions rather than reporting one is the frame in [`church-history` 1.2](../../church-history/lessons/01-02-bishops-canon-and-the-parting-of-the-ways.md). None of that unsays ch. 16; *Nostra Aetate* (1965) repudiates the charge of collective guilt, and the dialogue with Judaism belongs to `apologetics-foundations`. A patristics student owes the passage an accurate report first.

It also has the sentence the next lessons need. At ch. 100 Justin sets Eve, "a virgin and undefiled," who conceived the serpent's word, against Mary, who received the angel's word with faith and joy — the disobedience undone "in the same manner in which it derived its origin." That is the **new Eve** in its first surviving form; Irenaeus makes it structural in [2.2](02-02-irenaeus-recapitulation.md).

## Watch out

- **You might think the seeds of the Word make philosophy equal to revelation.** His own qualifiers forbid it. He is annexing Greek philosophy, not endorsing it.
- **You might think *First Apology* 66 settles the eucharistic question.** The ANF editors footnoted it as "claimed alike by Calvinists, Lutherans, and Romanists." What it does settle is often overlooked: by about 155 at Rome the meal is restricted to the baptized who believe and live accordingly, it is "not as common bread," and it is bound to the Incarnation by an analogy. Stating each tradition's reading and the words it leans on is this course's job; which is right belongs to `apologetics-catholic-claims`, the doctrine to `sacraments-and-liturgy`.
- **You might think the Sunday description proves a fixed liturgy.** The president "offers prayers and thanksgivings, according to his ability" (*ὅση δύναμις αὐτῷ*) — extempore prayer within a fixed shape, not a set text.

## One-liner

> Justin told the emperor that the Word who seeded Socrates had become the man whose Sunday meal he was about to describe — and told Trypho, with the same machinery, that Jerusalem's ruin was deserved.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢) *(Exegetical.)*** *Dialogue with Trypho* 100 (ANF, Dods and Reith):

> And that He became man by the Virgin, in order that the disobedience which proceeded from the serpent might receive its destruction in the same manner in which it derived its origin. For Eve, who was a virgin and undefiled, having conceived the word of the serpent, brought forth disobedience and death. But the Virgin Mary received faith and joy, when the angel Gabriel announced the good tidings to her that the Spirit of the Lord would come upon her, and the power of the Highest would overshadow her: wherefore also the Holy Thing begotten of her is the Son of God; and she replied, "Be it unto me according to your word."

(a) Which words carry the parallel between the two women, and what exactly is being paralleled — their state, their act, or both? Quote the words.
(b) The passage gives a *reason* for the Incarnation by a virgin. State it in one sentence, in Justin's terms.
(c) Name one thing about Mary this passage does **not** assert. **Two sentences per part, maximum.**

**P2 (🟡) *(Exegetical.)*** An invented parish bulletin note (not a real publication):

> "Saint Justin's account of the Mass around AD 155 shows that the early Roman liturgy was already what we have today. He describes the Eucharistic Prayer, the reading of the Gospel, and the ministry of deacons, and he states plainly that the bread and wine become the flesh and blood of Christ, exactly as the Church teaches. Anyone who says the Real Presence was a later invention need only read *First Apology* 66."

(a) List three things the note claims that the text of *First Apology* 61, 65–67 does not support, and for each say what the text actually says.
(b) Catholic, Lutheran and Reformed readers all cite chapter 66. State the reading each gives and the words in the passage each leans on. Do not say which is right. **150 words or fewer in total.**

**P3 (🔴, optional) *(Exegetical.)*** A student's footnote reads: "Justin Martyr, *Epistle to Diognetus* 5, and *Second Apology* 13 (Paris gr. 450)."

(a) One of the two attributions is wrong. Which, and what should the footnote say instead?
(b) The other citation is to a genuine work, but the parenthesis is doing something the student probably did not intend. Say what a reader is entitled to conclude from it, and what the honest form of the claim is.
(c) Give the one further fact about that manuscript a careful citer of Justin should state when the wording of a passage is the point. **120 words or fewer in total.**

<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical — strict.)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** **both** state and act, and the text says so twice over. The shared *state* is virginity: Eve is "a virgin and undefiled," Mary is "the Virgin Mary." The contrasted *act* is the reception of a word: Eve "having conceived the word of the serpent," Mary "received faith and joy" at the angel's announcement, answering "Be it unto me according to your word." The two verbs and the two words (serpent's, angel's) are the hinge; credit for naming the shared virginity as what makes the contrast of acts legible rather than a contrast of persons.

**Must hit, strict (b):** so that the undoing runs along the same track as the damage — "the disobedience which proceeded from the serpent might receive its destruction in the same manner in which it derived its origin." A virgin's assent reverses a virgin's assent; that symmetry is the reason Justin gives for the virgin birth.

**Must hit, strict (c):** any one of: it does not assert Mary's sinlessness or immaculate conception; it does not assert her perpetual virginity (only her virginity at the Annunciation); it does not call her *Theotokos* or Mother of God; it does not assign her a role in redemption beyond her consent; it does not say she was free from death. Naming the doctrine and noting that it is not in *this* text is the whole skill.

**Wrong turns:** answering (a) with "Eve disobeyed and Mary obeyed" without quoting the words — the question asks which words decide it, and "virgin and undefiled" is the one most readers skip. Reading later Marian doctrine back into the passage for (c), i.e. treating what the Church later defined as what Justin here says: that is the overstatement this course grades strictly. Treating "brought forth disobedience and death" as a claim about Eve's physical motherhood rather than about what her assent produced.

**Model answer:** (a) Both. "Eve, who was a virgin and undefiled" against "the Virgin Mary" gives the shared state; "having conceived the word of the serpent" against "received faith and joy … Be it unto me according to your word" gives the opposed acts. (b) The Incarnation comes by a virgin so that the disobedience "might receive its destruction in the same manner in which it derived its origin" — the reversal must match the fall in kind. (c) It says nothing of her sinlessness; her virginity here is at the Annunciation, not asserted as perpetual.

---

**P2** *(Exegetical — strict.)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** three of the following, each with the text's actual content.
- **"Already what we have today."** Justin describes a shape — readings, preaching, common prayer, kiss, bread and mixed cup, thanksgiving, *Amen*, distribution, collection — not a rite. The president prays "according to his ability," which points to extempore prayer, not a fixed anaphora.
- **"The reading of the Gospel."** The text says "the memoirs of the apostles or the writings of the prophets are read, as long as time permits." *Memoirs of the apostles* is Justin's term, the length is governed by the clock, and nothing marks off a gospel reading as a distinct liturgical act.
- **"He states plainly that the bread and wine become the flesh and blood of Christ."** He says the food "is the flesh and blood of that Jesus who was made flesh," reached by an analogy with the Incarnation and by way of "the prayer of His word." "Become," and any account of how, are the reader's supply. The ANF editors' own note records that three traditions claim the sentence.
- **"Exactly as the Church teaches."** Justin has no language of substance or change; the later formulation is not in the passage. (Credit also for noting that the note's final sentence, "the Real Presence was a later invention," is an apologetic claim the passage is being recruited to settle.)

**Must hit, strict (b):** each reading, each with its words, no verdict.
- **Catholic:** a real change in what the elements are — "not as common bread and common drink," "is the flesh and blood," and the causal "blessed by the prayer of His word."
- **Lutheran:** the flesh and blood truly present with the bread, which is still bread — the passage keeps calling it food from which "our blood and flesh by transmutation are nourished," and says nothing about the bread ceasing to be bread.
- **Reformed:** the Incarnation analogy is the controlling term — "in like manner as Jesus Christ … had both flesh and blood," a likeness, and "we have been taught," a matter of teaching received, with the benefit attached to faith and right living.

**Wrong turns:** deciding between the three readings, which this course does not do (see `apologetics-catholic-claims`); conceding the note's third claim because the sentence sounds strong in English, instead of noting what it does and does not specify; missing that the note's strongest actual support — the restriction of the food to the baptized who believe — is a point it never makes.

**Model answer:** (a) "What we have today" overreads a described shape whose president prays "according to his ability." "The reading of the Gospel" flattens "the memoirs of the apostles or the writings of the prophets … as long as time permits." "States plainly that the bread and wine become" supplies both the verb and the mechanism: Justin says the food "is the flesh and blood," by analogy with the Incarnation. (b) Catholic readers lean on "not as common bread" and "is the flesh and blood"; Lutheran readers on the food that still nourishes as food, with no denial that bread remains; Reformed readers on "in like manner as," an analogy, and on "we have been taught," with the meal restricted to those who believe and live as Christ enjoined.

---

**P3** *(Exegetical — strict.)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** the *Epistle to Diognetus* is not Justin's. It is an anonymous second- or early third-century apology; older editions (including the ANF list) printed it among works "ascribed by some" to Justin, but it is not held to be his. The footnote should read simply *Epistle to Diognetus* 5, anonymous, with the translation named.

**Must hit, strict (b):** a parenthetical manuscript siglum normally means the citer consulted the manuscript, or an edition's reading of it. Here it also unintentionally states something true and important: Paris gr. 450 is the **sole** witness to the three genuine works, so *every* citation of Justin rests on it, and naming it adds no support to this passage over any other. The honest form is either to cite a printed edition or translation (e.g. ANF, Dods and Reith; Otto; Krüger) or to say explicitly that the text of Justin depends on a single fourteenth-century manuscript.

**Must hit, strict (c):** any one of — the manuscript was finished on 11 September 1364 and the only other copy (Claromontanus 82, 1571) derives from it; it is defective, with most of *Second Apology* 2 missing and restored from Eusebius, *Church History* IV.17, the *Dialogue*'s dedication lost and a large gap at *Dialogue* 74; the chapter division and the order "First / Second Apology" are editorial, going back to Maran (1742), against the manuscript's own order. Where wording is the point, the reading may rest on an editor's conjecture, and the edition should be named.

**Wrong turns:** calling *Diognetus* spurious in the sense of forged — nobody put Justin's name in it; it is anonymous and was merely transmitted in his company, which is a different failure mode. Treating the single manuscript as making Justin's text untrustworthy: one line of transmission is a limit on certainty, not a verdict against the text.

**Model answer:** (a) *Diognetus* is not Justin's — it is anonymous, early, and only printed alongside him; cite it as anonymous. (b) The siglum implies manuscript consultation; in fact Paris gr. 450 is the one witness to all three genuine works, so it supports this passage no more than any other. Cite an edition, or say plainly that Justin's text hangs on a single manuscript. (c) That manuscript (1364) is defective — *Second Apology* 2 is restored from Eusebius, and the *Dialogue* has a large gap at ch. 74 — and the chapter numbering and order are Maran's, not the manuscript's.

## Flashback

**From Lesson [1.2](01-02-first-clement-and-the-didache.md) (1 Clement and the Didache):** *(Exegetical.)* Before it reaches the apostles, the letter argues from two everyday pictures. Soldiers (ch. 37, John Keith, *Ante-Nicene Fathers* vol. 9): "All are not prefects, nor commanders of a thousand, nor of a hundred, nor of fifty, nor the like, but each one in his own rank performs the things commanded by the king and the generals." Then the body: the head is nothing without the feet and the feet nothing without the head, the smallest members are necessary to the whole, and all work harmoniously together under "one common rule" for the preservation of the whole body. Chapter 38 draws the application — let each be subject to his neighbour according to the gift bestowed on him, the strong not despising the weak, the rich providing for the poor.

(a) Set the argument of chapters 37–38 out in three numbered premises and a conclusion. **One sentence each.**
(b) Name the kind of warrant these two chapters rest on, and say what the letter is *not* appealing to here. **Two sentences.**

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):** three premises of this shape, in any equivalent wording — P1: in an army not everyone holds the same command, yet each man in his own rank does what the king and the generals order. P2: great and small cannot subsist without each other, as the body's head and feet are useless apart and its smallest members are necessary to the whole. P3: what preserves the whole is that unequal parts work harmoniously under one common rule. ∴ C: so let no one exalt himself; let each be subject to his neighbour according to the gift given him — which, in the letter's setting, means the Corinthians should keep the order they have overturned rather than level it.

**Must hit, strict (b):** the warrant is **analogy from ordinary created and civic order** — the ranks of an army, the members of a body — of a piece with the letter's appeals to the pattern in Scripture (the high priest, priests, Levites and layman of ch. 40, Aaron's rod in ch. 43). What it does *not* appeal to here is Rome's own standing or the writer's office, and not yet to the apostolic appointment of the particular men at Corinth, which is a separate argument made later at chs. 42–44.

**Wrong turns:** reading "one common rule" as one human ruler, a bishop presiding — in the image it is the single order under which unequal members work, and the letter names no bishop of Corinth at all; taking the army ranks as ranks of clergy, when the application in ch. 38 falls on strong and weak, rich and poor; importing the succession argument of chs. 42–44, which these chapters have not yet made.

**Model answer:** (a) P1 In an army the ranks are unequal, and each carries out what the king and generals command in his own place. P2 Great and small need each other, as head and feet do, and the smallest member is necessary to the body. P3 The whole is preserved because unequal parts work harmoniously under one common rule. ∴ C Therefore let no one exalt himself above another but be subject to his neighbour according to the gift he has received — and so the Corinthians should not have displaced their presbyters. (b) The warrant is analogy from the order of creation and of ordinary civic life, the same move as the letter's appeals to the pattern set out in Scripture. It is not an appeal to Rome's authority, nor yet to the apostolic appointment of those particular presbyters, which comes only at chs. 42–44.
