# The Church Fathers · Lesson 4.5: Augustine against the Pelagians

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 4: The Latin Doctors · Builds on: [4.3 Augustine: Confessions](04-03-augustine-confessions.md), [4.4 Augustine against the Donatists](04-04-augustine-against-the-donatists.md) · Unlocks: [5.1 Cyril of Alexandria](05-01-cyril-of-alexandria.md)

## Why this matters

In Rome, around 405, a bishop quoted a line from the book everyone was reading, and a lay ascetic in the room could not bear it. The line was Augustine's prayer at *Confessions* 10.29 — [give what you command, and command what you will](../reference.md#give-what-you-command) — and the ascetic was Pelagius; Augustine tells the story himself in *On the Gift of Perseverance* 20.53. Out of that quarrel came the Latin West's whole vocabulary for grace — prevenient, operative, cooperative, original sin, the bondage of the will — and also Augustine's last books, which the Church did not receive whole. Holding both facts at once is the exercise.

## The idea

Pelagius (at Rome from about 380 to 410) was a layman, probably British, an ascetic teacher admired by serious Christians of the Roman aristocracy. Take his case at the strength his ablest defenders gave it — enough to get him acquitted by a synod.

- **Nature is God's good gift, and a gift is not an alibi.** "I am only human, I could not help it" accuses the Creator to excuse the creature. Augustine grants the target: he opens *On Nature and Grace* (415) praising this man's zeal against that evasion.
- **God does not command the impossible** — a law addressed to someone incapable of keeping it is a taunt. And the claim is about possibility, not fact: he never said anyone had in fact lived sinlessly, only that one *can* — a distinction his opponents kept collapsing.
- **Sin is an act, and nobody inherits an act.** Adam damaged us by example; infants arrive in the condition Adam was in before he fell, and charging a newborn with a crime committed before it existed is not justice.
- **Grace is everywhere in this account.** It made nature free, gave the law, forgives sins in baptism, gave Christ as teacher and pattern; Pelagius anathematized anyone denying grace is needed for every hour, moment and act (*On the Grace of Christ* 2).

It worked: at Diospolis in 415 a synod of fourteen bishops heard the charges and acquitted him. His sharper follower Caelestius, tried first at Carthage in 411, refused to condemn two propositions — that Adam's sin injured Adam only, and that infants are born as Adam was before his transgression — while insisting infants must be baptized and calling the transmission of sin "a matter for inquiry, but not a heresy" (*On Original Sin* 2–3).

## Source

*On the Spirit and the Letter* 4–5 (412), Peter Holmes's translation, *Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers*, first series. His opponents' view, then his own:

> But they allege that such attainments are not made without God's help … because God both created man with the free choice of his will, and, by giving him commandments, teaches him … how man ought to live; and indeed assists him, in that He takes away his ignorance …
>
> We, however … affirm that the human will is so divinely aided … that he receives the Holy Ghost, by whom there is formed in his mind a delight in, and a love of, that supreme and unchangeable good which is God …

The first paragraph is a hostile summary, not a quotation, though it matches what Pelagius published. Watch where the weight falls: there the operative word is *ignorance*, so grace informs; here it is *delight* and *love*, so grace makes you want what you ought to want.

## The argument

### Pelagius on the three faculties

Pelagius's own words, from the lost third book of his *On Free Will* (*On the Grace of Christ* 5, NPNF, Holmes):

> The 'ability' we place in our nature, the 'volition' in our will, and the 'actuality' in the effect. The first … properly belongs to God …; the other two … must be referred to man, because they flow forth from the fountain of the will.

1. **Three things differ: the ability to do good, the willing of it, the doing of it.** *In words: being able, wanting and doing are not the same.*
2. **The ability is God's gift, permanent and inalienable — ours whether we will or not.** *In words: you did not earn your eyes and cannot lose them by misuse.*
3. **The willing and the doing are ours, flowing from the will itself.** *In words: what you do with your eyes is your own credit or fault.*
4. **God's help therefore attaches to the ability, not to the willing.** *In words: God keeps the capacity supplied; the choosing is where we come in.*

∴ **A human being can, at any moment, not sin.** See [Pelagius on the three faculties](../reference.md#pelagius-on-the-three-faculties).

### Augustine's reply

He denies premise 3, and the rest follows.

1. **Fallen human beings keep free choice and lose freedom.** The power to choose (*liberum arbitrium*) is intact; the freedom to love the good (*libertas*) is not — where [`ancient-medieval-philosophy` 5.2](../../ancient-medieval-philosophy/lessons/05-02-augustine-evil-and-the-will.md) hands the will over. *In words: the sick man still chooses; he has lost the taste for health.*
2. **Grace therefore goes first — it is prevenient — and it both works and works with.** It does not wait for a good will to assist; it produces one, shedding love abroad in the heart (Romans 5:5). "He operates, therefore, without us, in order that we may will; but when we will, and so will that we may act, He co-operates with us" (*On Grace and Free Will* 17.33, NPNF, Holmes and Wallis revised by Warfield). *In words: the first stirring is operative grace, done in us; the rest is cooperative, done with us.* See [prevenient, operative and cooperative grace](../reference.md#prevenient-operative-and-cooperative-grace).
3. **Scripture puts the willing on God's side of the line.** Philippians 2:13: it is God who worketh in you, both to will and to accomplish. Pelagius needs that verse to say "to be able to will." *In words: Paul names the very faculty Pelagius reserved for us.*

∴ **The first movement of a good will is itself a gift, not a merit** — otherwise grace is proportioned to something we brought, "and so grace is no more grace" (Romans 11:6).

**The crux is Pelagius's premise 3.** Both men affirm free choice and affirm grace; everything turns on whether God's help reaches the *willing itself* or only the *capacity to will*. Infant baptism, original sin and predestination are downstream of it.

## Map of positions

| Position | Is Adam's sin inherited? | Where does the first good willing come from? |
|---|---|---|
| **Pelagius and Caelestius**, 411–418 | No. Adam harmed by example; infants baptized, but not for inherited sin | From us. God gives and sustains the *capacity* to will |
| **Augustine, c. 394** | Yes | From us. God elects on foreseen faith; "what we believe is our own" |
| **Augustine from 396**, *To Simplician* | Yes | From God. Faith itself is a gift (1 Corinthians 4:7) |
| **Augustine, 426–430** | Yes | From God, to a fixed number chosen before creation, perseverance a further gift |
| **Cassian and the Massilians**, 420s–430s | Yes | Sometimes from us; grace then meets and strengthens it |
| **Orange II, 529** | Yes | From God, the beginning of faith included; predestination to evil anathematized |

Rows two and three matter most: the change of mind in 396 is Augustine's own, twenty years before Pelagius was a public problem.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 — the clean case: Romans 5:12 in the Latin.** Augustine's Latin ended the verse *in quo omnes peccaverunt*, in the Douay–Rheims "in whom all have sinned." Read so, Paul says the whole race sinned *in Adam*, and what passes by generation is not mortality only but guilt — how Augustine uses it at *On Nature and Grace* 9, against Pelagius's claim that "all sinned" means all imitated. The Greek is *eph' hō pantes hēmarton*, and *eph' hō* is now usually read as causal: death spread to all *because* all sinned, which does not by itself say the sin is Adam's. See [Romans 5:12 in the Latin](../reference.md#romans-512-in-the-latin).

How much rested on the Latin? Less than the story implies; three other planks stand. The rest of Romans 5 makes the Adam–Christ parallel without the disputed clause: by one man's disobedience many were made sinners. Ephesians 2:3 says we were by nature children of wrath (*On Nature and Grace* 3). And his heaviest argument is not a proof-text but the Church's practice: infants are exorcised and baptized *for the remission of sins*, and Caelestius conceded the rite while refusing its reason — Basil's move about the Spirit in [3.2](03-02-basil-the-great.md).

**Example 2 — where it strains: the hardening, 396 to 430.** The turn came early and privately. Writing to Simplicianus of Milan in 396, Augustine took up Romans 9 and could not make his own earlier answer work: "In the solution of this question I laboured indeed on behalf of the free choice of the human will, but God's grace overcame" (*On the Predestination of the Saints* 4.8). What broke him was 1 Corinthians 4:7 — what have you that you did not receive? If faith is a gift, election cannot rest on foreseen faith, and his 394 exposition of Romans had said it did.

Thirty years later he pushed the logic to its end. In *On Rebuke and Grace*, *On the Predestination of the Saints* and *On the Gift of Perseverance* (426–429) the elect are a fixed number known before creation, final perseverance is a separate unmerited gift, and nobody knows in this life whether he has it. Monks in southern Gaul, whose lives were ascetic effort, heard a doctrine that made exhortation pointless; Cassian's *Conference* 13 held that a good will sometimes begins from us and grace then meets and strengthens it ([`church-history` 2.3](../../church-history/lessons/02-03-the-desert-and-the-monastery.md)).

Orange II (529) received the core: grace goes before every good movement, the beginning of faith included, and no merit precedes it. It did not receive the late Augustine entire: it says nothing of a fixed number chosen before creation, and its epilogue anathematizes predestination to evil. The late books are a Doctor's opinion, partly received and partly not. What Orange defined, and at what weight, belongs to [`creation-grace-last-things`](../../creation-grace-last-things/syllabus.md) and [`fundamental-theology` 4.4](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-04-the-ladder-of-doctrinal-authority.md). See [opinions the Church did not receive](../reference.md#opinions-the-church-did-not-receive).

## Watch out

- **"Roma locuta, causa finita" is not in Augustine.** The tag is fastened to the close of Sermon 131.10, preached at Carthage, usually dated 23 September 417: *Iam enim de hac causa duo concilia missa sunt ad Sedem Apostolicam: inde etiam rescripta venerunt. Causa finita est: utinam aliquando finiatur error!* In the lesson's own translation: "For on this matter two councils have now been sent to the Apostolic See; and from there replies have come back too. The case is finished — would that the error were finished some day!" The slogan drops the two African councils, the exchange of documents and the prayer, and hides that he was wrong: Zosimus reopened the case within weeks, and it took the council of Carthage of 418 and his *Epistula Tractoria* to close it. See [Roma locuta, causa finita](../reference.md#roma-locuta-causa-finita).
- **You might think Pelagius denied grace.** He affirmed it loudly and was acquitted on the strength of it; the dispute is over what grace *does*. Augustine's diagnosis at *On Nature and Grace* 12: Pelagius's examples — a tongue, wings, legs — are equipment issued with our nature, so his grace is the grace of *constitution*, while the question is the *cure* of a wounded one.
- **We read Pelagius mostly through his prosecutor.** *On Nature* and *On Free Will* survive only in Augustine's and Jerome's quotations, and what survives whole travelled under other men's names: his commentary on thirteen Pauline epistles came down as Jerome's, disentangled by Alexander Souter only in the 1920s.
- **[A Doctor's title](../reference.md#doctor-of-the-church) endorses no particular opinion,** and "semi-Pelagian" is a later label and a verdict — the ancient name was "remnants of the Pelagians", Orange's canons name nobody, and Cassian is venerated as a saint. Treating *The Gift of Perseverance* as Catholic doctrine because a Doctor wrote it is the authority error this course grades strictly.

## One-liner

> Both men said free will is real and grace is necessary; the whole controversy is whether God's help reaches the willing itself, or only the power to will.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢) *(Exegetical.)*** Augustine reports Pelagius answering an objection, *On Nature and Grace* 11 (NPNF, first series, Holmes):

> He then starts an objection to his own position, as if, indeed, another person had raised it, and says: 'A man,' you will say, 'may possibly be \[without sin\]; but it is by the grace of God.' … Now, is it I that reject grace, who by acknowledging the thing must needs also confess the means by which it may be effected, or you, who by denying the thing do undoubtedly also deny whatever may be the means through which the thing is accomplished? … He says, indeed: Whether he confesses it to be by grace, or by aid, or by mercy, whatever that be by which a man can be without sin — every one acknowledges the thing itself.

(a) State, in Pelagius's own terms, what he takes himself to have granted about grace here, and the counter-charge he makes.
(b) Augustine says he read these words with sudden joy and then grew suspicious when Pelagius illustrated with a tongue for speaking, wings for flying, legs for running. Say what the illustrations reveal about which grace he means, and quote the clause in the passage above that left that question open. **Two sentences per part.**

**P2 (🟡) *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)*** Romans 5:12.

(a) Augustine's Latin ends the verse *in quo omnes peccaverunt*, in the Douay–Rheims "in whom all have sinned." State what he takes the verse to assert; state how the underlying Greek *eph' hō* is now usually read; and say what that difference does to what the verse, by itself, establishes.
(b) Suppose the Latin clause is simply set aside. Name **two** other supports the lesson gives for Augustine's claim that infants contract something from Adam, and for each say what it establishes and what it leaves open. Then give a one-line verdict on whether his case stands without the clause. **150 words or fewer for (b); any verdict.**

**P3 (🔴, optional) *(Exegetical.)*** An invented note in a parish bulletin:

> "Our reading group finished *The Gift of Perseverance* this week. St Augustine is a Doctor of the Church, and he teaches there that God has fixed the number of the elect before the foundation of the world, that He gives final perseverance to those and not to others, and that nobody can know which he is. Since the Council of Orange later endorsed St Augustine against the monks of Gaul, this simply is the Catholic doctrine of grace, and anyone who says otherwise is a semi-Pelagian."

Name every error, and for each say whether it is **false**, **overstated as to level**, or **a misuse of a source**. Say also which part of the note accurately reports the book. **150 words or fewer.**

<details>
<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical — strict.)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** he grants that a man can be without sin only *by the grace of God* — and claims that granting a possibility together with its condition is a fuller acknowledgement of grace than his opponent's, since to deny the possibility is to deny whatever would bring it about. The counter-charge is therefore that the *objector* is the one rejecting grace. Credit for noticing he is deliberately indifferent to the label — grace, aid, or mercy, "every one acknowledges the thing itself" — which is exactly what leaves the content unspecified.

**Must hit, strict (b):** tongue, wings and legs are natural equipment issued at creation, so what Pelagius means is grace concerned with the **constitution** of nature, not with its **cure** (*On Nature and Grace* 12) — the grace of having been made, which nobody disputes, and which does nothing for a nature now wounded. The clause that left it open is the one indifferent to the label: "Whether he confesses it to be by grace, or by aid, or by mercy, whatever that be by which a man can be without sin." Accept also "must needs also confess the means by which it may be effected" — both concede *that* there is a means without saying what it does.

**Wrong turns:** saying Pelagius denies grace here — he is affirming it, which is the whole difficulty; reading "or by aid, or by mercy" as a retraction rather than as indifference to the word; answering (b) by quoting a clause that names grace without showing that its content is left unspecified.

**Model answer:** (a) He grants that sinlessness is possible only by God's grace, and argues that admitting a possibility along with its condition acknowledges grace more fully than denying the possibility does — so the charge of rejecting grace rebounds on his objector, and he adds that he does not care whether it is called grace, aid or mercy. (b) The tongue, wings and legs are natural endowments, so his examples secure only the grace that constitutes nature, not the grace that cures it — and the disputed question is the help a wounded will needs to love the good. What left that open is his own indifference to the word: "Whether he confesses it to be by grace, or by aid, or by mercy, whatever that be by which a man can be without sin."

---

**P2** *(a) exegetical — strict; (b) evaluative — any verdict.*

**Must hit, strict (a):** Augustine takes Paul to say that the whole race sinned *in Adam* — all were somehow implicated in his one act — so that what passes by generation is guilt and not merely mortality; this is what he sets against Pelagius's reading of "all sinned" as "all imitated." *Eph' hō* is now usually read causally — death spread to all *because* all sinned — or at any rate not as "in whom." Difference: on the Greek, the verse on its own attests universal sin and universal death without specifying that the sin transmitted is Adam's, so it stops being a one-verse proof of inherited guilt. Credit for noting the mistranslation is not Augustine's doing; he received the Old Latin text.

**Must hit, any verdict (b):** two of — (i) the rest of Romans 5, where the Adam–Christ parallel runs without the disputed clause ("by one man's disobedience many were made sinners"); (ii) Ephesians 2:3, children of wrath by nature, quoted at *On Nature and Grace* 3; (iii) the Church's practice of exorcising and baptizing infants for the remission of sins, which Caelestius conceded while refusing its reason. For each, both halves are required: what it establishes and what it leaves open. Then a one-line verdict, either way.

**Wrong turns:** attributing the mistranslation to Augustine; treating the Greek as settling the *doctrine* rather than the verse — what the Church defines about original sin, and at what weight, belongs to [`creation-grace-last-things`](../../creation-grace-last-things/syllabus.md), not to Greek prepositions; giving supports without saying what each leaves open; calling the infant-baptism argument a proof of the *explanation* when it is evidence of the *practice*.

**Model answer (b), one of several:** Romans 5:15–19 establishes a real Adam–Christ parallel in which one man's disobedience made many sinners, without depending on verse 12's relative clause; it leaves open whether "made sinners" means inherited guilt or inherited consequence. The practice of baptizing infants for the remission of sins, with exorcism, establishes that the Church treated newborns as needing what baptism gives; it leaves open the theological account of why, which is exactly what Caelestius exploited by conceding the rite and not the reason. Verdict: the case stands but loses its knockdown form — the clause turned an argument from converging evidence into what looked like a single decisive text, and without it Augustine has to argue from Paul's larger structure and from the rule of prayer.

---

**P3** *(Exegetical — strict.)*

**Must hit, strict:**
1. "Doctor of the Church, therefore Catholic doctrine" — **overstated as to level.** A Father's opinion is never as such the Church's teaching, and the title of Doctor endorses no particular opinion of its holder. What is taught on grace, and at what weight, is fixed by the Magisterium ([`fundamental-theology` 4.4](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-04-the-ladder-of-doctrinal-authority.md); [`creation-grace-last-things`](../../creation-grace-last-things/syllabus.md)), not by the eminence of an author.
2. "Orange endorsed St Augustine, so this is the Catholic doctrine of grace" — **a misuse of a source.** Orange II (529) received the prevenient necessity of grace and that the beginning of faith is God's gift; it did not adopt the fixed number chosen before creation, nor perseverance withheld from those God could have saved, and its epilogue anathematizes any predestination to evil. Its canons name nobody, so it endorses no author.
3. "anyone who says otherwise is a semi-Pelagian" — **false**, twice over: the term is a much later label, not an ancient one (the ancient name was "remnants of the Pelagians"), and applying it to any Catholic who declines the late Augustine mistakes a disputed question for a settled one.
4. **The accurate part** is the report of what *The Gift of Perseverance* teaches: a fixed number of elect, perseverance as a further unmerited gift, and no certainty of one's own election. Every error in the note is about authority, not about Augustine.

**Wrong turns:** arguing that Augustine's late doctrine is *wrong* — not asked, and this course reads the texts rather than settling the doctrine; denying that Augustine taught what the note reports; saying Orange condemned Augustine, which it did not.

**Model answer:** The report of the book is accurate; every error is an authority error. Calling it Catholic doctrine because a Doctor wrote it overstates the level, since a Father's opinion is not the Church's teaching and the title endorses no opinion. Saying Orange endorsed Augustine misuses the council: it received prevenient grace and the beginning of faith as God's gift, named no one, said nothing of a fixed number chosen before creation, and anathematized predestination to evil. And "semi-Pelagian" is false here — a later label, not an ancient one, and a verdict on a question Catholics may still dispute.

## Flashback

**From Lesson [4.3](04-03-augustine-confessions.md) (Augustine: *Confessions*):** *(Exegetical.)* An invented blurb for a new edition (nobody's actual words):

> "*Confessions*: the first autobiography. This abridgement gives the reader the story itself — Books 1 to 9, from infancy at Thagaste to his mother's death at Ostia — and sets aside the four books of philosophy and Genesis commentary that Augustine appended afterwards. Nothing of the life is lost."

(a) Two of the blurb's claims are about the book's **form**, and both are wrong. Name each and correct it in one sentence. (b) The title carries three senses of the Latin *confiteri* at once. Give them, and say what the abridgement costs Augustine's *argument* — not his biography. Two sentences for (b).

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):** (i) "**the first autobiography**" (with "nothing of the life is lost") — the book is a prayer, addressed to God from the first sentence to the last, with the reader overhearing; it is not trying to supply a life, and its silences show it, since the woman he lived with for some fifteen years, the mother of Adeodatus, is never named. (ii) "**appended afterwards**" — Books 10–13 are not an appendix but the same confession continued: having confessed his past (1–9), Augustine confesses what he still is (10), then what God is and what the Scripture is that made him (11–13). Credit for adding that Book 10, written at about 43 with temptations still live in a bishop, is exactly what stops Books 1–9 being read as a finished cure.
**Must hit, strict (b):** to **acknowledge** a fault, to **praise**, and to **profess** the faith. Held together they force the retrospective structure — each episode told twice, once as what Augustine did and once as what God was doing in it while he could not see it — so cutting the book back to a life story keeps the acknowledgment and drops the praise and the profession. What is left is a memoir with a guilty conscience, which is the form the book would honestly have taken if the decisive move had been Augustine's own; the genre is the conclusion of his argument, so the abridgement deletes the conclusion.
**Wrong turns:** correcting the blurb's *facts* about which books hold what — it has them right, and the error is about what kind of book this is; answering (b) with the contents of Books 10–13 (memory, time) rather than with what their loss does to the argument, which is `ancient-medieval-philosophy`'s material; reading "first autobiography" as a claim about priority to be met by naming an earlier one.
**Model answer:** (a) Not an autobiography: every sentence is addressed to God and the reader is overhearing, which is why the book can leave unnamed the woman of fifteen years who bore his son. And Books 10–13 are not appended: the same prayer that confessed his past goes on to confess his present self, then God and the Scripture that made him. (b) *Confiteri* means to acknowledge a fault, to praise, and to profess the faith, and holding the three together is what makes every episode get told twice — as his act, and as God's work inside it. Cut to Books 1–9 and sold as a life, the book keeps the acknowledgment and loses the praise and profession, so it becomes the memoir it was written not to be.

</details>
