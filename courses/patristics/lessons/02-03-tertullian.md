# The Church Fathers · Lesson 2.3: Tertullian

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 2: The rule of faith, Africa and Alexandria · Builds on: [2.1](02-01-irenaeus-against-the-gnostics.md), [2.2](02-02-irenaeus-recapitulation.md) · Unlocks: [2.4](02-04-cyprian-of-carthage.md), [2.5](02-05-clement-of-alexandria-and-origen.md)

## Why this matters

Tertullian is the most quoted Father who is not a Father. He gave Latin theology the words it still uses for God — *trinitas*, *substantia*, *persona* — and he died outside the communion of the church at Carthage. He is also the writer whose most famous sentences are least likely to be his: three tags circulate under his name in popular histories, homilies and parish bulletins, all three altered, one made to say roughly the opposite of what its passage argues. So this lesson does two jobs. It reads a brilliant, combative theologian who invented a style of argument, and it gives the check of [1.1](01-01-how-to-read-a-church-father.md) its hardest workout so far.

## The idea

Carthage, the second city of the Latin West, around the year 200. Tertullian was born there about 155–160 and died about 220. Almost everything biographical comes from one page: Jerome, *On Illustrious Men* 53, written nearly two centuries later, which calls him a presbyter, the son of a proconsular centurion, and says he "lapsed to the doctrine of Montanus." Timothy Barnes (*Tertullian*, 1971) showed that most of this is unsupported, including the claim that he was the jurist Tertullianus of the *Digest*. What the writings prove is narrower and enough: a Carthaginian of high rhetorical training, at home in Roman legal language, writing Latin of enormous force.

Four works matter, on conventional datings: the *Apology* (197), addressed to provincial magistrates; the *Prescription against Heretics* (c. 200); *On the Flesh of Christ* (c. 206), against docetism; and *Against Praxeas* (c. 213), against modalism.

That last date carries a warning. From about 207 Tertullian attached himself to the New Prophecy, the movement his opponents called Montanism, and *Against Praxeas* 2 says so openly: he believes what he believes, "more especially since we have been better instructed by the Paraclete." The treatise that handed the Latin West its Trinitarian vocabulary was written from inside the movement. That is why the manual tradition files him as an **ecclesiastical writer** rather than a Father ([the distinction is 1.1's](../reference.md#father-doctor-and-ecclesiastical-writer)): antiquity and learning, but not the Church's approval and not, at the end, orthodoxy. Vincent of Lérins made him the standing example of the danger, calling him more eloquent by far than faithful (*Commonitorium* 18, NPNF 2nd series, Heurtley) — see [`fundamental-theology` 5.1](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/05-01-vincent-of-lerins.md). Jerome reports that Cyprian ([2.4](02-04-cyprian-of-carthage.md)) never let a day pass without reading him, and would ask for "the master."

## Source

*Prescription against Heretics* 19, in the *Ante-Nicene Fathers*, Peter Holmes's translation (public domain):

> Our appeal, therefore, must not be made to the Scriptures; nor must controversy be admitted on points in which victory will either be impossible, or uncertain, or not certain enough. … the natural order of things would require that this point should be first proposed, which is now the only one which we must discuss: With whom lies that very faith to which the Scriptures belong.

Read the first clause slowly. It is not a claim that Scripture is obscure, nor reading advice. It is a ruling about **who may be argued with, and in what order**.

## The argument

The title is a legal term. A *praescriptio* in Roman procedure is a plea entered before a case reaches its merits, stopping the action from being heard at all. Tertullian's book is that plea ([the prescription argument](../reference.md#the-prescription-argument), from chs. 15–21, 32 and 37):

1. **The heretics come armed with Scripture, and they are good at it** (15). They rearrange, interpret and occasionally cut; in debate they tire the strong and capture the weak. *In words:* proof-texting is a game both sides can play forever.
2. **A scriptural contest with them therefore produces no verdict** (17–19). *In words:* if every text can be answered with a reading, the onlooker leaves more confused than he came in.
3. **So the appeal must not be made to Scripture first. The prior question is whose faith the Scriptures belong to** (19, the Source). *In words:* settle ownership before you argue about meaning.
4. **Ownership is traceable.** Christ taught the apostles; the apostles founded churches; those churches hand on the [rule of faith](../reference.md#rule-of-faith), stated in full at ch. 13. Doctrine agreeing with the apostolic churches is true, doctrine at odds with them prejudged false (20–21). *In words:* the faith has a paper trail, and the trail is the title deed.
5. **The test is public and anyone can demand it** (32). Let the heretics unfold the roll of their bishops back to an apostle — Smyrna produces Polycarp from John, Rome Clement from Peter — while a church founded yesterday counts as apostolic if it is "akin in doctrine." *In words:* two tests, lineage or agreement, and they pass neither.
6. **Therefore they have no standing to argue from Scripture at all** (37). Tertullian turns it into a property suit: who are you, when did you come, what have you to do with what is mine? I am the heir of the apostles. *In words:* you are not a party to this contract, so put the book down.

**Conclusion:** "what does this verse mean?" is never reached, because the objection is filed first.

Two things it is not. It is not a claim that Christians should not reason from Scripture: Tertullian spends five books doing exactly that against Marcion. And it is not self-executing — everything rests on premise 4, that the rule of faith and the succession really are what he says, which is [2.1](02-01-irenaeus-against-the-gnostics.md)'s territory and which the [syllabus](../syllabus.md)'s Boss problem 2 tests directly.

## Three tags and what the texts say

Each row is a sentence you will meet as his.

| As usually quoted | What the text says | Where | What the change does |
|---|---|---|---|
| "I believe because it is absurd" | Two clauses, neither in the first person. Of the death: "it is by all means to be believed, because it is absurd." Of the burial and rising: "the fact is certain, because it is impossible." | *On the Flesh of Christ* 5 (ANF, Holmes); Latin *prorsus credibile est, quia ineptum est* … *certum est, quia impossibile* | Fuses two clauses, moves "believe" into the first person, and turns a paradox aimed at Marcion into a profession that faith owes reason nothing |
| ["The blood of the martyrs is the seed of the Church"](../reference.md#the-blood-of-the-martyrs) | "The oftener we are mown down by you, the more in number we grow; the blood of Christians is seed." | *Apology* 50 (ANF, Thelwall); Latin *semen est sanguis Christianorum* | A paraphrase, not a quotation. The original has no "martyrs" and no "Church": the subject is Christians under judicial killing, and what grows is more of them |
| ["What has Athens to do with Jerusalem?"](../reference.md#athens-and-jerusalem) as his verdict on philosophy | The question closes a list of parentages: the aeons from Plato's school through Valentinus, Marcion's better god from the Stoics, the soul's death from Epicurus | *Prescription* 7 (ANF, Holmes) | Converts a genealogy of specific heresies into a general ban on thinking, which the next four chapters refute |

The third row is the one that turns Tertullian into a cartoon. Chapter 7 argues that named heresies have named philosophical parents, and the Athens line is its punchline. Then in chs. 8–12 he does what a man opposed to reasoning could not: he takes the heretics' favourite verse, "seek and you shall find," and analyses it under three heads — the thing sought, the time, the manner — to show that seeking has a term. He is hostile to *curiositas*, speculation without a stopping rule, not to argument.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean: running the check on the absurdity tag).** Someone hands you "Tertullian said *credo quia absurdum* — I believe because it is absurd." Run [the five steps](../reference.md#how-to-check-a-patristic-quotation).

*Find it.* You cannot, in that form: no surviving work of Tertullian contains the sentence. What the searcher lands on instead is *On the Flesh of Christ* 5.

*Name the edition.* ANF vol. 3, Holmes; the Latin is standard and not disputed here.

*Read the context.* The chapter attacks Marcion, who held that Christ only appeared to have flesh. Tertullian's point is that a phantom Christ empties the crucifixion, death and resurrection — "falsely, therefore, is our faith also." He then piles up three parallel clauses giving the shameful, the unfitting and the impossible as the reason the thing is to be believed, and three sentences later asks how any of it could be true "in Him, if He was not Himself true." The paradox is a move inside an argument, not a substitute for one.

*Compare with the original.* The verbs matter. *Credibile est* is impersonal — "it is to be believed" — not *credo*. *Ineptum* is "unfitting, silly"; Holmes renders it "absurd," and that rendering is most of the tag's English afterlife.

*State the finding.* **Tertullian did not write "credo quia absurdum."** He wrote, of the resurrection, [that the fact is certain because it is impossible](../reference.md#the-absurdity-tag), in a treatise arguing that Christ's flesh was real. It matters because the tag is almost always produced as evidence that Christian faith is proudly irrational — the position called fideism, which the Church has condemned ([`fundamental-theology` 2.3](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/02-03-fideism-and-rationalism.md)). A misquotation is being used to saddle the tradition with a doctrine it rejects.

**Example 2 (where it strains: the vocabulary the West kept and the doctrine it did not).** *Against Praxeas* answers modalism — Father, Son and Spirit as the same person under three names, so that the Father himself was born and suffered. Tertullian's charge is a famous double blow: Praxeas put the Paraclete to flight and crucified the Father.

To answer him he needed words Latin did not have, and supplied them ([Latin Trinitarian vocabulary](../reference.md#latin-trinitarian-vocabulary)). God's unity, he writes at ch. 2, is arranged into a *trinitas*; the three are three "not in condition, but in degree; not in substance, but in form; not in power, but in aspect," and yet of one substance, one condition and one power. *Substantia* for what is one, *persona* for what is three, *trinitas* for the whole: the skeleton of the Western creed, two centuries early.

Now the strain. At ch. 9 the same treatise says the Father is the entire substance while the Son is "a derivation and portion of the whole," which is why he can say *my Father is greater than I*. A portion of a substance is not what Nicaea would mean by *homoousios* ([3.1](03-01-athanasius.md)), and "greater" ranks the Son below the Father in a way the fourth century worked to exclude. Scholars divide over whether that ranking belongs to the *oeconomia*, the ordered mission of Son and Spirit into the world, or to the Godhead itself.

Two disciplines apply. Exactness: *una substantia, tres personae* is a later condensation, and searching *Against Praxeas* will not find *tres personae*, though *trinitas*, *substantia* and *persona* are all there. And authority: this is one writer's opinion, an ecclesiastical writer's at that, while what the Church teaches about the Trinity takes its weight from the ladder in [`fundamental-theology` 4.4](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-04-the-ladder-of-doctrinal-authority.md) and from `trinity-and-god`. The Church received his vocabulary; it did not receive his ranking.

## Watch out

- **You might think the *Prescription* forbids arguing from Scripture.** It bars one move: letting a heretic open the debate on the text. Reading ch. 19 as a general rule makes nonsense of *Against Marcion*.
- **You might date *Against Praxeas* to his Catholic years.** Chapter 2 appeals to the Paraclete's later instruction, placing it in the New Prophecy period — not a reason to discount its Trinitarian terms, but a reason to say where they come from when you cite them.
- **You might promote him to a Father, or demote him to a heretic.** Neither. He fails the marks of orthodoxy and approval, so he is not a Father; he is nonetheless quoted with respect across the tradition, and how far his break with the Carthaginian church went is not certain.
- **You might think a striking sentence is safe because everyone repeats it.** All three rows of the table are repeated constantly, by careful writers. Frequency is not verification.

## One-liner

> Tertullian's move was to file the objection first — before you tell me what the verse means, show me by what right you hold the book — and his own afterlife has been spent in words he never wrote.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢) *(Exegetical.)*** An invented parish-bulletin column, written for this problem:

> **From the pastor's desk.** In an age that demands a reason for everything, remember Tertullian's great cry: *credo quia absurdum* — "I believe because it is absurd." The Fathers knew that faith begins exactly where argument ends. As Tertullian also asked, what has Athens to do with Jerusalem? Nothing at all. Leave the philosophers to their debates and simply believe.

(a) Name the two distinct defects in the column's use of the first quotation — one about the wording, one about the context — and give the work and chapter where the passage is found. (b) Name the error about faith and reason that the column's conclusion states, and say which course owns it. 120 words or fewer in total.

**P2 (🟡) *(Exegetical.)*** *Prescription against Heretics* 37 (ANF, Holmes):

> Thus, not being Christians, they have acquired no right to the Christian Scriptures; and it may be very fairly said to them, Who are you? When and whence did you come? As you are none of mine, what have you to do with that which is mine? Indeed, Marcion, by what right do you hew my wood? By whose permission, Valentinus, are you diverting the streams of my fountain? By what power, Apelles, are you removing my landmarks? This is my property. I have long possessed it; I possessed it before you. I hold sure title-deeds from the original owners themselves, to whom the estate belonged. I am the heir of the apostles.

(a) The same chapter says that "we, without the Scriptures, prove that they have nothing to do with the Scriptures." State precisely what "without the Scriptures" means here, and one thing it does not mean. (b) Name the two grounds the passage gives for the claim of ownership, pointing to the words that carry each. Two sentences per part.

**P3 (🔴, optional) *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)*** *Against Praxeas* 9 (ANF, Holmes):

> Now, observe, my assertion is that the Father is one, and the Son one, and the Spirit one, and that They are distinct from Each Other. … it is not by way of diversity that the Son differs from the Father, but by distribution: it is not by division that He is different, but by distinction; because the Father is not the same as the Son, since they differ one from the other in the mode of their being. For the Father is the entire substance, but the Son is a derivation and portion of the whole, as He Himself acknowledges: My Father is greater than I.

(a) Quote the words in this passage that Tertullian uses to **protect** the unity of God, and the words that **rank** the Son below the Father. (b) Scholars divide over whether Tertullian should be called a subordinationist. State the reading that takes the ranking as a statement about the Son's mission in the world and the reading that takes it as a statement about the Godhead, naming for each the words in the passage it leans on; then give your verdict. 150 words for (b).

<details>
<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical.)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** (i) **Wording.** The sentence does not occur in Tertullian in that form. *On the Flesh of Christ* 5 has two separate clauses, and neither is first-person: of the death, "it is by all means to be believed, because it is absurd" (*prorsus credibile est, quia ineptum est*); of the burial and resurrection, "the fact is certain, because it is impossible" (*certum est, quia impossibile*). The tag fuses them and changes "it is to be believed" into "I believe." (ii) **Context.** The clauses sit inside an argument against Marcion's docetism whose whole force is that a phantom Christ makes the faith false; Tertullian goes on to ask how any of it could be true if Christ were not true. It is a rhetorical paradox inside a reasoned case, not a rejection of reasoning. Work and chapter: *On the Flesh of Christ* 5.
**Must hit, strict (b):** **fideism** — the claim that reason can establish nothing about God or about the fact of revelation, so faith must rest on something else. Owned by [`fundamental-theology`](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/02-03-fideism-and-rationalism.md) 2.3, which reads it against *Dei Filius*.
**Wrong turns:** saying the quotation is "basically accurate in substance" — the fusion and the change of person are the whole difference; treating the Athens line as a second wording error (it is quoted accurately from *Prescription* 7, and is misused rather than misquoted); calling the column's position traditionalism, which is the specific nineteenth-century French form, not what the column says.
**Model answer:** The tag is not in Tertullian. *On the Flesh of Christ* 5 has two impersonal clauses — believed because absurd, certain because impossible — which the column merges and puts in the first person. And the context is inverted: the clauses belong to an argument against Marcion that a Christ without real flesh would make the faith false, so Tertullian is reasoning, not refusing to. The conclusion "faith begins where argument ends" is fideism, condemned by Vatican I and owned by `fundamental-theology` 2.3.

---

**P2** *(Exegetical.)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** it means that the plea Tertullian is entering does not require exegesis — the question of who may appeal to the Scriptures is settled by ownership (title and descent), before any verse is opened, so he can defeat the heretics' appeal without interpreting a text. It does **not** mean that Scripture is unnecessary, obscure, or subordinate to tradition as a source, nor that Christians should not argue from it: the same author argues from it at length against Marcion and against Praxeas, and ch. 13 sets out the rule of faith in full.
**Must hit, strict (b):** (i) **title by inheritance** — "I hold sure title-deeds from the original owners themselves"; "I am the heir of the apostles"; and (ii) **priority in time, i.e. possession before the claimant** — "I have long possessed it; I possessed it before you," reinforced by "When and whence did you come?" Credit for adding that the heretics are cast as strangers with no relation to the testator ("As you are none of mine").
**Wrong turns:** giving "they are not Christians" as one of the two grounds — that is the conclusion the ownership claim supports, not a separate ground; reading the property language as a claim that the Church may withhold the Bible from the laity, which is not what the estate metaphor is about.
**Model answer:** (a) It means the objection is procedural: Tertullian establishes who owns the Scriptures from apostolic descent alone, so the heretics' appeal fails before a single verse is construed. It does not mean Scripture is unclear or that Christians should avoid scriptural argument — he conducts such arguments at book length elsewhere. (b) First, inherited title: "I hold sure title-deeds from the original owners themselves" and "I am the heir of the apostles." Second, prior possession: "I have long possessed it; I possessed it before you," set against the challenge "When and whence did you come?"

---

**P3** *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** *Protecting unity* — "not by way of diversity … but by distribution"; "not by division … but by distinction." *Ranking* — "a derivation and portion of the whole"; "My Father is greater than I." "The Father is the entire substance" may be offered on either side, and counts if the student says which way he is taking it: as naming the one substance the Son derives from, or as making the Son something less than that substance.
**Must hit, any verdict (b):** state both readings with their textual anchors. The **economic** reading leans on *derivatio* and on the language of sending and distribution — the Son is described in his going forth, in the ordered dispensation Tertullian calls the *oeconomia*, and "greater" is Christ's own word in John 14:28, spoken in his humanity. The **ontological** reading leans on *portio*: a portion of a substance is quantitatively less than the whole, which is a statement about what the Son is, not about what he is sent to do; and the ranking language of ch. 2 ("not in condition, but in degree") is general, not confined to the mission. Then: note that the Nicene alternative — same substance, no degrees — was not yet available as a formula, so the question is what the words can bear, not whether he passed a later test. Then a verdict, either way.
**Wrong turns:** answering (b) by condemning Tertullian as an Arian — Arius denies the Son is from the Father's substance, which this passage asserts; treating the question as settled by the fact that the Church later received *trinitas*, *substantia* and *persona* (vocabulary received is not doctrine received); using a Father's title to decide it, which is doubly wrong here since Tertullian has no such title.
**Model answer (b), one of several:** The economic reading takes "derivation" and the whole vocabulary of distribution and sending as describing the Son's going forth into the world; on this reading "greater than I" is Christ's own word about his state in the flesh, and Tertullian's real target is Praxeas's collapse of the three, not their equality. The ontological reading fixes on "portion." A portion of a substance is less than the whole, and ch. 2's "not in condition, but in degree" speaks of the three as such, not of a mission. Verdict: the ranking is real and not merely economic, but calling it subordinationism imports a later category. Tertullian is doing what pre-Nicene Latin could do — securing distinction inside one substance — with a part-and-whole image that Nicaea had to discard.

## Flashback

**From Lesson [2.1](02-01-irenaeus-against-the-gnostics.md) (Irenaeus against the Gnostics):** *(Exegetical.)* Asked why the gospels are four and not some other number, Irenaeus answers (*Against Heresies* 3.11.8, ANF, Roberts and Rambaut): "since there are four zones of the world in which we live, and four principal winds, while the Church is scattered throughout all the world … it is fitting that she should have four pillars". He maps the four to the four faces of the cherubim of Revelation 4:7 and to four covenants. In the next section he names people who want fewer — Marcion — and others whose *Gospel of Truth* he says agrees with the apostles' gospels in nothing (3.11.9).

(a) **As an argument** for the number four, what does this establish? Name the step that fails. **Two sentences.**
(b) **As evidence** about the churches Irenaeus knew around 180, what does the same passage establish, and which feature of the text makes it good evidence? **Two sentences.**

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):** nothing. It runs from a number found in the world — zones, winds, cherubic faces — to the number of the gospels with nothing connecting the two but fitness, so the inference would license whatever number you happened to start from, and it quietly assumes the four it claims to account for.

**Must hit, strict (b):** that a fourfold gospel was already fixed in the churches of Gaul and Asia by about 180 — Irenaeus is defending a number, not proposing one. The feature that makes it good evidence is the shape of the defence: he reaches for cosmology rather than give up a gospel, and he is pressed from both sides at once, by Marcion wanting fewer and by opponents circulating another book; nobody builds an elaborate argument for something optional.

**Wrong turns:** letting the weakness of the argument discredit the testimony — holding the two verdicts apart is the whole skill here; reading the passage as Irenaeus *fixing* the canon, or as settling how the canon was formed, which belongs to `new-testament` and, as defined, to [`fundamental-theology` 3.3](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/03-03-the-canon-as-defined.md); stretching "the churches he knew" into "everywhere", which one bishop's testimony cannot carry.

**Model answer:** (a) As an argument it establishes nothing: the step from four winds and four zones to four gospels is carried only by "it is fitting", and the same form would justify three or seven as readily. It also presupposes the four it is supposed to explain. (b) As evidence it is excellent — around 180, in Gaul and the Asia he came from, four gospels were settled enough that a bishop would defend the number from cosmology rather than concede one. The giveaway is that he is arguing against pressure in both directions at once, from Marcion's fewer and from a rival book, which is not how people write about something still open.

</details>
