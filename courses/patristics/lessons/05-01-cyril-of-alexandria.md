# The Church Fathers · Lesson 5.1: Cyril of Alexandria

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 5: The Christological Fathers and the close of the patristic age · Builds on: [3.3 Gregory of Nazianzus and Gregory of Nyssa](03-03-the-two-gregories.md), [3.1 Athanasius](03-01-athanasius.md) · Unlocks: [5.2 Leo the Great](05-02-leo-the-great.md)

## Why this matters

From the outside the quarrel of 428–433 looks like an argument about a Marian title. It is not. It is an argument about whether the sentences Christians say about Christ have **one subject or two** — whether the one who was begotten before the ages is the same one who was hungry, wept and died, or whether those are two things said of two closely joined somebodies. Every later Christological word turns on the answer. And the phrase Cyril used to answer it turns out not to be his source's at all: he took it for Athanasius, and it came from an Apollinarian forgery. That misattribution is still visible in the map of world Christianity.

## The idea

**Nestorius, at his strongest, first.** He came to Constantinople in 428 from Antioch, trained in the Antiochene habit of guarding the completeness of Christ's manhood. Socrates the historian records that it was a presbyter Nestorius had brought with him, Anastasius, who preached that no one should call Mary *Theotokos*, "God-bearer," since Mary was a woman and God cannot be born of a woman; Nestorius, unwilling to let his man be convicted of blasphemy, preached a series of sermons defending the point (*Ecclesiastical History* VII.32, NPNF second series, vol. 2).

His case has three moves, and each is serious.

1. **The title, taken flatly, is false.** God-bearer sounds like a claim that the divine nature began in Mary. It does not; but Arians had used it for a Son who came to be, and Apollinarians for a Word who replaced a human soul. A word both heresies can use needs guarding.
2. **The manhood must be a whole human life, not a costume.** If the Word is the only subject in Christ, what is the human nature? Apollinaris had answered by cutting out the human mind, and [Gregory of Nazianzus had to answer him](../reference.md#what-is-not-assumed-is-not-healed): what is not assumed is not healed ([3.3](03-03-the-two-gregories.md)). Nestorius hears Alexandrian one-nature talk as Apollinarianism by another road.
3. **So say something more careful.** Call Mary [*Christotokos*](../reference.md#christotokos), Christ-bearer: she bore Christ, who is God and man. The divine and the human are joined in one *prosopon* — one presented face, one Christ, one object of worship — by a conjunction (*synapheia*) that leaves each nature intact.

Socrates, no admirer — he calls Nestorius vain and ill-read — still refuses the charge that he denied Christ's divinity: he says Nestorius was simply frightened of the word, and that his published homilies nowhere destroy the Word's personal existence.

**Cyril's reply goes past the title to the grammar.** If Mary bore the man, and the Word then joined that man, then the sentence "the Son of God died" is false and only "the man Jesus died" is true — and the one who redeemed you is a man whom God assisted. Cyril's case is that Christian language has *one* subject running through the divine and the human verbs, and that breaking it gives you two sons.

## Source

The anathemas the two men hurled at each other in 430, printed side by side in NPNF second series, vol. 14 (Percival): Cyril's first, from his Third Letter, then the first of the twelve counter-anathemas issued under Nestorius's name.

> **Cyril:** If anyone will not confess that the Emmanuel is very God, and that therefore the Holy Virgin is the Mother of God (*Theotokos*), inasmuch as in the flesh she bore the Word of God made flesh: let him be anathema.
>
> **Nestorius:** If anyone says that the Emmanuel is true God, and not rather God with us, that is, that he has united himself to a like nature with ours, which he assumed from the Virgin Mary, and dwelt in it; and if anyone calls Mary the mother of God the Word, and not rather mother of him who is Emmanuel; and if he maintains that God the Word has changed himself into the flesh, which he only assumed in order to make his Godhead visible, and to be found in form as a man, let him be anathema.

Read the two verbs. Cyril's "she bore the Word of God made flesh" makes the Word the object of Mary's bearing. Nestorius's "assumed... and dwelt in it" makes the manhood a thing the Word takes and inhabits, so the one Mary bore is Emmanuel, the composite. His last clause shows what he thinks he is refuting: a Word who *changed into* flesh and wore a human appearance — which Cyril denied too. Each was answering a position the other did not hold.

## The argument

Cyril's case, reconstructed from the Second Letter to Nestorius (his Letter 4) and the Third:

1. **The creed has one subject.** The same one begotten of the Father came down, was incarnate, suffered, rose, ascended. *In words:* Nicaea does not change the grammatical subject halfway through the sentence.
2. **The union is not an indwelling.** The Word did not join himself to a man already constituted, nor unite with him by dignity, authority, goodwill or shared honour; he made a human body animated by a rational soul his own, from the womb, *kath' hypostasin* — personally. *In words:* he did not move in with somebody; he became somebody.
3. **The union changes neither nature.** The divine nature is not converted into flesh, the flesh is not absorbed, and "the difference of the natures is not taken away by the union."
4. **So what belongs to either nature may be said of the one subject.** This is the [communication of idioms](../reference.md#communication-of-idioms): the properties are exchanged at the level of the person, not traded between the natures.
5. **Hence "the Word suffered in the flesh" is not a category mistake.** Cyril is explicit that it is not the divine nature that bleeds: the Word suffered "not as if God the Word suffered in his own nature," but because the body that suffered was his own. *In words:* the subject who suffered is God; the capacity by which he suffered is the manhood.
6. **Hence [*Theotokos*](../reference.md#theotokos).** Not that Mary is the source of the Godhead — Cyril denies that twice over — but that the one she bore is God.
7. **And the alternative is two sons.** If the one born of Mary is not the Word, then the title "Son" is given once by nature and once by honour, and the worship of Christ divides. *In words:* refuse premise 2 and you cannot stop at one Christ.

**What carries which weight.** The Second Letter was read at the first session of Ephesus in 431 and approved by the bishops one by one; it was read again at Chalcedon in 451 and acclaimed, together with Cyril's peace letter to John of Antioch. The Third Letter, with [the Twelve Anathemas](../reference.md#the-twelve-anathemas), was sent from an Alexandrian synod as an ultimatum, and was read into the acts at Ephesus — but the acts record no discussion or vote on the anathemas, and Percival's NPNF note observes that any discussion appears to have dropped out of the record. Chalcedon did not read it. So the Second Letter carries conciliar endorsement in a way the Twelve Anathemas do not, and citing the anathemas as though a council had defined them overstates their level (levels: [`fundamental-theology` 4.4](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-04-the-ladder-of-doctrinal-authority.md); the doctrine as defined belongs to `christology`; the council politics to [`church-history` 2.2](../../church-history/lessons/02-02-ephesus-chalcedon-and-leos-rome.md)).

## Map of positions

| | Who is the subject of Christ's acts? | The natures after the union | Mary |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Nestorius** (428–431) | one *prosopon* of union; divine and human sayings sorted to their natures | two, joined by conjunction, each complete and unmixed | *Christotokos*; *Theotokos* only if carefully glossed |
| **Cyril** | one and the same Son, the Word, who is the subject of both sets of verbs | united *kath' hypostasin*, unconfused, undiminished | *Theotokos*, as a claim about her son |
| **Formula of Reunion** (433) | one Christ, one Son, one Lord | "a union of two natures," each complete | *Theotokos*, with the Antiochene gloss attached |
| **Later miaphysite reading** | one, on Cyril's "one incarnate nature" | one composite nature out of two, neither confused nor lost | *Theotokos* |
| **Chalcedonian reading** | one person and hypostasis | two natures, unconfused, unchangeable, undivided, inseparable | *Theotokos* |

The rows disagree about **where the oneness is located**, not about whether Christ is one. Good will could not settle it, because *physis* meant "concrete individual subject" in Alexandria and "kind of thing" in Antioch and at Chalcedon.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean: running the rule on sample sentences).** The communication of idioms licenses a predicate of either nature to be said of *the person*, named by a concrete term — the Word, the Son, God, this man. It does not license trading predicates between the natures, named by abstract terms — divinity, Godhead, humanity.

- *"The Word died."* Passes. Concrete subject, human predicate; this is exactly premise 5.
- *"The divine nature suffered."* Fails. Abstract subject; Cyril rejects it in the Second Letter in so many words.
- *"God was two feet long."* Formally passes — the subject is concrete — but it is ugly, because the hearer will attach the predicate to the Godhead. The tradition adds the qualifier Cyril himself supplies: *according to the flesh*. Nestorius's refusal to take this step is on the record: at Ephesus, Socrates reports, he said "I cannot term him God who was two and three months old" (*Ecclesiastical History* VII.34).
- *"Mary is the mother of the divine nature."* Fails, and Cyril says so himself. *Theotokos* is a statement about who her son is.

**Example 2 (where it strains: "one incarnate nature of God the Word").** Cyril's favourite formula — *mia physis tou theou logou sesarkomene* — he believed came from Athanasius, and played as a trump card from the tradition. It did not. It comes from the school of Apollinaris, whose followers circulated their tracts under the names of Athanasius, Gregory Thaumaturgus and Pope Julius precisely so that they would be read. Cyril was quoting the heresy he thought he was refuting ([1.1](01-01-how-to-read-a-church-father.md) on [works under the wrong name](../reference.md#spurious-and-misattributed-works)).

Two different things follow. First, on the **argument**: the misattribution does not make the formula false. It removes the authority Cyril thought it carried, and throws the sentence back on its sense — which is ambiguous. Read *physis* as "concrete individual subject," and [the formula](../reference.md#one-incarnate-nature-of-god-the-word) says what "one hypostasis" says. Read it as "nature" in Chalcedon's sense, and it says there is one nature after the union, which Chalcedon denies. Second, on the **history**: both readings were taken. In 433 Cyril accepted the [Formula of Reunion](../reference.md#formula-of-reunion) drafted by the Antiochenes, which speaks of a union of two natures and calls Christ perfect God and perfect man — evidence that Cyril could say what Chalcedon would later say. Dioscorus and the churches that refused Chalcedon took the "one incarnate nature" formula instead as the test of Cyril's real mind. A forged sentence therefore sits under a division that has lasted fifteen centuries.

## Watch out

- **"Nestorius taught two persons" is the charge, not his claim.** He insisted on one *prosopon*. Cyril's reply is that his account cannot sustain it, which is an argument, not a quotation. A Syriac version of his late apology, the *Bazaar of Heracleides*, came to light in the modern period and has driven a long argument over how far the condemned teaching was his. The condemnation of the teaching stands either way; what you may not do is put the caricature in his mouth.
- **The Twelve Anathemas are not a conciliar definition.** See the weighting above. They were also read as Apollinarian by John of Antioch's party at the time — a charge Cyril spent years answering.
- **Miaphysite is not monophysite.** The churches that refused Chalcedon condemn Eutyches, who held that the humanity was swallowed up ([`church-history` 2.2](../../church-history/lessons/02-02-ephesus-chalcedon-and-leos-rome.md)).
- **Cyril is a Father and a Doctor, and neither title endorses a formula.** His letters are weighed here as arguments and witnesses; what the Church defined is at Ephesus and Chalcedon, and belongs to `christology`.

## One-liner

> Nestorius guarded the manhood by loosening the subject; Cyril held the subject to one and sealed it with a sentence Athanasius never wrote.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢) *(Exegetical.)*** Using the rule of Example 1, mark each sentence **licensed**, **licensed but misleading without a qualifier**, or **forbidden**, and give the one-clause reason in each case. (a) "The Son of God was crucified." (b) "The Godhead tasted death." (c) "God was laid in a manger." (d) "The humanity of Christ created the world." One line per part.

**P2 (🟡) *(Exegetical.)*** An invented parish-bulletin note, written for this problem:

> *A word on Mary.* At Ephesus the Church taught us to call Our Lady the Mother of God. Cyril of Alexandria put it beyond doubt when he wrote: "Whosoever shall not recognize that the Word of God suffered in the flesh, that he was crucified in the flesh, and that likewise in that same flesh he tasted death: let him be anathema." No higher honour has ever been paid to any creature, and this is why the title tells us what Mary is in herself.

The quotation is genuine (Cyril's twelfth anathema, NPNF second series, vol. 14). Name (a) the exegetical work the quotation is being made to do that it does not do, and (b) the misreading of *Theotokos* in the last clause, stating what the title actually predicates and of whom. 120 words or fewer in total.

**P3 (🔴, optional) *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)*** The Formula of Reunion of 433, as Cyril quotes it in his letter to John of Antioch (NPNF second series, vol. 14, Percival):

> We confess, therefore, our Lord Jesus Christ, the Only Begotten Son of God, perfect God, and perfect Man of a reasonable soul and flesh consisting; begotten before the ages of the Father according to his Divinity, and in the last days, for us and for our salvation, of Mary the Virgin according to his humanity, of the same substance with his Father according to his Divinity, and of the same substance with us according to his humanity; for there became a union of two natures. Wherefore we confess one Christ, one Son, one Lord. According to this understanding of this unmixed union, we confess the holy Virgin to be Mother of God.

(a) Quote the words that give Cyril what he was fighting for, and the words that give the Antiochenes what they were fighting for. (b) Scholars disagree about whether Cyril's acceptance of this text was a change of position or a restatement of one. In 150 words or fewer, any verdict, argue it — using the lesson's numbered premises and the "one incarnate nature" formula as your two tests.

<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical.)*

**Must hit, strict:** (a) **Licensed** — concrete subject ("the Son of God"), human predicate, said of the one person; this is premise 5. (b) **Forbidden** — "Godhead" is an abstract term naming the divine nature, and the rule never trades predicates between natures; Cyril denies that the Word suffered in his own nature. (c) **Licensed but misleading without a qualifier** — the subject is concrete, so the form is sound, but the hearer will attach the predicate to the divinity; add "according to the flesh." (d) **Forbidden** — "the humanity" is abstract, and creation is a divine act; the person creates, the human nature does not.

**Wrong turns:** calling (b) merely awkward rather than forbidden, which loses the abstract/concrete distinction the rule turns on; forbidding (a) or (c) on the ground that God cannot suffer or be an infant, which is Nestorius's move and denies premise 5; treating (d) as licensed because "Christ created the world" is true — the sentence's subject is the nature, not the person.

**Model answer:** as above, one line per part.

---

**P2** *(Exegetical.)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** the anathema is about the **subject of the Passion**, not about Mary. It says the one who suffered, was crucified and tasted death in the flesh is the Word of God; nothing in it mentions Mary, the title *Theotokos*, or any honour paid to her. Using it as proof of the Marian title skips the step that actually carries it — anathema 1 and premise 6, where the title follows from who her son is. Credit also for noting that the Twelve Anathemas do not carry the conciliar weight "the Church taught us" implies, even though the title itself was received at Ephesus.

**Must hit, strict (b):** the bulletin makes *Theotokos* a predicate of Mary considered in herself. The title predicates something of **Christ**: that the one Mary bore is God. Cyril states the limit himself — it is not that the Word or his divinity took its beginning from her. Mary's honour is a consequence, not the content.

**Wrong turns:** saying the quotation is spurious (it is Cyril's, and genuine); objecting to the title itself, which Ephesus received; treating "no higher honour paid to any creature" as the error — the error is the last clause's "in herself," not the honour.

**Model answer:** The quotation settles who suffered, not what to call Mary: it says the Word of God was crucified and tasted death in the flesh, and never mentions her. The Marian title rests on a further step the note omits — the one she bore is the Word, so she is his mother in the flesh. And the last clause inverts the title. *Theotokos* is a statement about Christ, that Mary's son is God; it says nothing about Mary's own nature, and Cyril insists the Godhead did not begin in her. Her honour follows from her son's identity rather than standing on its own.

---

**P3** *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** Cyril's gains — "one Christ, one Son, one Lord" and "we confess the holy Virgin to be Mother of God." The Antiochene gains — "perfect God, and perfect Man of a reasonable soul and flesh consisting," "of the same substance with us according to his humanity," above all "there became a union of **two natures**," and "unmixed." Credit for noting that the run of "according to his Divinity ... according to his humanity" is a clause both sides could sign: it sorts the sayings, which Antioch wanted, while leaving one subject, which Cyril wanted.

**Must hit, any verdict (b):** state what the Formula commits Cyril to that his own preferred vocabulary did not say (two natures, in the plural, after the union); test it against the premises — 1, 2 and 6 are untouched, and 3 is arguably what the two-natures clause spells out, so on the premises he conceded wording rather than content; then test it against "one incarnate nature," which is the harder case, since the Formula's plural is what that formula appears to refuse. A verdict either way passes if both tests are run and the conclusion says which reading of *physis* it assumes.

**Wrong turns:** citing Cyril's later defences of the Formula as though they settled the question the Formula raises; arguing from the fact that Cyril signed it to the claim that nothing changed, which skips the wording; arguing from the fact that his Egyptian allies were alarmed to the claim that he capitulated, which is evidence of how it looked, not of what he meant.

**Model answer (b), one of several:** Restatement, not reversal. Every premise of his case survives the Formula intact: one subject through both sets of verbs, a union that is not an indwelling, and *Theotokos* affirmed in the same sentence as the two natures. What he conceded was vocabulary — the plural "two natures," which his own Alexandrian usage of *physis* as concrete subject had made him avoid. That concession matters, and it is the strongest evidence that when Cyril said "one incarnate nature" he meant one subject rather than one kind of thing; otherwise he could not have signed a text that counts natures in the plural. The cost is that the two formulas now sit in one corpus, and later readers could take either as the real Cyril, which is what happened after 451.

## Flashback

**From Lesson [4.4](04-04-augustine-against-the-donatists.md) (Augustine against the Donatists):** *(Exegetical.)* Suppose the disputed fact had gone the Donatists' way — suppose it were proved beyond argument that one of the bishops who consecrated Caecilian really had handed over the scriptures to be burned in the persecution.

(a) On the Donatist premises, trace what follows for Caecilian's ordination, for the sacraments of the African Catholic communion, and for a convert arriving from it; name the **two** premises that carry the chain.
(b) On Augustine's premises, trace what follows, naming the move that blocks each of those two. **Two sentences per part.**

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):** the consecration is empty, because a sacrament is holy with the holiness of its minister and a traitor's hands are empty — so Caecilian is no bishop. And the guilt travels along the chain of communion, so every ordination and every sacrament flowing from his communion is empty too, and a convert from it is not rebaptized but baptized: on these premises he never had anything. The two premises are **the minister's holiness** and **the transmission of guilt through communion**; the first alone would taint one man's acts, not a whole communion.
**Must hit, strict (b):** nothing follows for the sacraments. Against the first premise: the one who baptizes is Christ, so we consider not who gives but what is given, and baptism given in the words of the gospel is real even outside the Church and is not repeated. Against the second: guilt does not travel, because the Church is a mixed body until the harvest (Matthew 13:24-30) and no one is damned by his neighbour's sin; what the schismatic lacks is the charity unity supplies, so the remedy is correction, not repetition. Full credit notes the structural point — Augustine's reply is built so that the historical question need never be won, and the concession costs him nothing.
**Wrong turns:** answering that Augustine would simply deny the charge — his party did contest it, but his theology is designed not to rest on it, which is the whole force of the counterfactual; saying that on the Donatist view the convert's baptism is "repeated", when their claim is that there is nothing there to repeat; dropping the second premise and treating the case as resting on the minister's holiness alone, which leaves the spread through an entire communion unexplained.
**Model answer:** (a) The consecration is void, since what a traitor hands over he hands over spoiled, so Caecilian was never a bishop; and because communion with traitors shares their guilt, everything ordained or celebrated in that communion is void as well, and a convert must be baptized rather than reconciled, having had nothing. The chain needs both the minister's holiness and the travelling of guilt through communion. (b) On Augustine's premises the proof changes nothing: Christ is the one who baptizes, so what matters is what is given rather than who gives it, and baptism in the words of the gospel is real outside the Church and not repeated. And the guilt does not travel, because the field carries tares until the harvest; the convert is received, corrected rather than rebaptized, so that what he already has may begin to profit him.
