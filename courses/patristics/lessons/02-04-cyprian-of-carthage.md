# The Church Fathers · Lesson 2.4: Cyprian of Carthage

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 2: The rule of faith, Africa and Alexandria · Builds on: [2.3 Tertullian](02-03-tertullian.md), [2.1 Irenaeus against the Gnostics](02-01-irenaeus-against-the-gnostics.md) · Unlocks: [2.5 Clement of Alexandria and Origen](02-05-clement-of-alexandria-and-origen.md), [4.4 Augustine against the Donatists](04-04-augustine-against-the-donatists.md)

## Why this matters

Every later argument about who belongs to the Church runs through a Carthaginian bishop beheaded in 258. Cyprian gave the Latin West its vocabulary for unity — the Church as mother, the one episcopate, no salvation outside — then within five years used that vocabulary against the bishop of Rome and lost. The Church kept his ecclesiology, kept him as a saint and a martyr, and quietly did not follow his conclusion. That is this course's cleanest case of the [authority discipline](../reference.md#opinions-the-church-did-not-receive): a Father's argument can be magnificent, his holiness beyond question, and his verdict still not the Church's. It is also the best example of why you check which text you are quoting before you quote it.

## The idea

Cyprian was a wealthy, rhetorically trained Carthaginian who converted around 246 and was elected bishop between mid-248 and early 249 — fast enough that some of his presbyters never accepted it. Within a year Decius ordered everyone in the empire to sacrifice; Cyprian went into hiding and governed by letter while thousands of African Christians sacrificed or bought certificates saying they had. The events belong to [`church-history` 1.3](../../church-history/lessons/01-03-persecution-and-martyrdom.md); ours is the question they left behind. **Who readmits the fallen, on what terms, and what happens to a church that splits over the answer?**

Cyprian's reply is a single idea pressed hard. The Church is not a federation of congregations that agree; it is **one thing**, and being in it is not a matter of opinion but of communion with the bishop, who holds the one episcopate whole. From that he draws a consequence readers find obvious or outrageous: outside that communion there is no Church, so there is nothing there to receive. *On the Unity of the Catholic Church* (251) is that idea in twenty-seven short chapters, against Novatian's schism at Rome and a rival faction in Carthage.

Five years later the same idea put him in open conflict with Pope Stephen I over whether converts from heresy had to be baptized again. He died before the quarrel was resolved — right about the Church, wrong about the baptism. That those two come apart is the lesson.

## Source

*On the Unity of the Catholic Church*, chapters 5 and 6, in the *Ante-Nicene Fathers* (Wallis's translation, public domain):

> The episcopate is one, each part of which is held by each one for the whole. The Church also is one, which is spread abroad far and wide into a multitude by an increase of fruitfulness. As there are many rays of the sun, but one light; and many branches of a tree, but one strength based in its tenacious root; and since from one spring flow many streams … yet the unity is still preserved in the source. … she is one mother, plentiful in the results of fruitfulness: from her womb we are born, by her milk we are nourished, by her spirit we are animated. …
>
> He can no longer have God for his Father, who has not the Church for his mother. If any one could escape who was outside the Ark of Noah, then he also may escape who shall be outside of the Church.

Two images and one technical sentence. The images (sun, tree, spring, mother, ark) say one thing: the many are real, the source is one, severance kills. The technical sentence is the one to slow down over.

## The argument

Cyprian's case:

1. **The Church is one because its origin is one.** Many rays, one light; many streams, one spring. *In words:* multiplicity is fine; a second source is not.
2. **Christ built on one man to show that oneness** — though "the rest of the apostles were also the same as was Peter, endowed with a like partnership both of honour and power" (ch. 4, ANF). *In words:* Peter is the demonstration of unity, not a rank above the others.
3. **[The episcopate is one, and each bishop holds the whole of it](../reference.md#the-one-episcopate),** not a slice — *in solidum*, the Roman-law phrase for joint liability where each party answers for the entire obligation. *In words:* a bishop does not own a franchise; he holds the single thing all bishops hold, undivided, with them or not at all.
4. **So a rival altar in a city that already has a bishop is not a church.** *In words:* schism is not disagreement inside the Church; it is departure from it.
5. **[The Church is the only mother](../reference.md#the-church-as-mother),** and baptism is birth from her womb. *In words:* there is exactly one place where Christians are made.
6. **Therefore a schismatic or heretic has nothing to give.** Water poured outside is not baptism, and "there is no salvation out of the Church" — even the blood of a heretic's martyrdom does not profit him (Letter to Jubaianus, ANF Epistle 72.21 = Hartel Epistle 73.21). *In words:* the outsider's rites are empty, not merely irregular.

∴ Whoever leaves the Church has left the Father, and whoever is baptized outside must be baptized when he comes in.

Premises 1–5 became standard Latin doctrine. Premise 6 did not. Hold that split in view; everything below is about it.

### The chapter that exists in two texts

*On the Unity* chapter 4, the Peter passage, survives in **two versions**. One — the Primacy Text — speaks of a primacy given to Peter and of "the chair of Peter" on which the Church is founded (the lesson's own rendering of the Latin). The received text, which Schaff's ANF prints and which you read above, has neither phrase, and speaks of resisting "the Church" where the first has deserting Peter's chair. Both contain the sentence about the other apostles being Peter's equals.

The leading account is Maurice Bévenot's (*St Cyprian's De Unitate Chap. 4 in the Light of the Manuscripts*, 1938; critical edition 1971): **both are Cyprian's own**, the Primacy Text is the earlier, and Cyprian revised it when its wording proved open to a use he had not intended. Scholars differ over when and why, and the reconstruction has never been unanimous. What is settled enough to act on is the rule: **when you cite chapter 4, say which version you are quoting and in whose edition.** You cannot drop "the chair of Peter" from this chapter into an argument unannotated. Boss 2 sets the full comparison; here, learn the habit.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean: run the argument on Novatian).** In 251 Cornelius was elected bishop of Rome; Novatian, who held that those who had sacrificed could never be readmitted, was consecrated against him. Feed this to the premises. There is one episcopate and one chair per city (P3), and Cornelius holds it; Novatian's consecration therefore made no second bishop of Rome, only a man outside (P4). Those he baptizes are born of no mother (P5), so they are not born at all (P6) — and Cyprian's councils required them to be baptized on entering the Church.

Notice three things. First, the criterion is **communion, not doctrine**: Novatian was orthodox, and Cyprian's verdict needs no error in his creed. Second, the argument needs an input it cannot supply — *which* claimant is the bishop; Cyprian decided by sending investigators to Rome and seeing whom other bishops recognised. Third, the logic runs both ways: Cyprian's own standing rested on the same recognition, which is why his Carthaginian enemies went to Rome to unseat him.

**Example 2 (where it strains: run the same argument on Stephen).** In 255–6 the African councils ruled for rebaptism; Stephen of Rome rejected the ruling. Cyprian quotes him:

> If any one, therefore, come to you from any heresy whatever, let nothing be innovated (or done) which has not been handed down, to wit, that hands be imposed on him for repentance. (Letter to Pompey, ANF Epistle 73.1)

Now the premises turn on their author. Stephen is a bishop; by P3 he holds the whole episcopate, exactly as Cyprian does. They disagree about something that decides whether thousands of people are Christians. What resolves it? Cyprian, opening the council of 1 September 256:

> For neither does any of us set himself up as a bishop of bishops, nor by tyrannical terror does any compel his colleague to the necessity of obedience.

That is a real position, and the African church took it. But look at the cost: an episcopate "one" in the strong sense of P3, with no procedure for settling a question its members each hold whole. Stephen reaches instead for a criterion **outside** the bishops' agreement — what has been handed down — and, on Firmilian's hostile report, claims to speak for it from the succession of Peter. Firmilian's furious reply concedes that the claim is made and denies what Stephen draws from it. Neither finishes: Stephen died in 257, Cyprian in 258.

**The outcome.** The Church did not receive premise 6. Within sixty years Western councils were admitting converts baptized in the name of the Trinity by the laying on of hands alone, and Augustine built the theology of it against the Donatists — Christ, not the minister, baptizes, so a baptism given outside is valid but not fruitful ([4.4](04-04-augustine-against-the-donatists.md)). Note his tactic: he grants premises 1–5 in full, praises Cyprian at length, denies 6. See [`fundamental-theology` 4.4](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-04-the-ladder-of-doctrinal-authority.md) for the levels an individual Father's opinion does not occupy, and `ecclesiology-and-mariology` for the doctrine of the Church.

## Watch out

- **You might think ["no salvation outside the Church"](../reference.md#no-salvation-outside-the-church) is Cyprian's general theory of who is saved.** In his own texts it is said of **schismatics and heretics** — people who have left, or who seek entry from outside — and its job is to deny that their rites work. What it means as Catholic doctrine, invincible ignorance included, is fixed by the Magisterium and owned by `ecclesiology-and-mariology`.
- **You might think Cyprian's error damages his standing as a witness.** It does not: he was never condemned, and is venerated as a martyr and named in the Roman Canon. His case shows something narrower — holiness plus a Father's title plus a good argument still does not equal Church teaching.
- **You might think Cyprian either denied or asserted Roman primacy.** Both come from the same letters, and this course does not adjudicate them — what the texts prove is argued in `apologetics-catholic-claims`. Note only that "bishop of bishops" names nobody, and that the two texts of chapter 4 sit on opposite sides.
- **You might trust Schaff's editorial headings.** The ANF prooemium to the Seventh Council of Carthage dates it to 258; it met on 1 September **256**. Schaff's *text* is the course's default translation; the nineteenth-century apparatus around it is not ([1.1](01-01-how-to-read-a-church-father.md)).
- **You might read *in solidum* as "each bishop is sovereign".** It means the opposite of independence: joint and several liability, where each holds the whole *because* there is only one. Cyprian's problem in 256 is the strong sense of unity with no mechanism inside it.

## One-liner

> Cyprian rested the Church's unity on one episcopate held whole by every bishop, then found that a body in which no one is a bishop of bishops has no way to settle what its members owe each other.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢) *(Exegetical.)*** Three claims about Cyprian. For each, say whether it is **accurate**, **false**, or **overstated as to level**, and correct the ones that are not accurate in one sentence each.

(a) "Cyprian taught that converts baptized by heretics must be baptized in the Church. Since he is a canonized martyr and a Father, this is the Church's teaching on the matter."
(b) "'There is no salvation out of the Church' is Cyprian's own, from his letter to Jubaianus."
(c) "When you quote *On the Unity of the Catholic Church* chapter 4 from Schaff, you are quoting what Cyprian wrote."

**P2 (🟡) *(Exegetical.)*** Firmilian of Caesarea to Cyprian, autumn 256 (ANF Epistle 74.17 = Hartel Epistle 75.17; Wallis's translation):

> And in this respect I am justly indignant at this so open and manifest folly of Stephen, that he who so boasts of the place of his episcopate, and contends that he holds the succession from Peter, on whom the foundations of the Church were laid, should introduce many other rocks and establish new buildings of many churches; maintaining that there is baptism in them by his authority. … Nor does he understand that the truth of the Christian Rock is overshadowed, and in some measure abolished, by him when he thus betrays and deserts unity.

(a) What does Firmilian report Stephen as claiming, and what exactly does Firmilian deny? Point to the words that carry each.
(b) Catholic readers and Orthodox or Protestant readers both cite this paragraph. State the reading each gives it and the words each leans on. Do not say which is right. **Two sentences per part.**

**P3 (🔴, optional) *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)*** An invented parish-bulletin note:

> "St Cyprian settled this in the third century: 'He cannot have God for his Father who has not the Church for his mother,' and 'there is no salvation outside the Church.' So the answer to your neighbour who was baptized down the road is simple — whatever he received there, he received nothing, and our parish should say so plainly."

(a) Name the two moves the note makes beyond what Cyprian's texts support, and give the setting each quotation actually has. **150 words or fewer.**
(b) Cyprian holds that the episcopate is one and held whole by each bishop, and also that no bishop may compel a colleague. Scholars disagree about whether these two commitments are consistent. Argue either way in **150 words or fewer**, using the events of 256 as your test case.

<details>
<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical — strict.)*

**Must hit, strict:**
- (a) **Overstated as to level.** The first sentence is accurate: Cyprian and the African councils of 255–6 did require baptism for converts from heresy. The second is the error — a Father's opinion is never by itself Church teaching, however holy the Father ([`fundamental-theology` 4.4](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-04-the-ladder-of-doctrinal-authority.md)); the Church did not receive this position, and settled the other way.
- (b) **Accurate**, with a citation duty. It is Cyprian's, in the letter to Jubaianus, ANF Epistle 72.21 (= Hartel Epistle 73.21). Credit for adding that the sentence is said of a heretic seeking entry, not offered as a general account of who is saved.
- (c) **False**, or at best radically incomplete. Chapter 4 survives in two versions; Schaff's ANF prints the received text, not the Primacy Text, and a citation of this chapter has to say which text and which edition it uses.

**Wrong turns:** calling (a) simply "false", which throws away the accurate first half and misses that the fault is a level claim; calling (c) "accurate" because Schaff is a reputable edition — the question is which of two Latin texts is being translated, not whether the translator is sound; treating (b) as false because the phrase is usually quoted in Latin.

**Model answer:** (a) Overstated as to level: he did teach it, but a Father's opinion is not Church teaching, and this one was not received. (b) Accurate — ANF Epistle 72.21, to Jubaianus — though it is said there of a heretic seeking baptism. (c) False: chapter 4 exists in two versions and Schaff prints the received text, so the version must be named.

---

**P2** *(Exegetical — strict.)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** the claim reported is that Stephen **boasts of the place of his episcopate and holds the succession from Peter, "on whom the foundations of the Church were laid"** — so Firmilian concedes, as a matter of what Stephen says and what is said about Peter, that Rome's bishop claims a Petrine succession and that Peter is foundational. What Firmilian denies is the **conclusion** Stephen draws from it: that by his authority there can be baptism among heretics. His charge is that recognising those baptisms "introduces many other rocks" and "establishes new buildings of many churches", i.e. multiplies the foundation and so "betrays and deserts unity". Full credit requires seeing that the denial is about the *use* of the claim, not a denial that the claim is made.

**Must hit, strict (b):** the Catholic reading leans on the fact that Stephen's Petrine claim is reported as made in 256 and is not answered by denying that he holds Peter's succession — Firmilian attacks its exercise, and the words "contends that he holds the succession from Peter" and "the place of his episcopate" are what it rests on. The Orthodox or Protestant reading leans on "folly", "boasts", and the whole tenor of a senior Eastern bishop treating a Roman decision as resistible error, arguing that a jurisdiction the East recognised could not have been spoken of this way. Both readings must be stated without a verdict.

**Wrong turns:** reading Firmilian as denying that Peter is the rock — the passage affirms it and complains that Stephen's practice multiplies rocks; giving a verdict in (b), which belongs to `apologetics-catholic-claims`; treating Firmilian's vehemence as evidence of what Stephen actually wrote, when the letter is a hostile report.

**Model answer:** (a) He reports Stephen claiming the place of his episcopate and the succession from Peter, on whom the Church was founded; he denies the inference that Stephen's authority can make baptism valid among heretics, since that would set up "many other rocks" and desert unity. (b) Catholic readers lean on the unanswered Petrine claim — Firmilian disputes its use, not its existence. Orthodox and Protestant readers lean on "folly" and "boasts", and on an Eastern metropolitan treating a Roman ruling as error to be resisted.

---

**P3** *(a) exegetical — strict; (b) evaluative — any verdict.*

**Must hit, strict (a):** two moves, either pair of which earns credit if precisely stated. (i) **From schism to any other baptism.** Cyprian's mother sentence (*On the Unity* 6) is aimed at those who have **left** the Church — Novatian's party and the Carthaginian dissidents — and the Jubaianus sentence (Epistle 72.21) at heretics seeking entry; neither is a ruling on a baptized neighbour's standing centuries later. (ii) **From a Father's opinion to the parish's teaching.** The conclusion "he received nothing" is precisely the premise the Church did not receive; the settled position is Augustine's — valid but not fruitful ([4.4](04-04-augustine-against-the-donatists.md)) — and what the formula means as doctrine is fixed by the Magisterium, not by Cyprian. Credit also for noting that the note gives no citations, so a reader cannot check either quotation.

**Wrong turns:** objecting that the quotations are inauthentic — both are Cyprian's and both are quoted accurately; answering with a view about salvation, which is not what the question asks.

**Must hit, any verdict (b):** state both commitments precisely (the one episcopate held *in solidum*; no bishop is a bishop of bishops, from the opening of the council of 1 September 256); run them on 256, where two bishops each holding the whole episcopate reached contradictory rulings on whether specific people were baptized; say what your verdict implies about the *kind* of unity P3 asserts. A consistency verdict has to say what, on Cyprian's account, was supposed to resolve such a case — conciliar agreement, the judgement of God, or nothing. An inconsistency verdict has to say which of the two commitments is the one that has to give, and must not simply assert that Stephen was right.

**Wrong turns:** deciding the question by whether Stephen or Cyprian had the better baptismal theology, which is a different question; treating "no bishop of bishops" as a claim about Rome specifically, when the sentence names no one; reading *in solidum* as each bishop being independent, which dissolves the tension instead of examining it.

**Model answer (b), one of several:** They are consistent only if disputes are rare. Cyprian's *in solidum* means each bishop is answerable for the whole, which is a claim about liability, not about jurisdiction, and he expects the whole to be visible in the bishops' agreement — hence a council of eighty-seven, each voting in turn. In 256 that expectation failed: Stephen and Cyprian each held the whole and ruled opposite ways about whether particular people were baptized. Cyprian's stated remedy is that each bishop answers to the Lord alone, which is not a remedy but a deferral, and it left a question of fact about thousands of Christians open at his death. So I judge the commitments consistent in principle and inoperative in practice — the account is coherent, but it has no procedure, and the Church later supplied one rather than choosing between his two claims.

</details>

## Flashback

**From Lesson [2.2](02-02-irenaeus-recapitulation.md) (Irenaeus: recapitulation):** *(Exegetical.)* At *Against Heresies* 3.21.10 Irenaeus notes that Adam was formed by the hand of God from soil that was untilled and as yet virgin, God not having sent rain and no man having tilled the ground. He then asks why God did not simply take dust a second time for the second Adam, but had the formation made from Mary. His answer (ANF, Roberts–Rambaut): "It was that there might not be another formation called into being, nor any other which should [require to] be saved, but that the very same formation should be summed up".

(a) State the matched pair the passage sets up, and say what makes the match exact in Irenaeus's eyes — why a son of Joseph would not have served. **Two sentences.**
(b) A reader concludes: "so Christ's flesh is a fresh making, a different stock from ours." Answer him from this passage, naming the words that decide it. **Two sentences.**

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):** Adam formed by God from earth no one had worked, against Christ formed by God from a woman who had known no man — untouched material in both cases, and in both the maker is God directly, with no human seed. That is why Joseph will not do: Irenaeus wants an analogy of *origin*, and if Adam had no human father the second Adam cannot have one either, or the recapitulation is not point for point.

**Must hit, strict (b):** the passage says the opposite, and says why. Fresh dust would have been "another formation called into being", a second humanity standing beside the one that actually needs saving; the deciding words are "nor any other which should be saved, but that the very same formation should be summed up" — Christ takes his flesh through Mary precisely so that what is repaired is the stock that fell.

**Wrong turns:** hearing "virgin soil" as a statement about Mary's holiness or about later Marian doctrine, when the passage is about the mode of formation and nothing else; treating the parallel as ornament, when Irenaeus makes its exactness his reason for rejecting a birth from Joseph; reading "formed as man by God" as loosening Christ's descent from Mary, which is the very thing the "same formation" clause is there to exclude.

**Model answer:** (a) Adam from untilled, as yet virgin soil, formed by God's own hand; Christ from Mary, as yet a virgin, formed as man by God. The match is of origin — neither has a human father — and a son of Joseph would break it, since the first Adam came from no human seed either. (b) On the contrary: the whole reason for using Mary rather than a second handful of dust is "that there might not be another formation called into being, nor any other which should be saved". Christ takes up "the very same formation", which is what makes his obedience count for the race that fell in Adam.

</details>
