# The Church Fathers · Lesson 4.1: Ambrose of Milan

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 4: The Latin Doctors · Builds on: [3.2 Basil the Great](03-02-basil-the-great.md), [3.6 John Chrysostom](03-06-john-chrysostom.md) · Unlocks: [4.2 Jerome](04-02-jerome.md), [4.3 Augustine: Confessions](04-03-augustine-confessions.md)

## Why this matters

Everything in Module 3 happened in Greek. Module 4 is Latin, and Ambrose is the hinge: the Latin bishop who read Greek well — Origen, Basil, Philo — and spent twenty-three years pouring what he read into Latin sermons. He also leaves us the West's first full course of instruction on the sacraments given to the newly baptized, whose ninth chapter every later Western argument about the Eucharist has had to read. And because he was famous and endlessly copied, a great deal that is not his travels under his name, which makes him this course's best exercise in the checking procedure of [1.1](01-01-how-to-read-a-church-father.md).

## The idea

Start with the career, because it explains the books. Ambrose was born about 340, son of a praetorian prefect of Gaul (the city is disputed: Trier, Arles and Lyons all claim him). He trained as a lawyer and became consular governor of Liguria and Aemilia, resident at Milan, an imperial capital. In 374 the Arian Auxentius, who had held the see of Milan for some twenty years, died, and Ambrose went to the basilica officially, to keep order at a contested election. The crowd acclaimed him bishop. He was still an unbaptized catechumen. He was baptized, passed through the intervening orders, and was consecrated on 7 December 374.

So the West got a bishop with no theological training and an excellent Greek library, and that combination is his structural importance. The 1907 *Catholic Encyclopedia* article makes the point twice: he had "a thorough mastery of the Greek language and literature," which Augustine and later Leo lacked, and to repair his training he read Scripture and the Fathers "with a marked preference for Origen and St. Basil." Jerome (*Letter* 41) says the same, calling him a disciple of Origen as modified by Hippolytus and Basil; Philo is a third source, visible throughout his Genesis commentaries. Ambrose is a translator in the broad sense — a Roman who digests Greek material and casts it into Latin a congregation can act on.

The second thing he does is [mystagogy](../reference.md#mystagogy): explaining the rites *after* you have been through them. The Church of his day practised a deliberate reserve about the sacraments, the [disciplina arcani](../reference.md#disciplina-arcani), and Ambrose says at the start of *On the Mysteries* that to explain them before baptism would be to betray rather than to portray them, and that the rites strike harder on someone who did not know what was coming. So the book walks the newly baptized back through what happened to them: the opening of the ears, the renunciation facing west and the turn to the east, the water, the anointing on the head and the washing of the feet (ch. 6), the white robe and the seal of the Spirit (ch. 7), and finally the altar (chs. 8–9). It is a running argument that the visible thing is not the whole thing.

## Source

*On the Mysteries* 9.50 and 9.52, NPNF 2nd series, vol. 10, translated by H. de Romestin, E. de Romestin and H. T. F. Duckworth (public domain):

> 50. Perhaps you will say, I see something else, how is it that you assert that I receive the Body of Christ? … Let us prove that this is not what nature made, but what the blessing consecrated, and the power of blessing is greater than that of nature, because by blessing nature itself is changed.
>
> 52. … if the blessing of man had such power as to change nature, what are we to say of that divine consecration where the very words of the Lord and Saviour operate? … Shall not the word of Christ, which was able to make out of nothing that which was not, be able to change things which already are into what they were not?

## The argument

Chapter 9 is one argument, and it is an *a fortiori* — from a case the objector already grants to the case in dispute.

1. **The objection is put in the objector's own terms: my eyes report bread.** *In words:* Ambrose does not begin by denying the appearance. He concedes it and says this is exactly what has to be answered.
2. **Scripture records blessings that changed what things were: Moses' rod became a serpent and went back; the Nile ran blood; the sea stood up in walls; Jordan turned back; a rock gave water; bitter Marah went sweet; Elisha made iron float** (9.51). *In words:* the premise is not a theory about nature but a list, and the objector is assumed to accept the list.
3. **All of those were a prophet's blessing — a man's.** *In words:* the granted cases are the weaker ones.
4. **So the word of Christ, which made what was not out of nothing, can at least change what already is into what it was not** (9.52). *In words:* if the lesser power reshapes nature, the greater power can.
5. **The words said over the elements are Christ's own: "This is My Body"** (9.54). *In words:* the thing doing the work is not the priest's blessing but a reported saying of the Lord.
6. **And the case that controls the whole analogy is the Incarnation: the body on the altar is the body born of the Virgin, where the order of nature was already set aside** (9.53). *In words:* you already believe in one body that was not produced the way nature produces bodies.

Notice what the argument does *not* contain. There is no distinction between substance and appearance, no technical vocabulary at all. Ambrose's operative word is "nature," and he uses it of rods, rivers and iron. Everything later theology built here had to supply terms he did not have.

## Map of the readings

Catholic, Lutheran and Reformed readers all use this chapter, and each leans on different words in it. Naming the readings is this course's job; arguing out which the text will bear belongs to [`apologetics-catholic-claims`](../../apologetics-catholic-claims/syllabus.md), and the doctrine itself to [`sacraments-and-liturgy`](../../sacraments-and-liturgy/syllabus.md).

| Reader | Takes chapter 9 to say | Leans on |
|---|---|---|
| **Catholic** | Christ's words at the consecration change what the elements are; the change is real and hidden, as in the Incarnation. This is read as a pre-technical witness to what Trent (Session XIII, 1551) later defined as transubstantiation — defined dogma, and dogma because Trent defined it, not because Ambrose wrote it | "by blessing nature itself is changed"; "change things which already are into what they were not"; and 9.53, "this body which we make is that which was born of the Virgin" |
| **Lutheran** | The word of Christ makes his body truly present with the bread; Ambrose's own analogies leave the creature standing | "made what it is by the word of Christ"; the rod, the river and the iron, none of which are annihilated |
| **Reformed** | A change of signification and use, received spiritually by faith | 9.54, "after the consecration the Body is signified"; 9.58, the sacrament is "not bodily food but spiritual" |

For what "defined dogma" commits a Catholic to, and the rest of the ladder, see [`fundamental-theology` 4.4](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-04-the-ladder-of-doctrinal-authority.md).

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean: why 9.53 is the load-bearing section).** Run the *a fortiori* and watch where it nearly fails. Premises 2–4 argue that a blessing can change nature, and every instance is a change you can *see*: the rod visibly becomes a serpent, the water visibly becomes blood. On the altar nothing visible happens at all, which is the objection of 9.50. So the list of wonders, by itself, proves the wrong kind of thing.

Ambrose knows this, and 9.53 is the repair. He switches from proofs to a single case: the Lord was born of a virgin, so a body of his has already come to be outside the order of nature, and it looked from outside like an ordinary birth. That gives him a precedent for a real change under an unchanged appearance, and it lets him identify the two bodies. The chapter's strength is not the miracle list; it is that the Incarnation does the analogical work.

**Example 2 (where it strains: his own vocabulary).** Two sections later Ambrose writes that before the heavenly words "another nature is spoken of, after the consecration the Body is signified," and at 9.58 that the sacrament "is not bodily food but spiritual." A Reformed reader takes those as the chapter's own gloss on "changed": the alteration is in what the thing signifies and how it is received. A Catholic reader answers that *significatur* in fourth-century Latin need not mean "is merely a sign of," and that 9.58 is contrasting spiritual with carnal eating, not real with figurative presence.

The honest description is that the chapter will not settle the question in its own terms, because Ambrose has no terms for it: he asserts a change and offers no account of what survives it. That gap is why Western writers on opposite sides of later Eucharistic disputes have all been able to quote him with a straight face. Reading him as an anticipation of a thirteenth-century distinction is an anachronism; reading him as a mere symbolist flattens "nature itself is changed."

## Watch out

- **The hymns are a genre before they are a corpus.** Ambrose wrote in iambic dimeter, eight four-line stanzas, sung antiphonally by a congregation split in two; during the basilica crisis of 385–386 with the empress Justina this became a weapon. Augustine was in Milan and saw it: the people kept guard in the church with their bishop, and at that time, "after the manner of the Eastern Church, hymns and psalms should be sung" was instituted (*Confessions* 9.7.15, NPNF 1st series vol. 1). But "[Ambrosian hymn](../reference.md#ambrosian-hymn)" soon named the *metre*, and imitators are numerous. The 1907 *Catholic Encyclopedia* article on Ambrosian chant reports that of the many hymns ascribed to him only fourteen are judged certainly his and four more probable. *Aeterne rerum conditor* is normally counted among the genuine; the certainty is *Deus creator omnium*, which Augustine quotes from memory (*Confessions* 9.12.32).
- **[Ambrosiaster](../reference.md#ambrosiaster) is the sharpest attribution case in Module 4.** An anonymous Latin commentary on the thirteen Pauline epistles, written at Rome in the time of Damasus, travelled inside Ambrose's works and was read as his for a thousand years. The 1907 *Catholic Encyclopedia* states flatly that he "is not the author." Worse for anyone who trusts old attributions: Augustine quotes its comment on Romans 5:12 — "wherein, that is, in Adam, all have sinned" — and ascribes it to "the sainted Hilary" (*Against Two Letters of the Pelagians* 4.7, NPNF 1st series vol. 5). So a sentence that matters enormously for [4.5](04-05-augustine-against-the-pelagians.md) has circulated under two wrong names. The name "Ambrosiaster," the would-be Ambrose, goes back to Erasmus, who rejected the attribution; the author remains unidentified.
- **[On the Sacraments](../reference.md#on-the-sacraments) is *On the Mysteries*' disputed twin.** Six books, plainer, apparently taken down as spoken, covering the same rites and containing the earliest Latin witness to a form of the Roman Canon of the Mass. Its authenticity was doubted from the sixteenth century, and the 1907 *Catholic Encyclopedia* still reports Bardenhewer's view that it is *On the Mysteries* "indiscreetly published by some hearer of Ambrose." The twentieth-century majority, after Faller and Botte, accepts it as a stenographic record of Ambrose's own catechesis. Say which view you assume before citing it: whether it is evidence for Ambrose or for a listener turns on it ([spurious and misattributed works](../reference.md#spurious-and-misattributed-works)).
- **Ambrose and Theodosius is not taught here.** The penance after the massacre at Thessalonica in 390, and the doubtful later story of the emperor barred at the church door, belong to [`church-history` 2.1](../../church-history/lessons/02-01-nicaea-and-the-imperial-church.md). Do not use it as evidence about his theology.
- **[Doctor of the Church](../reference.md#doctor-of-the-church) is an honour, not a warrant.** Ambrose is one of the four original Latin Doctors. The title does not raise any sentence of his to the level of Church teaching, and it certainly does not cover works that turn out not to be his.

## One-liner

> Ambrose argues from a rod, a river and a virgin birth to the altar, in Latin, out of a Greek library — and left so large a shadow that the first thing to check about any sentence of "Ambrose" is whether it is his.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢) *(Exegetical.)*** *On the Mysteries* 9.53, NPNF 2nd series, vol. 10 (de Romestin and Duckworth):

> But why make use of arguments? Let us use the examples He gives, and by the example of the Incarnation prove the truth of the mystery. Did the course of nature proceed as usual when the Lord Jesus was born of Mary? If we look to the usual course, a woman ordinarily conceives after connection with a man. And this body which we make is that which was born of the Virgin. Why do you seek the order of nature in the Body of Christ, seeing that the Lord Jesus Himself was born of a Virgin, not according to nature? It is the true Flesh of Christ which crucified and buried, this is then truly the Sacrament of His Body.

(a) The section switches from one kind of proof to another. Name both kinds and quote the words that mark the switch.
(b) State the analogy as a correspondence — what answers to what — and name the one thing it asks the objector to concede beyond the virgin birth itself.

Two sentences or fewer per part.

**P2 (🟡) *(Exegetical.)*** Two readers agree that chapter 9 teaches a change and disagree about what is changed. The first takes 9.51–53 to fix the sense of "changed"; the second takes 9.54 and 9.58 to fix it.

(a) Which step in the lesson's reconstruction does the second reader have to weaken, and on what feature of Ambrose's examples does he rest that objection?
(b) Name one thing in the chapter that each reader has to explain away, quoting the words in each case.

**150 words or fewer** in total.

**P3 (🔴, optional) *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)*** An invented parish blog post (not by any real author):

> "A wonderful line from St Ambrose for this week: 'the power of blessing is greater than that of nature.' And in his commentary on Romans he tells us that in Adam all have sinned. So the Doctor of Milan hands us the Eucharist and original sin in two sentences, and whoever disagrees is contradicting a Doctor of the Church."

(a) The note makes three moves: one accurate quotation and two failures. Identify each, say what is wrong with the two failures, and state what a usable citation of the quoted line would have to name. Two sentences or fewer per item.
(b) Scholars disagree about how far Ambrose's theology is his own and how far it is Origen, Basil and Philo recast in Latin. Take either side in **150 words or fewer**, using one piece of evidence from this lesson, and state the strongest point against your side.

<details>
<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical — strict.)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** he drops **argument** (the chain of premises and scriptural wonders in 9.50–52) in favour of **example**, that is, a single authoritative case offered as a precedent rather than as a premise. The words that mark it: "But why make use of arguments? Let us use the examples He gives." Credit for noting the examples are said to be *given by Christ*, so the switch is also from reasoning to authority.

**Must hit, strict (b):** the correspondence is virginal conception : consecration :: the body born of Mary : what is on the altar — in both, something comes to be outside "the usual course" of nature while looking ordinary from outside. The extra concession is the **identity of the two bodies**: "this body which we make is that which was born of the Virgin." Credit for seeing that without that identification the passage would only show that God can act outside nature, not that he has done so here.

**Wrong turns:** treating 9.53 as one more miracle on the 9.51 list, which misses that it is offered as the controlling case; answering (b) with "he must concede the virgin birth," which the question explicitly excludes; saying the switch is from Old Testament to New, which is true of the material but not the kind of proof.

**Model answer:** (a) He abandons argument from premises for argument from an authoritative example, marked by "But why make use of arguments? Let us use the examples He gives." (b) The virginal conception stands to the consecration as the body born of Mary stands to the body on the altar: in each, the usual order is set aside under an ordinary appearance. Beyond the virgin birth, the objector must concede that the two bodies are one and the same — "this body which we make is that which was born of the Virgin."

---

**P2** *(Exegetical — strict.)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** the step from the granted cases to the disputed one — premise 4, the *a fortiori* transfer (equivalently, the sense of "changed" it carries over to the identification made at 6). The feature he rests on: **every example in 9.51 is a change open to the senses** — rod into visible serpent, water into blood, floating iron — whereas nothing observable changes at the consecration, so "nature is changed" in Ambrose's usage need not mean a change of what a thing is under an unaltered appearance.

**Must hit, strict (b):** the first reader must account for 9.54, "after the consecration the Body is signified," and/or 9.58, "not bodily food but spiritual." The second must account for 9.50, "by blessing nature itself is changed," and/or 9.53, "this body which we make is that which was born of the Virgin." One quoted item per side is enough; the quotation is required.

**Wrong turns:** naming premise 2 (that blessings changed nature in Scripture), which the second reader grants; saying the second reader denies that any change occurs, when the stem states he grants one; answering with the traditions' labels instead of the text.

**Model answer:** (a) Premise 4, the transfer from a prophet's blessing to the consecration. His ground is that all Ambrose's cases are sensible changes — a rod visibly becomes a serpent, iron floats — while on the altar nothing observable alters, so "nature is changed" need not carry the weight the first reader puts on it. (b) The first reader must deal with 9.54, "after the consecration the Body is signified," and with 9.58's "not bodily food but spiritual." The second must deal with 9.50, "by blessing nature itself is changed," and with the identification in 9.53, "this body which we make is that which was born of the Virgin."

---

**P3** *(a) exegetical — strict; (b) evaluative — any verdict.*

**Must hit, strict (a):** three items.
(i) **Accurate:** "the power of blessing is greater than that of nature" is genuinely Ambrose, *On the Mysteries* 9.50. A usable citation names the **work, chapter and section, and the translation** — here NPNF 2nd series vol. 10, de Romestin and Duckworth — because the bare name is not checkable.
(ii) **Wrong attribution:** the commentary on Romans is not Ambrose's. It is Ambrosiaster, an anonymous Roman commentary of Damasus's time that travelled with Ambrose's works; Augustine quotes that very comment on Romans 5:12 under the name of "the sainted Hilary."
(iii) **Wrong use of authority:** "Doctor of the Church" honours holiness and teaching; it does not make a Father's sentences Church teaching, and the weight of Catholic Eucharistic doctrine comes from Trent's definition, not from Ambrose's authorship. Full credit requires saying the inference form is the error, not the conclusion.

**Must hit, any verdict (b):** state the two positions; pick one; give one concrete piece of evidence from the lesson (for "derivative": the *Catholic Encyclopedia*'s marked preference for Origen and Basil, Jerome's *Letter* 41, the Philonic material in the Genesis commentaries, and his arrival in the see with no theological training. For "his own": the mystagogical project itself, aimed at a congregation rather than at a school; the choice of the Incarnation as the controlling case; the hymns and antiphonal singing as a pastoral invention); and state the strongest point against your side. Either verdict passes; an answer with no counter-point does not.

**Wrong turns:** in (a), calling the Ambrose quotation fabricated — it is accurate and badly cited, which is the far commoner failure; treating the original-sin sentence as false rather than misattributed; saying the Doctor title is worthless rather than misused. In (b), settling the question by noting that Ambrose wrote in Latin, which both sides grant.

**Model answer (b), one of several:** Mostly derivative in material, his own in form. He came to the episcopate with no theological training and read his way in, with a marked preference for Origen and Basil; Jerome says as much in *Letter* 41, and the Philonic matter in the Genesis commentaries is plain. What is his is the shaping: the Greek exegesis arrives aimed at catechumens, in a mystagogy delivered after the rite, and the decisive move in chapter 9 — making the Incarnation rather than the miracle list carry the analogy — is a pastoral instinct, not a borrowed argument. The strongest point against me is that "form" may be doing too much work: a theologian who reorganizes an argument so that a different premise bears the weight has changed the argument, and on that description Ambrose is an original after all.

## Flashback

**From Lesson [3.5](03-05-allegory-and-history-alexandria-and-antioch.md) (Allegory and history: Alexandria and Antioch):** *(Exegetical.)* A study-guide sidebar, invented for this problem:

> "Alexandria allegorized; Antioch read literally. So for the plain sense go to the Antiochenes — Chrysostom and Theodoret hold to the letter and refuse figurative readings — whereas Origen held that the Old Testament narratives did not happen and stood for something else."

(a) Two of those statements are false as the lesson states the case. Name each and correct it in one sentence. (b) In one sentence, say what the Antiochenes actually denied in Origen's procedure, and what they did where he found an impossibility. **100 words or fewer.**

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):** (i) **"Antioch refuses figurative readings."** Chrysostom allegorizes constantly and Theodoret's commentaries are full of figures; what Antioch refuses is a further sense that *replaces* the narrative instead of resting on it — their name for the right procedure is *theoria*, in which the near historical referent and the further one both stand. (ii) **"Origen held that the Old Testament narratives did not happen."** He argues the opposite about most of Scripture, whose historical sense stands and is useful; his claim is the narrower and stranger one that at particular marked points the letter is impossible, and that the impossibility was put there on purpose.

**Must hit, strict (b):** they denied the **entrance condition** — the claim that the letter has failed. Where Origen found an impossibility they found an idiom, a figure or a translator's artefact, solvable at the level of the letter, so the summons to climb never arises. Credit for adding that the objection is about the survival of the narrative, not about figurative reading as such.

**Wrong turns:** correcting the sidebar by saying allegory is illegitimate, which neither school holds; stating the Antiochene complaint as "too figurative" rather than "the sequence has been broken"; answering (b) with Origen's later condemnation, which is a different question and not about this rule; replying that the sidebar is right about Alexandria and wrong only about Antioch — both halves are misstated.

**Model answer:** (a) Antioch does not refuse figures: Chrysostom allegorizes often and Theodoret is full of figures, and what they refuse is a sense that replaces the history rather than resting on it. And Origen does not deny the narratives: he holds the historical sense of most of Scripture, claiming only that at marked points the letter is deliberately impossible. (b) They denied his entrance condition, the claim that the letter had broken down, and treated his impossibilities instead as idiom, figure or translator's artefact, solved at the level of the letter so that no climb is required.

</details>
