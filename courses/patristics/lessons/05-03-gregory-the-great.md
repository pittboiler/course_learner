# The Church Fathers · Lesson 5.3: Gregory the Great

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 5: The Christological Fathers and the close of the patristic age · Builds on: [5.2 Leo the Great](05-02-leo-the-great.md), [3.3 Gregory of Nazianzus and Gregory of Nyssa](03-03-the-two-gregories.md) · Unlocks: [5.4 Maximus the Confessor and John of Damascus](05-04-maximus-and-john-of-damascus.md)

## Why this matters

Almost every Father you have read so far was driven by a controversy: a Gnostic, an Arian, a Nestorius. Gregory has no heresy to fight. His great book is a manual for bishops, his longest is a commentary that keeps turning into spiritual direction, and his most quoted letters are about a title. That makes him easy to file as an administrator who happened to write. Wrong file. Gregory is where the patristic age in the West stops being a series of arguments and becomes a set of working habits — how to preach to this person rather than that one, how to read Scripture for conduct, how to hold Roman authority and refuse a Roman title in the same paragraph. Those habits ran the Latin Church for five hundred years.

## The idea

Gregory was born at Rome about 540 into a senatorial family, and about 573, barely past thirty, held the city prefecture, Rome's highest civil office. Then he gave it up: he turned the family house on the Caelian into a monastery and became a monk in it, served as papal envoy at Constantinople, and in February 590, with plague in the city and the Lombards in Italy, was elected pope against his own loud protest. He died on 12 March 604. He is the last of the four great Latin Doctors — Ambrose ([4.1](04-01-ambrose-of-milan.md)), Jerome ([4.2](04-02-jerome.md)), Augustine ([4.3](04-03-augustine-confessions.md)), Gregory — and the Western patristic age is conventionally closed with him.

His governing conviction, stated on the first page of the [*Pastoral Rule*](../reference.md#pastoral-rule), is that ruling souls is a **skill**, and a harder one than medicine:

> the government of souls is the art of arts

(*Pastoral Rule* I.1, NPNF 2nd series, Barmby's translation; the book is addressed to John, bishop of Ravenna, and dates from the start of his pontificate.) The comparison is exact and he presses it: nobody untrained dares practise on bodies, yet men with no knowledge of spiritual precepts cheerfully call themselves physicians of the heart, though "the sores of the thoughts of men are more occult than the sores of the bowels." Hidden wounds require diagnosis before treatment. That single move generates everything else in the book.

One standing caution, since this is the course's discipline. Gregory is a Father and a Doctor, and none of that makes any particular opinion of his Church teaching; where his texts touch doctrine, the level is fixed by the owning course and read off the ladder in [`fundamental-theology` 4.4](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-04-the-ladder-of-doctrinal-authority.md).

## Source

*Pastoral Rule*, Book III, Prologue (NPNF 2nd series, Barmby):

> Since, then, we have shown what manner of man the pastor ought to be, let us now set forth after what manner he should teach. For, as long before us Gregory Nazianzen of reverend memory has taught, one and the same exhortation does not suit all, inasmuch as neither are all bound together by similarity of character. For the things that profit some often hurt others; seeing that also for the most part herbs which nourish some animals are fatal to others; and the gentle hissing that quiets horses incites whelps; and the medicine which abates one disease aggravates another; and the bread which invigorates the life of the strong kills little children. Therefore according to the quality of the hearers ought the discourse of teachers to be fashioned, so as to suit all and each for their several needs, and yet never deviate from the art of common edification.

Two things to notice before anything else. Gregory names his source: Gregory Nazianzen, whose defence of his own flight from ordination lies behind the whole book ([3.3](03-03-the-two-gregories.md)). And the four images are all *dosage* images. Nothing here says the truth varies; it says the quantity and the manner do.

## The argument

The *Pastoral Rule* in five steps, each dense line followed by its translation.

1. **The care of souls is an art, and its object is hidden.** *In words:* you cannot treat what you have not diagnosed, and thoughts are less visible than intestines.
2. **Therefore the ruler's first qualification is his own life**, since the unexamined physician misreads the patient by misreading himself. *In words:* Book II is about the bishop's character — pure in thought, discreet in silence, useful in speech, neighbour to all in compassion and above all in contemplation — because character is the diagnostic instrument.
3. **Hearers are not uniform, so one exhortation cannot fit them** (the Source). *In words:* the same sermon that heals the despairing feeds the complacent.
4. **So the art must be catalogued**: Book III sorts hearers into **thirty-six paired opposites** and gives each pair its own remedy, usually by crossing them — the joyful hear about judgement, the sad about the kingdom. *In words:* preaching is triage, and the list is the formulary.
5. **Every remedy carries its own risk**, so the cure for one fault must be dosed against the fault it invites. *In words:* comfort the fearful too hard and you make them presumptuous; that is why the pairs are pairs.

The same instinct governs his exegesis. In the *Moralia on Job*, thirty-five books begun as lectures at Constantinople and dedicated to Leander of Seville, Gregory lays out the plan he follows throughout: expound the historical sense first, then build the typical or allegorical sense on it, and then finish with the [moral sense](../reference.md#the-moral-or-tropological-sense), the *moralitas* that tells the reader what to do (dedicatory letter to Leander, section 3). All three are old — Origen had a threefold scheme and the Antiochenes objected to how he used it ([3.5](03-05-allegory-and-history-alexandria-and-antioch.md)). What Gregory does is put the *moral* sense last and make it the point of arrival, and it is his version, not Origen's, that the Latin Middle Ages inherits. The full four-sense doctrine as Catholic teaching belongs to [`old-testament`](../../old-testament/syllabus.md) 1.2.

## Map of positions

Between 595 and 602 Gregory wrote repeatedly against John the Faster, patriarch of Constantinople, for taking the title [ecumenical or universal](../reference.md#the-title-universal-bishop). Catholic, Orthodox and Protestant readers all quote these letters, and quote different sentences of them. State both readings; **which is right is argued in `apologetics-catholic-claims`, not here.**

| | Catholic reading | Orthodox and Protestant reading |
|---|---|---|
| **What Gregory refuses** | A title that would make one bishop *the* bishop and so unchurch the rest — a claim about the episcopate, not about Rome's authority | Any claim by any see, Rome included, to be bishop of the whole Church |
| **Words it leans on** | Chalcedon "offered" the name to the popes and none "wished to be called by such a title" (Letter V.18): a title Rome could have had and declined. In V.43 Gregory approves his predecessor Pelagius II annulling John's synod "by the authority of the holy apostle Peter" — and does so in the same letter that condemns the title | Peter himself "is not called the universal apostle" though "the care and principality of the whole Church is committed to him" (V.20). Whoever "desires to be called, Universal Priest, is in his elation the precursor of Antichrist" (VII.33) — a sentence later Protestant polemic turned back on Rome |
| **What it must explain** | Why Gregory says the honour was *offered* to Rome and refused, rather than that it belongs to Rome | Why a pope who denies that any bishop is universal annuls an Eastern synod by Peter's authority, confirms and rebukes patriarchs, and is obeyed |

Both read the same letters honestly; the disagreement is over which sentence is load-bearing, and re-reading alone does not settle that — which is the lesson.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean: the formulary in use).** Take the pair "the joyful and the sad" (Book III, Admonition 4). Gregory crosses them: set before the joyful the sad things that follow on punishment, and before the sad the promised joys of the kingdom. Then he adds the move that makes the *Rule* more than a table of opposites. Some people are joyful or sad not by circumstance but by temperament, and each temperament has a characteristic vice standing next to it — "the joyful have lechery close at hand, and the sad wrath." So the preacher must warn each hearer not only about the fault he has but about the one he thinks he is safe from. Notice the structure, because every one of the thirty-six admonitions has it: *name the condition, cross the remedy, then name the vice the remedy risks feeding.*

**Example 2 (where it strains: scaling the rule to a people).** In July 601 Gregory wrote to the abbot Mellitus, travelling to join Augustine in Kent, that the idol temples of the English should not be destroyed, only the idols in them; let the buildings be blessed and reused, and let the people keep their feast-day cattle, now killed for food and thanksgiving rather than sacrifice. His reason is Book III's reason, applied to a whole nation:

> it is undoubtedly impossible to cut away everything at once from hard hearts, since one who strives to ascend to the highest place must needs rise by steps or paces, and not by leaps

(Letter XI.76 in Barmby's numbering; the same letter is preserved in Bede, *Ecclesiastical History* I.30.)

Here is the strain. A month earlier Gregory had written to King Æthelberht urging him to overthrow the temple structures (Bede I.32). Either he changed his mind within weeks, or he was telling a king to lead from the front while telling his missionaries to go gently — which is precisely what "according to the quality of the hearers" would prescribe, with the king and the countryside as the pair. The reading you take determines whether "steps, not leaps" is pastoral diagnosis or an improvised concession, and the letters do not say. The mission itself, and the debate over what "conversion" meant, belong to [`church-history` 3.1](../../church-history/lessons/03-01-gregory-the-great-and-the-conversion-of-the-peoples.md), which reads this same letter for a different question.

## Watch out

- **You might think the [*Dialogues*](../reference.md#the-dialogues-of-gregory) are simply Gregory's.** Four books of miracle stories, of which Book II is our principal life of St Benedict, and Book IV an early Latin witness to prayer for the dead and purifying fire (doctrine: `creation-grace-last-things`). Francis Clark argued in 1987 that the work is a late-seventh-century compilation built around genuine Gregorian fragments. **This is a minority view**; specialists such as Paul Meyvaert and Adalbert de Vogüé answered it and the authenticity of the *Dialogues* remains the majority position. Say "disputed by Clark, majority against him" — not "spurious", and not "undisputed".
- **You might think Gregory coined [servant of the servants of God](../reference.md#servus-servorum-dei) to rebuke John the Faster.** John the Deacon, writing in the ninth century, says so. But Gregory used the phrase of himself in April 591, addressing Leander, four years before the controversy, and a document of 570 under John III already opens *servus servorum Dei*. He made it the standing papal style; he did not invent it, and the rebuke story is a later gloss. The same ninth-century *Life* is also the earliest authority for Gregory's reform of the chant that bears his name, which is disputed for the same reason.
- **You might think the "universal" letters settle a question about the papacy.** They are letters of one pope in one quarrel, not a definition. Treating a Father's polemic as Church teaching is the standing exegetical error of this course ([1.1](01-01-how-to-read-a-church-father.md)).
- **You might think Book III is casuistry about audiences.** It rests on the preacher's self-knowledge: the catalogue only works if the man using it can tell a despairing hearer from a lazy one, which Book II makes a function of his own character.

**One line on the Western close.** Isidore of Seville (c. 560–636), brother of the Leander to whom Gregory dedicated the *Moralia*, wrote the *Etymologies*, an encyclopedia packing what survived of ancient learning into a form the early Middle Ages could carry; with him the Latin Fathers end and the schoolbooks begin.

## One-liner

> Gregory turned the care of souls into an art with a formulary — diagnose the hearer, cross the remedy, watch the vice the remedy feeds — and applied the same rule to Scripture, to the English, and to a patriarch's title.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢) *(Exegetical.)*** *Pastoral Rule* III, Admonition 4 (NPNF 2nd series, Barmby):

> Differently to be admonished are the joyful and the sad. That is, before the joyful are to be set the sad things that follow upon punishment; but before the sad the promised glad things of the kingdom. … But some are not made joyful or sad by circumstances, but are so by temperament. And to such it should be intimated that certain defects are connected with certain temperaments; that the joyful have lechery close at hand, and the sad wrath. Hence it is necessary for every one to consider not only what he suffers from his peculiar temperament, but also what worse thing presses on him in connection with it; lest, while he fights not at all against that which he has, he succumb also to that from which he supposes himself free.

(a) The passage makes **two distinct** moves, not one. Name each, and quote the word or phrase that marks where the second begins.
(b) On Gregory's account, what is the danger of treating a hearer's stated condition as the whole diagnosis? Answer from the last sentence only.
Two sentences per part.

**P2 (🟡) *(Exegetical.)*** An invented parish-bulletin note, written for this problem:

> "Our new pastor has asked why he preaches so differently at the 8 a.m. and the 6 p.m. Mass. St Gregory the Great answers for him: 'one and the same exhortation does not suit all.' The Fathers teach that doctrine itself must be adapted to what each congregation is ready to hear. A pastor who hands everyone the same exhortation has not served them all."

(a) Identify the one claim in the note that Gregory's text does **not** support, and say in one sentence what the Source passage says instead — pointing to the words that decide it.
(b) The note quotes Gregory accurately and still goes wrong. Name the general move, and state the one-question test a reader could have applied to the quotation to catch it. 100 words or fewer in total.

**P3 (🔴, optional) *(Exegetical (a), (b) · Evaluative (c).)*** Attribution.

(a) A writer cites "Gregory the Great, *Dialogues* II" for a detail in the life of St Benedict. What must he say about the text's standing, and in what terms? One sentence.
(b) A second writer says Gregory adopted the style "servant of the servants of God" in order to shame the patriarch of Constantinople. State the evidence against this, and name the source of the claim and its date.
(c) The chant attributed to Gregory and the "servant of the servants" story rest on the same ninth-century *Life*. Does one later witness supporting two claims make them stand or fall together? Say what evidence would change your verdict. 120 words or fewer, any verdict.

<details>
<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical — strict.)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** Move one is the **crossed remedy** — the joyful are given the sad things that follow on punishment, the sad the promised joys of the kingdom; the treatment is the opposite of the condition. Move two shifts from condition to **temperament**, and to the vice that stands next to each temperament (lechery beside joy, wrath beside sadness). The marker is "**But some are not made joyful or sad by circumstances, but are so by temperament**" — credit for quoting "by temperament" or the "But some…" clause. Full credit requires seeing that the second move is about a *different* object, not a stronger version of the first.

**Must hit, strict (b):** a hearer who fights only the fault he can see will be overtaken by the one he assumes he is free of — Gregory's "lest, while he fights not at all against that which he has, he succumb also to that from which he supposes himself free." So the stated condition under-describes the patient; the adjacent vice has to be named as well.

**Wrong turns:** reading the two moves as one ("Gregory says give people the opposite of what they feel"), which loses the whole second half; treating "temperament" as simply a stronger mood; answering (b) with a general remark about humility rather than from the sentence.

**Model answer:** (a) First, the crossed remedy: judgement to the joyful, the kingdom's joys to the sad. Second, at "But some are not made joyful or sad by circumstances, but are so by temperament," he turns to settled temperament and the vice attached to it — lechery to the joyful, wrath to the sad. (b) Because the hearer defends himself only where he knows he is weak; the sentence warns that he "succumb also to that from which he supposes himself free," so the remedy must name the neighbouring vice too.

---

**P2** *(Exegetical — strict.)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** the claim that "**doctrine itself must be adapted to what each congregation is ready to hear**." Gregory's Source passage varies the *manner and measure* of exhortation, not its content: the images are all dosage images (herbs, hissing, medicine, bread), and the clause that fixes it is "yet never deviate from the **art of common edification**" — one doctrine, many exhortations. Credit also for "according to the quality of the **hearers** ought the **discourse of teachers** to be fashioned," which locates the variation in the discourse, not the thing taught.

**Must hit, strict (b):** the move is **widening an accurate quotation past what it claims** — using a text about pastoral method to license a claim about doctrinal content. The test: **read the rest of the sentence the quoted clause comes from**, and ask what the quoted words are predicated of. Here the subject is the *exhortation* and the *discourse of teachers*, and the same sentence ends by forbidding any deviation from the art of common edification, so the clause cannot be about the content of the faith. Equivalent formulations of the test pass: ask what noun the variation attaches to; ask what limit the passage itself sets.

**Wrong turns:** objecting to the first sentence, which is unobjectionable; calling the quotation itself inaccurate — it is accurate, which is the point; answering (b) with "quoting out of context" and stopping, without naming a test.

**Model answer:** (a) The claim that doctrine itself must be adapted. Gregory varies the exhortation, not the faith: his images are all about dosage, and he requires that the teacher "never deviate from the art of common edification." (b) The move is stretching a correct quotation past what it claims — method taken for content. The test is to read the whole sentence the clause sits in and ask what varies: here it is the discourse of the teacher, bounded by common edification, not the thing taught.

---

**P3** *(a), (b) exegetical — strict; (c) evaluative — any verdict.*

**Must hit, strict (a):** that the authenticity of the *Dialogues* is **disputed but the majority position holds them genuine** — Francis Clark's 1987 case for a late-seventh-century compilation is a minority view answered by Meyvaert, de Vogüé and others. The required terms are "disputed, majority for authenticity," given *as* a majority view. Saying "spurious" is an error; saying nothing at all is the error this course exists to prevent.

**Must hit, strict (b):** Gregory used *servus servorum Dei* of himself in April 591, writing to Leander, which is before the controversy with John the Faster (from 595); and the phrase is already found in a document of 570 under John III. The claim's source is **John the Deacon's *Life of Gregory*, ninth century** — roughly 250 years after the events. Credit for adding that Gregory did make it the standing papal style, so the tradition is not wholly wrong, only wrong about the origin.

**Must hit, any verdict (c):** distinguish the two claims — they are about different subject matter (a chancery style with independent documentary traces; a liturgical reform with few), so the evidence available to check each differs. Then take a position: either that a single late witness whose testimony is falsified on one point loses standing on the other (a credibility argument), or that each claim must be tested against its own independent evidence, so they can come apart (an evidential argument). Either verdict passes if the reasoning names *what independent evidence exists* for each. State whether your verdict would change if independent evidence turned up for the chant.

**Wrong turns:** (a) calling the *Dialogues* spurious on Clark's authority, or presenting the dispute as settled either way; (b) giving only the 591 letter and omitting the 570 document or the source of the story; (c) treating "one source, therefore false" as an argument — antiquity of attestation is one criterion among several, and much of what we know about Gregory's Rome comes from single witnesses.

**Model answer (c), one of several:** They come apart. John the Deacon is a late witness for both, but the *servus servorum* claim can be checked directly against Gregory's own dated letters, and it fails there — so on that point he is demonstrably reconstructing a motive rather than reporting. That lowers his value as a witness to motives generally, and the chant story is a motive-and-origin story too. But it does not falsify the chant claim, which has to be tested against Roman liturgical evidence on its own. My verdict: treat John the Deacon as unreliable for origins and neutral for facts he had no interest in shaping, and report the chant attribution as disputed rather than as refuted.

## Flashback

**From Lesson [5.1](05-01-cyril-of-alexandria.md) (Cyril of Alexandria):** *(Exegetical.)* Three Cyrilline texts of the Nestorian controversy: the **Second Letter to Nestorius**; the **Twelve Anathemas** attached to the Third Letter; and the **Formula of Reunion of 433**, which reaches us inside Cyril's peace letter to John of Antioch.

(a) For each, state what a council did or did not do with it — at Ephesus in 431, and at Chalcedon in 451. **One sentence each.**
(b) A footnote reads: "the Council of Ephesus condemned Nestorius's Christology in twelve canons (Ephesus, Anathemas 1-12)." Say what is wrong with it, name the kind of error, and give the citation the writer should have used. **Two sentences.**

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):** *Second Letter* — read at the first session of Ephesus in 431 and approved by the bishops one by one, and read again at Chalcedon in 451 and acclaimed. *Twelve Anathemas* — sent from an Alexandrian synod as an ultimatum and read into the acts at Ephesus, but the acts record no discussion and no vote on them, and Chalcedon did not read the letter at all. *Formula of Reunion* — a reconciliation document of 433, so no act of Ephesus stands behind it; it was acclaimed at Chalcedon along with the Second Letter, as part of Cyril's peace letter.
**Must hit, strict (b):** the twelve are **Cyril's**, issued by an Alexandrian synod before the council and never voted as canons of it; reading a text into the acts is not defining it, and Chalcedon's reception fell on the Second Letter, not on the anathemas. The error is an **overstatement of the level of authority** — a private or synodal text promoted to a conciliar definition, the authority error this course grades strictly (levels: [`fundamental-theology` 4.4](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-04-the-ladder-of-doctrinal-authority.md)). Correct citation: Cyril of Alexandria, Third Letter to Nestorius, the twelve anathemas (NPNF second series, vol. 14); for what the council received, cite the Second Letter.
**Wrong turns:** saying the anathemas are spurious or that Ephesus rejected them — neither is true, and the point is the weight of a genuine text, not its authenticity; treating the Formula of Reunion as an act of Ephesus, when it postdates the council by two years and is a settlement between parties; answering (b) by arguing about whether the anathemas are theologically sound, which is not what the footnote gets wrong.
**Model answer:** (a) The Second Letter was read at the first session of Ephesus and approved by the bishops individually, then read and acclaimed again at Chalcedon. The Twelve Anathemas came from an Alexandrian synod as an ultimatum and were read into the Ephesine acts with no recorded discussion or vote, and Chalcedon never read them. The Formula of Reunion belongs to 433, so Ephesus did nothing with it, while Chalcedon acclaimed it inside Cyril's peace letter. (b) The twelve are Cyril's own, not canons of the council, and being read into the acts is not being defined; that is an overstatement of level, the standing authority error here. It should be cited as Cyril, Third Letter to Nestorius, anathemas 1-12 (NPNF second series, vol. 14), with the Second Letter cited for what Ephesus actually approved.
