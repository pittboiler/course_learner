# The Church Fathers · Lesson 1.3: Ignatius of Antioch

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 1: Reading the Fathers, and the first witnesses · Builds on: [1.1 How to read a Church Father](01-01-how-to-read-a-church-father.md), [1.2 1 Clement and the Didache](01-02-first-clement-and-the-didache.md) · Unlocks: [1.4 Justin Martyr](01-04-justin-martyr.md)

## Why this matters

Three things from these letters turn up in nearly every modern argument about the early Church. One bishop in each city, with presbyters and deacons under him. The Eucharist is the flesh of Jesus Christ. And the phrase "the Catholic Church", here for the first time in any surviving text. If the letters are what they claim to be — written under guard around 110 by the bishop of Antioch on his way to the beasts — then the churches of Asia Minor already looked startlingly like later Catholic dioceses, within living memory of the apostles. That "if" is the whole lesson. The letters survive in three versions, one of them a fourth-century expansion that went to work on exactly the sentences people quote. [1.1](01-01-how-to-read-a-church-father.md)'s [check](../reference.md#how-to-check-a-patristic-quotation) runs here before anything else.

## The idea

### The man and the road

Ignatius was bishop of Antioch in Syria, the empire's third city. He was condemned — we are never told for what, or by whom — and sent to Rome for the arena, guarded by soldiers he calls ten leopards, who got worse the better they were treated. The journey went overland across Asia Minor, and he wrote at two stops. From **Smyrna**: to the Ephesians, the Magnesians, the Trallians, and to the Christians at Rome. From **Troas**: to the Philadelphians, to the Smyrnaeans, and to Polycarp, bishop of Smyrna. Seven letters. Eusebius lists exactly these, with the two places of writing (*Church History* III.36.5–10).

The date is traditional, not documented. Eusebius sets Ignatius under Trajan (98–117), and "about 110" is conventional; nothing in the letters names a year, so treat it as a range. Why Rome executed Christians at all is [`church-history` 1.3](../../church-history/lessons/01-03-persecution-and-martyrdom.md); what is read here is what he wrote.

### Which letters are his

By the Middle Ages the Ignatian corpus circulated as thirteen letters, and even the genuine ones had grown. Untangling it took two centuries. **Ussher** (1644) found and published a Latin version, made about 1250 by Robert Grosseteste, of a shorter and unexpanded text. **Isaac Voss** (1646) printed the Greek of that same short form from the Medicean codex at Florence, and Pearson's *Vindiciae Ignatianae* (1672) defended it. **Cureton** (1843) then found Syriac manuscripts with only three letters, shorter still, and argued that *these* were original; by the end of the century **Lightfoot**, Zahn and Harnack had shown the Syriac to be an abridgement. What survived is the **middle recension**: the seven letters Eusebius names, in the form Ussher and Voss recovered, and the text printed in Schaff.

Two external checks matter. Polycarp, writing to Philippi soon after, says he is forwarding the letters of Ignatius he has (in Eusebius, *Church History* III.36.13–15). Irenaeus, about 180, quotes Ignatius's wheat-and-bread sentence as a saying of one of their own, without the name (*Against Heresies* V.28.4).

There is a live minority. Reinhard Hübner (1997) and Thomas Lechner (1999) argued that the seven are a later second-century forgery; T. D. Barnes (2008) kept them as Ignatius's but moved him to the 140s. Most patristic scholars follow neither. Say "majority view" and mean it: the seven are genuine and early-second-century, a scholarly consensus, not a doctrine.

## Source

Ignatius, *To the Smyrnaeans* 6–7, in the Roberts–Donaldson translation (*Ante-Nicene Fathers*, volume 1):

> But consider those who are of a different opinion with respect to the grace of Christ which has come unto us, how opposed they are to the will of God. They have no regard for love; no care for the widow, or the orphan, or the oppressed; of the bond, or of the free; of the hungry, or of the thirsty. … They abstain from the Eucharist and from prayer, because they confess not the Eucharist to be the flesh of our Saviour Jesus Christ, which suffered for our sins, and which the Father, of His goodness, raised up again.

Read the halves together. The charge is behavioural before it is doctrinal — these people do not come to the Eucharist — and the doctrine is given as the *reason* for the behaviour.

## The argument

Ignatius's opponents at Smyrna held that Christ only seemed to have a body and only seemed to suffer, the position later called [docetism](../reference.md#docetism), from the Greek for "seem". Chapters 1–7 are one argument.

1. **Christ was truly of David's line, truly born, truly nailed to the cross, truly raised in the flesh** (chs. 1–3). Ignatius piles up the adverb. *In words: what is under attack is not Christ's divinity but the reality of his body.*
2. **[The Eucharist is that same flesh](../reference.md#the-eucharist-against-docetism)** — the flesh that suffered and was raised (ch. 7). *In words: the sacrament is identified with the body premise 1 insists was real.*
3. **So a consistent docetist must stay away from the Eucharist — and these men do** (chs. 6–7). *In words: their practice is the evidence for their doctrine.*
4. **The denial also empties Christian suffering.** If Christ only seemed to suffer, then Ignatius is only seemingly in chains, and his coming death is theatre (ch. 4). *In words: he offers his own body as a premise.*
5. **The same contempt for flesh shows in their conduct**: no care for widow, orphan, prisoner, hungry (ch. 6). *In words: a theology that despises bodies will not feed any.*
6. **Therefore keep clear of them, and hold to the prophets and the Gospel** (ch. 7).

Notice the shape. This is no appeal to a proof-text or to office. It runs from *what the Church does* — how it eats, whom it feeds — back to what must be true for the practice to make sense. Basil argues the same way about the Holy Spirit three centuries later ([3.2](03-02-basil-the-great.md)).

## Map of the recensions

| [Recension](../reference.md#the-three-recensions-of-ignatius) | Contents | Date of the text | How it reaches us | Good for |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Short (Syriac)** | 3 letters, abridged: Ephesians, Romans, Polycarp | an abridgement, made later | Cureton's Nitrian manuscripts, 1843 | a Syriac abridgement; not the original |
| **Middle** | the 7 Eusebius names | the letters themselves; early second century on the majority view | Greek from the Medicean codex, which lacks *Romans*; *Romans* separately, attached to an account of the martyrdom; Latin in Grosseteste's version | **the text to quote** |
| **Long** | the 7, interpolated, plus 6 forged letters | late fourth century; often identified with the compiler of the *Apostolic Constitutions* | Greek and Latin manuscripts, and the old printed editions | fourth-century Syria, never Ignatius |

Two traps in the last row. The six extra letters are easy to catch: they are not on Eusebius's list. The interpolations *inside* the genuine seven are not, since they sit in a letter with the right name on it, and they cluster on the bishop, the Trinity and Christ's person — exactly the sentences later readers want. P2 makes you look at one.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean: the bishop).** Ignatius says a version of the same thing to three churches. To the Magnesians: "your bishop presides in the place of God, and your presbyters in the place of the assembly of the apostles, along with your deacons" (*Magnesians* 6). To the Trallians: reverence the deacons as an appointment of Jesus Christ, the bishop as Jesus Christ, the presbyters as the assembly of the apostles; apart from these there is no Church (*Trallians* 3). To the Smyrnaeans: follow the bishop as Jesus Christ follows the Father and the presbytery as the apostles; do nothing connected with the Church without the bishop; a proper Eucharist is one the bishop holds or entrusts (*Smyrnaeans* 8).

Run the check: all three are middle-recension, all three are in ANF, all three name the same threefold pattern. This is the earliest clear evidence for the [monepiscopate](../reference.md#monepiscopate): one bishop to a city, a college of presbyters under him, deacons in ministry. It stands a decade or two after 1 Clement, where "bishop" and "presbyter" still name one office and Rome writes as a church rather than through a man ([1.2](01-02-first-clement-and-the-didache.md)).

Now the limits, which are as much part of the reading as the passages. Ignatius writes to churches in western Asia Minor; his letter to Rome greets no bishop there and names none. He is urging the pattern, not taking a census — nobody exhorts at this pitch about what everyone already does. And his warrant is theological analogy (bishop to God or to Christ, presbyters to the apostles), not an apostolic command he can cite. Whether the move from Clement to Ignatius is the apostolic order becoming visible or a post-apostolic development is the historians' quarrel ([`church-history` 1.2](../../church-history/lessons/01-02-bishops-canon-and-the-parting-of-the-ways.md)); what it proves theologically belongs to [`ecclesiology-and-mariology`](../../ecclesiology-and-mariology/syllabus.md) and [`apologetics-catholic-claims`](../../apologetics-catholic-claims/syllabus.md), not here.

The same chapter carries the first "Catholic Church": wherever Jesus Christ is, there is the Catholic Church. The adjective means whole or universal, and the sentence sets it against the local assembly round its bishop in the clause before. It is a description, not yet a name marking one communion off from another.

**Example 2 (where it strains: the preface to *Romans*).** Ignatius greets the Roman church with two clauses built on the same verb. ANF: it "presides in the place of the region of the Romans", and it is the church "which presides over love". Lightfoot renders both verbs with "presidency". The Greek behind the second is a participle governing the genitive *agapē*, love, which modern versions often render "presiding in love". Three [readings](../reference.md#the-romans-preface), with the words each leans on.

- **A primacy over the whole Church.** *Agapē* names the communion of churches joined in love, so the second clause says something the first does not. Leans on the contrast between the clauses: why say it twice, unless the second is wider?
- **A primacy of love and witness, not of jurisdiction** — the reading of the Orthodox theologian Nicholas Afanasiev. *Agapē* is the eucharistic assembly; Rome presides among equal churches as the one whose charity and martyrs are exemplary. Leans on Ignatius's eucharistic use of the word, and on the letter's contents, which beg Rome for prayers and for silence and ask for no ruling.
- **Preeminence in charity, or in the love-feast.** Leans on the ordinary sense of *agapē* in the period, and on the first clause, which localizes the presidency geographically and so fixes the scope of both.

The passage will not decide between them: a genitive carries the weight, with little context to steady it. Whether it supports Catholic claims is argued in [`apologetics-catholic-claims`](../../apologetics-catholic-claims/syllabus.md); here you state the readings and the words.

## Watch out

- **You might think a sentence with "Ignatius" under it is Ignatius's.** Older editions and much devotional material print the long recension. Before using a line about the bishop, the Trinity or Christ's person, check it against the middle recension in ANF — precisely where the fourth-century editor worked.
- **You might think Ignatius describes.** He exhorts. Every one of the bishop passages is an imperative, and an imperative is evidence that something was being urged, not proof that it was universal.
- **You might think his eagerness for death is the Church's teaching on martyrdom.** Read *Romans* 4–6 as it stands: he begs the Roman Christians not to intervene, calls himself God's wheat to be ground by the beasts' teeth into pure bread, and says that if the animals hold back he will provoke them. His stated reason is to be an imitator of the passion of his God — [martyrdom as imitation](../reference.md#martyrdom-as-imitation), the idea the later cult of the martyrs is built on. It is a first-rate witness to one man's mind, and it sits at no level on the ladder of doctrinal authority ([`fundamental-theology` 4.4](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-04-the-ladder-of-doctrinal-authority.md)). Later Church discipline discouraged volunteering for martyrdom, and Ignatius nowhere tells anyone else to seek it.
- **You might think "the Catholic Church" in *Smyrnaeans* 8 carries its modern sense.** It means the whole Church as against the local one. Reading the later denominational sense back into 110 is a plain anachronism, and a common one.

## One-liner

> Seven letters from a condemned bishop are the earliest snapshot we have of a Church with one bishop, a flesh-and-blood Eucharist, and a name for itself — provided you are reading the right seven, in the right version.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢) *(Exegetical.)*** Using only the Source passage (*Smyrnaeans* 6–7):

(a) State in one sentence the inference Ignatius draws from his opponents' behaviour at the Eucharist, and quote the clause that carries it.
(b) He also charges them with having no care for the widow, the orphan and the hungry. In one sentence, say how that charge connects to the doctrinal one on Ignatius's own logic.

**P2 (🟡) *(Exegetical.)*** *Trallians* 3, in the two Greek recensions (Roberts–Donaldson, ANF).

Middle recension:

> In like manner, let all reverence the deacons as an appointment of Jesus Christ, and the bishop as Jesus Christ, who is the Son of the Father, and the presbyters as the sanhedrim of God, and assembly of the apostles. Apart from these, there is no Church.

Long recension:

> And do ye reverence them as Christ Jesus, of whose place they are the keepers, even as the bishop is the representative of the Father of all things, and the presbyters are the sanhedrim of God, and assembly of the apostles of Christ. Apart from these there is no elect Church, no congregation of holy ones, no assembly of saints.

(a) List the changes the long recension makes, and say which single change alters what the passage claims about the bishop.
(b) A writer wants to cite this passage as second-century evidence that the bishop stands in the place of Christ. Say which text he may use, and what he must say about the other. **120 words or fewer in total.**

**P3 (🔴, optional) *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)*** An invented parish-bulletin note:

> "St Ignatius of Antioch, martyred around 110, settles the question. He tells the Smyrnaeans that a proper Eucharist is the bishop's, and he greets the Romans as the church that presides in love. So by 110 the Church already had bishops with sacramental authority and a Roman primacy over everyone else — and whoever says these things developed later is arguing against a contemporary of the apostles."

The *Smyrnaeans* sentence is genuine and correctly reported.

(a) Name **two** distinct things the note does with the *Romans* preface that the text does not license, using the readings in Example 2.
(b) In **150 words or fewer**, any verdict: the note treats "a contemporary of the apostles wrote it" as settling a historical question. Say what one letter-writer's testimony can and cannot establish about the practice of the churches he writes to, and name one kind of further evidence that would strengthen or weaken the note's claim. Use the limits given in Example 1.

<details>
<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical — strict.)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** they stay away from the Eucharist *because* they do not confess it to be Christ's flesh — the clause is "because they confess not the Eucharist to be the flesh of our Saviour Jesus Christ". The inference runs from practice back to belief: the abstention is the visible sign of the denial, and Ignatius reads it that way.

**Must hit, strict (b):** on Ignatius's logic the two charges are one charge. A theology that denies Christ took real flesh has no reason to honour anybody else's flesh, so refusing the eucharistic body and neglecting hungry and oppressed bodies come out of the same denial. Credit for noting that the chapter presents them in a single breath, the conduct first.

**Wrong turns:** reading (a) as excommunication — Ignatius says they abstain, not that they are barred; importing later eucharistic doctrine (transubstantiation, the sacrifice of the Mass) that the passage does not contain; treating (b) as a separate moral complaint, unconnected to the doctrine.

**Model answer:** (a) Ignatius infers their doctrine from their practice: they will not come to the Eucharist "because they confess not the Eucharist to be the flesh of our Saviour Jesus Christ, which suffered for our sins". (b) The neglect of widow, orphan and hungry is the same denial worked out in conduct — if flesh is not what God took and saved, no particular flesh is worth feeding.

---

**P2** *(Exegetical — strict.)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** four changes, of which the second is the decisive one.
1. The deacons stop being "an appointment of Jesus Christ" and become men to be reverenced "as Christ Jesus, of whose place they are the keepers".
2. **The bishop's type changes from Christ to the Father.** Middle: "the bishop as Jesus Christ, who is the Son of the Father". Long: "the bishop is the representative of the Father of all things". The clause identifying Christ as Son of the Father disappears with it.
3. The presbyters' "assembly of the apostles" becomes "assembly of the apostles of Christ".
4. The bare "there is no Church" is expanded into a triplet: "no elect Church, no congregation of holy ones, no assembly of saints".

Change 2 is the one that alters the claim. In the middle recension the bishop images Christ; in the long recension he images the Father, and the passage no longer says the thing it is usually cited for. (Why a fourth-century editor made that switch is disputed — harmonizing with *Magnesians* 6, where the bishop presides "in the place of God", is one suggestion. The student is not asked to settle it.)

**Must hit, strict (b):** he may quote the **middle recension**, the text of the seven letters Eusebius names, as printed in ANF — there the bishop is typed to Jesus Christ and the claim holds. He must (i) say which recension he is quoting, (ii) say that the long recension reads differently at exactly this point, and (iii) note that the long recension is a late-fourth-century expansion and is evidence for its own period, not for the second century.

**Wrong turns:** calling the long recension a worthless forgery and stopping — it is real evidence for fourth-century Syria, and the genuine seven are still inside it, under the interpolations ([1.1](01-01-how-to-read-a-church-father.md)); concluding that because the recensions differ the passage proves nothing; listing only the triplet at the end and missing the Father-for-Christ substitution.

**Model answer:** (a) The long text reverences the deacons as Christ rather than as his appointment, drops "who is the Son of the Father", makes the presbyters the apostles "of Christ", and triples the final clause. The decisive change is the bishop: no longer "as Jesus Christ" but "the representative of the Father of all things". (b) He may use the middle recension in ANF, naming it as such. He must add that the long recension types the bishop to the Father instead, and that it is a late-fourth-century expansion, so it witnesses to its own century and not to Ignatius's.

---

**P3** *(a) exegetical — strict; (b) evaluative — any verdict.*

**Must hit, strict (a):** two of the following three.
1. **It picks one reading of *agapē* and reports it as the text's meaning.** "Presides in love" will bear a primacy over the whole communion, a primacy of love and witness among equals (Afanasiev), or preeminence in charity. The note needs the first and argues for none of them.
2. **It drops the first clause.** Ignatius also calls Rome the church presiding "in the place of the region of the Romans", which localizes the presidency and, on one reading, fixes the scope of both clauses. The note quotes the half that helps.
3. **It treats a greeting as a claim of jurisdiction.** The letter asks Rome for prayers and for silence and requests no ruling, and it names no bishop of Rome at all — awkward for an argument about the Roman see.

**Wrong turns:** attacking the *Smyrnaeans* sentence, which is genuine, middle-recension and correctly reported, so the argument fails downstream of it; arguing about whether Catholic primacy is *true*, which is `apologetics-catholic-claims` and not what an exegetical part asks; saying the preface is an interpolation, which is not the case in the middle recension.

**Must hit, any verdict (b):** separate what the letters establish from what they cannot, and name the reason. They establish that around 110 a bishop of Antioch urged a threefold order on churches in western Asia Minor, expected the vocabulary to be understood, and could appeal to named bishops in those cities: strong evidence that the pattern existed there and was being pressed. They cannot establish that it was universal (the letters are exhortations, and an exhortation implies resistance), that Rome had it (Ignatius names no bishop there, and 1 Clement shows no single bishop), or that it came from the apostles (Ignatius argues from analogy, not from a command he can cite). Further evidence, any one: what other churches in the same decades show; whether a single bishop appears at Rome and when; whether the date holds, since Barnes's 140s would put the letters a generation further from the apostles. Then a verdict on whether the note's conclusion survives. Either verdict passes.

**Model answer (b), one of several:** A single letter-writer establishes his own mind and, where he names names, the offices of the churches he addresses. Ignatius does that for western Asia Minor around 110, and it is real evidence. He cannot establish more, for three reasons the letters themselves supply: he is exhorting, which implies the pattern was contested; he greets Rome without naming a bishop, so his own corpus fails to show the order in the city the note most needs; and he grounds the office in analogy to God and the apostles, not in an apostolic ordinance. The evidence that would move me most is Rome: a securely dated single bishop there before 150 would strengthen the note, and its absence is why many historians read the monepiscopate as an early but post-apostolic development. Verdict: the first half of the note survives, narrowed to Asia; the Roman half does not.

## Flashback

**From Lesson [1.1](01-01-how-to-read-a-church-father.md) (How to read a Church Father):** *(Exegetical.)* A reading list sorts its authors into three boxes: **Father** (an ancient witness with the four marks), **Doctor of the Church** (a later honour — eminent learning, holiness of life, and a formal declaration, with no requirement of antiquity), and **ecclesiastical writer** (read, quoted, often indispensable, but missing a mark). Put each of the four below in the right box or boxes, giving in one clause the fact that decides it: (a) Tertullian; (b) Teresa of Ávila; (c) Basil the Great; (d) Origen. Then, in one sentence, say what a declaration as Doctor does **not** settle. **One sentence per name.**

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict:**
- (a) **Tertullian — ecclesiastical writer only.** He ended his life outside the Great Church, so the mark of the Church's approval fails; he is neither Father nor Doctor.
- (b) **Teresa of Ávila — Doctor, not a Father.** She is sixteenth-century, so antiquity fails; antiquity is not required for the doctorate, which is why most Doctors are not Fathers.
- (c) **Basil the Great — both.** All four marks, and one of the four Greek Doctors whose feasts Pius V's Breviary of 1568 raised.
- (d) **Origen — ecclesiastical writer.** His name was anathematized in the sixth century, so the marks of orthodoxy and approval fail, however indispensable he remains.
- **What the doctorate does not settle:** it honours the man and endorses no single opinion of his — Irenaeus was declared a Doctor in 2022, and his millenarianism is exactly as binding after that declaration as before, which is not at all.

**Wrong turns:** treating "Father" as a juridical status with an official roll to look up — no council promulgated the four marks, and they record a judgement the tradition has already made, so borderline cases are borderline; calling (a) and (d) worthless because they lack a mark, when a writer later found wanting can still be a first-rate witness to what the churches were doing in his lifetime; assuming the two titles track each other, when most Fathers are not Doctors and most Doctors are not Fathers.

**Model answer:** (a) Ecclesiastical writer: Tertullian ended outside the Great Church, so approval fails. (b) Doctor only: Teresa is sixteenth-century and fails antiquity, which the doctorate does not require. (c) Both: Basil has the four marks and is one of the four Greek Doctors. (d) Ecclesiastical writer: Origen's name was anathematized in the sixth century. The title of Doctor commends the man's teaching as eminent and decides nothing about any particular opinion he held.
