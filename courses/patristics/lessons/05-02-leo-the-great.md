# The Church Fathers · Lesson 5.2: Leo the Great

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 5: The Christological Fathers and the close of the patristic age · Builds on: [5.1 Cyril of Alexandria](05-01-cyril-of-alexandria.md), [3.3 Gregory of Nazianzus and Gregory of Nyssa](03-03-the-two-gregories.md) · Unlocks: [5.3 Gregory the Great](05-03-gregory-the-great.md)

## Why this matters

Cyril won at Ephesus in 431 and left behind a formula, "one incarnate nature of God the Word", which a monk in Constantinople pushed until it meant that Christ's flesh was not quite ours ([5.1](05-01-cyril-of-alexandria.md)). The reply that stuck came from the other end of the empire, in Latin, from a bishop who never attended the council that received it. Leo's *Tome* is the one patristic text whose sentences passed almost directly into a conciliar definition, and his anniversary sermons are the fullest statement any ancient bishop of Rome made about his own office. Both are quoted constantly, and usually at second hand. This lesson reads them.

## The idea

Leo was a Roman deacon trusted enough to be sent to Gaul on an imperial commission; Sixtus III died while he was away, and Leo was consecrated bishop of Rome on 29 September 440, holding the see until his death on 10 November 461. Ninety-six genuine sermons and 143 letters survive. He was not a speculative theologian in the Greek sense. He was a trained Latin rhetorician who preached to the same congregation for twenty-one years, and his instrument is the **balanced antithesis**: two clauses of the same shape, one naming what is divine and one what is human, held in a single sentence that refuses to choose.

That is not decoration. The heresy in front of him, **Eutychianism**, was a claim that after the union you can no longer count to two. Leo reports Eutyches's own words back to Flavian: he confessed two natures before the union, and after the union one. Against a claim that the two collapse into one, a sentence whose grammar keeps two clauses balanced and inseparable *is* the argument. Where Cyril starts from the one subject and works out how the natures can be said of him, Leo starts from the two forms and binds them into one person. Both want the same Christ. They approach from opposite ends, and the difference in approach is exactly what the next century fought about.

Leo's other great subject is Peter. The first five of his sermons were preached on the anniversary of that 29 September consecration, and they are the Source below.

## Source

From Leo's Sermon 3, preached on the anniversary of his consecration, sections 3 and 4. Translation by Charles Lett Feltoe, *Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers*, 2nd series, vol. 12 (1895), public domain:

> if anything is rightly done and rightly decreed by us, if anything is won from the mercy of God by our daily supplications, it is of his work and merits whose power lives and whose authority prevails in his See. […] in my humble person he may be recognized and honoured, in whom abides the care of all the shepherds, together with the charge of the sheep commended to him, and whose dignity is not abated even in so unworthy an heir. […] they will transfer the chief honour of this service […] to him whom they know to be not only the patron of this see, but also the primate of all bishops. […] believe that he is speaking whose representative we are.

The "he" throughout is Peter. Notice what the sentences are built to do: every honour offered to Leo is redirected to Peter, and the redirection is what carries the claim. The occasion is a liturgy in Leo's honour; the genre is a bishop's *natalis* sermon, not a decree. Both facts matter for Problem 2.

## The argument

The *Tome* is Letter 28, written to Flavian of Constantinople in 449 after Flavian had condemned Eutyches and Eutyches had appealed to Rome. Reconstructed (NPNF 2nd series, vol. 12, chapter numbers as printed):

1. **Start from the creed, not from a technical term.** Eutyches, Leo says in chapter 1, has not grasped the rudiments of what every candidate for baptism professes. *In words:* the baptismal confession already names two births, one from the Father and one from Mary, and Eutyches has to deny one of them before he can begin.
2. **The eternal birth and the birth in time are two, and the second adds nothing to the first** (ch. 2). *In words:* becoming man changed the man's situation, not God's nature.
3. **The humanity is really ours, taken from Mary's body** (ch. 2). Leo attacks the idea that Christ had the form of a man but not the reality of his mother's body. *In words:* a miraculous conception does not make an unreal body.
4. **One person; and neither nature loses what makes it that nature** (ch. 3). This is the sentence the close reading in P1 takes apart. *In words:* the union is real and the distinction survives it.
5. **Because both survive, the acts of Christ fall into two kinds, though one person does them all** (ch. 4):

   > For each form does what is proper to it with the co-operation of the other; that is the Word performing what appertains to the Word, and the flesh carrying out what appertains to the flesh.

   *In words:* miracles come from what he is as God, weakness from what he is as man, and neither happens without the other. The Latin is *Agit enim utraque forma cum alterius communione quod proprium est* — literally "with the communion of the other", which is a stronger binding than most English renderings suggest.
6. **Salvation requires both a death that is real and a death that cannot hold** (ch. 3). *In words:* the same soteriological engine as Athanasius and Nazianzen ([3.1](03-01-athanasius.md), [3.3](03-03-the-two-gregories.md)), aimed this time not at the Son's divinity but at the union itself.

∴ Eutyches's "two before, one after" must be refused. Chapter 6 adds the terms: if he recants in his own hand, he is received back. The letter is a pastoral judgment on a case, not a treatise.

## Map of positions

| Position | Starting point | After the union | What it must explain | Words it leans on |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Nestorius**, as Cyril read him | Two complete realities | Two natures, joined | How the joining is more than partnership | "conjunction"; *Christotokos* |
| **Cyril** | The one subject, the Word | One incarnate nature of the Word, out of two | How the humanity stays complete | *Theotokos*; "one incarnate nature" |
| **Eutyches** | Cyril's formula, pressed | One nature only | Whether that flesh is still ours | "after the union I confess but one" |
| **Leo** | Two forms, one person | Both natures, each acting with the other | How two forms are one agent | "each form does what is proper to it" |

Read across the bottom two rows and you can see the quarrel of the next two centuries. Cyril and Leo are not on the same axis: Cyril is guarding the unity of the acting subject, Leo the integrity of the two natures. A reader who holds Cyril's guard rail will hear Leo's sentence as slack, and a reader who holds Leo's will hear Cyril's formula as dangerous. Chalcedon in 451 tried to hold both, and its definition borrows Leo's clause almost word for word: "the peculiar property of each nature being preserved" is Leo's *salva proprietate utriusque naturae* (NPNF 2nd series, vol. 14). The definition itself, clause by clause, belongs to `christology`; the council as an event is [`church-history` 2.2](../../church-history/lessons/02-02-ephesus-chalcedon-and-leos-rome.md), which also owns canon 28 and the meeting with Attila.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean: Lazarus).** John 11 gives Jesus weeping at a friend's tomb and then calling the dead man out. Run premise 5. The tears belong to the flesh; the command that empties a four-day grave belongs to the Word. Leo writes the case out himself in chapter 4, and his summary of the pattern is that one of the two "sparkles with miracles, the other succumbs to injuries". But "with the co-operation of the other" forbids the obvious misreading: it is not that a divine person performed the miracle while a human person cried. One person wept and one person raised him, and the one person is both. This is the Latin form of what [5.1](05-01-cyril-of-alexandria.md) called the [communication of idioms](../reference.md#communication-of-idioms) — Cyril arrives at it from the single subject, Leo from the two forms, and they meet here.

**Example 2 (where it strains: the charge that the *Tome* divides).** Now give the same sentence to a reader formed on Cyril. The grammatical subject of "does what is proper to it" is *each form*, not *the Word*. Cyril's fourth anathema had condemned distributing the sayings of the Gospels between two subjects. So a Cyrilline reader can hear Leo assigning acts to natures — and natures that act look like persons. Severus of Antioch and later miaphysite writers made exactly this objection, and it is one reason the churches that rejected Chalcedon read the council as a retreat toward Nestorius.

The Chalcedonian reply is textual, and it is in the same paragraph. Leo writes there that one and the same is truly Son of God and truly son of man, and that in Christ God and man is one person; *cum alterius communione* binds the two operations into a single agent rather than distributing them between two. The council did not simply take this on trust. In the acts of the second session, in October 451, Cyril's letters are read first and the *Tome* after them; the bishops then acclaim it — "Peter has spoken thus through Leo" — and in the same breath that Leo and Cyril taught the same thing. The Illyrian bishops ask for explanations, a delay of some days is requested, and the session breaks up. Individual subscription comes at a later session, with a committee under Anatolius checking the *Tome* against Cyril's writings in between ([`church-history` 2.2](../../church-history/lessons/02-02-ephesus-chalcedon-and-leos-rome.md)). The record shows the *Tome* received **after being measured against Cyril**. On what ground it was received — because Peter had spoken, or because it matched Cyril — the acts support both readings, and arguing that out belongs to `apologetics-catholic-claims`.

## Watch out

- **The *Tome* is not the Chalcedonian definition, and quoting one for the other is an attribution error.** Leo's letter is a bishop's ruling on the case of Eutyches, sent to Flavian in 449. The definition is a conciliar text of 451 that borrowed from it. Only the second is defined dogma; the *Tome*'s authority in Catholic theology comes from the council's reception of it, not the other way round. Levels are [`fundamental-theology` 4.4](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-04-the-ladder-of-doctrinal-authority.md)'s.
- **"In communion with the other" is a translation choice, and arguments get built on it.** Feltoe's NPNF renders *cum alterius communione* as "with the co-operation of the other"; other English versions give "in communion with the other". The Latin noun is *communio*, and how strongly you bind the two operations is precisely what is at issue between Leo's critics and his defenders. Before you hang a claim on the English, say which translation you are quoting — the rule from [1.1](01-01-how-to-read-a-church-father.md).
- **The acclamation is not a doctrinal act.** "Peter has spoken through Leo" is a shout recorded by notaries, in an edited record, alongside "Leo and Cyril taught the same thing". It is evidence about how the bishops honoured the letter, not a conciliar decree about why.
- **A pope's sermon is not a definition, and Leo's is a Father's teaching.** Leo is a Doctor of the Church (declared by Benedict XIV in 1754), and the title endorses no particular opinion of his ([1.1](01-01-how-to-read-a-church-father.md)). What the Church teaches about the Roman primacy, and what these sermons do or do not establish about it, is owned elsewhere; here the sermons are texts.

## One-liner

> Cyril guarded the one who acts; Leo guarded the two he acts with — and Chalcedon kept Leo's sentence only after checking it against Cyril's.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢) *(Exegetical.)*** From the *Tome*, chapter 3 (NPNF 2nd series, vol. 12, tr. Feltoe):

> Without detriment therefore to the properties of either nature and substance which then came together in one person, majesty took on humility, strength weakness, eternity mortality: and for the paying off of the debt belonging to our condition inviolable nature was united with possible nature, so that, as suited the needs of our case, one and the same Mediator between God and men, the Man Christ Jesus, could both die with the one and not die with the other. Thus in the whole and perfect nature of true man was true God born, complete in what was His own, complete in what was ours.

(NPNF prints "possible nature"; the Latin is *naturae passibili*, "passible", able to suffer.)

(a) The passage contains a list of three paired opposites. Write them out and say, for each pair, which member is the one doing the taking.
(b) "Could both die with the one and not die with the other": name what "the one" and "the other" refer to, and state which of Eutyches's two claims (two natures before the union, one after) this clause is aimed at. Two sentences per part.

**P2 (🟡) *(Exegetical.)*** Return to the Source passage from Sermon 3. Catholic and Orthodox readers both use these sermons, and read them differently. State each reading and name the words in the passage it leans on. Do not say which is right; the argument over primacy belongs to `apologetics-catholic-claims`. 150 words or fewer in total.

**P3 (🔴, optional) *(Exegetical.)*** An invented parish-bulletin note (the quoted words of Leo are genuine; the surrounding claims are the bulletin's):

> "Pope St. Leo called himself 'not only the patron of this see, but also the primate of all bishops' — so papal supremacy was already defined doctrine in the 440s. And the Council of Chalcedon simply obeyed him: the bishops cried out 'Peter has spoken thus through Leo,' and that settled it."

Name three distinct errors and correct each in one sentence: one of attribution or reference, one about the level of authority claimed, and one about what the acts of Chalcedon actually record. 150 words or fewer.

<details>
<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical.)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** the three pairs are **majesty / humility**, **strength / weakness**, **eternity / mortality**. In each pair the first member is the subject of the verb — majesty, strength and eternity are what *took on* humility, weakness and mortality. The divine side is the agent; the human side is what is assumed. Full credit notes that this is why the sentence does not say the humanity took on divinity.

**Must hit, strict (b):** "the one" is the passible (human) nature, in which he can die; "the other" is the inviolable (divine) nature, in which he cannot. The clause is aimed at the **second** claim, "after the union, one nature": if the two collapsed into one after the union, there would no longer be a nature in which he can die and a nature in which he cannot, and the single Mediator could not be described in both ways at once.

**Wrong turns:** reversing the agency in (a) and saying humility took on majesty. In (b), naming Nestorius rather than Eutyches — the passage is about the natures surviving the union, which Eutyches denied. Also wrong: reading "could both die and not die" as a contradiction Leo failed to notice; the whole point of the clause is that the two predicates attach to one person by way of two natures.

**Model answer:** (a) Majesty took on humility, strength took on weakness, eternity took on mortality; in each pair the divine member is the one that takes, the human member the one taken on. (b) "The one" is the passible human nature and "the other" the inviolable divine nature. It targets Eutyches's claim that after the union there is only one nature: without both surviving, the one Mediator could not be the subject of both a real death and an immortality that death cannot touch.

---

**P2** *(Exegetical.)*

**Must hit, strict:** both readings, each tied to words in the passage.

- **Catholic reading:** Leo claims that Peter continues to govern the whole Church through the bishop of Rome, so the Roman see holds a real primacy over all bishops that does not depend on the holder's worth. Leaning on: "the primate of all bishops"; "the care of all the shepherds"; "whose power lives and whose authority prevails in his See"; "whose dignity is not abated even in so unworthy an heir"; "believe that he is speaking whose representative we are".
- **Orthodox reading:** the sermon is a bishop's anniversary panegyric in his own city, in which Rome's honour is Peter's honour; "primate" is read as a primacy of honour and of witness among the sees rather than jurisdiction over them, and the language of Peter speaking through the incumbent is a Roman theological claim that the East never received in those terms. Leaning on the same words plus the genre: everything is asserted about **Peter**, not about a juridical power over other bishops, and the audience is Leo's own clergy and the bishops present at his feast.

**Wrong turns:** answering which reading is correct, or arguing from Chalcedon's canon 28 that Leo was refuted (both are out of scope here). Giving only one reading. Naming no words from the passage — the whole exercise is which words each side leans on. Treating "primate of all bishops" as unambiguous in either direction.

**Model answer:** Catholic readers take Leo to be asserting a real primacy of the Roman see over the whole episcopate: Peter's "care of all the shepherds" abides in the incumbent, "whose power lives and whose authority prevails in his See", and Leo is "the primate of all bishops" whose dignity is undiminished by an unworthy heir — an office, not a personal standing. Orthodox readers grant every sentence about Peter and deny the inference to jurisdiction: the genre is an anniversary sermon in Peter's own city, every claim is made of Peter rather than of Leo, and "primate" can carry a primacy of honour and of witness among sees. Each side leans on the same words; they divide over whether "primate of all bishops" and "representative" name a power over other bishops or a first place among them.

---

**P3** *(Exegetical.)*

**Must hit, strict:** three errors, one from each named category.

1. **Attribution / reference.** The quoted phrase is from Sermon 3, an anniversary sermon on Peter, and Leo applies it to **Peter**, not to himself — the sentence asks his fellow bishops to transfer the honour to Peter as patron of the see and primate of all bishops. Reading it as Leo's self-description misreads the subject of the sentence.
2. **Level of authority.** A sermon is a Father's teaching, not a definition; nothing was "defined doctrine in the 440s" by being preached. Levels come from [`fundamental-theology` 4.4](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-04-the-ladder-of-doctrinal-authority.md), and calling a preached claim defined doctrine is an overstatement of level.
3. **The acts of Chalcedon.** The acclamation is a shout on the minutes, not a decree, and the same acclamation says Leo and Cyril taught the same thing. Cyril's letters were read before the *Tome*; the Illyrian bishops then asked for explanations and a delay, and subscription came only at a later session, after the *Tome* had been checked against Cyril. So "simply obeyed" is not what the record shows.

**Wrong turns:** listing three versions of the same error (e.g. three ways of saying the sermon is not a definition). Correcting the bulletin by arguing against papal primacy, which is not asked and is out of scope. Claiming the quotation is spurious — the words are Leo's; the misuse is in what they are made to say.

**Model answer:** First, the phrase is Sermon 3's and Leo says it of **Peter**: he is asking the bishops present to transfer the honour of the day to Peter, the see's patron and primate of all bishops, not describing himself. Second, a sermon defines nothing; it is one Father's teaching, and calling it defined doctrine overstates the level. Third, the acts do not show obedience. The same acclamation praises Leo for teaching what Cyril taught, and Cyril's letters had been read before the *Tome*; the Illyrian bishops asked for explanations and a delay, and the bishops subscribed individually only at a later session. The *Tome* was tested before it was received.

## Flashback

**From Lesson [4.5](04-05-augustine-against-the-pelagians.md) (Augustine against the Pelagians):** *(Exegetical.)* Three facts about how Pelagius reaches us. His *On Nature* and *On Free Will* are lost and survive only in the quotations of Augustine and Jerome; his commentary on thirteen letters of Paul came down under Jerome's name and was disentangled only in the 1920s, by Alexander Souter; and his most-quoted sentence — the ability in our nature, the volition in our will, the actuality in the effect — is preserved because Augustine copies it out at *On the Grace of Christ* 5.

(a) A writer quotes that sentence with the bare citation "Pelagius, *On Free Will*, book 3." State what the citation must add, and say why the omission matters more here than it would for a work that survives.
(b) In 415 a synod of fourteen bishops at Diospolis heard the charges against Pelagius and acquitted him. Name one thing that acquittal establishes about his position which the surviving quotations, by themselves, could not — and one thing it does not establish. **Two sentences per part.**

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):** the intermediary. The work is lost, so the citation has to say that the words survive as quoted by Augustine — "Pelagius, *On Free Will* III, quoted in Augustine, *On the Grace of Christ* 5 (NPNF, first series, Holmes)" — naming the translation as well, since that is what a reader can actually check. It matters more than usual because the intermediary is the prosecutor: the selection, the extent and the framing of every surviving sentence are his opponent's work, and there is no continuous text against which to test them. Credit for adding that the same corpus shows how far a name can drift from a text, the Pauline commentary having travelled for centuries as Jerome's.
**Must hit, strict (b):** it establishes that his account of grace could be stated in a form that fourteen bishops, with the charges before them, found unobjectionable — he affirmed grace loudly, and was acquitted on the strength of it, so "Pelagius denied grace" cannot be what the controversy is about. It does not establish that his teaching was orthodox, or that what the synod heard was what Augustine and the Africans were attacking: a synod's verdict on charges is a verdict on charges, and the quarrel ran on for years afterwards.
**Wrong turns:** calling the quoted sentence spurious — it is quoted as his, and nothing in the lesson suggests Augustine garbled it; treating "cite the intermediary" as mere pedantry, without naming that the intermediary is the opposing party; taking the acquittal as proof of orthodoxy, which is the overstatement the second half of (b) exists to catch.
**Model answer:** (a) It must name the intermediary and the translation: the book is lost, and these words exist because Augustine quotes them at *On the Grace of Christ* 5. That matters more than a missing edition normally would, because the man preserving the sentence is the man prosecuting its author, so the reader has no way to check the context unless the citation says whose context it is. (b) The acquittal shows that his position had a defensible public face — fourteen bishops hearing the charges could not convict him of denying grace, which is why the real dispute is over what grace does. It does not show that his doctrine was sound, only that what he said under examination passed.

</details>
