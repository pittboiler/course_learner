# The Church Fathers · Lesson 3.2: Basil the Great

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 3: The fourth-century East · Builds on: [3.1 Athanasius](03-01-athanasius.md) · Unlocks: [3.3 Gregory of Nazianzus and Gregory of Nyssa](03-03-the-two-gregories.md)

## Why this matters

Athanasius won a word for the Son ([3.1](03-01-athanasius.md)). Basil had to win a grammar. Fifty years after Nicaea the Greek churches could not state their faith without appearing to state a heresy: "one hypostasis" sounded like Sabellius, "three hypostases" like three gods, because the council itself had used *hypostasis* and *ousia* as one word. Basil pulled them apart, and that distinction is the vocabulary of every Trinitarian sentence after him. Then he wrote the most famous patristic book on the Holy Spirit and never, in it, calls the Spirit God — the most instructive thing about the treatise: a bishop arguing a doctrine from the way the Church prays, while withholding the term that would have cost him his see.

## The idea

Basil (c. 330–379), of a wealthy Christian family of Pontus and Cappadocia, studied rhetoric at Athens alongside Gregory of Nazianzus and was turned toward ascetic life by his sister Macrina. In 358 he gathered a community at Annesi; his *Asketikon*, in Rufinus's Latin of 397, is the "Rule of our holy father Basil" Benedict would cite (monasticism belongs to [`church-history` 2.3](../../church-history/lessons/02-03-the-desert-and-the-monastery.md)). As bishop of Caesarea from 370 he built a hospice, hospital and poorhouse Gregory called a new city. With Gregory he is also the traditional compiler of the *Philocalia*, an anthology of Origen ([2.5](02-05-clement-of-alexandria-and-origen.md)) — an ascription some scholars doubt.

Two problems define his theology, and both are about words.

**First, the deadlock over *ousia* and *hypostasis*.** Both words meant roughly "real being" — Nicaea's creed anathematizes anyone saying the Son is "of a different substance or essence" from the Father, the two nouns doing one job. Within a generation each side heard the other's confession as a condemned position (the map below sets them out), and neither was a heretic. The dictionary was.

**Second, the doxology fight.** At Caesarea Basil ascribed glory in two forms: the older "through the Son, in the Holy Spirit", and also "with the Son, together with the Holy Spirit". Accused of novelty, he answered in *On the Holy Spirit*, written in 375 for Amphilochius of Iconium — a case for the Spirit's rank resting on a preposition, a baptism and a hymn, not on a metaphysical term.

## Source

Basil, Letter 236.6 (NPNF 2nd series, vol. 8, Blomfield Jackson's translation, public domain), answering a correspondent who cannot see why the two words need distinguishing.

> The distinction between οὐσία and ὑπόστασις is the same as that between the general and the particular; as, for instance, between the animal and the particular man. Wherefore, in the case of the Godhead, we confess one essence or substance so as not to give a variant definition of existence, but we confess a particular hypostasis, in order that our conception of Father, Son and Holy Spirit may be without confusion and clear. … We must, therefore, confess the faith by adding the particular to the common. The Godhead is common; the fatherhood particular.

Read what the sentence does: not explain how God is three, but assign two words to two questions, *what* and *who*, so the answers stop colliding. Letter 214.4 puts it briefly: *[ousia](../reference.md#ousia-and-hypostasis)* is to *[hypostasis](../reference.md#ousia-and-hypostasis)* as the common to the particular.

## The argument

### The vocabulary settlement

1. **At Nicaea the two nouns are interchangeable.** The anathema pairs them as alternatives, rendered "substance or essence": nothing distinguishes them. *In words: in 325 the council had one word for two questions.*
2. **So each available confession names a condemned position.** Say "one hypostasis" and an Easterner hears Sabellius; say "three hypostases" and a Westerner hears three gods — Basil states both fears in Letter 214.3–4. *In words: orthodoxy had become unsayable.*
3. **Assign the words to different levels of generality.** *Ousia* answers "what is it?" and is shared; *hypostasis* answers "which one?" and is not. *In words: "man" is to "Peter" as Godhead is to Fatherhood.*
4. **Therefore: one *ousia*, three *hypostaseis*.** Each divine person is the common Godhead plus a distinguishing property — fatherhood, sonship, the power to sanctify. *In words: confess the faith by adding the particular to the common; both halves are required.*

What this buys is precision, not explanation: "three" and "one" now answer different questions, so they no longer contradict. What a divine *hypostasis* is he has not said, and knows it.

### The argument from worship

*On the Holy Spirit* reaches the Spirit through the liturgy:

1. **Christ himself conjoined the Spirit with the Father and the Son** in the baptismal command of Matthew 28:19 (ch. 10). *In words: the ranking under dispute is the Lord's, not Basil's.*
2. **Faith conforms to baptism, and glory to faith,** in an unbroken sequence (chs. 12, 27). Basil makes the opponents choose which link to break: "either not to baptize as we have received, or not to believe as we were baptized, or not to ascribe glory as we have believed." *In words: attack the doxology and you attack the rite that made you a Christian.*
3. **Worship of this kind is not offered to creatures.** Paul names angels as witnesses, not as recipients of glory; a fellow-slave can testify but cannot free (ch. 13). *In words: the company you may be glorified in says what you are.*
4. **What the Spirit does is not creaturely work.** He sanctifies, adopts, illuminates and deifies — the list in ch. 9 climbs to "the being made God", the deification Athanasius argued from ([3.1](03-01-athanasius.md)). *In words: no creature can make creatures divine.*
5. **Therefore the Spirit is numbered *with* the Father and the Son, not *under* them** (chs. 17–18), and glorified with them.

Notice the shape: honour to nature, run through practice. The Church already prays this way; Basil asks what must be true for that prayer not to be blasphemy — the *[argument from worship](../reference.md#basils-argument-from-worship)*.

### Unwritten tradition, chapter 27

Because opponents demanded written warrant for "with", Basil inventoried what every church did that no text commands: signing with the cross, turning east to pray, the words of invocation over the bread and the cup, blessing the font and the chrism, triple immersion, renouncing Satan, standing to pray on Sundays and through Pentecost. Of beliefs and practices, he says, some come from written teaching and others "we have received delivered to us in a mystery by the tradition of the apostles; and both of these in relation to true religion have the same force."

Catholic and Orthodox readers take that at face value: Tradition transmits content alongside Scripture. Protestant readers answer that every example is a rite, never a doctrine, and that his case for the Spirit runs from Scripture — so the chapter defends a liturgical word, not a second source of revelation. Both must reckon with the distinction in the same chapter between *dogma*, guarded in silence, and *kerygma*, proclaimed to all: *dogma* there is the unspoken content of the rites, nearly the opposite of the modern sense. It is argued out in [`apologetics-catholic-claims`](../../apologetics-catholic-claims/syllabus.md); here it is read, not adjudicated. See [unwritten tradition](../reference.md#unwritten-tradition-in-basil).

### What Basil does not say

He never, in the treatise, calls the Spirit *homoousios*, and never writes "the Spirit is God". He argues that the Spirit is glorified with the Father and the Son, shares their operations and is no creature — and stops.

Gregory of Nazianzus, preaching Basil's funeral oration (*Oration* 43.68–69), calls it a deliberate *oikonomia*, an economy or strategic reserve: enemies were watching for that phrase, which would have got Basil deposed and Caesarea handed to them, so he taught the same content in other terms and postponed the word. In private, Gregory adds, Basil confessed the Spirit consubstantial; the two divided the labour, nobody bothering to exile Gregory.

Weigh that testimony as testimony: a friend's eulogy answering critics who called the silence cowardice, our best evidence and not disinterested. Beyond dispute is the text: the words are not there. Two years after Basil's death the council of Constantinople confessed the Spirit as the one "who with the Father and the Son together is worshipped and glorified" — Basil's idiom, not Athanasius's word. That divinity is defined dogma, at the top of the [ladder of doctrinal authority](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-04-the-ladder-of-doctrinal-authority.md), owned by `trinity-and-god`; Basil's reticence is a fact about a book, not a doctrinal position — keeping those apart is the discipline of this course. See [Basil's reticence](../reference.md#basils-reticence-about-the-spirit).

## Map of positions

| | Meaning of *ousia* | Meaning of *hypostasis* | Confession of the Trinity | Heard by opponents as |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Nicaea, 325** | real being | real being (a synonym) | one *ousia*, term unfixed | — |
| **Old Nicenes and the Latin West** | being, *substantia* | being; hence one | one *substantia*, three *personae* | Sabellian, by Easterners |
| **Eastern "three hypostases" party** | being | a really distinct subsistent | three *hypostaseis* | tritheist, by Westerners |
| **Basil, from c. 370** | what is common | what is particular | one *ousia*, three *hypostaseis* | — |

The bottom row is not a compromise but a change of question: the middle rows disagree about how many *beings* there are; Basil answers that "being" was two questions in one name.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean: running the distinction on the deadlock).** A correspondent reports that Western Nicenes suspect Basil of tritheism while his neighbours suspect the Westerners of Sabellianism. Apply the settlement. One *substantia* means one *ousia*, what is common — what Nicaea defined, so nothing is lost. Three *hypostaseis* means three particulars, each the whole Godhead plus its own property, not three *ousiai*: tritheism is not entailed. Each side denied what the other never asserted; the two confessions say one faith, and the Arians lose their best weapon, the charge that Nicenes cannot say what they mean. Hence Letter 214.4 — those who refuse the distinction end by confirming the heresy they mean to refute.

**Example 2 (where it strains: Peter, Andrew and James).** The analogy that makes the distinction clear also makes it dangerous. Basil's own illustration: Peter is no more *man* than Andrew or John — one common nature, three particulars. But three men *are* three men, and we count them. If Godhead is common as humanity is common, why is the Trinity not three gods?

Basil does not close the gap; he marks it. Number in God is not addition, he insists: we do not proceed one, two, three toward a plurality (ch. 18), and the divine nature is above counting — which states the conclusion rather than deriving it. The full defence is his brother's: Gregory of Nyssa wrote *To Ablabius: Not Three Gods* because the objection would not go away, denying the parallel — divine operations are inseparable as three men's actions never are ([3.3](03-03-the-two-gregories.md)).

The strain shows in the transmission too. **Letter 38**, the fullest statement of the distinction, is addressed "to his brother Gregory" and stands among Basil's letters. Since Reinhard Hübner's 1972 study much scholarship has argued it is Gregory of Nyssa's, its metaphysics more worked out than anything securely Basil's; others defend the traditional ascription. The question is open, so cite it as "Letter 38, attributed to Basil, possibly by Gregory of Nyssa" — never a flat "Basil says". See [Letter 38](../reference.md#letter-38-attribution).

## Watch out

- **You might think Basil's *hypostasis* is Nicaea's.** It is not: reading his meaning back into the 325 anathema makes the council forbid what Basil taught. Date your vocabulary.
- **You might think the treatise says the Spirit is God.** Quoting it so is the attribution error [1.1](01-01-how-to-read-a-church-father.md) trains against: the doctrine is dogma, the sentence is not in Basil's book.
- **You might think chapter 27 claims a secret body of doctrine outside Scripture.** What Basil defends there is a word in a prayer, and his examples are rites; what weight that carries for Tradition is the contested question, so state both readings.
- **You might think a Doctor's greatness certifies his arguments.** The title endorses no argument of his — and chapter 29 shows Basil himself doing source criticism on earlier Fathers, pressing only what is verifiable in the works.

## One-liner

> Basil gave the Trinity a grammar — one *ousia*, three *hypostaseis*, the common plus the particular — and then argued the Spirit's divinity from how the Church prays, withholding to the end the word that would have proved it fastest.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢) *(Exegetical.)*** From *On the Holy Spirit*, ch. 13, section 29 (NPNF 2nd series, vol. 8, Jackson). Basil is answering the objection that Paul names angels alongside God and Christ (1 Timothy 5:21) without thereby making them divine.

> To this I reply … that possibly before a mild and gentle judge … one might be willing to offer as witness even a fellow-slave; but for a slave to be made free and called a son of God and quickened from death can only be brought about by Him who has acquired natural kinship with us, and has been changed from the rank of a slave. For how can we be made kin with God by one who is an alien? How can we be freed by one who is himself under the yoke of slavery? It follows that the mention of the Spirit and that of angels are not made under like conditions. The Spirit is called on as Lord of life, and the angels as allies of their fellow-slaves and faithful witnesses of the truth.

(a) State the objection the passage answers and the distinction Basil draws to defeat it, naming the two roles he assigns.
(b) Which words carry the inference from what the Spirit *does* to how he must be *ranked*?
(c) Does the passage state that the Spirit is of one substance with the Father? Answer yes or no and say what it states instead.
Two sentences or fewer per part.

**P2 (🟡) *(Exegetical.)*** Nicaea's creed anathematizes anyone who says the Son is "of a different substance or essence" from the Father (NPNF 2nd series, vol. 14), where the two Greek nouns are *hypostasis* and *ousia*.

(a) On the assumption that the two nouns are synonyms, what does the anathema forbid? On Basil's later distinction, what would the same sentence forbid? One sentence each.
(b) Using Basil's distinction, say in one sentence each why "one hypostasis" alarmed Eastern bishops and why "three hypostases" alarmed Western ones.
(c) Name one thing Basil's distinction does **not** do. One sentence.

**P3 (🔴, optional) *(Exegetical.)*** An invented parish bulletin note:

> "Our Thursday group has been reading St Basil the Great. In his book on the Holy Spirit he teaches that the Holy Spirit is God, consubstantial with the Father, and he proves it from the Church's unwritten traditions rather than from Scripture. He is a Doctor of the Church, so this is the Church's own teaching, and next time somebody insists that a doctrine must be provable from the Bible you can simply answer: Basil says tradition is enough."

Identify each thing the note gets wrong, and for each say whether it is (i) false as to what Basil's text says, (ii) an overstatement of the level at which something is taught, or (iii) a doctrine the Church does teach but wrongly sourced to Basil's wording. **150 words or fewer.**

<details>
<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical — strict.)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** the objection is that being *named together with* the Father and the Son proves nothing about nature, since Paul names the elect angels in the same breath. Basil's distinction is between two roles: the angels are **witnesses** (fellow-slaves called to testify), while the Spirit is invoked as the **agent of liberation and life** — "Lord of life". So being listed as a witness and being ranked as a giver of divine life are not the same kind of association.

**Must hit, strict (b):** "made free", "called a son of God", "quickened from death", "natural kinship", and the two rhetorical questions — how can we be made kin with God by **one who is an alien**, how can we be freed **by one who is himself under the yoke of slavery**. The inference runs: adoption and life are works only a non-creature can perform; the Spirit performs them; therefore the Spirit is not a fellow-slave.

**Must hit, strict (c):** **No.** It states that the Spirit is not of the servile or creaturely rank and is invoked as Lord of life — a denial of creaturehood and a claim about operation, not a statement of consubstantiality. Credit for noting this is the treatise's pattern throughout.

**Wrong turns:** reading "natural kinship with us" as a claim about the Spirit's incarnation — the clause describes the pattern of redemption (the one who frees must not himself be a slave), and Basil applies the principle, not the incarnation, to the Spirit; answering (c) "yes, implicitly", which confuses what an argument may entail with what a text says; taking "Lord of life" as a quotation of the creed of 381, which is later.

**Model answer:** (a) The objection is that mere co-listing proves nothing, since angels are co-listed; Basil replies that angels appear as witnesses and fellow-slaves, whereas the Spirit is invoked as Lord of life. (b) "Made free", "called a son of God", "quickened from death", and the questions about an alien and a fellow-slave. (c) No: it says the Spirit is not of the servile rank and is Lord of life, which denies creaturehood without asserting one substance.

---

**P2** *(Exegetical — strict.)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** *As synonyms*, the anathema forbids saying the Son derives from, or belongs to, any being other than the Father's — a single prohibition stated twice for emphasis, with no distinction between the two nouns. *On Basil's distinction*, the same sentence would forbid saying that the Son is of a different **particular** (*hypostasis*) as well as of a different **common nature** (*ousia*) — which on Basil's own theology is wrong, since the Son precisely *is* a distinct *hypostasis*. Full credit requires seeing that the sentence becomes unusable, even self-contradictory, if the later meanings are imported.

**Must hit, strict (b):** "One hypostasis" alarmed Easterners because on their usage *hypostasis* named the particular, so one of them collapsed Father, Son and Spirit into a single subject — Sabellius's position. "Three hypostases" alarmed Westerners because on their usage *hypostasis* translated *substantia*, so three of them meant three beings — tritheism.

**Must hit, strict (c):** any one of: it does not explain **how** three particulars are one God; it does not define what a divine *hypostasis* is; it does not tell you which particular properties are the right ones; it does not by itself answer the tritheism objection, which Gregory of Nyssa had to take up.

**Wrong turns:** saying Nicaea "used *hypostasis* wrongly" — the council used a fourth-century word in a fourth-century sense, and the anachronism is the reader's; saying Westerners feared tritheism from the word *ousia*; treating Basil's distinction as a new doctrine rather than a regimentation of terms.

**Model answer:** (a) As synonyms it forbids saying the Son is from any being other than the Father's, the two words doing one job. On Basil's meanings it would also forbid calling the Son a distinct particular, which Basil himself affirms, so the sentence stops working. (b) "One hypostasis" meant one particular subject, hence Sabellius, to Easterners; "three hypostases" meant three substances, hence three gods, to Westerners. (c) It does not explain how the three are one God.

---

**P3** *(Exegetical — strict.)*

**Must hit, strict —** four errors, with the right label on each:

1. **"He teaches that the Holy Spirit is God, consubstantial with the Father" — (i) false as to the text.** The treatise never calls the Spirit God outright and never uses *homoousios* of him; it argues that the Spirit is ranked with, not under, the Father and the Son and is not a creature. Credit for adding Gregory of Nazianzus's explanation (*Oration* 43.68–69) that the omission was a deliberate economy under threat of deposition.
2. **"Proves it from unwritten traditions rather than from Scripture" — (i) false as to the text.** The argument runs chiefly from the baptismal command of Matthew 28:19 and from the Spirit's scriptural titles and works; chapter 27's appeal to unwritten custom defends the *preposition* "with" against the charge of novelty, and Basil there says written and unwritten teaching have the same force, not that the unwritten replaces the written.
3. **"He is a Doctor of the Church, so this is the Church's own teaching" — (ii) overstatement of level.** A Father's or Doctor's opinion is never *ipso facto* Church teaching, and the title endorses no particular argument ([1.1](01-01-how-to-read-a-church-father.md)).
4. **"Basil says tradition is enough" — (iii) wrongly sourced, and in fact contested.** The Spirit's consubstantial divinity *is* defined dogma, but its authority comes from the councils and the Church's definition, not from Basil's wording; and what chapter 27 establishes about Tradition is precisely what Catholic, Orthodox and Protestant readers dispute, so it cannot be cited as settled.

**Wrong turns:** correcting the note by arguing which reading of chapter 27 is right — the error is the certainty, not the position; saying Basil "did not believe" the Spirit was God, which goes beyond the evidence in the other direction; treating the Doctor title as if it made no difference at all to a Father's weight.

**Model answer:** Four errors. First, false to the text: *On the Holy Spirit* never says the Spirit is God or uses *homoousios* of him; it argues he is ranked with the Father and Son and is no creature, and Gregory of Nazianzus explains the omission as a deliberate economy. Second, false to the text: the argument runs from Matthew 28:19 and the Spirit's scriptural works, while chapter 27 defends only the preposition "with". Third, an overstatement of level: a Doctor's opinion is not thereby Church teaching. Fourth, wrongly sourced: the Spirit's divinity is defined dogma on the Church's authority, not Basil's phrasing, and what chapter 27 proves about Tradition is exactly what the churches dispute.

</details>

## Flashback

**From Lesson [2.5](02-05-clement-of-alexandria-and-origen.md) (Clement of Alexandria and Origen):** *(Exegetical.)* Every quotation of Origen's *On First Principles* reaches a modern reader down one of four channels. Here they are out of order: (i) the sixth-century dossiers assembled against Origenism, including Justinian's letter to Menas of 543; (ii) Rufinus's Latin translation of 398; (iii) the *Philocalia*, an anthology compiled in the 360s; (iv) Jerome's literal Latin version of about 399, itself lost but excerpted at length in his Letter 124. (a) Put the four in chronological order and say for each, in one clause, what it preserves or which way it leans. (b) One channel delivers Origen's actual Greek. Say why it still cannot settle what Book I said about the Son. **One sentence for (b).**

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):** the order is **(iii), (ii), (iv), (i)** — 360s, 398, c. 399, 543 — with the bend or the content of each:
- ***Philocalia*, 360s:** genuine Greek, but only the pieces two admirers thought edifying; traditionally compiled by Basil and Gregory of Nazianzus.
- **Rufinus, 398:** the only complete text, bending toward orthodoxy by his own declared rule of omitting what he judged corrupt and not Origen's.
- **Jerome, c. 399:** a literal version made to convict Origen and to expose Rufinus, bending toward heresy, surviving only where Jerome excerpts and summarizes it.
- **Justinian's dossier, 543:** excerpts selected three centuries later to secure a condemnation, and aimed at the developed Origenism of sixth-century monks rather than at Origen's book.

**Must hit, strict (b):** the *Philocalia*'s Greek covers III.1 and parts of Book IV, not Book I, so for Book I every surviving witness is a Latin one with a declared agenda.

**Wrong turns:** putting Rufinus after Jerome, when Jerome's version was made in answer to Rufinus's; calling Rufinus a forger, when he states his method in his prologue and believed he was removing heretical interpolations; treating the sixth-century dossiers as evidence of Origen's wording rather than of what the sixth century condemned; calling the *Philocalia* neutral because it is Greek, when it is a selection made by admirers.

**Model answer:** (a) *Philocalia* (360s), real Greek but only the edifying extracts; Rufinus (398), complete and smoothed toward orthodoxy on his own stated rule; Jerome (c. 399), literal and sharpened toward heresy, preserved only in his own hostile excerpts; Justinian's dossier (543), excerpts chosen to convict a later Origenism. (b) Because the Greek that survives is III.1 and parts of Book IV, so nothing of Book I comes down except through the two Latin witnesses who each announce an agenda.

</details>
