# The Church Fathers · Lesson 3.6: John Chrysostom

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 3: The fourth-century East · Builds on: [3.5 Allegory and history: Alexandria and Antioch](03-05-allegory-and-history-alexandria-and-antioch.md), [3.4 Ephrem and Syriac Christianity](03-04-ephrem-and-syriac-christianity.md) · Unlocks: [4.1 Ambrose of Milan](04-01-ambrose-of-milan.md)

## Why this matters

More pages survive under John Chrysostom's name than under any other Greek Father's, and almost all were spoken aloud to a congregation before they were written down. That makes him the place to watch [3.5](03-05-allegory-and-history-alexandria-and-antioch.md)'s Antiochene method working on people rather than on a page. It also makes him this course's hardest case. The preacher who tells a rich congregation that the hungry man in the street is the body of Christ is the preacher of eight homilies against Christians who went to synagogue — homilies reprinted in the twentieth century in the service of antisemitism. Both are his. Reading him honestly means holding both.

## The idea

Chrysostom was born at Antioch around 349 — estimates run from the mid-340s — trained in rhetoric, drawn to the ascetic life, and ordained priest in 386 by Flavian, bishop of Antioch. For twelve years he preached there; in Lent 387, after a riot in which the emperor's statues were thrown down, he preached *On the Statues* to a terrified city. He was taken to the capital and ordained bishop of Constantinople on 26 February 398, fell foul of the empress and of Theophilus of Alexandria, was deposed at the Synod of the Oak in 403, recalled, exiled again in 404, and died on 14 September 407 at Comana in Pontus. The politics of all that belongs to [`church-history`](../../church-history/syllabus.md); here we read what he said.

"Chrysostom" — golden-mouthed — is an epithet his readers gave him, not a name he had. It points at the thing to watch. His homilies almost always run in two movements. First the **letter**: what the text says, why the evangelist put it here, what the speaker in the text was trying to do. Then the **turn**: he stops explaining and addresses the people in front of him, usually about money. The seam between the two movements is visible in almost every homily, and it is where his theology lives.

## Source

Chrysostom, *Homilies on the Gospel of St Matthew*, Homily 50, section 4 (NPNF 1st series, vol. 10, Prevost, revised Riddle). He has just been preaching on the Eucharist; then he turns.

> Would you do honor to Christ's body? Neglect Him not when naked; do not while here you honor Him with silken garments, neglect Him perishing without of cold and nakedness. For He that said, This is my body, and by His word confirmed the fact, This same said, You saw me an hungered, and fed me not. … For This indeed needs not coverings, but a pure soul; but that requires much attention. … For what is the profit, when His table indeed is full of golden cups, but He perishes with hunger? First fill Him, being an hungered, and then abundantly deck out His table also. Do you make Him a cup of gold, while you give Him not a cup of cold water?

## The argument

The passage looks like exhortation. It is an argument, and it turns on a single hinge.

1. **The same speaker said both sentences.** "This is my body" (Matthew 26:26) and "You saw me an hungered, and fed me not" (Matthew 25:42) come from one mouth, and Chrysostom marks the identity of the speaker explicitly. *In words:* the link between altar and street is not a preacher's metaphor; it is an argument from who is talking.
2. **Honour must be the kind the one honoured asked for.** His example is Peter at the washing of the feet: Peter tried to honour Christ by refusing, and was corrected. *In words:* you do not choose the currency in which you pay respect.
3. **The altar does not need what the poor need.** "This indeed needs not coverings, but a pure soul; but that requires much attention." *In words:* the consecrated body is not cold or hungry; the man in the doorway is.
4. **Therefore adornment offered while the poor go without is not honour but insult.** Chrysostom tests this with a homely case: overlay a starving man's table with silver and leave him unfed, and he will not thank you. *In words:* the insult is in the ordering, not in the gold.
5. **So: alms first, then adornment.** He is explicit that he is "not forbidding such offerings to be provided" but requiring alms "together with them, and before them."

∴ **Christ is honoured in the poor, and the priority is not negotiable.** This is [Christ in the poor](../reference.md#christ-in-the-poor), and it is the engine of everything Chrysostom says about money.

How far does it go? Further than most modern readers expect. Preaching on 1 Timothy 4 he argues that God deliberately made the greatest goods — sun, air, earth, water, sea, light, the stars — common, so that we would learn to hold lesser things in common too; that strife appears at exactly the point where someone appropriates; and that "mine and yours" are **cold words** (Homily 12 on 1 Timothy, NPNF 1st series, vol. 13). He goes on to ask bluntly how a man can be rich and good at the same time. Mark the level before you carry this anywhere: it is one bishop's preaching, at no level on the ladder of doctrinal authority ([`fundamental-theology` 4.4](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-04-the-ladder-of-doctrinal-authority.md)). The Church's teaching on property, its universal destination and its legitimate private ownership, belongs to `catholic-social-teaching`, which reaches a more balanced position than Chrysostom's sermons do.

## The corpus

| Work | Size and date | What it is | Trap |
|---|---|---|---|
| *Homilies on Matthew* | 90 homilies, c. 390 | The fullest patristic commentary on Matthew, preached at Antioch | — |
| *Homilies on Romans* and on the other Pauline letters | 32 on Romans; homilies on all the epistles | Paul read sentence by sentence, with constant attention to whom he is addressing and why | — |
| *On the Statues* | 21 homilies, Lent 387 | Crisis preaching to Antioch after the riot | The nineteenth is probably not authentic |
| *On the Priesthood* | 6 books, c. 386–387 | A dialogue on the weight and danger of the office | Its occasion — a story about evading ordination — may be literary, not historical |
| *Against the Judaizing Christians* | 8 homilies, 386–387 | See below | The Latin title *Adversus Judaeos* misdescribes the audience |
| Liturgy of St John Chrysostom | — | The standard Byzantine eucharistic liturgy | Named for him by tradition; he did not compose the text we have |

[*On the Priesthood*](../reference.md#on-the-priesthood) earns a line, because its argument mirrors the Source. The priest's hands do what angels are not commissioned to do: "they who inhabit the earth and make their abode there are entrusted with the administration of things which are in Heaven, and have received an authority which God has not given to angels or archangels" (III.5, NPNF 1st series, vol. 9, Stephens). Chrysostom draws the conclusion downward, not upward — such an office is terrifying, and most men should run from it. The same move as Homily 50: a high doctrine of the sacrament cashed out as a demand on the man holding it.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean: reading for the speaker's purpose).** At the end of Homily 50 Chrysostom faces an objection he has raised himself. If almsgiving is this urgent, what about "The poor always you have with you, but me you have not always" (Matthew 26:11)? The verse has been used ever since to blunt exactly the argument he has just made. Watch his reply. He does not allegorize the poor, and he does not deny the plain sense. He says the words "were uttered because of some economy" — *oikonomia*, the arrangement of a particular moment — and that the saying therefore tells us the opposite of what the objector wants: give alms now, precisely because Christ is not always here to be fed in person. Then he refers the hearer to the whole body of law on almsgiving in both Testaments.

This is [reading for the speaker's purpose](../reference.md#reading-for-the-speakers-purpose): hold the letter, ask what the speaker was doing in that scene, and let the answer control the general use of the sentence — [3.5](03-05-allegory-and-history-alexandria-and-antioch.md)'s Antiochene method in one paragraph. An Alexandrian would sooner have looked for a second sense in "the poor." Chrysostom stays with the occasion.

**Example 2 (where it strains: the homilies against Judaizing Christians).** In 386 and 387 at Antioch, Chrysostom preached eight homilies known in Latin as *Adversus Judaeos*. [What they say](../reference.md#the-judaizing-homilies) has to be said plainly. They are sustained invective. They attack the synagogue itself, in language of contempt that runs well past the Christians he is trying to keep out of it: the synagogue is presented as a place of demons, Jewish worship as something worse than pagan, the Jews as a people whose covenant is finished and whose sufferings since 70 are their punishment. The abuse is not incidental decoration on a pastoral argument; it is the argument's main instrument.

**Their later use, named first.** Because these pages carry the authority of a saint and a Doctor of the Church, they were quarried by later writers building a case against Jews, and in the twentieth century the Nazi party excerpted and reprinted them to enlist German and Austrian Christians in antisemitism (Walter Laqueur, *The Changing Face of Antisemitism*, 2006; Steven Katz makes the stronger claim that they helped shape it). After the war the churches repudiated that use; the Second Vatican Council's *Nostra Aetate* 4 (1965) rejected the charge that the Jews as a people are collectively guilty of the death of Christ.

**Then the setting.** The audience was his own congregation. Baptized Christians at Antioch were attending synagogue on sabbaths and Jewish festivals, keeping the fasts, and swearing oaths there, and the pull was strong enough to alarm him — one of [`church-history` 1.2](../../church-history/lessons/01-02-bishops-canon-and-the-parting-of-the-ways.md)'s pieces of evidence that the parting of the ways was still unfinished in the fourth century. The Benedictine editor Montfaucon noted long ago that the title misdescribes the target. And the register belongs to a recognized ancient genre, [*psogos*](../reference.md#psogos), formal invective, whose convention was to vilify an opponent without restraint; Chrysostom used comparable abuse on heretics and on bad Christians, and Robert Wilken (*John Chrysostom and the Jews*, 1983) argues from this that calling him an antisemite imports a modern category.

Hold the two apart. The setting explains the occasion and the register. It softens no sentence, and it does not touch the fact that the homilies generalize far beyond the Judaizers to the Jews as such — which is what made them reusable fifteen centuries later. An explanation of why a text sounds like that is not a defence of what it says.

## Watch out

- **The genre argument answers a different question from the reception argument.** *Psogos* is about what the words were doing in 386. The reception history is about what the words do when read. Someone who says "it was just the rhetorical convention" and stops has answered the first question and pretended it settles the second.
- **Sanctity is not an argument about a text.** Chrysostom is a saint and one of the four great Greek Doctors, and that title, as [1.1](01-01-how-to-read-a-church-father.md) insisted, endorses no page of his. The Church did not receive these homilies; it later condemned the use made of them.
- **The Liturgy of St John Chrysostom is not by John Chrysostom** in the form the Byzantine churches use. He may have modified an older rite; the text is later. Quoting "the Liturgy of St John Chrysostom" as evidence of *his* eucharistic theology is the attribution error of [1.1](01-01-how-to-read-a-church-father.md) in a very common disguise.
- **His Eucharistic realism is read three ways.** Catholic, Orthodox and Protestant readers all cite Homily 50 and its neighbours and divide over what the realism commits him to. This course states the readings; what the passages *prove* belongs to `apologetics-catholic-claims`.
- **Do not turn his preaching on wealth into an economic programme.** He is moving a congregation, not drafting law; the doctrine of property is `catholic-social-teaching`'s.

## One-liner

> Chrysostom's greatness and his worst pages come from one habit — he aimed every text at the people in front of him — and the course asks you to read both with the same eyes.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢) *(Exegetical.)*** Chrysostom, *Homilies on 1 Timothy*, Homily 12 (on 1 Timothy 4:1–3), NPNF 1st series, vol. 13, Schaff:

> Mark the wise dispensation of God. That He might put mankind to shame, He has made certain things common, as the sun, air, earth, and water, the heaven, the sea, the light, the stars; whose benefits are dispensed equally to all as brethren. … Other things then He has made common, as baths, cities, market-places, walks. And observe, that concerning things that are common there is no contention, but all is peaceable. But when one attempts to possess himself of anything, to make it his own, then contention is introduced, as if nature herself were indignant, that when God brings us together in every way, we are eager to divide and separate ourselves by appropriating things, and by using those cold words mine and yours. This state therefore is rather our inheritance, and more agreeable to nature.

(a) Put the passage into two premises and a conclusion.
(b) The passage is sometimes quoted to show that Chrysostom held private property to be unjust. State what the words themselves claim, and name one premise a reader must supply from outside the passage to reach that stronger conclusion. 100 words or fewer for (b).

**P2 (🟡) *(Exegetical.)*** An invented parish-bulletin note (no real parish, no real author):

> **From the Finance Council.** Several of you have asked why the sanctuary restoration comes before the food pantry. St John Chrysostom settles it: "Would you do honor to Christ's body?" The Fathers built beautiful churches because beauty is itself how the poor are raised up. And since our Sunday rite is the Liturgy of St John Chrysostom, composed by the saint himself, we are following his own mind in giving the altar first place.

(a) The note quotes Chrysostom's question accurately and then draws the opposite conclusion from the one he draws. Using the Source and "The argument", say what he concludes and quote the clause of his that makes it unavoidable.
(b) One factual claim in the note is false. State it and correct it. 100 words or fewer in total.

**P3 (🔴, optional) *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)***
(a) In three sentences: what the eight homilies of 386–387 are, whom they were preached to, and how they were used in the twentieth century.
(b) Wilken argues that the register belongs to *psogos* and that "antisemite" is therefore an anachronism; Laqueur and Katz weight the reception history instead. Say what each side has to concede to the other, and give a one-line verdict on what the genre argument can and cannot establish. Any verdict passes. 150 words or fewer for (b).

<details>
<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical.)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** P1 — God made the greatest and most necessary goods (sun, air, earth, water, sea, light, stars, and also baths, cities, market-places) common, and their benefit reaches everyone alike. P2 — over common things there is no contention; strife enters exactly at the point where someone appropriates something as his own. ∴ C — community of use is what God arranged and is "more agreeable to nature," and the possessive language of "mine and yours" is a symptom of our failure to learn that lesson. An acceptable variant, also in the passage: God made the greater things common *in order to* instruct us to hold the lesser things in common.

**Must hit, strict (b):** the words claim (i) a divine arrangement in the common goods, (ii) an empirical observation that appropriation is where strife starts, and (iii) a verdict that common use is more natural and possessive speech is cold. They do not say that private ownership is unjust, void, or to be undone, and they propose no mechanism. To get there a reader must add a premise the passage lacks — for example, that what is more natural must therefore be legally enforced, or that a homily exhorting almsgiving is legislating a rule of ownership.

**Wrong turns:** reading "market-places" as a claim about commerce rather than an example of a shared public space; treating a homily's exhortation as if it had canonical force; over-correcting in the other direction, so that the passage means only "be generous" — it really does say community of use is more according to nature.

**Model answer:** (a) P1: God made the greatest goods common and dispensed their benefit to all alike, to teach us to share the lesser ones. P2: where things are common there is no contention, and contention begins precisely when someone appropriates. ∴ Community of use is the arrangement more agreeable to nature, and "mine and yours" are cold words. (b) The passage claims that shared goods produce peace and that appropriation produces strife, and it calls community our inheritance. It never says ownership is unjust or must be abolished. That conclusion needs an added premise — that what is more natural must be enforced in law — which Chrysostom, preaching for alms, does not supply.

---

**P2** *(Exegetical.)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** Chrysostom's answer to his own question is the reverse of the note's: to honour Christ's body is to clothe and feed him in the poor, and adorning the church while the poor go hungry is an insult, not an honour. The decisive clause is that the same speaker made both statements — "For He that said, This is my body … This same said, You saw me an hungered, and fed me not." Credit also for noting that he does not forbid beautiful vessels; he requires alms "together with them, and before them," so the note's error is one of ordering.

**Must hit, strict (b):** the claim that the Liturgy of St John Chrysostom was composed by Chrysostom. The liturgy carries his name by tradition; the received text is later, and at most he modified an older rite.

**Wrong turns:** saying the note misquotes him — the words are his, and the fault is entirely in the inference; saying Chrysostom forbids church adornment, which he explicitly denies; naming the sentence about beauty instead, which is the council's own assertion rather than a false claim about a text; treating the liturgy's name as settling its authorship.

**Model answer:** (a) He concludes the opposite: honour paid to Christ's body means feeding and clothing him in the poor first, and gold on the table while he starves is an insult. The clause that fixes it is "For He that said, This is my body … This same said, You saw me an hungered, and fed me not" — one speaker, so one body. He allows adornment, but "together with them, and before them, to give alms." (b) False: the Liturgy of St John Chrysostom was not composed by him. It bears his name by tradition; the received text is later, and he at most reworked an existing rite.

---

**P3** *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** (i) eight homilies preached at Antioch in 386 and 387, known in Latin as *Adversus Judaeos*, consisting largely of sustained invective against the synagogue and against the Jews, whose covenant they present as finished; (ii) preached to his own congregation — baptized Christians who were attending synagogue on sabbaths and Jewish festivals, keeping the fasts and swearing oaths there — so the Latin title misdescribes the audience, as Montfaucon noted; (iii) in the twentieth century they were excerpted and reprinted by the Nazi party to enlist German and Austrian Christians in antisemitism, a use the churches later repudiated.

**Must hit, any verdict (b):** state both positions accurately before judging. The genre side must be stated as: invective of this register was conventional, Chrysostom aimed comparable abuse at heretics and lax Christians, and "antisemitism" as a modern racial category is not what he held. The reception side must be stated as: the homilies were usable later because of their *content*, not merely their register — they generalize past the Judaizers to the Jews as such and treat the calamities after 70 as punishment. Each side must concede something: the reception side, that Chrysostom's category is religious and rhetorical, not racial, and that the occasion was intra-Christian; the genre side, that a conventional register does not make the propositions conventional, and that the reuse was not a misreading of what the sentences say. The verdict line must distinguish the two questions the arguments answer (what the text meant when preached; what the text does when read), whichever way it comes down.

**Wrong turns:** letting the occasion ("he was addressing his own people") do the work of excusing what the sentences say; inferring from the Nazi use that Chrysostom intended it, which is a reading backwards; settling the question by his sanctity or his Doctor's title, neither of which is a claim about these pages; treating "it was rhetoric" as equivalent to "he did not mean it."

**Model answer (b), one of several acceptable:** Wilken has to be granted that fourth-century invective had conventions, that Chrysostom used the same weapons on heretics, and that no racial doctrine is in view. Laqueur and Katz have to be granted that the propositions survive the register: the homilies deny the Jews any standing after 70 and address the Jews as such, and that content, not the volume, is what antisemitic writers could lift intact. My verdict: the genre argument establishes what kind of speech act this was in 386, and it removes the anachronistic label; it cannot establish that the claims are harmless, because reception ran on the claims and not on the style.

## Flashback

**From Lesson [3.4](03-04-ephrem-and-syriac-christianity.md) (Ephrem and Syriac Christianity):** *(Exegetical.)* An essay makes five claims about Ephrem the Syrian (died 373). (a) Sort each into one box, with a phrase of reason: **A** rests on the genuine Syriac corpus · **B** reported only by later tradition · **C** an inference, not a record · **D** reported by named ancient witnesses, but the evidence is judged insufficient by some critics · **E** presumptively not his at all. The claims: (i) he was born at Nisibis early in the fourth century; (ii) he trained choirs of consecrated women to sing his *madrashe*; (iii) he went to Basil at Caesarea about 370 and was ordained deacon there; (iv) a sermon surviving in Greek under his name shows what he taught; (v) he turns a pearl in verse until it shows "faces on all sides" and concludes that the Son is incomprehensible. (b) Aphrahat's twenty-three *Demonstrations* were copied, quoted and translated for centuries under no name or the wrong one, and "Aphrahat" resurfaces only in the tenth century. In two sentences, say what that case does to the argument "this work must be his, since it has been transmitted under his name since antiquity."

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):** (i) **C** — the conventional birth year is inferred from his career and his link to Jacob of Nisibis (died 338); no record gives it. (ii) **B** — reported by later Syriac sources and repeated by modern scholars, attested by no contemporary document: "the Syriac tradition reports", not "we know". (iii) **D** — Sozomen and a panegyric transmitted among Gregory of Nyssa's works report the visit and the ordination, and some critics judge that evidence insufficient; note that his being a deacon is not what is in doubt, only the journey to Basil and the ordination there. (iv) **E** — a very large body of Greek works circulated under his name, mostly not translated from his Syriac and mostly not his, so the burden is to show Syriac origin before the text counts as evidence for Ephrem at all. (v) **A** — *The Pearl*, in the cycle on Faith (NPNF 2nd series, vol. 13, Gwynn's revision of Morris).

**Must hit, strict (b):** transmission under a name records what copyists believed, not who wrote. A corpus as large and as substantial as Aphrahat's lost its author entirely, travelling anonymously, then for a while under Jacob of Nisibis's name, so continuity of attribution is weak evidence of authorship and has to be replaced by the tests of [1.1](01-01-how-to-read-a-church-father.md) — earliest witnesses, language of origin, internal evidence.

**Wrong turns:** putting (ii) in A because the *madrashe* are genuine — the hymns are his; the report of who first sang them is not documented; putting (iv) in B, as though the Greek works were traditions *about* Ephrem rather than texts falsely carrying his name; treating (iii) as settled in either direction, when the lesson's point is that named reports exist and are contested; concluding from (b) that old attributions count for nothing, rather than that they are evidence about belief and weak evidence about authorship.

**Model answer:** (a) (i) C, a conventional date inferred from his career. (ii) B, later Syriac tradition with no contemporary attestation. (iii) D, reported by Sozomen and by a panegyric among Gregory of Nyssa's works, judged insufficient by some critics. (iv) E, the Greek corpus under his name is largely not his. (v) A, *The Pearl* in the Hymns on Faith. (b) It shows that a long chain of attribution tells you what copyists thought, not who wrote: Aphrahat's own name dropped out of a large corpus for six centuries and a wrong one was attached instead. So "always transmitted under his name" is weak evidence about authorship, and the question has to be settled by earliest witnesses, language and internal evidence.
