# The Church Fathers · Lesson 3.4: Ephrem and Syriac Christianity

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 3: The fourth-century East · Builds on: [3.3 Gregory of Nazianzus and Gregory of Nyssa](03-03-the-two-gregories.md) · Unlocks: [3.5 Allegory and history: Alexandria and Antioch](03-05-allegory-and-history-alexandria-and-antioch.md)

## Why this matters

Everything so far in this module has been argued in Greek, in prose, by premises: Athanasius from salvation to the Son's divinity, Nazianzen from "what is not assumed is not healed" to a complete humanity in Christ. Meanwhile, on and beyond the Persian frontier, the same faith was taught in a third language and a wholly different form — sung stanzas with a refrain, written for choirs — by a man the Church later made a Doctor of the universal Church. So this lesson asks two questions the course keeps needing. Can a symbol carry a theological argument, or is poetry decoration on doctrine done elsewhere? And what must you check before putting a sentence in Ephrem's mouth, given that most of what circulated under his name in Greek is not his?

## The idea

Start with geography. Syriac, a dialect of Aramaic, was the literary language of Christians around Edessa and Nisibis and Christianity's third ancient theological language beside Greek and Latin. It was not a translation zone: it had its own Bible (the Peshitta), its own gospel text (long Tatian's harmony, the *Diatessaron*, not four separate gospels), its own poetic forms, and for a while its own quietly different canon.

It also sat on a fault line: Nisibis and Edessa were Roman, and the Persian empire began just past them. That matters twice. It explains why **Aphrahat**, the earliest Syriac author we can read whole, writes as a Christian under Persian rather than Roman rule, and why his twenty-three *Demonstrations* barely notice the Arian controversy convulsing an empire he did not live in. And it explains Ephrem's life: when Jovian ceded Nisibis to Persia in 363 its Christian population left, and Ephrem, then in middle age, settled at Edessa for his last decade.

**Ephrem** (died 373, most authorities) was a deacon, never a bishop, and wrote in three forms: commentaries, verse homilies (*mimre*), and hymns (*madrashe*, roughly "teachings"). The *madrashe* are the heart of it: a stated melody, a refrain, syllable-counted lines, composed to be sung in church — later Syriac tradition says by choirs of women Ephrem trained.

Here is the move that makes him a theologian, not a hymn-writer. God has scattered **symbols** through nature and Scripture — *raze*, the word that also means "mysteries" and later "sacraments". A symbol is not an illustration of a truth you already have by another route; it is how a creature is given anything of the Creator at all. So the right theological act is to hold a symbol up, turn it, and let it show its faces — and the wrong one is to force the gap between Creator and creature by definition, which Ephrem calls prying.

## Source

*The Pearl: Seven Hymns on the Faith*, Hymn I.1 and I.3, in NPNF second series, vol. 13 (John Gwynn's revision of J. B. Morris's 1847 translation from the Syriac; public domain):

> On a certain day a pearl did I take up, my brethren; I saw in it mysteries pertaining to the Kingdom; semblances and types of the Majesty; it became a fountain, and I drank out of it mysteries of the Son. I put it, my brethren, upon the palm of my hand, that I might examine it: I went to look at it on one side, and it proved faces on all sides. I found out that the Son was incomprehensible, since He is wholly Light. …
>
> It answered me and said, "The daughter of the sea am I, the illimitable sea! And from that sea whence I came up it is that there is a mighty treasury of mysteries in my bosom! Search thou out the sea, but search not out the Lord of the sea!"

## The argument

Ephrem never sets this out as a syllogism; the hymn contains one anyway. Reconstructed:

1. **Creator and creature are separated by a gulf that nothing on the creature's side can cross.** *In words:* the difference between God and everything else is not one of degree, like big and small, so no amount of looking gets you across.
2. **Whatever knowledge of God a creature has is therefore given, not taken.** *In words:* you receive, you do not extract.
3. **God gives it by planting [symbols](../reference.md#raza) in what he has made and in what he has said.** *In words:* a pearl, a fire, a tree, a garment, a name in Scripture — each a real thing made to carry something true about God.
4. **A symbol is inexhaustible in a way a definition is not: it "proved faces on all sides."** *In words:* turn it and you get another true aspect, never a last one — what you would expect of a finite thing pointing at an infinite one.
5. **So the conclusion Ephrem actually draws from the pearl is negative: "the Son was incomprehensible, since He is wholly Light."** *In words:* contemplating the symbol yields not a definition of the Son but the discovery that he cannot be contained in one.
6. **Therefore searching the gift is right and searching the Giver a category mistake — not mere impiety, but an error about what kind of thing God is.** *In words:* "Search thou out the sea, but search not out the Lord of the sea!"

The sting is in the same hymn: prayer and prying come out of the same mouth, Ephrem says, and which should God listen to? That is aimed at real opponents — the Arian controversy did reach Edessa — and refuses the game of defining how the Son is generated, on the ground that the question is malformed. Compare [3.3](03-03-the-two-gregories.md): Gregory of Nazianzus also insists the divine essence is beyond us, then argues inside the technical vocabulary anyway. Ephrem declines to enter it.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean: reading the pearl).** Take the Source passage as an argument. Ephrem picks up an ordinary object. He does not say "the pearl represents Christ, and here is the doctrine it illustrates"; he *examines* it, and it has no single face. He lists what the faces show: brightness, purity, undividedness, a conception without loss of wholeness, the Church with the Son inside her, and her who bore him. Each is a real property of a real pearl doing theological work: undividedness against those who divide the Son's being, purity as the Body of the Lord, conception as Mary and as the Church.

Notice what the poem refuses to do next. Having reached "the Son was incomprehensible," it converts that not into a technical claim but into an instruction: "leave things hidden, take things revealed." Then it hands the last word to the pearl, whose divers came back up because they could not sustain the depth. The conclusion is a posture, not a proposition — which is why it is sung, not stated.

**Example 2 (where it strains: the same medium served the heresy).** Ephrem did not invent Syriac verse and he knew it. The 1909 *Catholic Encyclopedia* article reports Ephrem's own statement that the poems of the Edessene teacher Bardaisan and his son Harmonius had spread their doctrine around Nisibis and Edessa; later Syriac tradition adds that Ephrem answered with orthodox hymns set to their tunes.

Take that seriously and a problem appears: if a madrasha persuades by melody, memorability and the pull of an image, the form is neutral between truth and error — the heretics had it first and used it the same way, so the genre cannot be what makes Ephrem right. There is a sharper limit. Gregory of Nazianzus's Letter 101 hands an opponent a premise he must publicly deny ("what is not assumed is not healed"); a symbol does not. A reader who does not already grant that the pearl was *made* to show the Son hears only a pretty comparison, with nothing in the image to contradict.

Ephrem's own answer is not "my song is truer" but that his symbols are anchored in the Scriptures of the Church and the faith she already confesses, while Bardaisan's float free. That is a real answer and also an admission: the symbol does not stand on its own. It teaches a faith held on other grounds — what a hymn in a liturgy is for. Whether that makes his theology a *different method* from the Greeks' or the same theology in another genre is still argued.

## Watch out

- **You might think a symbolic theology must be an allegorical one. Ephrem is not an Alexandrian.** He prefers the literal sense, uses allegory sparingly, and his *raze* are **types** — one real thing prefiguring another — not a hidden spiritual meaning behind a discarded letter; the 1909 *Catholic Encyclopedia* even calls his exegesis strongly Antiochene. Keep the distinction: [3.5](03-05-allegory-and-history-alexandria-and-antioch.md) is built on it; the four senses as Catholic teaching belong to [`old-testament`](../../old-testament/syllabus.md) 1.2.
- **"St Ephrem says…" is a claim to check before repeating.** A large body of Greek works circulated under his name, most of it not translated from his Syriac. Before quoting, ask: Syriac or Greek in origin; which edition and translation; does the hymn number match that edition? Numbering differs between the Roman edition of 1732–46, the nineteenth-century editions of Morris, Gwynn and Lamy, and the modern critical Syriac editions, so a bare "Hymn 4" is not a citation.
- **Aphrahat is the Fathers' hardest attribution lesson in miniature.** A substantial fourth-century theologian whose works were copied, translated into Armenian and quoted for centuries under the wrong name or none at all — for a while as Jacob of Nisibis's. If a corpus this large could lose its author, "everyone has always attributed this to X" is weak evidence about authorship ([1.1](01-01-how-to-read-a-church-father.md)).
- **The Doctor title endorses no particular opinion.** Benedict XV's 1920 encyclical honours Ephrem's holiness and teaching; it does not raise any sentence of his hymns to Church teaching, and its own footnotes quote works of varying authenticity. For the levels, see [`fundamental-theology` 4.4](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-04-the-ladder-of-doctrinal-authority.md).
- **The women's choirs are tradition, not documentation.** That Ephrem trained choirs of consecrated women to sing the *madrashe* is reported by later Syriac sources and repeated by modern scholars, not attested by any contemporary document. Say "the Syriac tradition reports," not "we know."

## One-liner

> Ephrem argues by holding a symbol up until it shows its faces, and stops exactly where the Greeks start defining — which is a real theology, a real limit, and a real problem for anyone who wants to quote him.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢) *(Exegetical.)*** Using only the Source passage above:

(a) Ephrem examines the pearl and it "proved faces on all sides." State in one sentence what he concludes from that, and quote the words that carry the conclusion.
(b) The pearl's own command distinguishes two objects of search. Name them, and say which one Ephrem permits and why.

Two sentences or fewer per part.

**P2 (🟡) *(Exegetical.)*** From *Hymns on the Nativity*, Hymn I (NPNF, 2nd series, vol. 13; Gwynn's revision of Morris):

> Travail Adam on the woman brought, that from him had come forth. She to-day her travail ransomed, who to her a Saviour bare! To Eve our mother a man gave birth, who himself had had no birth. How much more should Eve's daughter be believed to have borne a Child without a man! The virgin earth, she bare that Adam that was head over the earth! The Virgin bare to-day the Adam that was Head over the Heavens.

(a) The middle two sentences contain an argument. Reconstruct it as two numbered premises and a conclusion.
(b) Name the one thing a reader must already grant for that argument to have any force, and say what is left for an opponent who refuses to grant it.

**150 words or fewer** in total.

**P3 (🔴, optional) *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)*** An invented parish-bulletin note (not by any real author):

> "Our Lenten reading this week is St Ephrem the Syrian, from his *Homily on the Pearl*, where he tells us: 'Search thou out the sea, but search not out the Lord of the sea!' and 'leave things hidden, take things revealed.' The Syrian Fathers knew what we have forgotten — that the councils' definitions and all our theology of natures and persons are precisely the prying he condemns, and that the simple heart needs none of it."

(a) Name what the note makes the quotation do, the words of the Source passage that tell against that use, and the two things wrong with the citation as given. 
(b) Scholars disagree over whether Ephrem's symbolic theology is a *different method* from the Greek Fathers' or the same theology in a different genre. Take either side in **150 words or fewer**, using one piece of evidence from this lesson for your side and stating the strongest point against you.

<details>
<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical — strict.)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** the conclusion is about the Son's **incomprehensibility**, not about any positive definition — "I found out that the Son was incomprehensible, since He is wholly Light." The inference runs from the object's inexhaustibility ("it proved faces on all sides") to the inexhaustibility of what it symbolizes. Credit for noting that the conclusion is negative: examining the symbol yields a limit, not a doctrine.

**Must hit, strict (b):** the two objects are **the sea** and **the Lord of the sea** — that is, the creature (the gift, the symbol, the pearl and the depth it came from) and the Creator. Ephrem permits searching the **sea**: the creature is given to be examined, and the symbols in it are meant to be turned over. The Creator is not a further, larger object of the same kind, so searching him is not hard but malformed.

**Wrong turns:** reading "search not out the Lord of the sea" as a general ban on theology, when the same sentence *commands* the other search; treating "the sea" as God because it is "illimitable" — the pearl calls herself the sea's daughter, so the sea is on the creature's side of the line; answering (a) with the pearl's list of symbolic meanings (light, purity, the Church) rather than the conclusion Ephrem draws from them.

**Model answer:** (a) That the Son cannot be comprehended: "I found out that the Son was incomprehensible, since He is wholly Light" — the many faces of the symbol show the limit of the seeker, not the shape of the thing. (b) The sea, meaning the created gift, and the Lord of the sea, meaning the Creator. The first is permitted, because it was given to be examined and carries the symbols; the second is refused because he is not one more item within the sea to be found.

---

**P2** *(Exegetical — strict.)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** the argument is an *a fortiori* (a "how much more") from a granted case to a disputed one.

- **P1.** Eve was brought forth from a man — Adam — who was himself never born.
- **P2.** That is the harder case: a birth with no mother at all, which Scripture's readers already accept.
- **∴ C.** So the easier case, Eve's daughter bearing a child with no father, is the more credible, not the less: "How much more should Eve's daughter be believed to have borne a Child without a man!"

Full credit requires naming the *form* (a fortiori, from the greater to the lesser) and identifying Adam-and-Eve as the conceded premise and the virginal conception as the conclusion. Credit for noticing that the last two sentences then re-run the same pairing the other way, with Adam matched to Christ and the virgin earth to the Virgin.

**Must hit, strict (b):** the reader must already grant **that the Genesis narrative is true history and that Adam and Eve are types of Christ and Mary** — that these two pairs are set in a real correspondence by God, not merely compared by a poet. An opponent who refuses this is not contradicted by anything in the hymn; he is left with a simile, and the argument has no purchase on him. Credit for the general point: the symbol persuades only inside a shared rule of faith.

**Wrong turns:** calling it an argument from analogy and stopping there — the "how much more" is doing specific work, moving from the harder conceded case to the easier disputed one; treating the passage as *proving* the virgin birth from Genesis rather than showing it to be credible; saying the opponent can dispute the Genesis story, which is true but not what the question asks — the point is that the typological link, not the story, is the unstated premise.

**Model answer:** (a) P1: Eve came from Adam, a man who had himself never been born. P2: that is the harder case, and it is already granted. ∴ C: a woman of Eve's line bearing a child without a man is the easier case and should be believed all the more. (b) He must grant that the two pairs stand in a real God-given correspondence — Adam to Christ, Eve to Mary — and not a poetic resemblance. Refuse that and there is nothing in the hymn to argue with: the stanza offers no premise the opponent must deny, only an image he may decline.

---

**P3** *(a) exegetical — strict; (b) evaluative — any verdict.*

**Must hit, strict (a):** the note turns a refusal to **define God's inner life** into a rejection of **doctrine and theology as such**, and enlists Ephrem against the councils. The Source tells against it twice: the same hymn commands the other search ("Search thou out the sea"), and it gives a reason for the refusal — the Son is *incomprehensible* because he is wholly Light — which is itself a doctrinal claim, not a retreat from doctrine. "Take things revealed" is an instruction to receive what has been given, and the conciliar definitions are precisely claims about what was revealed, so the quotation cannot bear the weight put on it. The citation fails on two counts: (i) **the wrong genre and title** — this is a *madrasha*, a hymn, from the cycle *On the Faith* known as *The Pearl*, not a homily; Ephrem's homilies are *mimre*, a different form; (ii) **no translation, edition or number** — a usable citation names the hymn and section (here *The Pearl* I.1 and I.2) and the translation (NPNF 2nd series, vol. 13, Gwynn's revision of Morris), because the numbering differs between editions.

**Must hit, any verdict (b):** state the two positions precisely; pick one; give one concrete piece of evidence from the lesson (for "different method": the argument concludes in a posture rather than a proposition, and he declines the technical vocabulary his Greek contemporaries use. For "same theology, different genre": the anti-Arian polemic, the shared conclusion that the divine essence is beyond us, which Gregory also holds, and the fact that the genre was equally available to Bardaisan); and state the strongest point against your own side. Either verdict passes; an answer with no counter-point does not.

**Wrong turns:** answering (a) by disputing the bulletin's piety rather than its use of the text; saying the quotation is fabricated — it is not, it is accurately quoted and badly cited, which is the more common failure; in (b), settling the question by asserting that Ephrem was pro-Nicene, which both sides grant and neither side's thesis turns on.

**Model answer (b), one of several:** Same theology, different genre. The evidence is what Ephrem does with his opponents: he is polemicizing against the same errors as the Greeks, and his conclusion — that the Son cannot be comprehended because he is wholly Light — is one Gregory of Nazianzus would sign, having reached it by argument rather than by turning a pearl. The genre was not even distinctively his; Bardaisan had used it first, which suggests the verse is a delivery system rather than a method. The strongest point against me is Example 1: Ephrem's argument terminates in an instruction and a refusal, not in a proposition an opponent must deny, and a method that systematically declines to produce such propositions is doing something different from what Letter 101 does, not the same thing in rhyme.

## Flashback

**From Lesson [3.2](03-02-basil-the-great.md) (Basil the Great):** *(Exegetical.)* Basil's settlement is one *ousia*, three *hypostaseis* — the common plus the particular — and his own illustration is that Peter, Andrew and John are one nature and three particulars. An objector presses the illustration: three men are three men and we count them, so three divine particulars are three gods. (a) Name the step in the analogy that has to be refused if the settlement is to survive, and say why the distinction between the two words does not by itself refuse it. (b) State what Basil asserts against the objection and what his answer does not do, then name the treatise that takes the objection up in full and its author. **Two sentences per part.**

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):** the step is the **transfer of counting** — the assumption that a common nature is pluralized above as it is below, so that particulars sharing the Godhead may be added up the way men are. The *ousia*/*hypostasis* distinction assigns the two words to two questions, "what is it?" and "which one?", so that "one" and "three" stop contradicting each other; that is precision about terms and leaves the counting question exactly where it was.

**Must hit, strict (b):** Basil asserts that **number in God is not addition** — we do not proceed one, two, three toward a plurality, and the divine nature is above counting (*On the Holy Spirit* ch. 18). What he does not do is derive it: he states the conclusion and marks the gap rather than closing it. The full defence is his brother's — Gregory of Nyssa's *To Ablabius: Not Three Gods*, which denies the parallel, since the divine operations are inseparable as three men's actions never are.

**Wrong turns:** saying Basil answers by denying a common nature in God, when he affirms one *ousia*; claiming the distinction itself defeats the objection, which is the mistake the analogy is designed to expose; attributing *To Ablabius* to Basil or to Gregory of Nazianzus; answering that the divine nature is simply mysterious, which is Basil's assertion restated rather than the argument asked for.

**Model answer:** (a) The move from "one common nature, many particulars" to "so count them", which is what works for men and is exactly what must fail for God; the two-word distinction only stops "one" and "three" answering the same question, and says nothing about whether divine particulars are countable. (b) Basil insists that number in God is not addition and that the nature is above counting (*On the Holy Spirit* ch. 18), but he asserts this rather than deriving it, marking the gap instead of closing it. Gregory of Nyssa's *To Ablabius: Not Three Gods* supplies the derivation, by denying the parallel at the human end and in the single undivided divine operation.
