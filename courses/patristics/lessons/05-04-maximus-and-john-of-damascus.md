# The Church Fathers · Lesson 5.4: Maximus the Confessor and John of Damascus

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 5: The Christological Fathers and the close of the patristic age · Builds on: [5.1](05-01-cyril-of-alexandria.md), [5.2](05-02-leo-the-great.md), [5.3](05-03-gregory-the-great.md) · Unlocks: Boss problem 5 in the [syllabus](../syllabus.md); `christology`; `thomistic-synthesis`

## Why this matters

Handbooks close the patristic age with Gregory the Great (d. 604) or Isidore (d. 636) in the West and John of Damascus (d. c. 749) in the East. That is a [convention of the manuals](../reference.md#the-close-of-the-patristic-age), not a fact of nature: nothing stopped in 749, and Greek theology kept working. The convention is worth understanding anyway, because these two did something earlier Fathers did not. Maximus took the Chalcedonian formula and asked what it commits you to about a single human act of willing. John gathered four centuries of Greek theology into one ordered book that the Latin West then used for five hundred years. After them the tradition has a summary of itself — and a summary is what you appeal to when you say "the Fathers."

## The idea

Two lives, neither comfortable.

**Maximus (c. 580–662)** was a monk. The Greek *Life* makes him a Constantinopolitan aristocrat turned imperial secretary; a hostile Syriac life, unknown until the twentieth century, makes him a Palestinian of obscure birth. He held no see and defined nothing. He fled the Persian and then the Arab advance to North Africa, and in July 645 at Carthage debated the deposed patriarch Pyrrhus in public on whether Christ has one will or two. The record of that debate, the *Disputation with Pyrrhus*, is the clearest statement of his case; with no public-domain English of it, this lesson paraphrases him and quotes him nowhere. He was tried at Constantinople for resisting the emperor's ban on the question, and in 662, old, he had his tongue and his right hand cut off — the organs of speech and of writing — and was exiled to Lazica, where he died that August. Nineteen years later the Sixth Ecumenical Council taught what he had argued.

**John of Damascus (c. 675–c. 749)** was born into a Christian family that served the caliph's treasury in Damascus and became a monk near Jerusalem. He wrote under Muslim rule, outside the reach of the Byzantine emperors who were then suppressing icons — which is exactly why his defence of images survived ([`church-history` 3.2](../../church-history/lessons/03-02-the-rise-of-islam-and-the-christian-mediterranean.md), which owns his setting). His *Fount of Knowledge* has three parts: philosophical chapters, a catalogue of heresies, and the *Exposition of the Orthodox Faith*, a hundred chapters that arrange the Greek Fathers into a single argument. He rarely claims originality; he says he is setting out what the Fathers taught, which is the point.

Behind both stands a third body of writing neither could do without, and neither could date: the **Dionysian corpus** (Example 2).

## Source

John of Damascus, *On the Divine Images*, First Apology (the passage on matter, usually cited as I.16), in Mary H. Allies's translation of 1898, public domain:

> Of old, God the incorporeal and uncircumscribed was never depicted. Now, however, when God is seen clothed in flesh, and conversing with men, I make an image of the God whom I see. I do not worship matter, I worship the God of matter, who became matter for my sake, and deigned to inhabit matter, who worked out my salvation through matter.

Read the hinge: *of old* … *now, however*. The whole argument turns on a change in the object, not a change in the rule.

## The argument

### Maximus on the two wills

The monothelite proposal, backed by the emperor Heraclius's *Ecthesis* of 638, was a peace offer to those who had refused Chalcedon: keep two natures, but confess **one will**, so that Christ is plainly one agent. Maximus's reply, reconstructed:

1. **Willing is a power of a nature, not of a person.** Every rational nature comes with the capacity to will what suits it; that is part of what it is to be that kind of thing. *In words: a will is standard equipment for a human being, like a mind or a body.*
2. **Christ has two complete natures** — the Chalcedonian definition, taken as given here and owned by `christology`. *In words: he is fully God and fully man, and "fully" is the load-bearing word.*
3. **So he has two natural wills.** *In words: deny the human will and you have subtracted something from the humanity.*
4. **A humanity with something subtracted cannot heal a whole humanity.** This is the soteriological rule the course has met twice — Gregory of Nazianzus against Apollinarius, [what is not assumed is not healed](../reference.md#what-is-not-assumed-is-not-healed) ([3.3](03-03-the-two-gregories.md)). *In words: if Christ's humanity has no will, then human willing, which is where sin happens, was never taken up or repaired.*
5. **Two wills need not mean two willers.** The one person wills in both ways, humanly and divinely, and the human willing never sets itself against the divine. *In words: the count of wills follows the natures; the count of agents follows the person.*

Premise 5 is where the objection lands, and it is why Maximus needs a distinction: between the **natural will** — the settled orientation of a human nature toward its own good and away from death — and the [deliberating, hesitating mode of willing](../reference.md#natural-will-and-gnomic-will) of a creature that does not know what is best and must make up its mind between options. Maximus assigns the first to Christ and denies him the second. John states the same denial later in flat prose: Christ, being God and knowing all things, had no need of inquiry and deliberation (*Exposition* III.14).

### John of Damascus on the image

John's defence of icons runs the Incarnation the same way Maximus runs it.

1. **The Old Testament prohibition was a prohibition on depicting the invisible.** You cannot image what has no form, and every attempt is idolatry. *In words: the commandment protects God's unrepresentability.*
2. **In Christ the invisible God became visible and circumscribed.** *In words: there was now something to see.*
3. **So depicting him denies nothing about God; refusing to depict him denies the Incarnation.** This is the Source passage. *In words: the icon is a claim about the flesh.*
4. **Matter is not contemptible**, since God saved through it — cross, tomb, book, bread. *In words: the iconoclast's disgust at painted wood proves too much, and takes the sacraments with it.*
5. **The honour paid to an image is not the worship paid to God.** John distinguishes ["the worship of *latreia*, which we show to God, who alone by nature is worthy of worship"](../reference.md#latreia-and-proskynesis) from the honour and reverence (*proskynesis*) paid to saints, places and things. And, quoting Basil, "the honour given to the image passes over to the prototype" (*Exposition* IV.16, S. D. F. Salmond, *Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers*, second series, volume 9). *In words: reverence travels through the picture to the person; it does not stop at the paint.*

The Second Council of Nicaea (787) built its definition on that distinction, reserving *latreia* to God alone.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean: Gethsemane).** Matthew 26:39, as John quotes it — "Father, if it be possible, let this cup pass from Me: yet, not as I will but as You will" — is the test case, because a single verse seems to show two wills in tension. Run the grammar. The shrinking from death is the human nature doing what a human nature does — Maximus's natural will, and John's "natural timidity." It is not sin, and not a second person bargaining with the first. "Not as I will" marks the human willing; "as You will" marks the divine; and the same one who says both is the one subject. The monothelite reading — one will, so the reluctance belongs to the Godhead or is play-acted — has to make the prayer either blasphemous or theatre. The dyothelite reading lets it be a real human recoil, really obedient. That is why a quarrel over one verse was never a quibble: the whole account of how a human will gets healed rides on it.

**Example 2 (where it strains: the Dionysian corpus).** Four works and ten letters — *The Divine Names*, *Mystical Theology*, the two *Hierarchies* — circulated from about 500 under the name of Dionysius the Areopagite, the man Paul converted at Athens (Acts 17:34). An apostolic convert writing Christian Neoplatonism was an enormous prize, and everyone from Maximus, who worked through his hardest passages in the *Ambigua*, to Aquinas treated him as near-apostolic authority. The scholia printed under Maximus's name are themselves now largely assigned to John of Scythopolis: even the commentary on the corpus has an attribution problem.

Run the [check from 1.1](../reference.md#how-to-check-a-patristic-quotation). Step two fails, and it was challenged early: at a colloquy in Constantinople in 532 the Severans cited these texts and Hypatius of Ephesus answered that no earlier Father had known them. Lorenzo Valla and then Erasmus revived the doubt in the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries; in 1895 Hugo Koch and Josef Stiglmayr independently showed passages dependent on the pagan Neoplatonist Proclus, who died in 485. The author is anonymous, writes around 500, and is now cited as **Pseudo-Dionysius** ([the standing list](../reference.md#spurious-and-misattributed-works)).

Now separate what the correction destroys from what it leaves. Destroyed: every argument of the form "the apostolic age already held this," and the corpus's weight as first-century evidence, which was doing real work in Byzantine disputes. Left standing: the content, which was never true because Paul's convert wrote it. The three ways of speaking about God — affirmation, negation, and eminence, the last correcting the first two — are [the route to the divine names](../reference.md#the-three-ways) that Maximus, John and eventually Aquinas all use, and Aquinas's version is owned by [`thomistic-synthesis`](../../thomistic-synthesis/syllabus.md) 4.2, with participation at 2.3. A false name bought the corpus its hearing; its arguments kept it.

## Watch out

- **You might think two wills mean a divided Christ.** Maximus's whole point is that the count of wills tracks *natures* and the count of agents tracks the *person*. One who wills, two ways of willing.
- **You might think Maximus's position was the Church's throughout.** For roughly a quarter-century it was imperial policy that he was wrong, the reigning emperor's edict forbade even raising the question, and a pope had written a letter that the Sixth Council would later condemn. He was a monk with no office, vindicated after his death. Nothing in that record is tidy, and the marks by which a Father is judged cut both ways: his rightness came from the argument and the Church's reception of it, not from his standing while he lived.
- **You might think John invented the case for icons.** He assembled it, and he says so, appending florilegia of earlier Fathers. His genius is arrangement — which is exactly what makes the *Exposition* usable and exactly what makes it weak evidence for what any *individual* earlier Father held.
- **You might think "deification" means becoming God.** In Maximus, as in Athanasius ([3.1](03-01-athanasius.md)), [it names a creature's share in God's life](../reference.md#deification) by grace, never a change of nature; and he extends it past the individual soul to the whole cosmos gathered in Christ, the theme a modern reader meets under Balthasar's title *Cosmic Liturgy*.

## One-liner

> Maximus proved that a nature you have subtracted a will from is not a nature; John gathered the Greek Fathers into one book and handed them to the Latin West — and both leaned on a corpus whose author's name was the one thing about it that was false.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢) *(Exegetical.)*** John of Damascus, *Exposition of the Orthodox Faith* III.18, on Gethsemane (Salmond, *Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers*, second series, volume 9):

> So, then, He had by nature, both as God and as man, the power of will. But His human will was obedient and subordinate to His divine will, not being guided by its own inclination, but willing those things which the divine will willed. … It was as man, then, that He wished the cup to pass from Him: but these are the words of natural timidity. Nevertheless, He said, not My will, that is to say, not in so far as I am of a different essence from You, but Your will be done, that is to say, My will and Your will, in so far as I am of the same essence as Thou.

(a) Quote the words that show John takes "not my will" and "thy will" as marking **two natures in one speaker**, not two speakers.
(b) A monothelite objects: if the human will only ever wills what the divine wills, it does no work and may as well not be there. State John's answer from this passage, in **two sentences or fewer**, and name the phrase it rests on.

**P2 (🟡) *(Exegetical.)*** An invented parish blog post:

> "People say icon-veneration is a late Byzantine import. But Dionysius the Areopagite — the man St Paul converted in Athens, so we are talking about the first generation — already describes the ranks of heaven in ordered triads, and the whole hierarchical, image-filled worship of the Church follows from it. So when we worship our icons, we are doing what the apostles' own convert taught."

(a) Run the check from [1.1](01-01-how-to-read-a-church-father.md) on the attribution: say what the corpus actually is, and name **one** claim in the post that the correction destroys and **one** that survives it.
(b) One word in the last sentence would have been refused by John of Damascus himself. Name it, and give the distinction and the words — from the Source, or from the fifth premise of his argument for images — that decide it.
**150 words or fewer in total.**

**P3 (🔴, optional) *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)***
(a) Reconstruct Maximus's argument for two wills in **numbered premises and a conclusion**, then name the single premise a monothelite must deny, and say what he must then say about Gethsemane.
(b) Maximus held no office; an imperial edict forbade the question he kept arguing; he was condemned, mutilated and exiled; and the council that defined his doctrine in 681 condemns the monothelite teachers by name without mentioning him. Some readers call that a vindication, others a doctrinal victory that was never a personal rehabilitation. In **150 words or fewer**, any verdict: say what evidence would decide between those descriptions, and whether the answer changes the standing of the doctrine.

<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical — strict.)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** the gloss John attaches to each half of the prayer: "**not in so far as I am of a different essence from You**" (for "not My will") and "**My will and Your will, in so far as I am of the same essence as Thou**" (for "Your will be done"). The explanation runs on *essence*, not on persons: one speaker who is of one essence with the Father as God and of another as man. Credit also for "He had by nature, **both as God and as man**, the power of will," which locates the doubling in the natures.

**Must hit, strict (b):** the human will's submission is not inactivity — it is itself an act of the human nature, willing what the divine will wills. The phrase is "**willing those things which the divine will willed**" (an answer resting on "obedient and subordinate," with that active sense drawn out, also passes). A strong answer adds that "the words of natural timidity" show the human will operating with a real motion of its own before it yields; an obedience that costs nothing to render would not have produced the prayer.

**Wrong turns:** answering (a) with "not My will, but Your will be done" alone — that is the verse, and the verse is exactly what the monothelite also quotes; the work is done by John's two gloss clauses. Saying in (b) that the human will is "overridden" or "absorbed," which concedes the objection and contradicts "willing those things which the divine will willed." Reading "natural timidity" as a fault or as sin; John's point is that it is *natural*, which is why it needed no correction, only obedience.

**Model answer:** (a) "not in so far as I am of a different essence from You," and "My will and Your will, in so far as I am of the same essence as Thou." Both clauses explain the prayer by two essences in one speaker. (b) The human will is not idle: it actively wills what the divine will wills, "willing those things which the divine will willed," and the "natural timidity" shows it moving on its own before it consents. So it does work — the work of a real human obedience, which is precisely what had to be healed.

---

**P2** *(Exegetical — strict.)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** the corpus is by an anonymous Greek author writing around 500, drawing on the Neoplatonist Proclus (d. 485); it is cited as Pseudo-Dionysius, never as Paul's convert. Doubted by Hypatius of Ephesus in 532, questioned by Valla and Erasmus, settled in 1895 by Koch and Stiglmayr. **Destroyed:** the dating claim, "so we are talking about the first generation" — the post's entire argument that this practice is apostolic in age, and with it the reply to "late import." **Survives:** the corpus's theological content and its influence — the hierarchies and the three ways really did shape Greek and Latin theology, and Maximus, John and Aquinas really did build on them. A different defence of icons, John's from the Incarnation, is untouched by the correction.

**Must hit, strict (b):** the word is **worship**. John divides *latreia*, "which we show to God, who alone by nature is worthy of worship," from the honour or veneration (*proskynesis*) given to saints, places and holy things; the icon receives the second, and the reason is that "the honour given to the image passes over to the prototype" — reverence terminates in the person, not the paint. "We venerate our icons" is what the post should say.

**Wrong turns:** calling the corpus a forgery and stopping there, which loses everything under "survives" and is also more than the evidence shows about the author's intent; saying the post is wrong because icons are late (it is the *argument*, not the practice, that fails here); answering (b) with "Catholics don't worship icons" without producing John's distinction and the words that carry it.

**Model answer:** (a) The corpus is an anonymous Greek work of about 500 that draws on Proclus, doubted from 532 and settled in 1895; it is Pseudo-Dionysius. Destroyed: the claim that this is first-generation evidence, which is the post's whole reply to "late import." Surviving: its content and influence on Maximus, John and Aquinas. (b) "Worship." John reserves *latreia* for God, "who alone by nature is worthy of worship," and gives images the honour of *proskynesis*, since "the honour given to the image passes over to the prototype." The post means venerate.

---

**P3** *(a) exegetical — strict; (b) evaluative — any verdict.*

**Must hit, strict (a):** the five premises, in any faithful wording. P1 willing is a power of a nature, not of a person. P2 Christ has two complete natures (Chalcedon). P3 therefore two natural wills. P4 a humanity missing its will could not heal human willing — "what is not assumed is not healed." P5 two wills are not two willers; the one person wills in both natures, the human never against the divine. ∴ Christ has two natural wills and one subject who wills. **The premise denied: P1.** The monothelite must make willing belong to the person (the *hypostasis*), so that one person yields one will. **What he must then say about Gethsemane:** the reluctance cannot be a human will's motion, so it is either the divine will recoiling from the cross — which is incoherent, since that will is what sends him there — or a display staged for our instruction. Either reading empties the prayer, which is the cost Maximus presses.

**Wrong turns:** denying P2 instead of P1 (that is the miaphysite dispute of [5.1](05-01-cyril-of-alexandria.md), not this one); denying P5, which is a monothelite *worry*, not a monothelite premise, and which Maximus concedes must be answered rather than asserted; folding P4 into P3 and losing the soteriological step that makes the argument matter.

**Must hit, any verdict (b):** distinguish the two things being claimed — that the doctrine was received, and that the man was rehabilitated — and say they can come apart. Then name evidence that bears on the second, not the first: whether the acts of 681 or any conciliar text name him; whether he was restored to communion or commemorated liturgically, and how soon; whether the men condemned at Constantinople III are the same men who condemned him; whether later Fathers and councils cite him as an authority, and under what title ("the Confessor" is itself a verdict). A complete answer takes a position on the last clause — whether personal rehabilitation is even relevant to the doctrine's standing — and defends it with the criteria of [1.1](01-01-how-to-read-a-church-father.md): the weight of a Father's testimony comes from the Church's reception of his teaching, so a doctrine can be received while the man's name waits.

**Wrong turns:** treating the council's silence about him as a condemnation, or as proof that it ignored him; arguing that because he was right, the process does not matter (that answers a question nobody asked); making the case turn on whether he was *popular*.

**Model answer (b), one of several:** They are two claims and they separate cleanly. The doctrine was received in 681; whether Maximus was is a question about his name, and the evidence for it is different — conciliar and synodal texts that mention him, the date of his liturgical commemoration, whether those who had condemned him were themselves condemned, and how quickly later writers cite him as an authority rather than as a defendant. I would call 681 a doctrinal vindication and leave personal rehabilitation to be shown from that later evidence. It changes nothing about the standing of the doctrine. By the criteria of 1.1, a Father's testimony is weighed by the Church's reception of his teaching, not by his standing at his death — which is just as well, since his standing at his death was that of a convicted man.

## Flashback

**From Lesson [5.2](05-02-leo-the-great.md) (Leo the Great):** *(Exegetical.)* Eutyches confessed two natures before the union and, after it, one. Leo's *Tome* (Letter 28, to Flavian of Constantinople, 449) opens not on a technical term but on the baptismal creed, charging that Eutyches has not grasped the rudiments every candidate for baptism professes — a confession that already names two births, one eternal from the Father and one in time from Mary. Two of the letter's early steps: **(i)** the birth in time adds nothing to the eternal one; **(ii)** the humanity is really ours, taken from the body of Mary, against the idea that Christ had the form of a man but not the reality of his mother's body.

(a) The quarrel is over the word *physis* / *natura*. Say why Leo begins from the creed instead of defining the term, and what it costs Eutyches to answer him there.
(b) Steps (i) and (ii) guard different things. Say what each rules out, and which of the two is aimed at where Eutychianism actually ends up. **Two sentences per part.**

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):** because a dispute about what "nature" means cannot be settled by supplying more of the disputed word — each party can hear the other's usage as either slack or dangerous, which is exactly what happened between Alexandria and Chalcedon. The creed is common ground, professed by both parties before the quarrel and by every candidate for baptism, so an argument run from it cannot be answered by a rival technical usage; the cost to Eutyches is that he must deny or explain away one of the two births named in the confession he was baptized into, which turns a quarrel over a formula into a question about what he professes.
**Must hit, strict (b):** (i) guards the **divinity**: becoming man changed the man's situation, not God's nature, so it rules out any suggestion that the second birth added to, altered or diminished the eternal Son. (ii) guards the **humanity**: it rules out a flesh that merely looks like ours, and it is the one aimed at where Eutychianism ends up, since pressing "one nature after the union" is what made Christ's flesh not quite ours — the claim Leo is actually judging.
**Wrong turns:** treating the appeal to the creed as piety or rhetorical warm-up rather than as the deliberate choice of a shared premise; assigning (i) to the humanity — it protects the immutability of the divine nature, and giving it the wrong object collapses the pair; naming Nestorius as the target, when the letter is a judgment in the case of Eutyches; answering (b) with "both guard the two natures" without separating what each rules out.
**Model answer:** (a) Defining *natura* would only hand the opponent another term to use his own way, whereas the creed is ground both sides already stand on and neither can renegotiate. Answering Leo there means denying one of the two births every baptizand professes, so Eutyches cannot treat the dispute as a technical disagreement among theologians. (b) The first step keeps the incarnation from touching the divine nature: the eternal birth is untouched by the birth in time, so nothing was added to or lost from the Son. The second keeps the flesh genuinely ours, ruling out a body that only has the appearance of a man's — and that is the one that bites, because "one nature after the union" is precisely what ends by making Christ's humanity something other than our own.

## Closing the course

You have read about twenty Fathers across six and a half centuries, and the last thing to fix is what the phrase "the Fathers" can carry. Three claims, in ascending order of what they cost to establish. *This Father said this* — cheap, and the [five-step check](../reference.md#how-to-check-a-patristic-quotation) settles it. *This Father taught this as the faith of the churches* — harder, because it needs the genre, the opponent and the argument. *The Fathers agree on this* — the expensive one, because it needs a defensible sense of "agree" before any counting starts, and the record's normal state is divergence on the letter with convergence on the doctrine ([1.1](01-01-how-to-read-a-church-father.md)).

Boss problem 5(d) in the [syllabus](../syllabus.md) is that third claim, run on the hardest possible case: whether there is a unanimous consent of the Fathers on the "rock" of Matthew 16:18. The method is 1.1's and the evidence is the whole course. What the answer proves about the papacy is a different course's job (`apologetics-catholic-claims`); what it proves about how to use a patristic consensus is yours.

From here the dogmatic courses take the same texts and ask what the Church defined from them: `trinity-and-god` on Module 3, `christology` on Module 5, `creation-grace-last-things` on Module 4. `thomistic-synthesis` shows what the thirteenth century made of the inheritance John packed. And the next time you meet a Father's name under a paragraph on a screen, you will do what almost nobody does, which is ask for the chapter.
