# The Church Fathers — Syllabus

> Catholic Theology · Tier 1 · ~24 lessons · Prereqs: [`fundamental-theology`](../fundamental-theology/syllabus.md) · Roadmap id: `patristics`

## Goal

This course reads the Fathers of the Church in their own words, from Clement of Rome to John of Damascus, as theologians working out the faith under pressure: against Gnostics, Arians, Donatists, Pelagians and Nestorius, and in sermons, catecheses and hymns. It is taught from within the Catholic tradition, which holds the Fathers to be privileged witnesses of Tradition. It is also historically honest about where they disagree with each other, where they were wrong (Irenaeus's millenarianism, Chrysostom's invective against the Jews), and where a text long quoted under a great name turns out to be by someone else. You will finish able to pick up a passage from Schaff's *Ante-Nicene* or *Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers* and say who wrote it (and whether we are sure), what it argues and against whom, which words decide its meaning, and what weight it carries as a witness, alone or as part of a real consensus. It deliberately skips the events and politics of the councils and persecutions (owned by [`church-history`](../church-history/syllabus.md)), Trinitarian, Christological and grace doctrine as systematically defined (`trinity-and-god`, `christology`, `creation-grace-last-things`: this course reads how the Fathers *argued toward* those definitions), Augustine as a political theorist ([`history-of-political-thought`](../history-of-political-thought/syllabus.md) 2.2) and as a philosopher (`ancient-medieval-philosophy`), and the use of patristic texts as evidence in Catholic–Orthodox–Protestant disputes (`apologetics-catholic-claims`). It also skips whole bodies of writing: Hilary, Cyril of Jerusalem, Evagrius, the desert sayings, Isidore and Bede appear only in passing, and the Coptic, Armenian and Ethiopic traditions not at all.

## How practice works in this course

Problems follow [HUMANITIES-BUILD-BRIEF.md](../../HUMANITIES-BUILD-BRIEF.md). **Close reading is the workhorse**: a public-domain passage of 150 words or fewer from Schaff's *Ante-Nicene Fathers* or *Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers* (or another pre-1930 translation, named), and the question of which reading its words support. Graded strictly as **exegesis**, as are reconstructions of a Father's argument in his own terms and questions of attribution ("is this sentence really Augustine's?"). **Evaluative** parts are verdict-neutral and reserved for questions genuinely open among patristic scholars or Catholic theologians: whether the Antiochene–Alexandrian contrast is overdrawn, whether Origen taught what was condemned under his name, whether a claimed consensus of the Fathers exists. Where Catholic, Orthodox and Protestant readers each appeal to the same passage (Ignatius on the bishop, Irenaeus on the Roman church, Cyprian's chapter 4, Basil on unwritten tradition, Gregory on the title "universal bishop"), the problem asks the student to state each reading and the words it leans on; arguing out which is right belongs to `apologetics-catholic-claims`, so this course has **no apologetic parts**. Other archetypes used: **reconstruct** (a Father's argument in numbered premises), **find the crux** (Cyril and Nestorius, Augustine and Pelagius, Cyprian and Stephen on rebaptism), and **diagnose** (an invented blog post or homily that quotes a Father, and what the quotation is being made to do). Where a doctrine's weight matters, it carries its level from [`fundamental-theology`](../fundamental-theology/syllabus.md) 4.4; treating an individual Father's opinion as Church teaching is an exegetical error, as is quoting a spurious work as genuine.

## Dangerous Checklist

When you finish, you can:

- [ ] Place any Father covered here in his century, city, language and controversy, and say whether he counts as a Father, a Doctor, or an ecclesiastical writer (Origen, Tertullian) and why
- [ ] Check a patristic quotation before using it: find it in Schaff, say which edition or recension it comes from, and flag the famous spurious or garbled ones ("credo quia absurdum", "Roma locuta, causa finita", the long recension of Ignatius, Pseudo-Dionysius, the Athanasian Creed)
- [ ] State what Trent and Vatican I mean by the unanimous consent of the Fathers, and test a claimed consensus on a specific question against the actual spread of patristic readings
- [ ] Read 1 Clement, the Didache and Ignatius side by side and say exactly what each shows, and does not show, about church offices and the Eucharist around the year 100
- [ ] Reconstruct Irenaeus's case against the Gnostics from the rule of faith and public apostolic succession, and explain recapitulation in his own terms
- [ ] Close-read the passages the churches argue over (Irenaeus on the Roman church, both versions of Cyprian's *On the Unity* ch. 4, Basil on unwritten tradition, Gregory on "universal bishop") and state each tradition's reading with the words it leans on
- [ ] Explain Origen's theory of the senses of Scripture and the Antiochene reply, apply each to a passage, and say where the textbook contrast between the two schools is overdrawn
- [ ] Reconstruct the soteriological arguments that drove Nicene and Chalcedonian theology ("he became man that we might be made God"; "what is not assumed is not healed") and say what each premise commits you to
- [ ] Explain what the Cappadocians did with *ousia* and *hypostasis*, and why Gregory of Nyssa thinks "three persons" does not mean "three gods"
- [ ] Read *Confessions*, the anti-Donatist and the anti-Pelagian writings as one developing theology of grace and the Church, and say where later Augustine went beyond earlier Augustine
- [ ] Find the crux between Cyril and Nestorius, and between Cyril's "one incarnate nature" and Leo's Tome, and explain why the first phrase's origin under a false name matters
- [ ] Say why the patristic age is conventionally closed with Gregory the Great in the West and John of Damascus in the East, and what Maximus and John handed on

## Modules

### Module 1: Reading the Fathers, and the first witnesses (c. 95-165)

How to read a Father at all, then the earliest writers after the apostles: a Roman letter, a church manual, a bishop on the way to martyrdom, and a philosopher who defended Christianity to the emperor.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1.1 | How to read a Church Father | Say who counts as a Father, check a quotation's authenticity, and weigh one Father against a consensus | the classical marks (antiquity, orthodoxy, holiness, the Church's approval); Father, Doctor, ecclesiastical writer; Schaff's ANF/NPNF and Migne; pseudepigrapha and misattributions (2 Clement, *Barnabas*, the Athanasian Creed); the unanimous consent of the Fathers (Trent Session IV, *Dei Filius* ch. 2) as witness to Tradition; Vincent cited from [`fundamental-theology`](../fundamental-theology/syllabus.md) 5.1 |
| 1.2 | 1 Clement and the Didache | Say what the two earliest non-canonical texts show about church order, baptism and the Eucharist | the Corinthian deposition and Rome's letter; apostles, appointed successors and "bishops and deacons" (1 Clement 42–44); presbyter and bishop not yet distinct; the Two Ways, baptism, the Eucharistic prayers and Sunday assembly (Didache 7, 9–10, 14–15); dating ranges |
| 1.3 | Ignatius of Antioch | Read the letters for the single bishop, the Eucharist and martyrdom, knowing which letters are genuine | the three recensions and the authenticity debate; bishop, presbyters, deacons; the Eucharist against docetists (*Smyrnaeans* 6–7); the first "catholic church" (*Smyrnaeans* 8); the preface to *Romans* and how readers take it; martyrdom as imitation of Christ |
| 1.4 | Justin Martyr | Reconstruct Justin's defence of Christianity and read his account of Christian worship | *First* and *Second Apology*, *Dialogue with Trypho*; seeds of the Word in the philosophers; the argument from prophecy; baptism and the Sunday Eucharist (*First Apology* 61, 65–67); Mary as the new Eve (*Dialogue* 100) |

**Boss problem 1:** Supplied passages (ANF translations): 1 Clement 42 and 44, Didache 15, and Ignatius, *Smyrnaeans* 8. (a) For each text, list the offices it names and say whether "bishop" and "presbyter" name one office or two; point to the words. (b) Before citing *Smyrnaeans* 8 on the bishop, what must you establish about which recension you are quoting, and why does it matter more for this topic than most (1.1, 1.3)? (c) What does "catholic church" mean in *Smyrnaeans* 8, and what in the sentence decides it? (d) Catholic and Orthodox readers take the move from Clement to Ignatius as the apostolic order becoming visible; many Protestant historians take it as a post-apostolic development. Name the crux, the single premise one side must deny, and say what further evidence would move it; in 150 words, any verdict. The theological question of what apostolic succession requires belongs to `ecclesiology-and-mariology`. Parts (a)–(c) are exegetical; (d) is evaluative.

### Module 2: The rule of faith, Africa and Alexandria (c. 180-258)

Against the Gnostics the Church names its criteria (the rule of faith, the public succession of bishops, the four gospels); in Latin Africa theology gets its vocabulary and its first great argument over unity; in Alexandria it meets Plato and learns to read Scripture spiritually.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2.1 | Irenaeus against the Gnostics | Reconstruct Irenaeus's case from rule of faith and public succession | the Valentinian myth as Irenaeus reports it, and Nag Hammadi as a check on a hostile source; the rule of faith (*Against Heresies* 1.10); public apostolic tradition and succession lists (3.3–3.4); the Roman-church passage (3.3.2) and the ambiguity of its Latin; the fourfold gospel (3.11) |
| 2.2 | Irenaeus: recapitulation | Explain Irenaeus's theology of salvation in his own terms | one God, creator and redeemer; recapitulation in Christ; Eve and Mary (3.22); Eucharist and the resurrection of the flesh (4.18, 5.2); "the glory of God is a living man" (4.20.7); his millenarianism and its non-reception; Doctor of the Church (2022) |
| 2.3 | Tertullian | Read Tertullian's prescription against heretics and the birth of Latin Trinitarian vocabulary, and fix his misquotations | *Prescription against Heretics* (Scripture belongs to the Church; "Athens and Jerusalem", ch. 7); *Against Praxeas* (*trinitas*, *substantia*, *persona*); the Montanist turn and his standing as an ecclesiastical writer; what *On the Flesh of Christ* 5 actually says versus "credo quia absurdum"; the "seed" of martyrs sentence as paraphrase |
| 2.4 | Cyprian of Carthage | Close-read *On the Unity of the Catholic Church* and follow Cyprian's argument with Rome over rebaptism | the lapsed and reconciliation (events in [`church-history`](../church-history/syllabus.md) 1.3); the two versions of ch. 4 and Bévenot's thesis that both are Cyprian's; the Church as mother (ch. 6); "no salvation outside the Church" in its setting; the rebaptism dispute with Stephen and Firmilian's letter |
| 2.5 | Clement of Alexandria and Origen | Explain the Alexandrian synthesis and read Origen knowing how his texts reached us | philosophy as a preparation for Christ (Clement, *Stromata*); Origen's *On First Principles* and Rufinus's avowedly adjusted Latin; eternal generation; the restoration of all things, and whether Origen taught what the sixth-century anathemas condemned; the Hexapla |

**Boss problem 2:** Supplied passages: Irenaeus, *Against Heresies* 3.3.1–3 (ANF), Tertullian, *Prescription against Heretics* 32 (ANF), and both versions of Cyprian, *On the Unity of the Catholic Church* ch. 4 (the lesson's own translation of the two Latin texts). (a) Reconstruct Irenaeus's argument against the Gnostics in numbered premises and say which premise Tertullian's chapter 32 restates. (b) Close-read 3.3.2: state the two main ways translators render the key verb and "more powerful pre-eminence", and what each rendering makes the passage claim about the Roman church. Say what the Greek original's loss does to certainty. (c) List every difference between the two versions of Cyprian's chapter 4, and state how Catholic readers and Orthodox or Protestant readers each use the passage. (d) Tertullian ended outside the Great Church and Origen's name was later condemned. In 150 words, any verdict, say what a writer's later standing does to the evidential value of his earlier testimony to tradition, using the criteria of 1.1. What the texts prove about primacy is argued in `apologetics-catholic-claims`. Parts (a)–(c) are exegetical; (d) is evaluative.

### Module 3: The fourth-century East: doctrine, poetry, and preaching (c. 318-407)

The Nicene Fathers argue from salvation to the full divinity of the Son and the Spirit; the Syriac Church does theology in hymns; Antioch answers Alexandria on how to read the Bible; and Chrysostom preaches it.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3.1 | Athanasius | Reconstruct Athanasius's argument from salvation to the Son's full divinity | *On the Incarnation*: the divine dilemma, the Word restoring the image, "he became man that we might be made God" (54); *Orations against the Arians* and *On the Decrees*: "there was when he was not", why *homoousios*; deification; Nicene doctrine as defined in `trinity-and-god`, events in [`church-history`](../church-history/syllabus.md) 2.1 |
| 3.2 | Basil the Great | Read *On the Holy Spirit* as an argument from worship and tradition | the doxology controversy ("with" vs "through" the Spirit); honour and nature; unwritten tradition and its examples (ch. 27); *ousia* vs *hypostasis*; the attribution question over Letter 38 (Basil or Gregory of Nyssa) |
| 3.3 | Gregory of Nazianzus and Gregory of Nyssa | Follow the Theologian's orations and Nyssa's case that three persons are not three gods | the *Theological Orations* (27–31) and the Spirit's divinity; "what is not assumed is not healed" (Letter 101, against Apollinarius); *To Ablabius* (*Not Three Gods*); apophatic theology and endless ascent (*Life of Moses*); Macrina; Nyssa's universalism as a disputed reading |
| 3.4 | Ephrem and Syriac Christianity | Read theology written as poetry, and say what the Syriac tradition adds | Nisibis and Edessa; Aphrahat; Ephrem's hymns (*On the Nativity*, *On Faith*) and his refusal to "pry" into the Son's generation; types and symbols in nature and Scripture; Doctor of the Church (1920); the Greek works circulated under Ephrem's name |
| 3.5 | Allegory and history: Alexandria and Antioch | Apply Origen's and the Antiochenes' rules to a passage, and weigh the textbook contrast | Origen's body–soul–spirit senses and the "stumbling blocks" that signal a spiritual meaning (*On First Principles* IV); Diodore, Theodore and *theoria*; typology vs allegory; Frances Young's case that the contrast is overdrawn; the four senses as Catholic teaching cited from [`old-testament`](../old-testament/syllabus.md) 1.2, the method read here in the Fathers' own texts |
| 3.6 | John Chrysostom | Read the golden-mouthed preacher for his exegesis, his ethics of wealth, and his worst pages | Antiochene homilies on Matthew and on Paul; *On the Priesthood*; wealth, the poor, and Christ in the poor; the Eucharist in the homilies; the homilies against Judaizing Christians read plainly, with their later use; exile (events ceded) |

**Boss problem 3:** Supplied passages: Athanasius, *On the Incarnation* 54, and Gregory of Nazianzus, Letter 101 to Cledonius (NPNF); Basil, *On the Holy Spirit* 27 (NPNF); Origen, *On First Principles* Book IV, the passage on the planting of paradise and God walking in the garden (ANF). (a) Reconstruct the argument pattern shared by Athanasius 54 and Letter 101 in numbered premises, and state the conclusion each draws against his opponent (Arius; Apollinarius). (b) Close-read Basil ch. 27: list the unwritten traditions he names, and say what he uses them to establish about the Spirit. (c) Catholic and Orthodox readers cite ch. 27 for Tradition alongside Scripture; Protestant readers answer that Basil's examples are liturgical customs and his doctrine still rests on Scripture. State each reading and the words it must explain. (d) Apply Origen's criterion to Genesis 2–3, then say how an Antiochene reader would handle the same verses. In 300 words, any verdict, evaluate whether the two methods differ in principle or only in degree, meeting Young's argument (3.5). Parts (a)–(c) are exegetical; (d) is evaluative.

### Module 4: The Latin Doctors: Ambrose, Jerome, Augustine (c. 374-430)

The West's great bishops and scholar: sacramental catechesis in Milan, the Bible in Latin from the Hebrew, and Augustine's theology of grace and the Church, forged in two controversies.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4.1 | Ambrose of Milan | Read Ambrose's catecheses on the sacraments and his mediation of Greek theology to the West | *On the Mysteries* (baptism, anointing, the Eucharist and the power of Christ's word, ch. 9); Ambrose as reader of Origen and Basil; the hymns; Ambrose and Theodosius ceded to [`church-history`](../church-history/syllabus.md) 2.1; Ambrosiaster (not Ambrose) |
| 4.2 | Jerome | Say what Jerome did for the Bible and what he held about the Hebrew canon | the Vulgate and *Hebraica veritas*; Jerome's translation principles in his prefaces (his canon position and the dispute with Augustine are owned by [`old-testament`](../old-testament/syllabus.md) 6.1, the canon as defined by [`fundamental-theology`](../fundamental-theology/syllabus.md) 3.3); Letter 53 on studying Scripture; "ignorance of Scripture is ignorance of Christ" (*Commentary on Isaiah*, prologue); *Against Helvidius*; the quarrels with Rufinus and Augustine |
| 4.3 | Augustine: *Confessions* | Read *Confessions* as a theology of grace and conversion | the restless heart (1.1); sin and the pears; the Platonists and the Word made flesh (Book 7); the garden at Milan (Book 8); "give what you command, and command what you will" (10.29) and Pelagius's reaction; the philosophy of Books 10–11 ceded to `ancient-medieval-philosophy` |
| 4.4 | Augustine against the Donatists | Reconstruct Augustine's theology of the Church and sacraments against the Donatists | the schism's origin ceded to [`church-history`](../church-history/syllabus.md) 1.4; the Church as a mixed body until the harvest; Christ as the true minister, valid but fruitless sacraments (*On Baptism*); "the whole world judges securely" and Newman; the coercion letters (93, 185) and Augustine's change of mind, read plainly |
| 4.5 | Augustine against the Pelagians | Find the crux between Augustine and Pelagius, and trace how Augustine's position hardened | Pelagius on nature, law and example; *On the Spirit and the Letter*, *On Nature and Grace*; original sin and Romans 5:12 in the Latin; prevenient and operative grace; the late works on predestination and perseverance, and the Gallic reaction (Cassian, Prosper); Sermon 131 versus "Roma locuta"; doctrine and its authority ceded to `creation-grace-last-things` |

**Boss problem 4:** Supplied passages (NPNF): *Confessions* 10.29; the passage of *On the Gift of Perseverance* where Augustine reports Pelagius's reaction to it; a passage of *On Baptism, against the Donatists* on sacraments given outside the Church; Letter 93 to Vincentius on why Augustine changed his mind about coercion; and the close of Sermon 131 (the lesson's own translation, since Schaff's sermon selection numbers and selects differently). (a) Reconstruct the objection Pelagius is reported to have made and Augustine's reply in numbered premises, and name the crux. (b) Reconstruct *On Baptism*'s distinction between a sacrament's validity and its fruit, and say which Donatist premise it denies. (c) Close-read Sermon 131's close: what does Augustine say was sent to the apostolic see and what came back, and what does the slogan "Roma locuta, causa finita" add or drop? State how Catholic and non-Catholic readers each use the passage. (d) In 300 words, any verdict, evaluate Augustine's case for coercing the Donatists in Letter 93: state the grounds he gives (his experience, and his reading of Scripture), the strongest objection a contemporary could have made from his own earlier view, and whether his theology of the mixed Church requires, permits or tells against it. Parts (a)–(c) are exegetical; (d) is evaluative.

### Module 5: The Christological Fathers and the close of the patristic age (c. 428-750)

Cyril and Leo argue toward Chalcedon, Gregory the Great pastors a ruined West, and Maximus and John of Damascus gather the Greek tradition; the course closes on what it means to appeal to "the Fathers" at all.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5.1 | Cyril of Alexandria | Find the crux between Cyril and Nestorius and trace Cyril's key formula to its source | *Theotokos*; the Second and Third Letters to Nestorius and the Twelve Anathemas; the communication of idioms; "one incarnate nature of God the Word" and its origin in Apollinarian writings circulated under Athanasius's name; the Formula of Reunion (433); events ceded to [`church-history`](../church-history/syllabus.md) 2.2, doctrine to `christology` |
| 5.2 | Leo the Great | Read the Tome as an answer to Eutyches, and Leo's sermons on Peter as texts | the Tome (Letter 28): two natures, one person, and the clause on each form acting with the other (Feltoe's NPNF: "each form does what is proper to it with the co-operation of the other"; Latin *cum alterius communione*); Cyril and Leo compared; the reception of the Tome at Chalcedon; the anniversary sermons on Peter's see and how Catholic and Orthodox readers take them; canon 28 ceded to [`church-history`](../church-history/syllabus.md) Boss 2 |
| 5.3 | Gregory the Great | Read Gregory as pastor, exegete and bishop of Rome | the *Pastoral Rule*; the *Moralia on Job* and moral exegesis; the letters against the title "ecumenical patriarch" and how each tradition reads them; "servant of the servants of God"; the *Dialogues* and the disputed challenge to their authenticity (Clark); Isidore as the Western close |
| 5.4 | Maximus the Confessor and John of Damascus | Say what the last Greek Fathers argued and handed on | the Dionysian corpus and its false apostolic attribution; Maximus on two natural wills and deification (paraphrased); John of Damascus, *Exposition of the Orthodox Faith* as a summary of the Greek Fathers; *On the Divine Images* and the defence of icons; Christians under Muslim rule ceded to [`church-history`](../church-history/syllabus.md) 3.2 |

**Boss problem 5:** (a) Supplied passages (NPNF): Cyril's Second Letter to Nestorius and the "each form" sentence with its context from Leo's Tome (quote Feltoe's NPNF wording, "with the co-operation of the other", and note the Latin *cum alterius communione*; the familiar "in communion with the other" is a different rendering). State what each affirms about the one subject of Christ's acts and about the natures, and name the crux a miaphysite reader sees between them and the claim a Chalcedonian reader makes that they agree. (b) Cyril used "one incarnate nature of God the Word" believing it Athanasius's. Say where it came from, and what the misattribution does and does not do to the formula's standing (1.1, 5.1). (c) Supplied passages: Gregory the Great's letters on the title "universal" or "ecumenical" (NPNF). State the Catholic reading, which sees a refusal of a title that would unchurch other bishops while Gregory exercises Roman primacy, and the Orthodox and Protestant reading, which sees a denial of any universal jurisdiction. Give the words each leans on. (d) Capstone, 400–500 words, any verdict: is there a "unanimous consent of the Fathers", in the sense of Trent Session IV, on the meaning of "rock" in Matthew 16:18? Gather at least five patristic readings from across the course (including Augustine's own remark in the *Retractations* that he had explained it two ways and left the reader to choose), classify them, say what "unanimous" must mean for the criterion to be usable (numerical, moral, on the literal sense or on the doctrine), and state what follows if the readings diverge while all are compatible with one doctrine. The argument over primacy itself belongs to `apologetics-catholic-claims`. Parts (a)–(c) are exegetical; (d) is evaluative.

## Sources of truth

- Philip Schaff et al. (eds.), *Ante-Nicene Fathers* and *Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers*, first and second series: the course's default translations for every close-reading passage; Migne's *Patrologia Graeca* and *Latina* for the original where a word is the point
- Johannes Quasten, *Patrology* (with the continuation edited by Angelo Di Berardino): the reference for attributions, dates and authenticity; *Clavis Patrum Graecorum* and *Clavis Patrum Latinorum* for doubtful and spurious works
- Robert Louis Wilken, *The Spirit of Early Christian Thought*, and Boniface Ramsey, *Beginning to Read the Fathers*: reading the Fathers from within the tradition
- Frances Young, *Biblical Exegesis and the Formation of Christian Culture* (patristic exegesis); Lewis Ayres, *Nicaea and its Legacy* (the fourth-century arguments); Peter Brown, *Augustine of Hippo* (Augustine's development); *The Oxford Dictionary of the Christian Church* for dates

## Notes

- **Lesson count.** The roadmap estimated ~20; the syllabus runs 24 (+20%, within 25%). The steering list names about twenty Fathers plus two method topics (the weight of the Fathers, patristic exegesis), and each lesson has to carry a primary text to read closely, so one Father per lesson is the natural unit. Merges already made to hold the line: Clement of Rome with the Didache; Clement of Alexandria with Origen; the two Gregories in one lesson; Athanasius in one lesson; Maximus with John of Damascus; the consensus question as the last boss problem rather than its own lesson. Irenaeus and Augustine get more than one lesson because the roadmap blurb and the rest of the field lean on them hardest. If a lesson overflows when built, split it and merge elsewhere rather than grow past 25.
- **Attribution discipline.** Every quotation in a lesson is checked against Schaff (or the named translation) before publishing, with the work and chapter given. The reference card keeps a **spurious and garbled quotations** table (at least: "credo quia absurdum", "Roma locuta, causa finita", "in essentials unity" (seventeenth-century, not Augustine), the long recension of Ignatius, 2 Clement, *Barnabas*, the Pseudo-Clementines, the Athanasian Creed, the Dionysian corpus, the Apollinarian forgeries, Ambrosiaster, the Greek Ephrem), each with the real source where one is known. Where authenticity is disputed among scholars rather than settled (the middle recension of Ignatius, Basil's Letter 38, Gregory's *Dialogues*, the date of *On the Incarnation*), the lesson says so and gives the majority view as a majority view. Dates for 1 Clement, the Didache and Ignatius are given as ranges.
- **Authority discipline.** An individual Father's opinion is never presented as Church teaching; a Doctor's title is not an endorsement of every opinion. Lessons mark where a Father held a view the Church did not receive (Irenaeus's millenarianism, Cyprian on rebaptism, Nyssa's universalism if he held it, late Augustine on predestination beyond what Orange II adopted), and they give doctrinal levels only by citing [`fundamental-theology`](../fundamental-theology/syllabus.md) 4.4 and the owning dogmatic course.
- **Honesty rule.** Chrysostom's homilies against Judaizing Christians, the anti-Jewish passages elsewhere, and Augustine's case for coercion are read as they stand, with their later use named, before any context or defence is offered.
- **Ownership collisions resolved.** (1) [`history-of-political-thought`](../history-of-political-thought/syllabus.md)'s scope table cedes "Augustine's theology of grace and his controversies" to this course, while the field plan gives grace and Pelagianism as doctrine to `creation-grace-last-things`. The split: this course reads the controversial texts and Augustine's arguments (4.4–4.5); the doctrine, Orange II, Trent and the later disputes, with authority levels, stay there. Augustine's coercion letters are claimed by neither political course and are read here as ecclesiology. (2) [`church-history`](../church-history/syllabus.md) cedes "the Fathers read as theologians, in primary texts" to this course and keeps the Fathers as actors and as sources (Eusebius, the *Life of Antony*); this course therefore does not teach Eusebius or monastic history. (3) [`fundamental-theology`](../fundamental-theology/syllabus.md) owns Vincent of Lérins (5.1) and the canon as defined (3.3); 1.1 and 4.2 cite them. (4) [`old-testament`](../old-testament/syllabus.md) owns the canon's formation including Jerome vs Augustine (6.1, Boss 6) and teaches the four senses with a first look at Origen and the Antiochenes (1.2); [`new-testament`](../new-testament/syllabus.md) owns the Fathers as witnesses to authorship and canon (1.5, 6.6). Both cede the Fathers as authors and theologians here. So 3.5 assumes OT 1.2 and goes deeper into the Fathers' own texts and the historiographical dispute, and 4.2 cites OT 6.1 rather than re-teaching Jerome's canon. Jerome's prefaces and the canon therefore drop out of the apologetic-texts list below. No built lessons in the library cover patristic topics.
- **Where this goes next.** `trinity-and-god` takes this course as a prerequisite for 3.1–3.3 (and Tertullian's vocabulary, 2.3); `christology` builds on 3.3, 5.1–5.2 and 5.4; `creation-grace-last-things` on 4.3–4.5; `apologetics-catholic-claims` on the contested passages gathered in the reference card (Ignatius, Irenaeus 3.3.2, Cyprian ch. 4, Basil ch. 27, Leo's sermons, Gregory's letters, the "rock" readings of Boss 5); `sacraments-and-liturgy` on the Didache, Justin and Ambrose (1.2, 1.4, 4.1); `ecclesiology-and-mariology` on Ignatius, Irenaeus and Augustine against the Donatists.
