# Public Economics — Syllabus

> Economics & Finance · Tier 2 · ~26 lessons · Prereqs: [`grad-micro`](../grad-micro/syllabus.md) · Roadmap id: `public-economics`

## Goal

Learn what government should do when markets fail or when society wants a different distribution than markets deliver — and what it costs to do it. The course runs on one question asked eight ways: *where is the wedge, who bears it, and is the cure cheaper than the disease?* It takes the market failures [`grad-micro`](../grad-micro/syllabus.md) introduced (public goods 6.4, externalities 6.3, adverse selection 5.1 and 5.3) and turns them into policy problems; then builds the theory of taxation from incidence and excess burden up through Ramsey, Diamond–Mirrlees, Mirrlees and the Saez formula, into capital taxation, social insurance, targeted redistribution, and the assignment of all of this across levels of government. You will finish able to say who really pays a tax in partial and general equilibrium, price the deadweight loss of raising one more dollar, choose between a carbon tax and a permit market under uncertainty, write down and sign an optimal tax formula from a handful of sufficient statistics, and derive the Baily–Chetty rule for unemployment insurance. The course is positive and technical: social welfare weights are *inputs* to its formulas, and which weights are right is cited out to `philosophy-of-economics` and [`political-philosophy`](../political-philosophy/syllabus.md). Deliberately skipped: the political determination of taxes and transfers (Meltzer–Richard and lobbying stay in [`political-economy`](../political-economy/syllabus.md)), public debt dynamics and Ricardian equivalence ([`grad-macro`](../grad-macro/syllabus.md) 3.4; `economics-of-debt`), stabilization policy, health economics beyond the adverse-selection core, tax administration and evasion, and the econometrics of estimating the elasticities these formulas need (used, with their estimation cited to [`econometrics`](../econometrics/syllabus.md)).

## Dangerous Checklist

When you finish, you can:

- [ ] Derive the Samuelson condition with income effects and explain why voluntary provision is neutral to redistribution among contributors
- [ ] Explain why no budget-balanced mechanism elicits public-good valuations with dominant-strategy truth-telling and voluntary participation, and name what each escape gives up
- [ ] Compute the incidence of a tax from supply and demand elasticities, and say how general equilibrium (Harberger) changes the answer for a corporate or capital tax
- [ ] Measure excess burden with compensated demand, compute a Harberger triangle, and compute the marginal cost of public funds from a revenue schedule
- [ ] Choose between an emissions tax and a cap-and-trade market using Weitzman's slope rule, and compute the expected welfare difference
- [ ] Derive the Ramsey rule, apply the inverse-elasticity special case, and state when Diamond–Mirrlees says not to tax intermediate goods
- [ ] Set up the Mirrlees problem as screening, solve a two-type case, and explain "no distortion at the top"
- [ ] Compute an optimal top marginal tax rate from the Saez formula and say what each sufficient statistic measures
- [ ] Explain Atkinson–Stiglitz and Chamley–Judd, compute the compounding wedge a capital-income tax puts on future consumption, and sign the savings wedge from the inverse Euler equation
- [ ] Draw the Einav–Finkelstein–Cullen diagram for an insurance market, locate the welfare loss from adverse selection, and price a mandate or subsidy that removes it
- [ ] Derive the Baily–Chetty formula and use it to judge whether unemployment benefits are too high or too low
- [ ] Analyze Tiebout sorting, a matching vs lump-sum grant, and horizontal tax competition between jurisdictions

## Modules

### Module 1: Public goods

Grad-micro showed the market underprovides a shared good; this module asks how much *should* be provided, why private giving can't fix it, and how a government could ever learn what people want.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1.1 | The Samuelson rule beyond quasilinearity | State the efficiency condition in general equilibrium and see when it depends on who is rich | $\sum_i \mathrm{MRS}_i = \mathrm{MRT}$, distribution-dependence of $G^*$ with income effects, impure and club goods, congestion |
| 1.2 | Voluntary provision and crowding out | Predict what private giving does when government provides or redistributes | Bergstrom–Blume–Varian contributor set, Warr neutrality, one-for-one crowd-out, warm glow (Andreoni) |
| 1.3 | Revealing demand for public goods | Know every route to eliciting valuations and what each one sacrifices | Lindahl manipulability, pivot mechanism and its budget problem (cited), expected-externality (d'Aspremont–Gérard-Varet) mechanism, Bowen median-voter provision |

**Boss problem 1:** Three residents have quasilinear utility $u_i = x_i + \theta_i \ln G$ with $\theta = (2, 4, 12)$, and the public good costs $c = 2$ per unit. (a) Find the Samuelson-efficient $G^*$ and the Lindahl prices that support it. (b) Find the Nash equilibrium of voluntary contributions and identify the contributor set. (c) With equal cost shares, find each resident's preferred $G$ and the majority-vote (Bowen) outcome; state the condition under which Bowen provision is efficient and why it fails here. (d) Compute the welfare loss of the voting and Nash outcomes relative to $G^*$. (e) Use neutrality to predict what happens to $G$ if the government taxes the largest contributor one unit and gives it to the smallest.

### Module 2: Tax incidence and excess burden

Who pays a tax is not who writes the check, and what a tax costs is not what it raises.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2.1 | Partial-equilibrium incidence | Split a tax between buyers and sellers from elasticities alone | $dp^d/dt = \varepsilon_S/(\varepsilon_S - \varepsilon_D)$, statutory irrelevance, unit vs ad valorem, overshifting under monopoly |
| 2.2 | General-equilibrium incidence: the Harberger model | Trace a sector-specific capital tax through factor markets | two-sector two-factor model, output vs factor-substitution effect, corporate tax as a partial factor tax, open-economy shifting to labor |
| 2.3 | Excess burden and the Harberger triangle | Measure what a tax destroys, correctly | equivalent/compensating variation, compensated demand, $\mathrm{DWL} \approx \tfrac12 \tau^2 \varepsilon\, pq$, why a lump-sum tax has none |
| 2.4 | Many distortions: second best and the marginal cost of public funds | Price the last dollar of revenue and correct the Samuelson rule for it | Lipsey–Lancaster, taxes in related markets, marginal excess burden, MCPF and the modified Samuelson rule |

**Boss problem 2:** A market has demand $q = 150 - 5p$ and supply $q = 15p - 50$. (a) Compute the pre-tax equilibrium and the demand and supply elasticities there, and predict the consumer share of a small unit tax from the incidence formula. (b) Impose a unit tax $t = 1$ and then $t = 2$: find buyer and seller prices, revenue, and deadweight loss in each case, and confirm the elasticity prediction. (c) Compute the marginal excess burden of raising $t$ from 1 to 2 and the implied MCPF, and explain why it exceeds the average burden. (d) By how much must the sum of marginal benefits of a public good exceed its marginal cost before it is worth financing at the margin with this tax? (e) Qualitatively: if the good is produced with internationally mobile capital and the tax is levied on that capital, who bears it in the Harberger model?

### Module 3: Choosing an externality instrument

Grad-micro fixed an externality with a Pigouvian tax in a world of certainty and no other taxes; real policy has neither.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3.1 | Taxes, standards, and tradable permits | Show why market instruments abate at least cost and a uniform standard does not | equal marginal abatement cost, Montgomery's permit equilibrium, allocation irrelevance (Coase, cited), auctioned vs grandfathered permits |
| 3.2 | Prices vs quantities | Pick the instrument when abatement costs are uncertain | Weitzman's slope rule, expected welfare gap, flow vs stock pollutants, hybrids (price collars, safety valves) |
| 3.3 | Pigou in a second-best world | Set an environmental tax when the revenue side is already distorted | Sandmo additivity, revenue recycling, tax-interaction effect, weak vs strong double dividend |

**Boss problem 3:** Two firms must jointly abate $A = 40$ units, with marginal abatement costs $\mathrm{MAC}_1 = 2a_1$ and $\mathrm{MAC}_2 = 6a_2$. (a) Find the least-cost split and the tax that achieves it; show a permit market reaches the same split whatever the initial allocation. (b) Compute the total abatement cost under a uniform standard of 20 each and the saving from the market instrument. (c) Aggregate marginal abatement cost has slope $C = 1.5$ and an additive shock with standard deviation $\sigma = 12$ that the regulator can't observe; marginal damage falls with abatement at slope $D = 0.5$. Using Weitzman, which instrument is better and by how much in expected welfare? (d) Which way does the answer flip if damages are a stock whose marginal damage is nearly flat in one year's emissions — and how would recycling the tax revenue through cuts in a labor tax change the optimal rate?

### Module 4: Optimal commodity taxation

If lump-sum taxes are off the table, which goods should carry the load?

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4.1 | The Ramsey rule | Minimize excess burden subject to a revenue requirement | Ramsey problem, equiproportionate reduction in compensated demand, inverse-elasticity rule, the equity objection |
| 4.2 | Equity and complementarity: many-person Ramsey and Corlett–Hague | Add distributional weights and untaxable leisure to the rule | Diamond's many-person Ramsey rule, distributional characteristic, Corlett–Hague (tax leisure complements), when uniform rates are optimal |
| 4.3 | Production efficiency: Diamond–Mirrlees | Decide whether to tax inputs, trade, or public production | production efficiency theorem, no taxes on intermediate goods, pure-profit condition, producer prices for public cost–benefit analysis |

**Boss problem 4:** A consumer has quasilinear utility generating independent demands $x_i = 100\, q_i^{-\varepsilon_i}$ with $\varepsilon_1 = 0.5$, $\varepsilon_2 = 2$, producer prices equal to 1, and consumer prices $q_i = 1 + t_i$. The government must raise 40. (a) Write the Ramsey problem and show the optimum satisfies $\varepsilon_i\, t_i/(1+t_i)$ equal across goods. (b) Solve numerically for $t_1, t_2$ and the resulting deadweight loss. (c) Find the uniform rate raising 40 and its deadweight loss, and state the percentage saving from Ramsey. (d) Suppose good 1 is a necessity bought mostly by the poor: explain in terms of Diamond's rule which way distributional weights move $t_1$. (e) A firm uses good 2 as an input to produce good 1: by Diamond–Mirrlees, should the input be taxed, and what assumption would you need to check?

### Module 5: Optimal income taxation

The government can see earnings but not ability or effort — so every redistributive tax is a screening problem.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5.1 | The linear income tax and the equity–efficiency trade-off | Derive the optimal flat rate paired with a universal grant | demogrant plus flat tax, social marginal welfare weights $g_i$, $\tau = (1-\bar g)/(1-\bar g + e)$, the revenue-maximizing (Laffer) rate $1/(1+e)$ |
| 5.2 | The Mirrlees problem | Set up nonlinear taxation as mechanism design and solve two types | unobservable skill, incentive compatibility and single crossing (screening, cited), no distortion at the top, why bounded skill implies a zero top rate |
| 5.3 | The Saez formula | Compute an optimal top rate from observable statistics | mechanical, behavioral and welfare effects of a perturbation, Pareto parameter $a$, elasticity of taxable income, $\tau = (1-g)/(1-g+ae)$ |
| 5.4 | The bottom of the schedule: participation and the EITC | Decide how transfers should phase in and out | intensive vs extensive margin (Saez 2002), negative participation tax rates, benefit phase-outs and the poverty trap, negative income tax vs in-work credit |

**Boss problem 5:** (a) With $\bar g = 0.6$ and $e = 0.4$, find the optimal linear tax rate and compare it with the revenue-maximizing rate. (b) Top incomes have Pareto parameter $a = 1.5$ and elasticity of taxable income $e = 0.25$; compute the optimal top rate for $g = 0$ and for $g = 0.2$, and say what empirical design would estimate $e$. (c) Two equally numerous types have wages $w_L = 1$, $w_H = 2$ and utility $c - \tfrac12 (y/w)^2$ over consumption and pre-tax income $y$. A maximin government maximizes the low type's utility subject to budget balance and incentive compatibility. Identify the binding constraint, solve for both bundles, verify no distortion at the top, and compute the low type's implicit marginal tax rate. (d) Check that the low type does not want the high type's bundle, and explain why the Saez top-rate formula does not contradict the zero top rate of (c).

### Module 6: Capital taxation

Taxing savings is taxing future consumption — and the theory says that is either a mistake, a necessity, or both.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 6.1 | Atkinson–Stiglitz: should savings be taxed at all? | Show when a good nonlinear income tax makes commodity and savings taxes redundant | weak separability from labor, uniform commodity taxation, savings as future consumption, heterogeneity in thrift correlated with skill as the exception |
| 6.2 | Chamley–Judd and the exploding wedge | See why a constant capital tax is a rising tax on the future | compounding consumption wedge, zero long-run capital tax in the Ramsey growth model (cited), the capital levy and time inconsistency, the Straub–Werning critique |
| 6.3 | New dynamic public finance: the inverse Euler equation | Find a positive savings wedge once skills are risky and private | idiosyncratic skill shocks, inverse Euler equation, Jensen's inequality and the savings wedge, implementation limits |

**Boss problem 6:** (a) A 25 percent tax on interest income with a 5 percent pre-tax return: compute the implicit tax on consumption 1 year and 30 years ahead, and connect the growth of that wedge to the Chamley–Judd result. (b) State Atkinson–Stiglitz for a two-period life-cycle model and name the separability condition that delivers a zero tax on savings. (c) A worker has log utility, $\beta R = 1$, and next-period consumption of 1 or 3 with equal probability under the optimal allocation. Find current consumption implied by the inverse Euler equation and by the standard Euler equation, and compute the implicit savings tax rate that makes the inverse-Euler allocation the agent's own choice. (d) Explain in one paragraph why the planner wants to discourage saving here even though (b) said not to tax it.

### Module 7: Social insurance and the welfare state

Private insurance markets unravel, public insurance blunts incentives, and transfers must find the needy without rewarding the mimic.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 7.1 | Adverse selection as a policy problem | Measure the welfare loss in an insurance market and price the fix | demand, average-cost and marginal-cost curves (Einav–Finkelstein–Cullen), equilibrium vs efficient coverage, mandates and subsidies, the positive-correlation test |
| 7.2 | Optimal unemployment insurance: Baily–Chetty | Judge a benefit level from a consumption drop and a duration elasticity | $\frac{u'(c_u)-u'(c_e)}{u'(c_e)} = \varepsilon_{1-e,b}/e$, envelope-theorem derivation, sufficient statistics, liquidity vs moral hazard (Chetty 2008) |
| 7.3 | Targeting transfers: tagging, ordeals, and in-kind benefits | Design redistribution that screens on something other than income | tagging (Akerlof), ordeals and self-targeting (Nichols–Zeckhauser), in-kind vs cash, take-up and stigma |

**Boss problem 7:** (a) In an insurance market with unit mass of buyers, willingness to pay of the $s$-th buyer is $120 - 60s$ and that buyer's expected cost is $110 - 80s$. Derive the average-cost curve, find the competitive equilibrium coverage and price, the efficient coverage, and the welfare loss. (b) Compute the premium under a full mandate and the per-contract subsidy that makes full coverage a competitive equilibrium. (c) A worker's employment probability is $e = 0.95$, the elasticity of the unemployment rate with respect to benefits is 0.5, relative risk aversion is 2, and consumption falls 10 percent on job loss. Derive the Baily–Chetty condition in a one-period model with a balanced-budget payroll tax, then decide whether benefits should rise or fall, using both the exact CRRA left-hand side and the $\gamma\, \Delta c/c$ approximation. (d) Find the consumption drop at which benefits would be optimal under each version. (e) Explain why a large response of duration to severance pay would change your verdict.

### Module 8: Fiscal federalism

Which level of government should do what — and what happens when jurisdictions compete for residents and capital.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 8.1 | Tiebout: voting with your feet | See when mobility produces efficient local public goods, and when it fails | Tiebout sorting, capitalization into house prices, fiscal zoning (Hamilton), Bewley's critique |
| 8.2 | Assignment, spillovers, and grants | Decide what to decentralize and how the center should pay for it | Oates decentralization theorem, interjurisdictional spillovers, matching vs lump-sum grants, the flypaper effect |
| 8.3 | Tax competition | Show why competing jurisdictions tax mobile capital too little | Zodrow–Mieszkowski–Wilson model, fiscal externalities, race to the bottom vs Leviathan restraint, source vs residence taxation |

**Boss problem 8:** A community with income 100 has utility $\ln x + \ln G$ over private consumption and a local public good priced at 1. (a) Compute $G$ and utility after a lump-sum grant of 20. (b) Find the matching rate (share of $G$ paid by the center) whose grant costs the center exactly 20 at the community's chosen $G$; compute $G$ and utility, and explain why the community prefers the lump sum. (c) Standard theory predicts how much of the lump-sum grant goes to $G$; state that number and what the flypaper effect finds instead. (d) Half of the marginal benefit of $G$ now spills over to a neighboring town: state the Pigouvian matching rate and why a lump-sum grant cannot correct the spillover. (e) Two identical towns tax mobile capital to fund $G$: explain the fiscal externality that drives both below the efficient $G$, and how the conclusion changes if governments are revenue-maximizing Leviathans.

## Sources of truth

- Salanié, *The Economics of Taxation* (optimal commodity, income and capital taxation at the right level of rigor)
