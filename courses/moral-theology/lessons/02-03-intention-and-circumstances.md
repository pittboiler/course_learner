# Moral Theology · Lesson 2.3: Intention and circumstances

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 2: Human acts and conscience · Builds on: [2.1 The voluntary](02-01-the-voluntary.md), [2.2 The moral object](02-02-the-moral-object.md) · Unlocks: [2.4 Conscience: its nature and its errors](02-04-conscience-its-nature-and-its-errors.md), [4.2 Double effect and cooperation as Catholic method](04-02-double-effect-and-cooperation-as-catholic-method.md)

## Why this matters

Catholic moral theology gives intention more weight than almost any rival system. Aquinas says the end is the *form* of the act, and Jesus judges the heart before the deed (Mt 6:1–18). Yet the same tradition says flatly that the end does not justify the means. Those two claims look like they pull against each other, and a lot of bad pastoral reasoning lives in that gap. This lesson closes it. It also finishes the three fonts, so from here you can take any act apart.

## The idea

Every human act has two sides. On the inside, the will goes out to an **end**: *why* I act. On the outside, there is a deed bearing on some matter: *what* I do, which is the [moral object](../reference.md#the-moral-object) of [2.2](02-02-the-moral-object.md). Around both sit the **circumstances**: who, where, when, how much, by what means.

Now an asymmetry that does all the work. Think of a bridge. It stands only if every span holds, and it falls if any one span fails. Moral goodness is like that. An act is good only if object, end and circumstances are *all* good. It is evil if *any one* of them is bad. So:

- A good end cannot rescue a bad object. Stealing to feed the poor is still stealing.
- A bad end spoils a good object. Almsgiving done to be admired is spoiled.
- Most circumstances make an act better or worse by degree. A few are serious enough to change *what kind* of act it is.

That asymmetry is the whole of "the end does not justify the means." Intention is not weak here. It is one of three conditions that must all hold at once.

## Source

Augustine, *Against Lying* (to Consentius, c. 420), §18, *NPNF* first series vol. 3. Aquinas cites this part of the work (as ch. 7) at ST I-II q.20 a.2, and *Veritatis Splendor* 81 quotes the passage:

> It does indeed make very much difference, for what cause, with what end, with what intention a thing be done: but those things which are clearly sins, are upon no plea of a good cause, with no seeming good end, no alleged good intention, to be done. Those works, namely of men, which are not in themselves sins, are now good, now evil, according as their causes are good or evil; as, to give food to a poor man is a good work, if it be done because of pity, with right faith … as, if for boasting sake a poor man is fed …

Read it slowly. **"Very much difference"** is a real concession: intention matters a great deal. **"Clearly sins … in themselves"** marks off a class of acts that intention cannot touch. For every other act, **"now good, now evil, according as their causes"** hands the verdict to the end. **"For boasting sake"** is the case the Catechism would reuse sixteen centuries later (CCC 1753). The context matters too: Consentius wanted to pose as a heretic in order to catch Priscillianists. Augustine's answer is that you cannot unmask lies by lying.

## The argument

Aquinas builds this in ST I-II q.18 aa.4–11 and q.20 aa.1–2. Reload [2.1](02-01-the-voluntary.md) first: an act is moral only insofar as it is voluntary.

1. **A voluntary act has two acts in it, each with its own object.** The interior act of the will has the end as its object. The exterior act has as its object the thing it bears on (q.18 a.6).
2. **The will is formal and the deed material.** The will uses the limbs as instruments, and the exterior act is moral only because it is willed. So the act takes its species formally from the end and materially from the object of the exterior deed. Aquinas cites Aristotle (*Ethics* V.2): "he who steals that he may commit adultery, is strictly speaking, more adulterer than thief" (a.6). *In words:* what you are really doing is what you are doing it *for*. See [the end as form of the act](../reference.md#the-end-as-form-of-the-act).
3. **When the deed is not of itself ordered to the end, the act falls under two unrelated species at once.** Theft does not of itself lead to adultery, so the thief-adulterer commits two malices in a single act (a.7). *In words:* form is not substitution. The end adds a species; it does not erase the one the deed already has.
4. **Good needs the whole cause; evil follows from any single defect.** Aquinas takes this from Dionysius (a.4 ad 3). Applied to the will (q.20 a.2): if the will is good both in the act it wills and in its end, the deed is good. A good intention alone is not enough. An evil end or an evil act willed makes the whole evil.
5. **Circumstances are accidents of the act**, the classical seven (who, what, where, by what aids, why, how, when; q.7 a.3). Most make an act more or less good or evil without changing its kind. The amount of a theft aggravates it but leaves it theft (q.18 a.11).
6. **But a circumstance with a special relation to reason becomes part of the object and specifies the act.** Stealing from a holy place adds a new repugnance to reason, so the theft is also sacrilege (a.10). *In words:* reason can always look again, and what was background at one look becomes the point at the next. See [circumstances that change species](../reference.md#circumstances-that-change-species).
7. **Therefore the end does not justify the means.** From 3 and 4, no end makes an act good whose object is evil. Paul rejects the slander that he teaches "let us do evil that there may come good" (Rom 3:8, Douay–Rheims). The tradition reads that verse as the scriptural anchor (Augustine, *Against Lying* 1; *Veritatis Splendor* 78; CCC 1756). See [the end does not justify the means](../reference.md#the-end-does-not-justify-the-means).

A careful point about premise 6. CCC 1754 says circumstances cannot of themselves change the moral quality of acts. Its next clause says what it means: they cannot make an evil act good. Aquinas's specifying circumstance does not do that. Sacrilege is a *worse* evil. And by the time a circumstance specifies, Aquinas has folded it into the object. The Catechism also counts **consequences** among the circumstances (CCC 1754; *Veritatis Splendor* 77). That one classification is the seed of the argument against [proportionalism](../reference.md#proportionalism) in [4.3](04-03-proportionalism-and-the-fundamental-option.md).

**Mark the levels.** That the morality of an act comes from object, end and circumstances together, and that a good intention cannot make an act evil by its object good, is **authentic ordinary magisterium**. The Catechism restates it (CCC 1750–1756), and *Veritatis Splendor* 77–82 teaches it at length as constant teaching, resting on Scripture (Rom 3:8) and Augustine. The Catechism is a compendium, so these paragraphs carry the weight of those sources ([fundamental-theology 4.5](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-05-reading-a-documents-weight.md)). The existence of [intrinsically evil acts](../reference.md#intrinsically-evil-acts) is **at least authentic ordinary magisterium**. *Veritatis Splendor* presents it as the tradition's constant teaching, and whether it is also *definitive* is argued ([4.4](04-04-veritatis-splendor.md)). Aquinas's machinery is different. The end as formal, the "twofold malice", the circumstance promoted into the object: this is **common teaching** of the Thomist school. *Veritatis Splendor* 78 commends his analysis as still valid, but no act defines it. Which description is the *true* object in contested cases is **freely disputed** ([4.5](04-05-describing-the-object.md)). Rungs as in [the levels](../reference.md#the-levels-in-moral-theology).

## Worked examples

**Example 1: the tool on clean cases.** Four acts, one font deciding each.

- *A lie told to raise money for a children's hospital.* The object is a lie, disordered in itself (CCC 1753), so the act is evil. The good end lowers the gravity but does not change the verdict.
- *Almsgiving to be praised.* The object is good and the end is vainglory, so the act is evil (q.20 a.1; CCC 1753).
- *Stealing a gold chalice from a church.* This is theft, and the place bears a special relation to reason, so it is also sacrilege: a new species (q.18 a.10).
- *Stealing 20 dollars versus 20,000 dollars from the same person.* Same species, different gravity (a.11).

**Example 2: where it strains.** A starving man with no other remedy takes bread from a bakery. Surely the good end is justifying the means here? Aquinas says no, but his reason is not that the end justifies. He says the act is **not theft**. In manifest and urgent need, with no other remedy, what a man takes to keep himself alive becomes his own by reason of that need (ST II-II q.66 a.7 and ad 2). The goods of the earth are ordered to human need, and private division of property cannot cancel that. So the *object* changes. "Taking what belongs to another" fails, because at that moment the bread is not another's in the relevant sense.

That is the strain. The line between *redescribing the object* and *letting the end justify* is exactly where [proportionalism](../reference.md#proportionalism) presses ([4.3](04-03-proportionalism-and-the-fundamental-option.md)). A proportionalist says the redescription is weighing in disguise: the need outweighs the owner's claim, so the act is right. A Thomist answers that the ground is a claim about what property *is*, one that holds before any weighing and has a fixed threshold: manifest and urgent need, with no other remedy. Both sides must explain why the same move cannot turn a lie into "protective speech". That fight belongs to [4.5](04-05-describing-the-object.md). Here, just notice that the three-fonts rule leaves the question open. The rule says a bad object cannot be rescued. It does not tell you, in every case, which object you have.

## Watch out

- **"The end is the form, so intention decides everything."** Form is not the whole. By premise 3 the end *adds* a species to the deed and never deletes one. Theft for alms is theft *and* almsgiving, which makes it evil.
- **"Circumstances never change the species."** Aquinas says some do (q.18 a.10). What the Catechism denies is that circumstances can make an evil act good (CCC 1754). Keep the two claims apart.
- **Two senses of "intention."** *Veritatis Splendor* 78 calls the object itself the proximate end of the deliberate choice. The *intention* among the three fonts is the further end, what the object is chosen *for*. Mix them up and you can "intend" any object out of existence.
- **Levels, both ways.** Calling Aquinas's theory of species "defined doctrine" overstates, since it is common teaching. Calling "the end does not justify the means" "just Thomism" or "a prudential rule of thumb" understates, since it is authentic ordinary magisterium resting on Rom 3:8, and the Church presents it as constant teaching.
- **Diminished responsibility is not a changed act.** Fear of death can lower imputability (CCC 1754; [2.1](02-01-the-voluntary.md)). It does not turn the object good.

## One-liner

> The end is the form of the act but not the whole of it: good needs object, end and circumstances all at once, and evil needs only one of them to fail, which is why no end justifies an evil means.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢)** *(Exegetical)* For each act, give the verdict (good or evil), name the font that decides it, and say whether any circumstance changes the species or only the gravity. **One sentence each.**

1. A city councillor fasts conspicuously through Lent so that voters will think him devout.
2. A volunteer embezzles 300 dollars from a youth league to pay a neighbour's overdue heating bill.
3. A burglar takes a silver monstrance from a parish sacristy, and on the same night takes an identical weight of silver from a jeweller.
4. A student pays for a classmate's lunch, anonymously, because the classmate cannot afford it.

**P2 (🟡)** *(Exegetical)* ST I-II q.18 a.10, from the reply (English Dominican Province):

> Thus to appropriate another's property is specified by reason of the property being "another's," and in this respect it is placed in the species of theft; and if we consider that action also in its bearing on place or time, then this will be an additional circumstance. But since the reason can direct as to place, time, and the like, it may happen that the condition as to place, in relation to the object, is considered as being in disaccord with reason: for instance, reason forbids damage to be done to a holy place. Consequently to steal from a holy place has an additional repugnance to the order of reason. And thus place, which was first of all considered as a circumstance, is considered here as the principal condition of the object, and as itself repugnant to reason.

(a) Which words show that the circumstance stops being a mere circumstance, and what does it become? (b) CCC 1754 (paraphrased) says circumstances cannot of themselves change the moral quality of acts, and cannot make an evil act good. Does this passage contradict it? **Two sentences per part.**

**P3 (🔴)** *(Exegetical)* An **invented** parish-bulletin column, not the words of any real person:

> "Catholic morality is about the heart, not rule-books. St Thomas himself says the end is the *form* of the act, and the form is what makes a thing what it is. So when a mother tells her dying son the scans came back clear, her act simply *is* an act of love. St Paul condemned only those who do evil for evil's sake. Anyone who calls her a liar has confused the Church's teaching with legalism."

(a) Name the error in the use of q.18 a.6, and the article that corrects it. (b) What does the column do to Rom 3:8? (c) Give the level of the teaching the column's conclusion contradicts, naming the texts that fix it. **150 words or fewer in total.**

<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict:**
1. **Evil. The end decides** (vainglory spoils a good object: q.20 a.1; CCC 1755 on praying and fasting to be seen by others). No circumstance changes the species.
2. **Evil. The object decides** (theft). The good end cannot rescue it (q.20 a.2; CCC 1753). The small amount is a circumstance of gravity only (q.18 a.11). The good end may lower the gravity.
3. **Both evil, by object** (theft). At the sacristy **the place changes the species**: the act is also sacrilege (q.18 a.10). At the jeweller it is theft alone. Same weight, different species.
4. **Good.** Good object, good end, due circumstances: good from the whole cause (q.18 a.4 ad 3). Anonymity is a circumstance that fits the end (Mt 6:3–4).

**Wrong turns:** calling 2 "good because the need was real". That is not the extreme-necessity case of Example 2: an overdue bill is not a manifest and urgent need with no other remedy, and he is embezzling from a trust. (Aquinas does allow taking to succour a neighbour in the same extreme need, q.66 a.7 ad 3, so the third party is not the problem.) Calling the difference in 3 "only more serious". Calling 1 "good but imperfect": an evil end makes the act evil.

**Model answer:** as above, one sentence each.

**P2** *(Exegetical (a) · Exegetical (b))*

**Must hit, strict (a):** the words **"first of all considered as a circumstance, is considered here as the principal condition of the object"**. The place becomes **part of the object**, and so it **specifies** the act (sacrilege). The pivot is **"the reason can direct as to place"**, which gives place "a special relation to reason".

**Must hit, strict (b):** **no contradiction.** CCC 1754 denies that circumstances can make an evil act good, and a.10 does not claim that: it makes theft a worse species. And a.10's specifying condition is no longer a mere circumstance, because it has been taken into the object. What CCC calls circumstances matches a.11's non-specifying kind.

**Wrong turns:** answering "yes, the Catechism corrects Aquinas". Reading "additional circumstance" as the specifying element; it is the *first* look, before reason treats it as principal.

**Model answer:** (a) "Place, which was first of all considered as a circumstance, is considered here as the principal condition of the object": reason, which can direct as to place, treats the holy place as itself repugnant to it, so the place joins the object and makes the theft a sacrilege. (b) There is no contradiction. The Catechism denies that circumstances can make an evil act good, while Aquinas shows one making it worse by being absorbed into the object; the circumstances CCC 1754 has in view are a.11's, which change gravity only.

**P3** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict:** (a) It takes "formal" to mean "the whole". **q.18 a.7** shows that when the deed is not of itself ordered to the end, the act falls under **two disparate species**, a double malice in one act. **q.20 a.2** requires goodness in the act willed as well as in the end. The lie keeps its species. Credit for citing *Against Lying* 18 or CCC 1753. (b) It **inverts** the verse. Paul rejects "let us do evil that there may come good", which is doing evil *for a good end*, and the column's case is exactly that. The tradition reads the verse this way (*Veritatis Splendor* 78; CCC 1756). (c) **Authentic ordinary magisterium**: CCC 1753–1756 restating *Veritatis Splendor* 78–82, on Scripture and Augustine, and presented as constant teaching. Credit for adding that the Catechism names lying as intrinsically disordered, and that whether intrinsic evil is also definitively taught is argued.

**Wrong turns:** treating her fear and grief as changing the object; they may **lower imputability** (2.1) but do not. Calling the teaching "defined dogma" (overstates). Drifting into the aggressor dispute (6.4); a dying son is no aggressor.

**Model answer:** The column reads "the end is the form" as "the end is the whole act". But Aquinas holds that a deed not of itself ordered to the end keeps its own species beside the end's: two malices in one act (q.18 a.7). Good requires the act willed as well as the end (q.20 a.2), so the lie remains a lie, done for love. Paul does not condemn evil done "for evil's sake". He rejects doing evil "that there may come good" (Rom 3:8), which is her case exactly. That a good intention cannot justify an act evil by its object is authentic ordinary magisterium (CCC 1753–1756; *Veritatis Splendor* 78–82), presented as the constant teaching. Her grief may lessen her guilt; it cannot change what she did.

## Flashback

**From Lesson [2.1](02-01-the-voluntary.md) (The voluntary):** *(Exegetical.)* Three cases written for this problem. For each, name the factor at work and say whether it increases, diminishes, removes or leaves unchanged the voluntary character of the act, citing the article. **One sentence each, then one sentence for the last part.**
(a) Jonah, a building inspector, refuses a contractor's bribe for weeks. Then he accepts it, because he badly wants the money for a new car.
(b) Mei watches a driver swerve into her child's bicycle. Flooded with anger, and still shaking, she chooses to scream an insult at him.
(c) Piet, a lock-keeper whose job is to close the flood gate at six, stays drinking at the pub until eight, and the lower street floods. Would the verdict change if a fallen tree had blocked his only road to the gate?
Last part: why does desire make an act *more* voluntary when fear only makes it mixed?

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict:** (a) **Concupiscence**: it **increases** the voluntary, or at least never lessens it, because desire inclines the will itself to want what it refused before (q.6 a.7). (b) **A passion that precedes choice and pushes it**: the act stays voluntary, since her reason is not taken away, but the passion **diminishes** the sin (q.77 a.6). (c) **A voluntary omission**: not closing the gate is imputed to him because he could and ought to have acted (q.6 a.3), so the flood is his. With the tree across the road he **could not** act, so the omission is not voluntary and the flood is not imputed. Last part: fear drives the will toward something it still dislikes, while desire changes the will so that it now wants the thing (q.6 a.6–7).

**Wrong turns:** treating Jonah's strong desire like fear, as if pressure lessened the act. Aquinas says the opposite. Calling Mei's act involuntary: only a passion that removes reason altogether does that (q.77 a.7), and she chose. Saying an omission cannot be voluntary because "he did nothing".

**Model answer:** (a) Concupiscence makes the act more voluntary, since the desire changes his will and he now wants what he refused (q.6 a.7). (b) Antecedent anger diminishes the sin without removing the voluntary, because it pushed her choice but did not take away her reason (q.77 a.6). (c) His omission is voluntary, because he could and ought to have closed the gate (q.6 a.3); the fallen tree would excuse him, since then he could not act. Fear leaves the will still disliking what it does, while desire makes the will want it.
