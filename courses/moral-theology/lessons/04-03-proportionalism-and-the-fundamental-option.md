# Moral Theology · Lesson 4.3: Proportionalism and the fundamental option

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 4: Method: from casuistry to *Veritatis Splendor* · Builds on: [4.2 Double effect and cooperation as Catholic method](04-02-double-effect-and-cooperation-as-catholic-method.md), [3.4 Sin: mortal and venial](03-04-sin-mortal-and-venial.md) · Unlocks: [4.4 *Veritatis Splendor*](04-04-veritatis-splendor.md), [4.5 Describing the object](04-05-describing-the-object.md)

## Why this matters

Between the council and 1993 the most influential Catholic moral theologians in Europe and North America rebuilt the discipline around two ideas. **Proportionalism** said that an act's rightness is fixed by weighing the goods and evils it brings about. The **fundamental option** said that a person's standing before God is settled at a depth deeper than any single choice. *Veritatis Splendor* was written largely to answer them, and you cannot read it fairly until you can state both at the strength Richard McCormick or Josef Fuchs would sign. That is this lesson's only job. The assessment is [4.4](04-04-veritatis-splendor.md)'s.

## The idea

Start with something the manuals already did. [Double effect](../reference.md#double-effect) lets a surgeon remove a cancerous uterus though the fetus dies, because the death is a side effect and there is a proportionate reason ([4.2](04-02-double-effect-and-cooperation-as-catholic-method.md)). Self-defence may kill. A just war kills. So the tradition *already* weighs a death against other goods. It weighs everywhere, the proportionalists said, except in a short list of acts it calls wrong "in themselves", whatever the reason. Why stop weighing there?

Their answer: stop drawing the line in the physical act. Death, pain, mutilation, a false statement are real evils, but not yet *moral* evils. They are **premoral** (or **ontic**) evils. An act becomes morally wrong only when it causes such an evil *without a proportionate reason*. That is [proportionalism](../reference.md#proportionalism) in one sentence.

The fundamental option makes a parallel move about the *person*. The manuals counted sins act by act. But a human being is more than a sequence of choices. Beneath them lies a basic self-disposal toward God or away from him, and a mortal sin is a reversal of *that*. A single act matters morally as it expresses or changes the [fundamental option](../reference.md#fundamental-option).

Both theories move morality away from the individual act, as the manuals described it: one toward the whole set of goods an act brings about, the other toward the whole person.

## Source

Fundamental-option theorists often point to Aquinas on the child's first moral act (ST I-II q.89 a.6, English Dominican Province):

> But when he begins to have the use of reason, he is not entirely excused from the guilt of venial or mortal sin. Now the first thing that occurs to a man to think about then, is to deliberate about himself. And if he then direct himself to the due end, he will, by means of grace, receive the remission of original sin.

Three phrases carry it. **"Deliberate about himself"**: the first free act has the *self* as its matter, not an object in the world. **"Direct himself to the due end"**: that act orders the whole person toward the last end ([1.2](01-02-beatitude-the-last-end.md)). And the reply to the third objection makes it binding: the child sins by omission "unless it turns to God as soon as possible". So a basic, whole-person orientation to God stands at the head of the moral life. That is the kernel the theorists claim. Notice what the text does *not* say: that later particular acts cannot overturn it.

## The argument

**Proportionalism, as its defenders would put it.**

1. **The distinction.** Goods and evils in the world (life, health, truth, property; death, pain, falsehood, loss) are premoral. Moral goodness and evil belong to the will that brings them about. *In words:* killing is always an evil, but not always a wrong.
2. **Knauer's reinterpretation (1965).** Peter Knauer argued in *Nouvelle Revue Théologique* (English 1967: "The Hermeneutic Function of the Principle of Double Effect") that double effect is not one principle among others but the key to all moral judgment. An evil is "directly intended", in the moral sense, exactly when there is **no commensurate reason** for causing it. Commensurate reason, for Knauer, means the act does not in the long run undermine the very value it pursues.
3. **The appeal to Aquinas.** q.64 a.7: "moral acts take their species according to what is intended, and not according to what is beside the intention". Knauer reads "what is intended" morally rather than physically. And Aquinas himself says that conclusions of natural law hold only "in the majority of cases": goods held in trust should ordinarily be returned, but not to a man who wants them to fight against his country (q.94 a.4). *In words:* concrete norms have exceptions, and a reason decides where they lie.
4. **Fuchs and Schüller on norms.** Josef Fuchs ("The Absoluteness of Moral Terms", 1971) distinguished formal norms ("do not murder", absolute because "murder" already means *unjust* killing) from material norms describing physical behaviour ("do not kill"). Material norms cannot be absolute, since no description in physical terms can foresee every morally relevant circumstance. Bruno Schüller argued that the tradition already reasons teleologically nearly everywhere, so its few deontological islands owe a justification.
5. **McCormick's proportionate reason.** Richard McCormick (*Ambiguity in Moral Choice*, 1973; *Doing Evil to Achieve Good*, ed. with Paul Ramsey, 1978) refined the test. In a common summary, a reason is proportionate when (a) the value sought is at least equal to the value sacrificed, (b) there is no less harmful way to protect it here and now, and (c) this way of protecting it will not undermine it in the long run.
6. **Not consequentialism.** Defenders insisted, rightly on their own terms, that this is not utilitarian summing. The weighing includes the *kind* of act and its meaning, goods that do not reduce to a common currency, and long-run effects on the institution or value at stake. McCormick held that some norms are, in practice, virtually without exception. Many defenders disliked the label "proportionalism", which came largely from critics.

∴ **C1.** No act can be judged morally wrong by its physical description alone, before its reasons are weighed.

**The fundamental option.**

7. **Two levels of freedom.** For Karl Rahner (essays of the 1960s; *Foundations of Christian Faith*, 1976), **categorical freedom** chooses among particular objects. **Transcendental freedom** is the subject's disposal of himself as a whole before God, the horizon of every choice. It is realized *in and through* categorical choices, but it is never identical with any one of them and never fully visible to reflection.
8. **Fuchs's basic freedom** (c. 1970) applies this to morals. A person's basic self-commitment, the fundamental option for or against God, lives at the core. Particular acts express it more or less deeply.
9. **Mortal sin relocated.** A mortal sin, a turning from the last end ([3.4](03-04-sin-mortal-and-venial.md)), is a reversal of the fundamental option. Such a reversal engages the whole person and cannot happen lightly.

∴ **C2.** An act of grave matter done under passion, habit or immaturity may leave a person's basic love of God intact. The theory's pastoral appeal is exactly there.

**Mark the levels.** Proportionalism and the fundamental option are **theological theories**, not magisterial positions. Their vocabulary (premoral evil, transcendental freedom) is theology's, not the Church's. The CDF's *Persona Humana* (29 December 1975, approved by Paul VI) **uses** the fundamental option: it grants that the option defines a person's moral disposition. But it rejects the claim that particular acts are not enough for mortal sin. Any free, knowing choice of something seriously disordered, it says, turns a person from God (§10, paraphrased). *Reconciliatio et Paenitentia* 17 (1984) repeats this, declines a threefold division into venial, grave and mortal sin, and says some acts are always gravely wrong by their object. Both documents are **authentic ordinary magisterium**. Neither condemns the notion of a fundamental option. The existence of [intrinsically evil acts](../reference.md#intrinsically-evil-acts) is at least **authentic ordinary magisterium**, and whether it is also **definitive** is argued; 4.4 takes both up. See [the levels](../reference.md#the-levels-in-moral-theology).

## The rule

```mermaid
flowchart TD
  A["An act that causes a real evil<br/>(a death, a mutilation, a falsehood)"] --> M{"Manualist: is the object<br/>evil in itself?"}
  M -->|"yes"| F["Forbidden, whatever the reason"]
  M -->|"no"| D["Double effect: side effect,<br/>not a means, proportionate reason?"]
  A --> P["Proportionalist: name the premoral<br/>goods and evils"]
  P --> R{"Proportionate reason?<br/>(a) equal value (b) no better way<br/>(c) not self-undermining"}
  R -->|"yes"| OK["Right: the evil is 'indirect'<br/>in Knauer's moral sense"]
  R -->|"no"| W["Wrong: the evil is 'directly intended'"]
```

The manualist asks about the object *before* any weighing. The proportionalist makes the weighing the only question. Knauer's move collapses the four manual conditions into the fourth.

## Worked examples

**Ex 1 (clean): amputating a gangrenous leg.** Premoral evil: the loss of a limb. Value sought: the patient's life, which outweighs it (a). No less drastic treatment works (b). Amputation does not undermine respect for bodily integrity (c). Verdict: right. The manualist agrees by the principle of totality: a part may be sacrificed for the whole body. The two methods coincide in almost every ordinary case, and proportionalists pressed exactly that point: the tradition was already weighing. They part company only where a manual says "intrinsically evil" and the weighing says otherwise. Contraception after [*Humanae Vitae*](06-02-humanae-vitae-the-argument.md) (1968) was the case everyone had in mind. Knauer's article predates the encyclical, but the public dissent of 1968 ([`church-history` 9.3](../../church-history/lessons/09-03-after-the-council.md)) turned a method into a flashpoint.

**Ex 2 (strain): Marta's weekend.** Marta has lived a devout life for thirty years. Over a planned weekend she knowingly commits adultery. Is it mortal sin?

- **Radical fundamental option** (the view *Persona Humana* describes): one "peripheral" act, especially in the sexual sphere and under passion, rarely reverses a lifetime's orientation. So probably not.
- **Moderate fundamental option** (closer to Fuchs): a single act *can* be a self-disposal of the core, and a lucid, planned act is the likeliest candidate. So perhaps yes, but only if it engaged her at depth. There is no external test for that, since transcendental freedom is not open to reflection.
- **Persona Humana 10 / RP 17:** grave matter, knowingly and freely chosen, is mortal sin. The fundamental option is *changed* by such an act. Neither document denies that passion can reduce culpability; *Persona Humana* itself urges caution in judging consent in sexual sins.

The strain: the moderate theorist and the documents may give the same verdict here, and differ on *why*. The documents let the act decide. The theory needs the act to reveal a depth no one can inspect. Whether that difference matters is 4.4's question.

## Watch out

- **"Proportionalism is utilitarianism in a cassock."** Its defenders denied aggregating welfare, counted the meaning of acts, and kept near-absolute norms. State it that way, then criticize it (4.4).
- **"The Church condemned the fundamental option."** It did not. *Persona Humana* uses the idea. What it rejects, with RP 17, is the claim that particular acts cannot be mortal sins.
- **Level errors both ways.** Calling RP 17 or *Persona Humana* "infallible" overstates: both are authentic ordinary magisterium. Calling their teaching on mortal sin "one theological opinion" understates it: it stands on Trent (3.4).
- **"Premoral" is not "morally neutral, so anything goes."** A premoral evil is a real evil. The theory says it always needs a reason; it never says it doesn't matter.

## One-liner

> Proportionalism made every act's rightness hang on a proportionate reason for the premoral evil it causes; the fundamental option made mortal sin a reversal of the self's basic stance toward God. The Magisterium kept the option but insisted particular acts can reverse it.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢)** *(Exegetical)* A case written for the problem. Ana has a heart condition that makes pregnancy very likely fatal. Her husband Tomás proposes a vasectomy so that they can continue marital relations without that risk. Reconstruct in numbered premises (P1, P2, … ∴ C) the argument McCormick's method would give that the vasectomy is **right**, naming the premoral evil and running criteria (a)–(c). Then name the one premise a manualist rejects, and say why the principle of totality does not rescue the vasectomy for him. **150 words or fewer.**

**P2 (🟡)** *(Exegetical)* A variant of Williams's Jim case ([`ethics` 1.4](../../ethics/lessons/01-04-integrity-and-demandingness.md)), written for the problem. A doctor held by a militia is told that if she gives one prisoner a lethal injection, it will spare five other prisoners; if she refuses, all six are shot. **Manualist:** the prisoner's death is the *means* to the others' release, so it is directly intended and the act is forbidden whatever the numbers. **Knauer:** an evil is directly intended only if there is no commensurate reason. (a) State each side's verdict on "direct". (b) Name the single premise about the meaning of "direct" one side must deny, and say which feature of q.64 a.7 each side would cite. **Two sentences per part.**

**P3 (🔴)** *(Exegetical)* An **invented** parish handout, not the words of any real person:

> "Since Vatican II the Church no longer teaches the fundamental option theory, which was condemned as heresy. Now we know that mortal sin is simply a list of forbidden acts: do one, and you lose grace, whatever your state of mind. Even so, the theologians were right that ordinary Catholics who sin out of weakness never commit mortal sin, because a single act cannot change a person's basic direction."

Name three errors. For each, give the text that corrects it and, where a level is involved, the level. **150 words or fewer.**

<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict:** the premoral evil is **the loss of fertility** (a mutilation of a healthy function), not "sin" and not "nothing". (a) The values protected are Ana's life and the good of marital union, weighed against his fertility. (b) No less harmful way is stipulated to be adequate; the student must *state* this as a premise, noting that the defender will say periodic abstinence is judged insufficient. (c) The act does not undermine respect for life or fertility in the long run. The manualist rejects the premise that **the object can be judged only after weighing**, since he holds direct sterilization wrong by its object. Totality fails because it lets a part be sacrificed for the good of **the same body as a whole**. Tomás's organs are healthy, and the danger is to Ana.

**Wrong turns:** calling the evil "moral evil" at step one, which is the conclusion the method is supposed to reach. Making totality cover the *couple* as one body, which is the stretch the manuals refused. Beginning 4.4's critique.

**Model answer:** P1. Tomás's loss of fertility is a premoral evil. P2. An act causing premoral evil is right if there is proportionate reason. P3. The good protected, Ana's life together with their union, is at least equal to what is lost (a). P4. No less harmful means is adequate here (b). P5. Sterilizing to protect life does not undermine respect for life or fertility in the long run (c). ∴ C. The vasectomy is right. A manualist denies P2 for this act: direct sterilization is wrong by its object, whatever the reason. Totality sacrifices a part for its own whole, and Tomás's healthy body gains nothing.

**P2** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** manualist: **direct**, because the death is the means to the good effect (the third manual condition fails), so the act is forbidden and proportion never gets weighed. Knauer: **not direct** in the moral sense *if* there is commensurate reason. Five saved and the victim dying anyway makes that at least arguable, so the verdict turns entirely on proportion.

**Must hit, strict (b):** the crux is whether "direct" is fixed by the **causal and intentional structure of the act, prior to weighing** (manualist), or **by the absence of proportionate reason** (Knauer). Both cite "moral acts take their species according to what is intended". The manualist reads "intended" as covering ends *and means* and points to the article's separate proportion clause. Knauer reads "intended" morally, as what one has no reason for.

**Wrong turns:** giving Knauer a flat "right, five outweigh one". His condition (c), the long-run value, must at least be mentioned. Treating the case as double effect's condition 4 only.

**Model answer:** (a) The manualist calls the death direct because it is the means, so the act is excluded before any weighing. Knauer calls it indirect in the moral sense if a commensurate reason exists, which five lives against one already doomed plausibly supply. (b) One side must deny that "direct" means "chosen as end or means, whatever the reasons". The manualist cites q.64 a.7's separate clause on proportion, which assumes intention is settled first; Knauer cites its principle that acts take their species from what is intended, read as what the reason makes one will.

**P3** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict:**

- **"Condemned as heresy."** False twice. No act declared it heretical. *Persona Humana* 10 (1975) and RP 17 (1984) use the notion, and both are authentic ordinary magisterium, not definitions.
- **"Whatever your state of mind."** Mortal sin needs grave matter *and* full knowledge *and* deliberate consent (CCC 1857; RP 17). Passion and immaturity can reduce culpability, as *Persona Humana* itself says.
- **"A single act cannot change a person's basic direction."** This is the claim both documents reject: a particular, knowingly and freely chosen grave act can reverse the fundamental option.

**Wrong turns:** correcting the second error by swinging to the handout's third claim. Calling *Persona Humana* infallible.

**Model answer:** First, the fundamental option was never condemned as heresy. *Persona Humana* 10 and RP 17 use it, and both are authentic ordinary magisterium. Second, mortal sin is not "a list": grave matter needs full knowledge and deliberate consent (CCC 1857), and passion can reduce culpability. Third, the handout ends by affirming exactly what those documents deny. A single free, knowing choice of grave matter can change a person's basic direction, and repeated weakness does not guarantee it cannot.

## Flashback

**From Lesson [4.1](04-01-casuistry-and-the-probabilism-controversy.md) (Casuistry and the probabilism controversy):** *(Exegetical, apply to a case.)* A case written for the problem. Rafael, a parish lector, has two doubts of law. In each, approved authors are **evenly split**, and each side rests on serious reasons.

1. A diocesan rule imposing a Friday of fasting certainly bound him last year. The authors disagree about whether it has **since lapsed**.
2. No one can find the text of a rule said to oblige lectors to attend a monthly vigil. The authors disagree about whether any such rule was **ever in force**.

(a) For each doubt, may Rafael follow the opinion favouring liberty (i) as an equiprobabilist and (ii) as a probabilist? Give the reason in a clause. (b) His pastor says: "Equiprobabilism is the Church's official moral system, since Rome canonized Alphonsus and named him a Doctor." Correct the claim and give the right level. **Two sentences per part.**

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):** Doubt 1 is a doubt about whether a known law has **ceased**. For the equiprobabilist the law **keeps possession** unless liberty is *more* probable, so with the opinions evenly split he **must fast**. Doubt 2 is a doubt about whether a law **exists**. There an equally probable opinion for liberty suffices, so he **may skip the vigil**. The probabilist **may follow liberty in both**, since an evenly balanced opinion resting on serious reasons is solidly probable, and a doubtful law does not bind.
**Must hit, strict (b):** the choice among probabiliorism, equiprobabilism and probabilism is **freely disputed**. The official sanction of Alphonsus's moral works (1803, 1831), his canonization (1839) and his doctorate (1871) mark his system as **safe to follow**, not as the Church's system.
**Wrong turns:** giving the equiprobabilist the same answer in both doubts, which erases the existence/cessation distinction that defines the position. Saying the probabilist must fast in doubt 1 because the law was once certain; only the equiprobabilist's possession rule says that. Overcorrecting in (b) with "equiprobabilism is suspect", when the condemned systems are laxism and absolute tutiorism.
**Model answer:** (a) As an equiprobabilist, Rafael must fast, because a known law keeps possession until liberty is more probable, but he may skip the vigil, because an equally probable opinion is enough against a law whose existence is in doubt. As a probabilist he may follow liberty in both, since each opinion for liberty is solidly probable. (b) No system is the Church's own; the choice among the moderate systems is freely disputed. Alphonsus's canonization and doctorate, with the earlier approval of his works, mark his system as safe to follow, not as defined or official.

</details>
