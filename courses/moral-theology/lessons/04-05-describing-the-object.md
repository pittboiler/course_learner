# Moral Theology · Lesson 4.5: Describing the object: the debate after *Veritatis Splendor*

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 4: Method: from casuistry to *Veritatis Splendor* · Builds on: [2.2 The moral object](02-02-the-moral-object.md), [4.2 Double effect and cooperation as Catholic method](04-02-double-effect-and-cooperation-as-catholic-method.md), [4.4 Veritatis Splendor](04-04-veritatis-splendor.md) · Unlocks: [5.2 Abortion](05-02-abortion.md), [5.3 Euthanasia and the end of life](05-03-euthanasia-and-the-end-of-life.md)

## Why this matters

*Veritatis Splendor* settled that some acts are wrong by their object, whatever the intention. It did not settle how to **describe** the object. A surgeon removes a fallopian tube containing an embryo. Did she choose to remove a diseased organ, or to end a pregnancy? Every hard case in medical ethics turns on that question. Since 1993, faithful Thomists who all accept the encyclical have answered it differently. This lesson gives you both answers at full strength and marks which cases the Church has settled and which she has not.

## The idea

Two things are both true of any act:

- **What your body does.** Its physical structure and what that structure naturally brings about.
- **What you choose.** The behavior as your practical reason proposes it to your will.

*Veritatis Splendor* 78 shuts both extreme exits. The object is not a physical event judged by the states of affairs it produces, which is the charge of physicalism against the manuals. It is the **proximate end of a deliberate choice**, grasped from "the perspective of the acting person" ([the perspective of the acting person](../reference.md#the-perspective-of-the-acting-person)). But that perspective is not a license to redescribe. The same paragraph insists that some kinds of behavior are always wrong to choose, and a good intention cannot rescue them.

So the real question is narrower: **how much of the act's physical structure enters the description of what I chose?** Answer "all of its natural ordering" and you get one school. Answer "what practical reason makes of it in the situation" and you get the other. The closeness problem from [`ethics` 3.2](../../ethics/lessons/03-02-the-doctrine-of-double-effect.md) is the secular form of the same question. Here it arrives with magisterial stakes.

## Source

Charles Coppens, "Abortion", *Catholic Encyclopedia* vol. 1 (1907), reporting the Holy Office's replies of 1884 and 1889 and stating the manual rule that governed the next century:

> Now this authority decreed, 28 May, 1884, and again, 18 August, 1889, that "it cannot be safely taught in Catholic schools that it is lawful to perform . . . any surgical operation which is directly destructive of the life of the fetus or the mother." … However, if medical treatment or surgical operation, necessary to save a mother's life, is applied to her organism (though the child's death would, or at least might, follow as a regretted but unavoidable consequence), it should not be maintained that the fetal life is thereby directly attacked.

Read the test the manual builds. **"Applied to her organism"** makes *directness* a matter of where the act lands: on the mother's diseased tissue, or on the child. That is a physical-target criterion, and it is clean. **"Cannot be safely taught"** fixes the replies' weight: a caution about what may be taught, not a definition. The two replies are the source of the craniotomy prohibition. Coppens also reports a 1902 reply refusing the early extraction of ectopic embryos. The later manual acceptance of removing the tube, defended above all by T. Lincoln Bouscaren (*Ethics of Ectopic Operations*, 1933; date hedged), worked by relocating the act onto the pathological organ.

## The argument

**Shared ground.**

1. The direct and voluntary killing of an innocent human being is always gravely immoral (*Evangelium Vitae* 57).
2. "Direct" means **willed as an end or as a means** (*Evangelium Vitae* 62, defining direct abortion). *In words:* a death you merely foresee is not direct; a death you aim at, or use, is.
3. The object is the proximate end of the choice, seen from the agent's perspective, not a bare physical event (*Veritatis Splendor* 78).

**Steven Long's account** (*The Teleological Grammar of the Moral Act*, 2007; paraphrased).

4. An exterior act has a natural *per se* ordering, what it of itself tends to bring about. That ordering is part of what the agent chooses when he chooses that act. *In words:* you cannot choose the behavior and leave out what the behavior is for.
5. *Veritatis Splendor* rejects a *merely* physical object. It does not make the physical irrelevant; natural teleology is taken up into the moral object.
6. So when an act is *per se* ordered to a death (crushing a child's skull), the death is chosen as means, whatever the further intention. When the act is *per se* ordered to something else (excising a diseased organ), a death that follows is *praeter intentionem*, beside the intention, as in Aquinas's self-defense (ST II-II q.64 a.7, read in `ethics` 3.2).

**Martin Rhonheimer's account** (*Vital Conflicts in Medical Ethics*, 2009; paraphrased).

7. The object is the basic intentional content of the act as practical reason structures it. The physical is its matter, not its form.
8. The norm against killing the innocent is a norm of **justice**: it forbids choosing against a life owed respect.
9. In a true *vital conflict*, the child cannot survive in any case, and without intervention mother and child both die. There, saving the one life that can be saved does not set the will against any good the child could still have. The act is not an act of killing in the morally relevant sense, even where the intervention physically ends the child's life. *In words:* no injustice is done, so the species "murder" is absent.

Rhonheimer, a sharp critic of proportionalism ([4.3](04-03-proportionalism-and-the-fundamental-option.md)), denies that he weighs one life against another. His claim is about what the choice *is*. Critics answer that this re-imports proportionate reason under the heading of justice. A third school needs a line. Finnis, Grisez and Boyle ("'Direct' and 'Indirect': A Reply to Critics of Our Action Theory", *The Thomist*, 2001; paraphrased, hedged) held that even a craniotomy might be chosen under a description, such as altering the dimensions of the skull, that leaves the death unintended. This is the furthest point to which intention has been asked to redescribe an act. Long's school regards it as exactly what "proximate end" was meant to prevent.

**The crux:** premise 4 against premise 9. Either the natural ordering of the behavior fixes the species regardless of situation, or a situation in which no good can be saved for the victim changes what the choice is.

**Mark the levels.** Premise 1 is **rung 1**: *Evangelium Vitae* 57 confirms it as taught by the ordinary and universal magisterium, and the CDF's 1998 commentary on the *Professio fidei* lists it among its first-paragraph examples. The definition of direct abortion in premise 2 belongs to the declaration in 62, which confirms that teaching as ordinary and universal too; neither is an *ex cathedra* definition. Premise 3 is **authentic ordinary magisterium** (rung 3). The Holy Office replies on craniotomy are **rung 3**: authoritative, with the formula "cannot be safely taught", and not definitive. That removing a diseased tube with the embryo inside is licit is **common teaching** (rung 4); no universal magisterial act approves it. Salpingostomy and methotrexate are **freely disputed** (rung 5). So are Long's and Rhonheimer's accounts of the object as theories. Neither is taught, and neither is condemned.

## Map of positions

| Case | Manual rule (physical target) | Teleological grammar (Long's school) | Vital-conflict account (Rhonheimer) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Craniotomy to save the mother | Illicit: the act lands on the child (Holy Office, 1884/1889) | Illicit: the act is *per se* ordered to death | Licit only in a true vital conflict |
| Ectopic: removing the tube (salpingectomy) | Licit: removes diseased organ (Bouscaren) | Licit: *per se* ordered to excising pathology | Licit |
| Ectopic: opening the tube and removing the embryo (salpingostomy) | Direct: the act lands on the embryo | Commonly judged direct | Licit: no good is taken from an embryo that cannot survive |
| Ectopic: methotrexate | Direct if the drug targets the embryo's own tissue | Commonly judged direct, since the trophoblast is the embryo's tissue | Licit |

Clinically, as far as the argument needs it: in a tubal pregnancy the embryo implants in the tube, cannot develop to viability, and threatens rupture and hemorrhage. Methotrexate stops cell division in the trophoblast, the embryo's own tissue that would form the placenta. Whether the drug "attacks the embryo" or "treats the invading tissue" is precisely the description in dispute. The table's middle column reports how the school is usually applied, not a verdict from Long himself on each procedure; the right-hand column reports Rhonheimer's verdicts as they are usually summarized from *Vital Conflicts*, not quoted. The US bishops' *Ethical and Religious Directives* forbid any intervention in an extrauterine pregnancy that constitutes a direct abortion. They do not say which procedures do.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: a clean case.** A pregnant woman has an aggressive uterine cancer, and the only effective treatment is removing the uterus before the child is viable. Manual rule: the act lands on her diseased organ, so it is indirect. Long: hysterectomy is *per se* ordered to removing the cancer, and the child's death is beside the intention. Rhonheimer: the choice is against the cancer, not against the child. **All three agree**, and so does the whole tradition. That agreement is the baseline. The schools diverge only where the act's physical target and the agent's purpose come apart.

**Example 2: where it strains, Phoenix.** In November 2009, at St. Joseph's Hospital and Medical Center in Phoenix, a woman eleven weeks pregnant had severe pulmonary hypertension, and her physicians judged that continuing the pregnancy would very probably kill her. The ethics committee, including Sister Margaret McBride, approved ending the pregnancy. It identified the placenta, whose effects strained her heart, as the organ being treated. Bishop Thomas Olmsted judged the act a direct abortion. He declared that McBride had incurred excommunication, which was publicly reported in May 2010, and in December 2010 he removed the hospital's Catholic status. The Doctrine Committee of the US bishops issued a statement on 23 June 2010 distinguishing direct abortion from legitimate procedures with an indirect effect.

Run the accounts. The committee's description is Bouscaren's move: relocate the act onto a pathological organ. Long's school replies that the placenta is not a diseased organ of the mother. Emptying the uterus is *per se* ordered to ending the pregnancy, so the death is the means of relieving her heart. It is not a side effect of treating something else. On Rhonheimer's account the case is argued differently, as a possible vital conflict: could the child have survived in any case? The facts reported (eleven weeks, mother near death) make that question central, and the verdict turns on it. **Levels:** the bishop's acts were acts of local governance and penal judgment, and the committee's statement is a committee's, not a doctrinal act of the whole conference. None changes the rung of anything in *The argument*.

## Watch out

- **"Methotrexate is condemned"** or **"salpingectomy is taught"** overstates. The first is freely disputed. The second is common teaching that no universal act approves.
- **"The object is undecided, so hard cases are matters of opinion"** understates. What is disputed is the *description* of particular acts. That direct killing of the innocent is always gravely wrong is rung 1.
- **Rhonheimer is not a proportionalist.** He denies weighing goods to fix the object. Whether his vital-conflict account *collapses* into proportionalism is the critics' charge, and it is an evaluative question, not a label.
- **"From the perspective of the acting person" is not "whatever the agent says he intended."** *Veritatis Splendor* 78 excludes the merely physical *and* the purely subjective.

## One-liner

> Everyone after 1993 agrees the object is what you choose, not what your body happens to cause. The fight is over whether a behavior's natural ordering is part of what you choose, and ectopic pregnancy, craniotomy and Phoenix are where that fight is decided case by case.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢)** *(Exegetical)* Assign each claim its rung, and **name the act that fixes it** or say that none does. **Two sentences per claim.**

1. It cannot safely be taught that craniotomy on a living child to save the mother is lawful.
2. Abortion willed as an end or as a means always constitutes a grave moral disorder.
3. When a uterus can no longer carry any pregnancy to viability, and physicians are certain of this, removing it is not sterilization and is licit.
4. In a true vital conflict, craniotomy is not a direct killing.

**P2 (🟡)** *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b))* A case written for the problem, modeled loosely on the Jodie and Mary case (English Court of Appeal, September 2000). Twins are joined at the pelvis. Twin B has no working heart or lungs and lives only through blood pumped by twin A's heart. Physicians are certain that A's heart will fail within months and both will die, and that separation will save A. Separation requires clamping and dividing the arteries they share, after which B dies within minutes. (a) Say what Long's teleological grammar asks of the act of separation, and what fact about the shared tissue its verdict turns on. Then say what Rhonheimer's vital-conflict account asks, and what verdict it gives. **Three sentences per account.** (b) Which description of the separation is better? **150 words or fewer.**

**P3 (🔴)** *(Exegetical)* Two positions, paraphrased:

- **Position L:** "Crushing the skull of a child in the birth canal is of itself ordered to the child's death. Whoever chooses that behavior chooses the death as the means, whatever the child's prognosis and whatever the surgeon hopes for."
- **Position R:** "The prohibition on killing the innocent protects a good owed in justice. If the child will die whatever is done, and both will die if nothing is done, saving the mother takes nothing the child could have had, so the choice is not a choice against his life."

Name the single premise one side must deny, and say what argument or text would move each side on it. **150 words or fewer.**

<details>
<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict:**

1. **Rung 3.** Holy Office replies of 1884 and 1889: authoritative acts using the formula "cannot be safely taught", which marks a caution about teaching and not a definitive judgment. The norm they apply, that the direct killing of the innocent is wrong, is higher (item 2 and *Evangelium Vitae* 57).
2. **Infallibly taught by the ordinary and universal magisterium, commonly placed at rung 1.** *Evangelium Vitae* 62: a papal declaration invoking Petrine authority in communion with the bishops, grounding the doctrine in natural law and the written Word of God and stating that it is taught by the ordinary and universal magisterium. It confirms that teaching; it is not an *ex cathedra* definition. (The 1998 CDF commentary's first-paragraph example is the wider norm against killing the innocent.)
3. **Rung 3.** The CDF's response of 10 December 2018, approved by Pope Francis; it states that the 1993 responses keep their validity. It is an authentic magisterial response on a case, not definitive.
4. **Rung 5: freely disputed.** No magisterial act adopts Rhonheimer's thesis or condemns it by name. Critics argue it falls under the 1884 and 1889 replies, and that dispute about their application is itself open.

**Wrong turns:** calling item 1 "definitive" because it concerns killing. The replies' formula is cautionary, and their weight is not the norm's weight. Calling item 2 "a solemn definition". Treating item 3 as a reversal of the 1993 responses, when it presents a different case. Calling item 4 "condemned" or "heretical".

**Model answer:** (1) Rung 3, fixed by the Holy Office replies of 1884 and 1889, whose "cannot be safely taught" is a caution, not a definition. (2) Rung 1, confirmed by *Evangelium Vitae* 62 as ordinary and universal teaching, without an *ex cathedra* definition. (3) Rung 3, the CDF response of 2018 approved by the pope, which leaves the 1993 responses standing. (4) Freely disputed: no act settles it, though critics hold that the 1880s replies already cover it.

**P2** *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b))*

**Must hit, strict (a):** *Long:* the question is what dividing the arteries is *per se* ordered to. The verdict turns on whether the shared vessels are B's body, making the act an attack on B that is *per se* lethal, or A's circulation, which A may withdraw from B, with B dying of B's own inability to live. *Rhonheimer:* the question is whether this is a true vital conflict: B cannot survive in any case, and both die without intervention. It is one, so saving A takes from B no good B could have had, and the act is licit on his account.

**Must hit, any verdict (b):** state which fact decides it, the ownership of the shared tissue or the vital-conflict structure. Give the strongest point for the other description, and say whether your answer generalizes to craniotomy.

**Wrong turns:** answering (a) with a proportionate-reason calculation ("one life saved is better than two lost"), which neither account uses. Saying B's death is "beside the intention" without saying what the act is *per se* ordered to. Giving Long's school a single fixed verdict, when the case turns on a factual-ontological question it has to answer first.

**Model answer (b), one of several:** The better description is "restoring A's circulation to A". The arteries are driven by A's heart and serve A's body; B depends on them as a patient depends on a donor's machine. Dividing them is *per se* ordered to ending A's support of B, and B's death follows from B's own condition. That keeps the verdict inside Long's grammar without Rhonheimer's premise that justice changes with prognosis. The strongest reply is that the tissue is genuinely shared, so cutting it is cutting B. I answer that shared tissue that only A's heart sustains is A's to withdraw. This does not generalize to craniotomy, where the skull is the child's alone.

**P3** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict:** the crux is **whether the natural *per se* ordering of the chosen behavior fixes its moral species independently of the situation**. L affirms it. R must deny it and hold that the species "killing of the innocent" is fixed by justice in the situation, so a vital conflict changes what the choice is. **Movers:** for L, an argument that Aquinas's species "according to what is intended" (q.64 a.7) is set by the agent's reasons rather than by natural ordering, or a reading of *Veritatis Splendor* 78's "proximate end" that allows the situation to enter the object. For R, a showing that the justice-based reading proves too much (it would license killing anyone certain to die soon), or magisterial texts such as the 1884 and 1889 replies read as covering his case.

**Wrong turns:** naming the crux as "whether the child's death is intended", on which both agree the surgeon does not *want* it. Calling R proportionalist without saying which premise makes it so. Treating the dispute as settled by *Evangelium Vitae* 57, which both sides accept.

**Model answer:** The premise is whether a behavior's natural ordering to death makes it a killing of the innocent whatever the circumstances. L says yes; R denies it, making the norm one of justice, so that where no good can be saved for the child the choice is not against him. L could be moved by showing that Aquinas specifies acts by the agent's reasons, or that *Veritatis Splendor* 78's proximate end admits the situation. R could be moved by showing that his principle licenses killing anyone about to die anyway, or that the 1880s replies already cover vital conflicts.

## Flashback

**From Lesson [4.3](04-03-proportionalism-and-the-fundamental-option.md) (Proportionalism and the fundamental option):** *(Exegetical.)* A case written for the problem. After a child is murdered, a town is on the edge of rioting, and the police chief is confident that riots would kill several people. He could end the crisis at once by fabricating evidence against a drifter he knows to be innocent, who would then be convicted and imprisoned for life. Stipulate that no other means would work in time. (a) Run McCormick's three criteria for proportionate reason as a proportionalist would, and say which criterion carries the verdict. (b) A manualist reaches the same verdict. Say by what route, and what the difference in route shows about the two methods. **Two sentences per part.**

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):** the premoral evils are the drifter's loss of liberty and the falsehood. On (a) the lives saved plausibly equal or outweigh the loss, and (b) is satisfied by stipulation. The verdict is carried by **criterion (c)**: framing the innocent **undermines in the long run the very value it pursues**, since public order rests on justice and on the assurance that courts punish only the guilty. This is Knauer's "commensurate reason" test in McCormick's form. So the act lacks proportionate reason and is **wrong**.

**Must hit, strict (b):** the manualist judges the **object first**. Knowingly procuring an innocent man's punishment by false evidence is evil in itself, so the act is **excluded before any weighing**, and proportion is never asked. The two methods **agree on the verdict and differ on why**. For the proportionalist the wrongness depends on how the long-run weighing comes out. For the manualist no weighing could make the act right.

**Wrong turns:** giving the proportionalist the verdict "right, several lives outweigh one", which is the "utilitarianism in a cassock" caricature 4.3 warns against. Skipping (a) and (b) and jumping to (c) without saying they are met. Starting 4.4's critique, for example "so proportionalism is refuted", when the question asks only for the difference in route.

**Model answer:** (a) The lives protected may well be worth at least the drifter's liberty, and no other means is available, so (a) and (b) are met. But criterion (c) fails: framing an innocent destroys the justice on which civil peace depends, so there is no proportionate reason and the act is wrong. (b) The manualist rules it out by its object, as the knowing punishment of an innocent through false witness, before any weighing begins. The methods agree here, but the proportionalist's "no" depends on the long-run weighing, and the manualist's does not depend on any weighing at all.

</details>
