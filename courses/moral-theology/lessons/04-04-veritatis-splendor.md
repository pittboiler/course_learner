# Moral Theology · Lesson 4.4: *Veritatis Splendor*

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 4: Method: from casuistry to Veritatis Splendor · Builds on: [4.3 Proportionalism and the fundamental option](04-03-proportionalism-and-the-fundamental-option.md), [2.2 The moral object](02-02-the-moral-object.md) · Unlocks: [4.5 Describing the object](04-05-describing-the-object.md), [5.1 The inviolability of innocent life](05-01-the-inviolability-of-innocent-life.md)

## Why this matters

*Veritatis Splendor* is the most misreported encyclical in moral theology. Its critics say it banned the weighing of consequences, and some admirers talk as if it ended every hard case. It did neither. It made one precise claim about **method**, drew two consequences from it, and left a large field open. This lesson says exactly what the encyclical teaches, what it rejects, what it leaves standing, and at what level.

## The idea

John Paul II signed the encyclical on 6 August 1993, the feast of the Transfiguration, and addressed it to the bishops. It says of itself that this is the first time the Magisterium has set out the foundations of moral teaching in detail (VS 115). Earlier encyclicals had ruled on particular questions. This one rules on how moral questions are to be answered.

It has three chapters. Chapter I (6–27) reads the rich young man (Mt 19:16–22): the moral question is a question about the good and eternal life. Chapter II (28–83) is the technical core. It discerns among tendencies in recent moral theology under four heads: freedom and law, conscience and truth, fundamental choice and specific kinds of behaviour, and the moral act. Chapter III (84–117) turns to martyrdom, the Church's witness and the bishops' duty.

The thread is **freedom and truth**. There is no freedom apart from or against the truth (VS 96). Freedom is not the power to make an act good by choosing it. It is the power to choose the good, and some choices are closed to it.

Recall the opponents from [4.3](04-03-proportionalism-and-the-fundamental-option.md). They said that killing, deceiving and taking another's property are **premoral** (ontic) evils. Such an evil becomes a *moral* evil only when it is caused without proportionate reason. So no description of an act that leaves out its reasons can settle whether it is wrong. Several of them, McCormick among them, answered that the encyclical attacked a position none of them held (paraphrased). Keep that complaint in view. The fair test is whether the encyclical's target is really theirs.

## Source

Augustine, *Against Lying* 18 (*NPNF* first series, vol. 3). *Veritatis Splendor* cites this passage at 81:

> It does indeed make very much difference, for what cause, with what end, with what intention a thing be done: but those things which are clearly sins, are upon no plea of a good cause, with no seeming good end, no alleged good intention, to be done. Those works, namely of men, which are not in themselves sins, are now good, now evil, according as their causes are good or evil … When, however, the works in themselves are evil, such as thefts, fornications, blasphemies, or other such; who is there that will say, that upon good causes they may be done, so as either to be no sins, or, what is more absurd, just sins?

The passage works by concession. **"Very much difference"** grants that intention matters, and matters a great deal, which is the encyclical's concession at 77. Then the passage splits human works in two. **"Not in themselves sins"** names acts, like feeding the poor, that take their moral character from their cause. **"In themselves evil"** names acts that no cause can redeem. Notice **"clearly"**. Augustine assumes we can recognize the second class, and a proportionalist will ask how. Everything in [4.5](04-05-describing-the-object.md) happens in that word.

## The argument

The core of Chapter II, reconstructed:

1. **The morality of a human act depends first on its object**, the kind of behaviour the deliberate will rationally chooses (VS 78, citing ST I-II q.18 a.6). The object is grasped **from the perspective of the acting person**. It is the proximate end of a deliberate choice, not a physical event in the outside world. *In words:* "what I am doing" is fixed by what I choose, not by where my muscles go. This is [the moral object](../reference.md#the-moral-object) of [2.2](02-02-the-moral-object.md).
2. **Some objects cannot be ordered to God**, because they radically contradict the good of the person made in his image. The tradition calls them [intrinsically evil](../reference.md#intrinsically-evil-acts), *intrinsece malum* (VS 80). Their evil holds always and *per se*, quite apart from further intentions and circumstances.
3. **A good intention cannot redeem such a choice** (VS 78, 81, with Augustine above and Rom 3:8). Intention and circumstances can lessen the evil. They cannot remove it.
4. **Foreseen consequences are circumstances** (VS 77). They can lessen an act's gravity but not change its moral species.
5. **So there are [negative precepts](../reference.md#negative-and-positive-precepts) binding *semper et pro semper***: always, for everyone, without exception (VS 52, 82). Positive precepts bind universally too, but what they require here and now depends on circumstances and prudence (VS 52, 67).
6. **Therefore teleologism as a method must be rejected** (VS 79). This is the thesis that no deliberately chosen kind of behaviour can be judged evil by its object, apart from the intention and the total foreseeable consequences. VS 75 names two forms. Consequentialism reads rightness off foreseeable consequences alone. [Proportionalism](../reference.md#proportionalism) weighs the proportion of good and bad effects, looking for the greater good or the lesser evil.

The same logic governs the [fundamental option](../reference.md#fundamental-option):

7. Scripture itself knows a fundamental choice, the decision of faith (VS 66). But that choice is **exercised in particular choices**. So one deliberate choice in grave matter can revoke it (VS 67). Separating the option from concrete acts breaks the unity of the person, body and soul.
8. **Mortal sin keeps its classical definition**: grave matter, full knowledge and deliberate consent (VS 70, citing *Reconciliatio et Paenitentia* 17). Gravity is not measured only by how deeply freedom is engaged.
9. **Martyrdom is the proof case** (VS 90–94). If a negative precept admits no exception, keeping it may cost one's life. Susanna refused the elders and chose death rather than sin (Dan 13:22–23, VS 91). The martyrs show that the absolute norm protects the person rather than crushing him.

**Mark the levels.** *Veritatis Splendor* is an encyclical, an act of the authentic papal magisterium. It defines nothing, so by can. 749 §3 none of it is *ex cathedra*. [The levels](../reference.md#the-levels-in-moral-theology), claim by claim:

- **The existence of intrinsically evil acts** is **at least authentic ordinary magisterium** (rung 3). The encyclical presents it as the constant teaching of the tradition, and restates it "with the authority of the Successor of Peter" (VS 115). **Whether it is also definitive (rung 2) is disputed**, and the syllabus's standing test case forbids settling it here. The CDF's 1998 commentary on the Profession of Faith does not list the general thesis among its examples. It does list particular norms: the direct and voluntary killing of an innocent human being under the first paragraph, and euthanasia, prostitution and fornication among doctrines taught definitively.
- **The rejection of proportionalism as a method** is authentic papal teaching (rung 3 at least). It stands or falls with the thesis in steps 2–5, which it protects.
- **The fundamental-option critique** restates *Persona Humana* (1975; see 4.3) at the same rung. The distinction of mortal from venial sin rests on Trent, and [3.4](03-04-sin-mortal-and-venial.md) marks its level.
- **How to describe the object in a hard case is freely disputed.** The encyclical says the Magisterium does not intend to impose any particular theological system (VS 29).

## Map of positions

| Question | Proportionalist (4.3) | *Veritatis Splendor* |
|---|---|---|
| Do intentions and consequences matter? | Yes, decisively | Yes, responsibility requires it (77), but they cannot change the species |
| Can a kind of act be wrong whatever the reasons? | Only if the description already includes "without proportionate reason" | Yes (79–80) |
| Is weighing goods legitimate? | It *is* the method | In prudence and in applying positive precepts, yes (67). Not as the test of the object |
| Tolerating a lesser evil? | Special case of weighing | Permitted. Doing evil for good is not (80, quoting *Humanae Vitae* 14) |
| Fundamental option? | Transcendental and athematic, rarely reversed | Real, but lived in particular choices and revocable by one (65–67) |

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean): the judge and the riot.** An invented case: a judge can end a riot that will kill dozens by convicting and hanging a man he knows to be innocent. Take the object from the judge's perspective. He chooses to have an innocent man put to death as a means. That is on the encyclical's list, taken from *Gaudium et Spes* 27 (VS 80). The lives saved are foreseen consequences, so they are circumstances (step 4), and the act is forbidden. Fairness requires a second point. Proportionalists generally condemned the judge too, because they held that framing the innocent undermines the very good of justice it seeks, so no truly proportionate reason exists. The dispute is not about this verdict. It is about the reason. For the encyclical the verdict holds whatever the numbers. For the proportionalist it holds because of the numbers, rightly counted.

**Example 2 (where it strains): the starving man and the loaf.** A man in manifest and urgent need, with no other remedy, takes bread from a rich man's store. Aquinas says this is not properly theft at all (ST II-II q.66 a.7). Theft is on Augustine's list of things "in themselves evil". So has an exception crept in? The encyclical's answer is no. The object is different: in extreme need, goods are common by natural right, and taking what one needs is not taking *what belongs to another*. The proportionalist replies that the object has just been redescribed by looking at circumstances and goods at stake, which is his method under another name. That reply is the live question of [4.5](04-05-describing-the-object.md). The encyclical settles *that* some objects are always evil. It does not settle, for every hard case, *which* description is the object.

## Watch out

- **"VS forbids weighing consequences."** It forbids treating the weighing as the test of the object (79). It requires attention to intention and consequences (77), and it leaves the application of positive precepts to prudence (67).
- **"Every moral rule is exceptionless."** Only negative precepts about intrinsically evil acts are. Sunday worship and honouring parents bind universally but depend on circumstances (52).
- **"VS abolished the fundamental option."** It affirms a fundamental choice (65–66) and rejects only its separation from particular acts (67–70).
- **Overstating the level:** "The existence of intrinsically evil acts was infallibly defined in 1993." Nothing in the encyclical is defined. Whether the teaching is definitive is disputed.
- **Understating it:** "It's one pope's theological opinion." It is authentic papal teaching restated with Petrine authority (115), and religious submission is owed at minimum.

## One-liner

> *Veritatis Splendor* teaches that some objects are evil whatever the reasons, rejects the weighing of goods as the test of the object, and leaves open how hard cases are described.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢)** *(Exegetical.)* Assign each claim its rung on the [ladder](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-04-the-ladder-of-doctrinal-authority.md), **name the act that fixes it**, and say in one clause what is disputed, if anything. **Two sentences per claim.**

(a) There exist acts which, by reason of their object, are always gravely wrong, whatever the intention and circumstances.
(b) Fornication is illicit.
(c) The object that VS 78 says is grasped "from the perspective of the acting person" should be specified by Rhonheimer's intention-centred account, not by Long's, which gives more weight to the natural teleology of the act.
(d) *Veritatis Splendor*'s teaching on intrinsically evil acts is an *ex cathedra* definition.

**P2 (🟡)** *(Exegetical.)* An **invented** magazine paragraph, not the words of any real person:

> "In 1993 Rome shut the door on modern moral theology. *Veritatis Splendor* declares that intentions and consequences are morally irrelevant: only the act itself counts. It teaches that all moral commandments, from 'keep holy the Sabbath' to 'do not kill', hold without any exception. It condemns the very idea of a fundamental option. And by condemning proportionalism, it forbids Catholics ever to choose the lesser evil."

Name **four** misreportings. For each, give the section of the encyclical that corrects it and say in one clause what the encyclical actually holds. **150 words or fewer.**

**P3 (🔴)** *(Exegetical (a) · Exegetical (b).)* ST I-II q.18 a.4 ad 3 (English Dominican Province):

> Nothing hinders an action that is good in one of the way mentioned above, from lacking goodness in another way. And thus it may happen that an action which is good in its species or in its circumstances is ordained to an evil end, or vice versa. However, an action is not good simply, unless it is good in all those ways: since "evil results from any single defect, but good from the complete cause," as Dionysius says.

(a) Which words make it impossible for a good end to outweigh an evil species, and why do they block the *weighing* of the parts rather than merely warn against it? (b) A proportionalist replies: "I accept Dionysius's axiom entirely. But whether the species is defective is exactly what proportionate reason decides: a killing with proportionate reason is not murder." Name the premise on which he and the encyclical divide, and cite the section of *Veritatis Splendor* that denies his version of it. **150 words for (a) and (b) together.**

**P1** *(Exegetical — strict both ways.)*

**Must hit, strict:**
- (a) **At least rung 3.** The act is *Veritatis Splendor* 79–80 and 115: an encyclical that presents the thesis as the tradition's constant teaching and restates it with Petrine authority. **Whether it is rung 2 is disputed.** The 1998 CDF commentary does not list the general thesis among its examples.
- (b) **Rung 2.** The act is the ordinary and universal magisterium, as classified by the CDF's 1998 commentary, which names the illicitness of fornication among moral doctrines taught definitively. The classification is the CDF's own and explicit. Credit for noticing that a particular norm can be classified higher than the general thesis in (a).
- (c) **Freely disputed (rung 5).** No act settles it, and VS 29 disclaims imposing any theological system. The Rhonheimer–Long debate of [4.5](04-05-describing-the-object.md) is exactly this.
- (d) **False.** The encyclical defines nothing, and can. 749 §3 forbids presuming a definition. The teaching is at least rung 3, with rung 2 disputed.

**Wrong turns:** calling (a) "definitive" or "infallible" flatly, which is the overstatement, or "a theological opinion", which is the understatement. Putting (b) at rung 3 because the Catechism states it: the Catechism does not fix a level, the classification does. Treating (c) as settled because the phrase appears in an encyclical.

**Model answer:** (a) At least authentic ordinary magisterium (VS 79–80, 115); whether it is definitive is argued, and the 1998 commentary does not classify it. (b) Definitive, rung 2: the 1998 commentary lists it among doctrines taught definitively by the ordinary universal magisterium. (c) Freely disputed: VS 29 imposes no system, and 4.5's rival readings are both open. (d) False: nothing in the encyclical is defined (can. 749 §3).

**P2** *(Exegetical.)*

**Must hit, strict (any four):**
1. **"Intentions and consequences are irrelevant."** VS 77: both must be taken into account, and responsibility demands it. They cannot alter the species of an act whose object is evil.
2. **"All commandments are exceptionless."** VS 52: only negative precepts bind *semper et pro semper*. Positive precepts, Sabbath worship among them, bind universally, but what they require depends on circumstances (52, 67).
3. **"Condemns the fundamental option."** VS 65–66 affirm a fundamental choice. VS 67 rejects only separating it from particular acts.
4. **"Forbids choosing the lesser evil."** VS 80, quoting *Humanae Vitae* 14, allows *tolerating* a lesser evil. What it forbids is *doing* evil that good may come.
5. (Also creditable) **"Shut the door on modern moral theology."** VS 29 praises the theologians' renewal and imposes no system. The encyclical rejects specific theses.

**Wrong turns:** conceding "only the act itself counts" as accurate. The object is not the physical act; it is what is chosen from the agent's perspective (78). Answering from the Catechism rather than the encyclical.

**Model answer:** VS 77 requires attention to intention and consequences; it denies only that they change an evil object's species. VS 52 makes only negative precepts exceptionless; positive ones, like worship, depend on circumstances. VS 65–67 affirm a fundamental choice and reject only cutting it off from particular acts. VS 80, quoting *Humanae Vitae* 14, allows tolerating a lesser evil and forbids doing evil for good.

**P3** *(Exegetical (a) · Exegetical (b).)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** **"not good simply, unless it is good in all those ways"** and **"evil results from any single defect, but good from the complete cause."** Goodness is a conjunction, not a sum. One failing condition makes the whole act evil, so there is nothing for a good end to be added to: goods and evils of different parts are not on one scale. "Or vice versa" shows the same holds for a good end with an evil species.

**Must hit, strict (b):** the crux is **whether the object (species) can be specified independently of the proportion of goods and evils**. The proportionalist builds proportion into the species; the encyclical holds that some kinds of behaviour are evil by their object "apart from" the intention and the totality of foreseeable consequences (**VS 79**, with 75 and 77). Credit for adding VS 78, the object as the proximate end of a deliberate choice.

**Wrong turns:** saying the proportionalist rejects Dionysius's axiom. He explicitly accepts it, which is why the crux lies in the species. Answering (b) by citing the list in VS 80: it presupposes the answer rather than locating the premise.

**Model answer:** (a) "Not good simply, unless it is good in all those ways": goodness requires every element, so a defect in the species cannot be offset; there is no sum to tip. (b) Both accept the axiom. They divide over whether the species of an act can be fixed before and apart from the weighing of goods. VS 79 rejects the thesis that it cannot.

## Flashback

**From Lesson [4.2](04-02-double-effect-and-cooperation-as-catholic-method.md) (Double effect and cooperation as Catholic method):** *(Exegetical, apply to a case.)* Three agents, invented for the problem. For each, say which tool applies: **double effect** (an evil effect of my own act) or **cooperation** (another agent's evil, which my act helps). Then classify: direct or indirect; or principal agent, formal, or material (immediate or mediate, proximate or remote). Finally say whether the act is licit, illicit, or licit only given a proportionate reason. **Two sentences each.**

(a) To stop a wildfire reaching a town, a fire chief burns a firebreak through open scrub, foreseeing that the smoke will ruin a neighbouring vineyard's harvest.
(b) A notary is told by his client that the date on a deed has been falsified to cheat the client's siblings of an inheritance. To keep the client's business, he certifies the date as genuine, telling the client he disapproves.
(c) A courier delivers sealed envelopes for dozens of law offices. She has heard a rumour that one envelope she carries this week holds that deed, on its way to be filed.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict:**
- (a) **Double effect**: no other agent's sin is involved. The harvest loss is **indirect**, because the town is saved by removing fuel, not by ruining the vineyard; if the smoke had drifted elsewhere, the plan would have fully succeeded. Licit **given a proportionate reason**, which a town's safety supplies.
- (b) Not a mere cooperator. Certifying as genuine a date he knows is false is **his own act of false attestation**, so he is a **principal agent** of it. Since that act is ordered to the client's fraud and to nothing else, his part in the fraud is at least **implicit formal**. **Illicit.** His disapproval does not change what he chooses to do, and keeping a client is a motive, not a remedy for the object.
- (c) **Cooperation**, **material, mediate and remote**. Her act, delivery, has its own object, serves every office alike, adds nothing the fraud depends on, and rests on a rumour. Licit **given a proportionate reason**: her livelihood suffices, since she has no duty to prevent the fraud and there is no scandal.

**Wrong turns:** treating (a) as cooperation, when the only agent is the chief. Calling (b) material cooperation "because it's the client's fraud", which is the "I disapprove, so it's material" error. Calling (c) illicit because any link to the fraud taints, which erases the formal/material distinction.

**Model answer:** as above, two sentences each.
