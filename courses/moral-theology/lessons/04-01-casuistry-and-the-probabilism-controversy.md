# Moral Theology · Lesson 4.1: Casuistry and the probabilism controversy

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 4: Method: from casuistry to *Veritatis Splendor* · Builds on: [2.4 Conscience: its nature and its errors](02-04-conscience-its-nature-and-its-errors.md), [3.4 Sin: mortal and venial](03-04-sin-mortal-and-venial.md) · Unlocks: [4.2 Double effect and cooperation as Catholic method](04-02-double-effect-and-cooperation-as-catholic-method.md)

## Why this matters

[2.4](02-04-conscience-its-nature-and-its-errors.md) said you must follow a *certain* conscience. Most hard cases don't come with certainty. Learned authors split, the law is unclear, and you still have to act by Friday. For about two centuries, from the 1570s to Alphonsus Liguori, Catholic moral theology fought over one question: **when the experts disagree, which opinion may you follow?** Popes condemned both extremes. Pascal made the fight famous. The middle positions are still **freely disputed** today. This lesson gives you the map, and Boss 4 will ask you to use it.

## The idea

The manuals start from a rule that is common teaching: **you may not act while in practical doubt about whether the act is sinful.** Doing so accepts the risk of sinning, and accepting that risk is already a fault. So a doubting agent has two options. One is to settle the doubt directly, by study or by asking. The other, when the doubt won't go away, is to use a **reflex principle**, a rule about how to act *when in doubt*. It turns speculative uncertainty ("I don't know whether this law binds me") into practical certainty ("I know I may act").

Three pieces of vocabulary:

- **Casuistry.** The discipline of *cases of conscience*: concrete cases, sorted by kind, decided by maxims and by analogy to clearer cases. After Trent it served the confessor, who had to judge sins by kind and circumstance ([1.1](01-01-what-moral-theology-is.md), [the manual tradition](../reference.md#the-manual-tradition)).
- **The safer opinion** (*tutior*) favours the law. **The opinion favouring liberty** says the law does not bind here.
- **"Probable"** does not mean "statistically likely". An opinion is *probable* when it rests on serious grounds, either intrinsic (reasons) or extrinsic (the authority of competent authors), so that a prudent person could hold it.

The [moral systems](../reference.md#the-moral-systems) differ on one thing only: **how strong the opinion favouring liberty must be before you may follow it.**

## Source

Pascal, *Provincial Letters*, Letter V (dated 20 March 1656; M'Crie's translation). A Jesuit character explains his order's doctrine:

> "An opinion is called probable, when it is founded upon reasons of some consideration. Hence it may sometimes happen that a single *very grave doctor* may render an opinion probable."

The quoted definition is respectable. The damage is done by **"some consideration"** and **"single"**. The first lowers the bar from *solid* reasons to *some* reasons. The second lets one author create probability all by himself. Pascal's satire works by pushing on those two phrases. In the same letter his Jesuit concludes that a penitent may simply follow whichever opinion suits him. The later papal condemnations went after the same slippage, and they spared the definition itself.

## The argument

**Probabilism, as Bartolomé de Medina, a Dominican, first stated it** (commentary on the *Prima Secundae*, Salamanca, 1577):

1. A law binds only if it is sufficiently promulgated, that is, made known. *In words:* obligation needs knowledge.
2. A law whose existence or scope is seriously and objectively doubtful is not sufficiently promulgated. So **a doubtful law does not bind** (*lex dubia non obligat*).
3. If a solidly probable opinion holds that the law does not bind here, the law is seriously doubtful, *even if the opposite opinion is more probable*. *In words:* a stronger case for the law does not erase a serious case against it.
4. ∴ One may follow a solidly probable opinion favouring liberty, even when the opinion favouring the law is more probable.

**The rivals deny premise 3 at different points.**

5. **Probabiliorism:** liberty may be followed only when it is *more* probable. A weaker case for liberty leaves the law "more known than not", and it binds.
6. **Equiprobabilism** (Alphonsus Liguori, 1696–1787, from about 1762): when the doubt is whether a law *exists*, liberty may be followed if it is equally or nearly equally probable. When the doubt is whether a known law has *ceased*, the law keeps possession unless liberty is more probable.
7. **Tutiorism (rigorism):** always take the safer side, unless liberty is most probable or certain. In its *absolute* form it holds that not even the most probable opinion for liberty may be followed.
8. **Laxism:** any slightly probable opinion suffices, even one resting on a thin reason or a single author.

**Three limits** that every moderate system accepts:

9. **Doubt of law versus doubt of fact.** Reflex principles resolve [doubts about obligation](../reference.md#doubt-of-law-and-doubt-of-fact). When a *certain* end has to be secured, they do not apply. That covers the validity of a sacrament, another person's life or established rights, and one's own salvation. There you must take the safer means, however the law question is resolved. A hunter who doubts whether the movement in the bushes is a deer or a man may not shoot on the strength of a "probable opinion" that it is a deer. A doubt of fact that bears only on whether an act is *lawful* can be reduced to a doubt of law.
10. **Probability is lost when the Church decides.** An opinion that contradicts a clear magisterial decision is no longer probable, whoever holds it.
11. **Casuistry works where the law is uncertain.** *Veritatis Splendor* 76 (paraphrased) says the tradition's casuistry dealt only with cases of uncertain law. It never questioned negative precepts that bind without exception. That boundary is what separates it from [proportionalism](04-03-proportionalism-and-the-fundamental-option.md).

**The condemnations.** Alexander VII condemned forty-five laxist propositions in 1665–66. Innocent XI's decree of 2 March 1679 condemned sixty-five more. One of them was the claim that, when administering a sacrament, one may follow a merely probable opinion about its validity and abandon the safer one. In 1690 Alexander VIII condemned a set of rigorist propositions. Among them was absolute tutiorism: *non licet sequi opinionem vel inter probabiles probabilissimam* ("it is not lawful to follow even the most probable among probable opinions"; the lesson's translation). In 1680 Innocent XI authorized the Jesuit Tirso González to teach probabiliorism. Whether that decree was meant against probabilism was itself argued by later authors, and it condemned nothing.

**Mark the levels.** The [levels](../reference.md#the-levels-in-moral-theology) are as follows. The condemnations of 1665–66, 1679 and 1690 are **papal acts of the authentic magisterium** censuring particular propositions. So **laxism and absolute tutiorism are excluded**, and holding either is not a free option. The censures are global, though. They do not call each proposition heretical, so don't upgrade them to "defined dogma". The practical-doubt rule, *lex dubia non obligat*, and the limits in 9–10 are **common teaching**. The choice among **probabiliorism, equiprobabilism and probabilism** (and compensationism, which weighs the doubt against a proportionate reason) is **freely disputed**. The Holy See gave Alphonsus's moral works official sanction, by a decree of 1803 and a reply of the Sacred Penitentiary in 1831. It canonized him (1839) and named him a Doctor (1871). Those acts mark his system as *safe to follow*. They do not mark it as *the Church's system*.

## Map of positions

| System | May you follow the opinion favouring liberty when it is… | Status |
|---|---|---|
| Absolute tutiorism | never, unless certain | **Condemned** (1690) |
| Mitigated tutiorism | most probable | Not condemned; rarely defended |
| Probabiliorism | more probable than the law | Freely disputed |
| Equiprobabilism | equally probable (doubt of existence); more probable (doubt of cessation) | Freely disputed |
| Probabilism | solidly probable, even if less probable | Freely disputed |
| Laxism | slightly or tenuously probable | **Condemned** (1665–66, 1679) |

The table runs from strict to lax. Both ends are condemned, and the middle four rows are where Catholic moral theologians may disagree.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: a doubt of law.** *An invented case.* Clare lives 70 minutes from the nearest Sunday Mass by an unreliable bus. Suppose approved authors split on whether a journey that long excuses from the obligation. A solid minority says it does; most say it does not, absent other burdens. This is a doubt about whether a law's obligation extends to her case, not a doubt about a fact. A **probabilist** says she may stay home, because the minority opinion is solidly probable. A **probabiliorist** says she must go, because the case for liberty is weaker. An **equiprobabilist** asks whether the doubt concerns the law's existence or its cessation. A law she knows binds her in general is presumed to keep possession, so she must go unless liberty is more probable. None of the three is condemned. The disagreement is exactly the freely disputed zone.

**Example 2: where it strains.** A penitent says: "One well-known theologian rejects this non-definitive teaching, so the permissive view is probable, and I may follow it." That is Pascal's "single very grave doctor" against limit 10. Two questions decide it. The first is whether the teaching is a *clear decision*. Here it is at least [authentic magisterium](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-03-religious-submission.md), which asks for religious submission, and the manuals held that a decision of that kind strips contrary opinions of probability. The second is whether "probable" still means solid reasons and not just *a name*. The systems give no mechanical answer here. What counts as "solidly probable" is a judgment of prudence ([ethics 4.2](../../ethics/lessons/04-02-aristotle-virtue-and-practical-wisdom.md)). This is why [6.3](06-03-humanae-vitae-its-authority.md) cannot be settled by probabilism.

Jonsen and Toulmin, *The Abuse of Casuistry* (1988), paraphrased, argue that the method deserved better than Pascal's verdict. Case-based reasoning from paradigm cases, maxims and circumstances is how practical ethics actually reaches agreement. They drew on their work on a US bioethics commission, where members who disagreed about principles still agreed about cases. On their account, what discredited casuistry was the laxist abuse, not the method. Usury is the classic case of casuistry doing development: the theory of extrinsic titles loosened a strict prohibition case by case. That history belongs to [`theology-of-debt`](../../theology-of-debt/syllabus.md).

## Watch out

- **Level, overstated:** "The Church adopted probabilism" or "equiprobabilism is Catholic doctrine." Alphonsus's canonization marks his system as *safe*, not as *defined*. The choice among the moderate systems is freely disputed.
- **Level, understated:** "It's all opinion; you can be a rigorist or a laxist as you like." Both extremes were condemned by papal acts. The freedom lies only between them.
- **"Probable" means over 50 percent.** It doesn't. The core of probabilism is that a *less* probable opinion can still be *solidly* probable.
- **Using probabilism on a doubt of fact with a certain end at stake.** The hunter, the validity of a sacrament, a patient's life: take the safer course.
- **Treating Pascal as the verdict of history.** The *Provincial Letters* were themselves placed on the Index (1657). Rome condemned laxism *and* rigorism.

## One-liner

> When the law is truly doubtful, a doubtful law does not bind; the tradition condemned both "any opinion will do" and "only the safest will do," and still argues over how strong the case for liberty must be.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢)** *(Exegetical)* Classify each invented position by system, and give its status (condemned / freely disputed). **One line each.**

1. "If the law is even arguably inapplicable, you're free; one respectable author is enough."
2. "You may set the law aside only when the case against it outweighs the case for it."
3. "Where there is any doubt, obey the law, even if the case for liberty is the strongest one available."
4. "If the reasons are serious, you may follow liberty even though most authors think the law binds."

**P2 (🟡)** *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b))* *An invented case.* A parish rule of uncertain authority says that catechists must attend a Saturday training day; Tom doubts whether it binds volunteers at all. Separately, Tom's friend asks whether a batch of wine, which he suspects may have been adulterated with other liquids, is valid matter for Mass. (a) Which of the two doubts may a probabilist resolve with *lex dubia non obligat*, and why not the other? **Two sentences.** (b) Take a side between probabilism and probabiliorism on Tom's first doubt, and state the strongest point for the other side. **150 words or fewer.**

**P3 (🔴)** *(Exegetical)* Pascal, *Provincial Letters*, Letter V (M'Crie). The narrator asks how one is to choose between contrary opinions:

> "Not at all," he rejoined; "he has only to follow the opinion which suits him best."—"What! if the other is more probable?" "It does not signify."—"And if the other is the safer?" "It does not signify," repeated the monk; "this is made quite plain by Emanuel Sa, of our Society, in his Aphorisms: 'A person may do what he considers allowable according to a probable opinion, though the contrary may be the safer one. The opinion of a single grave doctor is all that is requisite.'"

(a) Which sentence in the passage states no more than Medina's probabilism, and which goes beyond it? (b) Name the phrase that, read one way, makes the passage laxism, and say which papal acts reach that reading. **150 words or fewer.**

<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict:** (1) **Laxism**: "arguably" and "one author" are a tenuous probability. **Condemned** (Alexander VII 1665–66; Innocent XI 1679). (2) **Probabiliorism**: freely disputed. (3) **Absolute tutiorism**: "even if the case for liberty is the strongest" is the proposition condemned by Alexander VIII in 1690. **Condemned.** (4) **Probabilism**: solid reasons, even against the majority. Freely disputed.

**Wrong turns:** calling (3) "strict but permitted", which misses that the *absolute* form is condemned while mitigated tutiorism is not. Calling (4) laxism because it goes against most authors; what matters is whether the probability is *solid*, not whether it is the majority view. Calling (2) or (4) "the Church's teaching".

**Model answer:** as above, one line each.

**P2** *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b))*

**Must hit, strict (a):** the **training-day rule**: whether it binds volunteers is a doubt of law about a precept's scope, so *lex dubia non obligat* can apply. **Not the wine:** the validity of a sacrament is a *certain end* that must be secured, and Innocent XI (1679) condemned following a merely probable opinion on validity. Its doubt is a doubt of fact, so the safer course (certainly valid matter) is required.

**Must hit, any verdict (b):** state the crux as whether a less probable but solid case for liberty makes the law "doubtful" in the sense that stops it binding (Medina's premise 3). Give one reason for your side, and fairly state the other side's strongest point: for probabiliorism, that acting against the more probable view is imprudent; for probabilism, that a law not sufficiently known does not bind, and that probabiliorism burdens consciences with endless weighing. Mark the question freely disputed.

**Wrong turns:** treating the wine as a doubt of law. Declaring either system "condemned". Arguing from the importance of catechesis, which is a reason to *keep* the rule, not a way of settling the doubt.

**Model answer (b), one of several:** Probabilism. A precept binds by being made known. If serious authorities doubt it reaches volunteers, it has not been sufficiently promulgated to them, and the stronger contrary case does not change that. Probabiliorism also makes everyone a referee of fine differences in probability, which scrupulous people cannot do. The strongest reply is that choosing what you yourself judge the weaker view looks like choosing convenience over truth. My answer is that the reflex principle asks what binds, not what is most likely true. Freely disputed.

**P3** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** "A person may do what he considers allowable according to a probable opinion, though the contrary may be the safer one" is **Medina's probabilism** (liberty may be followed even against the safer or more probable side). **"The opinion which suits him best"** and **"it does not signify"** go beyond it. They make the agent's preference the criterion and drop the requirement of *solid* probability.

**Must hit, strict (b):** **"a single grave doctor is all that is requisite"**. Read as making any single author's view enough, whatever his reasons, it turns extrinsic probability into laxism. That reading is reached by the laxist condemnations of **Alexander VII (1665–66) and Innocent XI (1679)**, which are authentic papal acts censuring propositions. They are not definitions.

**Wrong turns:** calling the whole passage condemned, when the Sa sentence on its own states the tolerated system. Citing Alexander VIII, whose 1690 condemnation hit *rigorism*. Treating Pascal's report as the Jesuits' verbatim doctrine; it is satire built on real quotations.

**Model answer:** The Sa sentence ("allowable according to a probable opinion, though the contrary may be the safer") is simply probabilism: liberty may be followed against the safer side. The monk goes beyond it with "the opinion which suits him best" and "it does not signify", which make preference the test. The phrase that tips it into laxism is "a single grave doctor is all that is requisite", read as making one name enough without solid reasons. That reading is what Alexander VII (1665–66) and Innocent XI (1679) condemned, as papal acts of authentic magisterium, not definitions.

</details>

## Flashback

**From Lesson [3.4](03-04-sin-mortal-and-venial.md) (Sin: mortal and venial):** *(Exegetical.)* ST I-II q.88 a.3, "Whether venial sin is a disposition to mortal sin?", *respondeo* (English Dominican Province). An act of sin disposes to something in two ways:

> First, directly, and thus it disposes to an act of like species. In this way, a sin generically venial does not, primarily and of its nature, dispose to a sin generically mortal, for they differ in species. Nevertheless ... a venial sin can dispose, by way of consequence, to a sin which is mortal on the part of the agent: because the disposition or habit may be so far strengthened by acts of venial sin, that the lust of sinning increases, and the sinner fixes his end in that venial sin ... Secondly, a human act disposes to something by removing an obstacle thereto. ... he that commits a sin generically venial ... through accustoming his will not to be subject to the due order in lesser matters, is disposed not to subject his will even to the order of the last end, by choosing something that is a mortal sin in its genus.

In the next article (a.4), Aquinas denies that many venial sins added together make one mortal sin, and grants that they make one "dispositively".

(a) Name the two routes by which venial sin disposes to mortal sin, with the words that carry each. For each, say whether the mortal sin it leads to is mortal **by genus** or **on the part of the agent**. (b) An invented penitent: "My sins are small: shading the truth with clients, snapping at my kids, skipping prayers. Small sins never add up to a big one, so the pattern doesn't matter." Which half of this does Aquinas accept, and why does the passage still defeat the conclusion? **Two sentences per part.**

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):** **Route 1, by habit:** "the disposition or habit may be so far strengthened ... the sinner fixes his end in that venial sin." The result is mortal **on the part of the agent**: a generically venial act becomes mortal because he makes it his last end (3.4's q.88 a.2). **Route 2, by removing an obstacle:** "accustoming his will not to be subject to the due order in lesser matters." The result is mortal **by genus**: "choosing something that is a mortal sin in its genus."

**Must hit, strict (b):** Aquinas **accepts** that no number of venial sins sums to a mortal sin (a.4). He **rejects** "the pattern doesn't matter": the pattern disposes to mortal sin by both routes, a habit that can end in fixing his end in these sins, and a will trained out of subjection to order in small things and so weakened toward the last end.

**Wrong turns:** saying enough venial sins *become* a mortal sin, which a.4 denies except dispositively. Swapping the routes' outcomes: the habit route ends in a sin mortal on the agent's part, the obstacle route in a sin mortal in its genus. Reading route 1 as saying venial sin directly causes generically mortal sin; the text denies that "primarily and of its nature".

**Model answer:** (a) By habit, venial sins so strengthen the lust of sinning that a man can fix his end in them, which makes a generically venial sin mortal on his part. By removing an obstacle, they train his will out of subjection to order in small things, so that he later chooses something mortal in its genus. (b) Aquinas agrees that venial sins never add up to a mortal sin. But the pattern still matters because it disposes to mortal sin in both of these ways.

</details>
