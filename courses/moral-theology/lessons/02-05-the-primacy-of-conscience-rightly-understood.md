# Moral Theology · Lesson 2.5: The primacy of conscience rightly understood

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 2: Human acts and conscience · Builds on: [2.4 Conscience: its nature and its errors](02-04-conscience-its-nature-and-its-errors.md), [`fundamental-theology` 4.3 Religious submission](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-03-religious-submission.md) · Unlocks: [3.1 Virtue, acquired and infused](03-01-virtue-acquired-and-infused.md), [4.4 Veritatis Splendor](04-04-veritatis-splendor.md)

## Why this matters

"The Church teaches the primacy of conscience" is true, and it is also the sentence most often used to mean something the Church denies. Both sides of the argument quote Newman, both quote Vatican II, and both are sincere. Conscience really is supreme in one sense and not in another. This lesson finds the seam between the two. Once you have it, most of the argument stops being a clash of slogans and turns into one narrow question.

## The idea

[2.4](02-04-conscience-its-nature-and-its-errors.md) gave the mechanics: conscience is a judgment of practical reason applying what I know of the law to *this* act, and a certain conscience binds even when it is wrong ([erroneous conscience](../reference.md#erroneous-conscience)). Primacy follows directly. No authority can make it right for me to do what I am certain is evil. When I act, conscience is the last court.

But a last court is not a legislature. Conscience is primary as **the place where the law reaches me**. It is not primary as **the source of the law**. A judge's verdict binds the parties, and it binds because it claims to apply a law the judge did not write. Take away that claim and you do not have a freer judge, just a man with a gavel.

So there are two claims, and only the first is Catholic teaching:

- **Primacy of obligation:** I must never act against my certain conscience.
- **Primacy of authorship:** my conscience decides what is good, so no norm can override its verdict.

Everything in this lesson is a way of keeping them apart.

## Source

John Henry Newman, *A Letter Addressed to His Grace the Duke of Norfolk* (1875), §5 "Conscience". Gladstone had argued that since the Vatican decrees of 1870 no Catholic's loyalty could be relied on. Newman replies by grounding conscience in God:

> Conscience is the aboriginal Vicar of Christ, a prophet in its informations, a monarch in its peremptoriness, a priest in its blessings and anathemas…

The section ends with the famous line:

> I shall drink—to the Pope, if you please,—still, to Conscience first, and to the Pope afterwards.

Read the first sentence for its **noun**. A *vicar* is a deputy who acts in another's name. Newman ranks conscience first because it is the earliest ("aboriginal") representative of Christ, not an independent power. Earlier in the section he calls it a messenger from God, and he contrasts it with a "counterfeit", the right of self-will. The toast is funny because it comes after pages of argument that the two can hardly collide. His reasons: conscience judges particular acts, not doctrines; papal infallibility covers general teaching, not commands or policy; so a clash is possible only over a practical order, and even then the presumption lies with obedience. He then lists what a conscience must do before it can stand against a papal command. You will close-read that list in Boss 2. Here, note only that the list exists and that obedience holds the presumption.

The Catechism quotes the first sentence at CCC 1778, in its article on conscience.

## The argument

**The primacy that is taught.**

1. In conscience a person finds a law he did not give himself, and God's voice echoes there (*Gaudium et Spes* 16, 1965, paraphrased; CCC 1776 quotes it). Conscience is the person's "sanctuary". *In words:* the dignity of conscience is that it is the place where God's law is heard.
2. So one must always follow the certain judgment of conscience; to act against it is to condemn oneself (CCC 1790; *Veritatis Splendor* 60; ST I-II q.19 a.5, from [2.4](02-04-conscience-its-nature-and-its-errors.md)).
3. Conscience can err from invincible ignorance without losing its dignity. It does lose it when a person cares little for truth and grows blind through habitual sin (GS 16; VS 62–63).

**The primacy that is refused.**

4. *Veritatis Splendor* (1993) 54–56 describes a "creative" understanding of conscience. On this view norms offer only a general orientation. Conscience makes "decisions" rather than "judgments". A pastoral solution may then allow in practice what the moral law calls intrinsically evil. VS 56 rejects this "double status of moral truth". Conscience applies the law. It does not grant exceptions to negative precepts.
5. Conscience's authority derives from the truth it expresses: it bears witness to the law and does not establish it (VS 60–61). The Magisterium does not bring the Christian conscience truths foreign to it. It helps conscience see what it ought already to hold (VS 64).
6. A different point: conscience is a practical judgment about what to do, not a verdict on whether a doctrine is true. So appealing to conscience cannot justify dissent from a doctrinal pronouncement. Setting up a "supreme magisterium of conscience" against the Church's is ruled out (*Donum Veritatis*, CDF 1990, 38; see also 28). *In words:* conscience governs acts, not doctrines, which is Newman's own distinction.

**A theologian's deepening: Ratzinger's anamnesis.** In "Conscience and Truth" (Dallas, February 1991, given as a cardinal), Joseph Ratzinger kept the two scholastic levels, *synderesis* and *conscientia* ([2.4](02-04-conscience-its-nature-and-its-errors.md)). He proposed renaming the first level [*anamnesis*](../reference.md#anamnesis): an original memory of the good implanted in us, the law "written on their hearts" (Rom 2:14–15). On this account the Pope's authority is the authority of that memory. He does not impose commandments from outside. He is the advocate of the Christian memory, so the toast to conscience rightly comes first. Ratzinger also moves the question of guilt earlier. He rejects the view, which he says he heard defended, that the SS were justified by certainty of conscience. The guilt of an untroubled wrong conscience, he argues, lies further back: in stifling the memory until it could no longer protest. That is 2.4's "vincible" ignorance, given a phenomenology.

**The live dispute: *Amoris Laetitia* ch. 8.** *Amoris Laetitia* (2016) 301–302 builds on the traditional distinction between an objective situation and a person's imputability (CCC 1735). Then 303 goes further. It says conscience should be better incorporated into the Church's practice in situations that do not objectively match the Church's understanding of marriage. Conscience, it says, can recognize what is for now the most generous response a person can give to God, and see with "a certain moral security" that this is what God is asking, although it is not yet the full objective ideal. One reading says 303 is about culpability and growth, the "law of gradualness" that *Familiaris Consortio* 34 distinguished from a "gradualness of the law" (AL 295 repeats the distinction). The other reading says 303 lets conscience rest in an objectively disordered act as God's will for now, in tension with VS 56. In 2016 four cardinals put formal questions (*dubia*) to Francis. The fifth asked whether VS 56's rejection of a "creative" conscience still held after AL 303. Francis did not reply to them publicly. The sacramental question is ceded to [`sacraments-and-liturgy`](../../sacraments-and-liturgy/syllabus.md). Here only the reading of conscience matters.

**Mark the levels.** That one must never act against a certain conscience is taught by an ecumenical council (GS 16), repeated in VS 60 and CCC 1790: **authentic ordinary magisterium**, never defined, and long held as common teaching. VS's rejection of the "creative" conscience is **at least authentic ordinary magisterium**. It is tied to the existence of intrinsically evil acts, whose status as *definitive* teaching is itself argued (4.4). *Donum Veritatis* is a CDF instruction approved by the pope: **authentic ordinary magisterium, non-definitive**. *Amoris Laetitia* is an apostolic exhortation, so its teaching is **authentic ordinary magisterium**. **How 303 should be read is freely disputed.** Newman's *Letter* and Ratzinger's lecture are **theology**, carrying no magisterial level. CCC 1778's quotation adopts Newman's sentence, not the rest of his theory.

## Map of positions

```mermaid
graph TD
    Q["'Conscience is supreme' -<br/>supreme over what?"] --> A{"Is the question what I must do<br/>here and now, or what is true or good?"}
    A -->|"what I must do now"| B["Primacy of obligation:<br/>never act against a certain conscience<br/>GS 16, VS 60, CCC 1790"]
    A -->|"whether a doctrine is true"| C["Not conscience's field:<br/>a practical judgment, not a doctrinal one<br/>Donum Veritatis 38; Newman §5"]
    A -->|"whether this act is good"| D{"Does conscience apply the law,<br/>or make it?"}
    D -->|"applies it, and must be formed"| E["Conscience as herald or vicar<br/>VS 58-64; Newman; Ratzinger"]
    D -->|"decides it, even against<br/>a negative precept"| F["'Creative' conscience:<br/>rejected, VS 54-56"]
    B -.->|"binds even when in error;<br/>excuses only if the error is invincible"| G["2.4: q.19 a.5-6"]
```

The dashed edge is where primacy and culpability meet. A person who must follow a wrong conscience may be excused, or may be guilty for having let it go wrong. Either way, following it does not make the act good (VS 63).

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean): the practical collision Newman allows.** Invented case: a religious sister is ordered by her superior to sign a document backdating a patient's consent. She is certain this is a lie, and she has prayed, taken advice and reread the relevant teaching. Run the map. This is a question of *what I do*, not of a doctrine's truth. Her certain conscience binds her (step 2), so she refuses. She does not claim a right to decide what lying is: she applies the constant teaching that lying is wrong, so conscience is herald here, not legislator. The superior's order is a command, not teaching. It is exactly Newman's case of a legitimate collision, and she accepts the consequences patiently, as the authority Newman cites (Jacobatius) says one must.

**Example 2 (strain): the theologian who cannot assent.** A theologian has studied a non-definitive teaching for years and remains unpersuaded. Does "primacy of conscience" settle what he may hold? Point 6 says no. His difficulty concerns the truth of a proposition, and conscience does not judge that. *Donum Veritatis* does allow the difficulty. It tells him to lay it before the competent authority, and it rules out a public campaign ([`fundamental-theology` 4.3](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-03-religious-submission.md)). Conscience returns at the level of action. He must not teach, as the Church's teaching, what he believes false, and he must not pretend to assent he lacks. How much room religious submission leaves such a man is the question Boss 2 asks you to argue. The map only shows that "conscience" is the wrong name for his problem.

## Watch out

- **You might think CCC 1778 makes Newman's theory Church teaching.** The Catechism adopts one sentence, whose meaning it fixes by its context: conscience as God's messenger. Newman's *Letter* stays theology, however good. Calling it magisterial overstates it.
- **You might think "primacy of conscience" is a Vatican II novelty or a dissenter's slogan.** The binding force of an erring conscience is Aquinas (q.19 a.5), and the manuals Newman cites held it as certain. The novelty VS rejects is conscience as author.
- **You might treat the reading of AL 303 as settled either way.** The exhortation is authentic magisterium; which reading is right is disputed. Calling the permissive reading "heresy", or the restrictive reading "dissent from Francis", overstates a level.
- **You might equate sincerity with a formed conscience.** GS 16 and Ratzinger both place guilt *before* the certain judgment, in the neglect that produced it.

## One-liner

> Conscience is supreme as the place where God's law reaches me, never as the author of that law; Newman's toast puts it first because it is Christ's oldest vicar, not his rival.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢)** *Diagnose.* An invented talk to a parish adult-education group:

> "Newman taught that conscience comes before the Pope, and the Catechism itself quotes him. So when my conscience tells me a Church teaching is mistaken, say on some moral question, I am obliged to reject it. Anything else would be acting against conscience, which Vatican II calls a sanctuary."

**(a)** *(Exegetical.)* Name two distinct errors in the argument, each with the text or distinction that corrects it. **(b)** *(Exegetical.)* State what is *true* in the talk, with its level. **Three sentences for (a), one for (b).**

**P2 (🟡)** *Find the crux.* Two readings of *Amoris Laetitia* 303, paraphrased:

- **Reading A:** 303 is about imputability and growth. Conscience can recognize that a situation falls short of the Gospel and that the person, given real limits, cannot yet do more. It can judge that God asks this next step, without judging the objectively disordered act to be good. This is the "law of gradualness" of *Familiaris Consortio* 34, fully compatible with VS 56.
- **Reading B:** 303 says conscience can see with moral security that God himself is asking this response now, even though it falls short of the objective norm. That is a judgment that the act is what God wills for this person, which VS 56 calls a "double status of moral truth".

**(a)** *(Exegetical.)* Name the single phrase in 303 whose interpretation divides them, and state what each reading takes it to mean. **(b)** *(Evaluative.)* Which reading fits the text of AL 295–303 better? Take either side, and state the strongest point for the other. **150 words or fewer for (b).**

**P3 (🟡)** *Level assignment.* Give each claim its level on the [ladder](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-04-the-ladder-of-doctrinal-authority.md), **naming the act that fixes it** (or saying that none does). **Two sentences each.** *(Exegetical.)*

(i) A person must never act against the certain judgment of his conscience.
(ii) Conscience cannot legitimately authorize an exception to a negative precept forbidding an intrinsically evil act.
(iii) The first level of conscience is best understood as *anamnesis*, a memory of the good.
(iv) Conscience is the aboriginal Vicar of Christ.

<details>
<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical (a) · Exegetical (b))*

**Must hit, strict (a):** any two of: (1) **category error**: whether a teaching is *true* is not a judgment of conscience, which concerns acts here and now (DV 38; Newman §5 says conscience judges no "speculative truth"). (2) **Authorship**: conscience applies the law and does not decide it; the Magisterium helps conscience see what it should already hold (VS 60, 64), and "creative" conscience is rejected (VS 54–56). (3) **Level inflation**: CCC 1778 quotes one sentence of Newman; it does not make his *Letter*, or the speaker's gloss on it, Church teaching. (4) **Newman misused**: he denies that conscience and papal infallibility can collide directly, and the collision he allows is over a practical command.
**Must hit, strict (b):** one must never act against a certain conscience: GS 16, VS 60, CCC 1790, **authentic ordinary magisterium** (not defined).
**Wrong turns:** answering with Newman's conditions for resisting a command (the question is what the speaker gets wrong about the *object* of conscience, not whether he met the conditions). Saying the true part is "infallible" or "dogma".
**Model answer:** (a) The speaker treats conscience as a judge of whether teachings are true, but conscience is a practical judgment about acts, and DV 38 says it cannot legitimate dissent from a doctrinal pronouncement. Newman makes the same point: conscience judges conduct, not doctrine. He also treats conscience as author of the norm, which VS 54–56 rejects as "creative" conscience. (b) True: one must never act against a certain conscience (GS 16, CCC 1790), authentic ordinary magisterium.

**P2** *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b))*

**Must hit, strict (a):** the phrase: conscience sees "with a certain moral security" that the response is "what God himself is asking", while it is not yet the objective ideal. A takes "what God is asking" to mean *the step this person can take now*, a judgment about growth and culpability, with the norm untouched. B takes it as a judgment that *this act* is what God wills for this person, so conscience approves conduct the norm forbids.
**Must hit, any verdict (b):** use the text: A can cite AL 301–302's framing in imputability (CCC 1735) and 295's explicit "not a gradualness of law". B can cite 303's move *beyond* recognizing limits ("can do more than recognize…") and the positive language of what God asks. State the other side's best point and meet it. Mark the level: the exhortation is authentic magisterium, and its reading is freely disputed.
**Wrong turns:** deciding by who defends which reading, or by the *dubia*'s unanswered status. Treating either reading as heresy or dissent. Drifting into the sacramental question, which is ceded.
**Model answer (b), one of several:** Reading A fits better. AL 295 expressly keeps FC 34's line against a "gradualness of the law", and 301–302 frame the whole section in imputability. "What God is asking" most naturally names the next step a limited person can take, not approval of the act. The strongest point for B is that 303 says conscience "can do more than" recognize a shortfall. If it only confirmed reduced culpability, that clause would add nothing. My reply: what it adds is a positive discernment of growth, which is more than excuse and less than approval. The phrase "while yet not fully the objective ideal" keeps the norm in force. The text is authentic magisterium; its reading remains freely disputed.

**P3** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict:** (i) **Authentic ordinary magisterium**: taught by an ecumenical council (GS 16), repeated in VS 60 and CCC 1790, and common teaching since Aquinas (q.19 a.5); no act defines it. (ii) **At least authentic ordinary magisterium**: VS 56, with 54–64. Because it rests on the existence of intrinsically evil acts, whether it is also *definitive* is argued (4.4), and either answer is acceptable if the dispute is named. (iii) **No magisterial level**: a theologian's proposal (Ratzinger, 1991 lecture, as cardinal). His later election as pope does not raise it. (iv) **The sentence as the Catechism uses it** is adopted in CCC 1778, an authentic magisterial text, to mean that conscience is God's messenger. **Newman's theory as a whole** has no magisterial level.
**Wrong turns:** calling (i) *de fide* or (ii) simply "infallible". Promoting (iii) because the author became Benedict XVI. Treating the Catechism's quotation as canonizing Newman's whole *Letter*, or dismissing (iv) as "just Newman" when the Catechism adopts it.
**Model answer:** (i) Authentic ordinary magisterium: GS 16, VS 60 and CCC 1790 teach it, and it has been common teaching since Aquinas, but nothing defines it. (ii) At least authentic ordinary magisterium (VS 56); whether it is definitive depends on the disputed status of the teaching on intrinsically evil acts. (iii) A theological opinion (Ratzinger, 1991) with no magisterial level, whoever later held office. (iv) The Catechism adopts the sentence in CCC 1778, meaning conscience as God's messenger, but Newman's wider theory stays theology.

</details>

## Flashback

**From Lesson [2.3](02-03-intention-and-circumstances.md) (Intention and circumstances):** *(Exegetical, close reading.)* ST I-II q.20 a.2, "Whether the whole goodness and malice of the external action depends on the goodness of the will?" (English Dominican Province), from the body and the second reply:

> ... for a thing to be evil, one single defect suffices, whereas, for it to be good simply, it is not enough for it to be good in one point only, it must be good in every respect. If therefore the will be good, both from its proper object and from its end, ... the external action is good. But if the will be good from its intention of the end, this is not enough to make the external action good: and if the will be evil either by reason of its intention of the end, or by reason of the act willed, it follows that the external action is evil.
>
> *Reply to Objection 2.* A man sins by his will, not only when he wills an evil end; but also when he wills an evil act.

The second objection had argued from Augustine that there is no sin without the will, so all malice in the deed comes from the will. (a) Which two things can make the will good or evil here, and which font of the three does each correspond to? Which words show that a good intention is necessary but not sufficient? (b) Does Reply 2 deny Augustine's principle? What does it change? **Two sentences per part.**

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):** the will is good or evil from its **proper object**, which the parallel clause calls **"the act willed"**: the **object** font. It is also good or evil from its **end**, "its intention of the end": the **end** font. Not sufficient: "if the will be good from its intention of the end, this is not enough to make the external action good". Necessary: "if the will be evil ... by reason of its intention of the end ... the external action is evil", resting on "one single defect suffices".

**Must hit, strict (b):** **No.** It keeps "no sin without the will" but widens what the will wills. The will wills the act as well as the end, so willing an evil act is already a sin of the will, even with a good end. The objection assumed the will's goodness is fixed by the end alone.

**Wrong turns:** reading "proper object" as the end. Elsewhere (a.1) Aquinas calls the end the will's proper object, but here the passage's own parallel, "the act willed", fixes the sense. Reading Reply 2 as abandoning Augustine.

**Model answer:** (a) The will is good or evil from the act it wills, which is the object font, and from the end it intends, which is the end font. "If the will be good from its intention of the end, this is not enough" shows a good end is not sufficient; "evil ... by reason of its intention of the end" shows it is necessary, since one defect suffices. (b) No: Reply 2 keeps the principle that all sin is in the will. It denies that the will is judged by its end alone, since willing an evil act is itself a sin of the will.

</details>
