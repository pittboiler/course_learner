# Moral Theology · Reference Card

> Open book. This card is meant to be open while you work — including during
> quizzes. Nothing here is something you're expected to recall cold.

Every question in this course is one of three: **what is the act** (its object,
end and circumstances, and how voluntary it was), **what does the law say about
acts of that kind** (natural law, the New Law, the Magisterium's teaching), and
**how much weight does that teaching carry**. The card is the lookup surface for
all three: the Thomist machinery, the manual tools that survive, the documents
with their dates, and the rung of every teaching whose weight matters. Levels come
from [`fundamental-theology` 4.4](../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-04-the-ladder-of-doctrinal-authority.md),
which owns the ladder; where that course and this card differ, that course wins.
Levels are graded **strictly both ways**: promoting a teaching past its act and
demoting it below its act are the same error facing opposite directions.

## Levels at a glance

The spine of the course. Each row is a teaching, its rung on the ladder of
[`fundamental-theology` 4.4](../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-04-the-ladder-of-doctrinal-authority.md),
the act that fixes it, and where Catholics dispute it. Rung 1 is **defined** or
otherwise **proposed as divinely revealed** (by solemn judgment or by the ordinary
and universal magisterium); rung 2 **definitive**, to be held; rung 3 **authentic
ordinary magisterium**, owed religious submission; rung 4 **common teaching** of the
schools; rung 5 **freely disputed**. Rungs 1 and 2 differ in motive, not firmness.
Rungs 4 and 5 are theologians' notes: no magisterial act reaches them.

### The levels in moral theology

**Every moral claim whose weight matters carries a rung, and the rung is fixed by
an act, not by how grave the matter is or how often it is said.** The ladder is
[`fundamental-theology` 4.4](../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-04-the-ladder-of-doctrinal-authority.md)'s;
this course applies it to moral teaching, where four features make grading harder
than in dogma.

1. **Much moral teaching reaches rung 1 or 2 without a definition**, through the
   ordinary and universal magisterium: the bishops dispersed through the world, in
   communion with the pope, concurring in one judgment *as definitively to be held*
   (*Lumen Gentium* 25). "Never defined, so not binding" is the commonest
   understatement.
2. **An encyclical's genre sets a default (rung 3), and a formula can override
   it.** *Evangelium Vitae* 57, 62 and 65 use a formula of *confirmation*: by the
   authority of Peter, in communion with the bishops, the pope confirms a teaching
   of the ordinary and universal magisterium (citing LG 25). The verb is
   *confirm*, not *define*: these are not *ex cathedra* definitions, and the route
   must be named correctly as well as the rung.
3. **The 1998 CDF *Doctrinal Commentary* on the *Professio fidei* (§11) sorts
   examples.** Under its first paragraph (divinely revealed) it lists the doctrine
   on the grave immorality of directly and voluntarily killing an innocent human
   being, citing EV 57. Under its second paragraph (definitive) it discusses
   euthanasia (EV 65) and lists the illicitness of prostitution and fornication.
   It does **not** name abortion, contraception, or the general thesis that
   intrinsically evil acts exist; its list is not exhaustive, and its note 17 allows
   that the ordinary universal magisterium can be read *diachronically*.
4. **A catechism keeps its sources' weight** ([`fundamental-theology` 4.5](../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-05-reading-a-documents-weight.md)).
   CCC paragraphs neither raise nor lower a teaching; they report it.

**The four standing test cases** — present each exactly, neither collapsing it
upward nor downward:

- ***Evangelium Vitae* 57, 62, 65.** Three confirmations, not definitions. The
  commentary's placements (EV 57 under paragraph one, euthanasia under paragraph
  two, abortion unnamed) are the evidence the lessons weigh; the grading itself is
  reserved for the Boss problems of Modules 4 and 5: see [5.1](lessons/05-01-the-inviolability-of-innocent-life.md),
  [5.2](lessons/05-02-abortion.md), [5.3](lessons/05-03-euthanasia-and-the-end-of-life.md).
- **The existence of intrinsically evil acts.** At least rung 3 (VS 79-81, 115;
  RP 17); whether also rung 2 is **disputed**, and the commentary does not list the
  general thesis. See [Intrinsically evil acts](#intrinsically-evil-acts).
- ***Humanae Vitae*.** Not a definition; floor rung 3 at its heaviest grade;
  whether the norm was infallibly taught before 1968 is **disputed**. See
  [The level of Humanae Vitae](#the-level-of-humanae-vitae).
- **The 2018 text of CCC 2267.** A papal rescript inserting a Catechism text, with
  no defining formula and no appeal to the bishops' concurring teaching; see
  [5.4](lessons/05-04-the-death-penalty-and-ccc-2267.md) for the grading and
  [The death penalty and CCC 2267](#the-death-penalty-and-ccc-2267) for the dispute.

**Five habits keep the grading straight.**

- **Find the act.** A Father's axiom, a manual's consensus, a famous tag or a
  catechism paragraph is not an act.
- **Separate the norm from its application.** "Direct killing of the innocent is
  always wrong" and "this procedure is a direct killing" are two claims with two
  levels ([4.5](lessons/04-05-describing-the-object.md), [5.1](lessons/05-01-the-inviolability-of-innocent-life.md)).
- **Separate the norm from its arguments.** A weak argument does not lower a norm
  and a strong one does not raise it ([6.1](lessons/06-01-the-theology-of-the-body-and-chastity.md), [6.2](lessons/06-02-humanae-vitae-the-argument.md)).
- **Separate dogma from its explanation.** That grace is lost by mortal sin is
  defined; *which* acts are mortal is not ([3.4](lessons/03-04-sin-mortal-and-venial.md)). That charity is infused is
  defined; that it is the form of the virtues by efficient causality is the
  schools' ([3.2](lessons/03-02-faith-hope-and-charity.md)).
- **Rung 2 is not "less binding" and rung 3 is not "optional".** Rungs 1 and 2
  differ in motive; rung 3 asks religious submission of intellect and will
  ([`fundamental-theology` 4.3](../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-03-religious-submission.md)).

*Introduced:* [1.1](lessons/01-01-what-moral-theology-is.md); applied in every lesson, each ending its argument with a "Mark the levels" paragraph.

## The architecture of I-II

Aquinas's *Prima Secundae* begins with the end, then the acts that reach it, then
their intrinsic principles (passions, habits, virtues, and sin as their disorder),
then the extrinsic principles that instruct and help (law and grace). The course
follows that order, with II-II supplying the particular virtues and vices.

### The plan of ST I-II

**The end comes first; law and grace come last, as the principles that move us from
outside.** Sin sits among the intrinsic principles, as the disorder of a being made
for virtue. Conscience is not a treatise of I-II at all: Aquinas treats it as an
act of practical reason in Part I.

### What makes moral theology theology

**Revelation supplies two things reason cannot: the end (God himself, seen) and the
power to reach it (grace, the New Law).** Philosophy's natural law stays; theology
adds what makes it a road to *that* end.

- Sacred doctrine treats all things "under the aspect of God", as God or as
  referred to him as beginning and end (ST I q.1 a.7).
- Revelation was needed because man is directed to an end surpassing reason, and
  the end must be known before acts can be directed to it (ST I q.1 a.1).
- *Veritatis Splendor* chapter I (6-27) reads the rich young man (Mt 19:16-22):
  morality is asked about as the way to eternal life; the commandments are its
  entry; "follow me" is what is still lacking.

A moral theology that keeps only the commandments becomes minimalism; one that
keeps only vocation and happiness becomes sentiment without verdicts. The
confessor's verdicts are one part of the discipline, not the whole.

*Introduced:* [1.1](lessons/01-01-what-moral-theology-is.md)

### Freedom of indifference and freedom for excellence

**Is freedom greatest before any choice, or in the saint?** Servais Pinckaers, *The
Sources of Christian Ethics* (French 1985; English 1995), paraphrased: the manuals
inherited Ockham's *freedom of indifference*, the bare power to choose between
contraries prior to reason and inclination, so law could only arrive from outside
as a limit and obligation became the centre. His alternative, *freedom for
excellence*, is rooted in the natural inclinations to truth and goodness and grows
with virtue, as a musician grows freer by practice.

- **Level.** The genealogy has **no magisterial act** and is disputed: critics say
  the manuals were more varied and more Thomist than it allows. That freedom
  grows as one does good (CCC 1733) is **at least rung 3**, and CCC 1731 keeps the
  power of choice in the definition of freedom.
- Freedom for excellence does not deny choice; the dispute is what freedom is
  *for*. Whether choice is causally determined is
  [`metaphysics` 6.4](../metaphysics/lessons/06-04-libertarianism-and-its-critics.md)'s question.

*Introduced:* [1.1](lessons/01-01-what-moral-theology-is.md)

### The manual tradition

**The post-Tridentine textbooks for confessors: precise about sins, organized by
commandments and sacraments, with virtue moved elsewhere.** Trent (Session XIV,
1551, ch. 5 and can. 7) taught that every mortal sin remembered after diligent
examination must be confessed by divine law, with the circumstances that change its
species, and that the priest judges as well as absolves. Confessors needed a precise
science of sins; the *Institutiones morales* supplied it (Juan Azor's, vol. 1 1600,
is usually taken as the model). A general part treated human acts, conscience, law
and sin; virtue and growth in holiness moved to "ascetical" theology.

- At their best the manuals were precise, pastorally humane and often Thomist in
  principle; **casuistry** was their method ([4.1](lessons/04-01-casuistry-and-the-probabilism-controversy.md)).
- *Optatam Totius* 16 (1965) asked for a renewed scientific exposition, drawing
  more on Scripture and showing the faithful's calling in Christ. It is a
  directive, not a doctrine.
- *Veritatis Splendor* 76, paraphrased: the tradition's casuistry concerned only
  cases where the law was uncertain; it never questioned negative precepts that bind
  without exception. That is the line between casuistry and proportionalism.
- **Level:** Trent XIV can. 7 is **defined**; *Optatam Totius* 16 is off the
  ladder (a directive on teaching).

*Introduced:* [1.1](lessons/01-01-what-moral-theology-is.md); casuistry and its defence in [4.1](lessons/04-01-casuistry-and-the-probabilism-controversy.md)

### The last end

**Ask "and why that?" until the chain stops: the stopping point is happiness, and
everyone wants it; the question is where it is.** ST I-II q.1:

- no infinite regress of ends, or nothing would move desire (a.4);
- one last end per person, since two perfect goods would each leave the other
  still to want (a.5; Mt 6:24);
- everything is willed for it, without thinking of it each time (a.6 ad 3);
- all agree on its *aspect* (the fulfilment of their perfection) and disagree on
  the *thing* in which it lies; the sinner seeks happiness in the wrong place (a.7
  and ad 1).

**The elimination (q.2).** Wealth serves something else (a.1); honour and fame are
signs of a good, not the good (aa.2-3); power can be used ill (a.4); bodily goods
serve something beyond themselves (a.5); pleasure is a proper accident following
possession of a good (a.6); the soul's own goods are potencies (a.7). Four general
reasons dispose of the external goods together: bad people have them; they leave
needs unmet; they can harm their owner; they come from fortune (a.4). The
Source-sentence of a.8: happiness is the perfect good "which lulls the appetite
altogether"; the will's object is the universal good, found in no creature, because
every creature has goodness "by participation".

- **Level.** That no created good is man's last end is **common teaching (rung 4)**
  restated in CCC 1723; each of Aquinas's proofs weighs only what it proves.
- The elimination demotes created goods; it does not despise them. Each is good
  when ordered to the end.

*Introduced:* [1.2](lessons/01-02-beatitude-the-last-end.md)

### The beatific vision

**The intellect rests only when it knows *what* the first cause is, so perfect
happiness is seeing God's essence.** ST I-II q.3: happiness is an operation, the
last act of the highest power (a.2); its essence is an act of the intellect, with
delight in the will as its proper accident (a.4); "final and perfect happiness can
consist in nothing else than the vision of the Divine Essence" (a.8; 1 Jn 3:2).

- **The magisterial text.** Benedict XII, *Benedictus Deus* (1336), says with
  apostolic authority that the souls of the blessed see the divine essence face to
  face and are made blessed by that vision and enjoyment. The level is reserved to
  Boss 1(c): see [1.2](lessons/01-02-beatitude-the-last-end.md).
- **Disputed.** Whether beatitude is *essentially* an act of intellect (Thomists)
  or of will (Scotus and the Franciscans) is **freely disputed (rung 5)**; the
  Scotist view is fully Catholic.
- CCC 1718-1723 reads the Beatitudes as the answer to the desire for happiness.

*Introduced:* [1.2](lessons/01-02-beatitude-the-last-end.md)

### Perfect and imperfect happiness

**Aristotle's happiness is real but belongs to this life; Aquinas keeps it and
demotes it.** Contemplative happiness makes us happy "only as men" (q.3 a.2 ad 4,
quoting *Ethics* I.10). The two differ in species: imperfect happiness, the life of
virtue and contemplation, is reachable by natural powers; perfect happiness exceeds
every created nature and comes only as God's gift (q.5 a.5). Earthly life has "a
certain participation" of happiness (q.5 a.3); speculative science is itself such a
participation (q.3 a.6).

- **The Beatitudes** (Mt 5:3-12): the Fathers split on whether their rewards
  belong to this life (Augustine), the next (Ambrose) or both (Chrysostom); Aquinas
  answers both: perfect hereafter, begun here, "the first signs of fruit" (I-II q.69
  a.2).
- That beatitude is supernatural and gratuitous is **at least rung 3** (*Humani
  Generis* 26; CCC 1722, 1727).
- Contrast [`ethics` 4.1](../ethics/lessons/04-01-aristotle-the-human-good.md):
  Aristotle's completeness and self-sufficiency tests survive, as marks of a goal
  no earthly life meets.

*Introduced:* [1.2](lessons/01-02-beatitude-the-last-end.md)

### Natural desire to see God

**Does human nature itself desire the vision, and if so, how can the vision be a
free gift?** Henri de Lubac's *Surnaturel* (1946) read Aquinas as teaching an innate
desire for the vision and attacked the "pure nature" hypothesis he traced to
Cajetan. The Thomist commentators read the desire of q.3 a.8 as *elicited*,
awakened by knowledge, with a natural end God could in principle have given
humanity. Both must respect *Humani Generis* 26 (1950): God could create
intellectual beings without ordering and calling them to the vision. Whether de
Lubac's own position fell under that clause is itself argued.

- Aquinas's argument needs the premise that nature makes no desire in vain; that
  is exactly where the dispute begins ([1.2](lessons/01-02-beatitude-the-last-end.md), Example 2).
- The level of this claim is reserved to Boss 1(c): see [1.2](lessons/01-02-beatitude-the-last-end.md). The debate
  itself belongs to [`creation-grace-last-things`](../creation-grace-last-things/syllabus.md).

*Introduced:* [1.2](lessons/01-02-beatitude-the-last-end.md)

### Eternal law and natural law

**Natural law is God's reason governing us, received the way a rational creature
receives things: by understanding it.** Eternal law is God's reason governing
everything (q.91 a.1). Every creature shares in it through its inclinations; the
rational creature shares "in the most excellent way", by being provident for itself
and others, and that rational share is natural law (q.91 a.2; CCC 1951-1952).

- **Scripture.** Romans 2:14-15: the Gentiles "shew the work of the law written in
  their hearts, their conscience bearing witness to them" (Douay-Rheims). Aquinas
  uses it as the *sed contra* of q.91 a.2 and q.100 a.1. Augustine read these
  Gentiles chiefly as Christian Gentiles, allowing pagans as a second possibility
  (*On the Spirit and the Letter* 43-48); the exegesis is
  [`new-testament` 5.2](../new-testament/lessons/05-02-romans-1-4-the-righteousness-of-god.md)'s.
- **Level.** That a natural moral law exists and is knowable is **at least rung 3**
  (HV 4; CCC 1954-1960). The participation account is **common teaching (rung 4)**:
  the Catechism adopts the vocabulary, not the metaphysics.
- The philosophical build (first principle, inclinations, precepts) is
  [`ethics` 4.4](../ethics/lessons/04-04-natural-law-aquinas.md) and
  [4.5](../ethics/lessons/04-05-the-new-natural-law-theory.md), not retaught.

*Introduced:* [1.3](lessons/01-03-natural-law-in-light-of-revelation.md)

### The Decalogue as natural law

**Every moral precept of the Old Law is natural law, "but not all in the same way"
(q.100 a.1).** Three tiers sort ways of *knowing*, not importance:

1. grasped by any reason at once: honour parents, do not kill, do not steal;
2. grasped by the wise, who teach others: honour the aged (Lev 19:32);
3. grasped only with divine instruction: no graven images, no taking God's name in
   vain.

The Decalogue gathers what man has "immediately from God" (q.100 a.3). The Sabbath
is a split case: moral in giving time to God, ceremonial in fixing the day (q.100
a.3 ad 2). CCC 2070 calls the Decalogue a privileged expression of natural law and
quotes Irenaeus: God planted the precepts in the heart, then reminded us of them.

- **Revelation adds certainty, not obligation** (CCC 2071).
- **Level.** That the commandments bind Christians is **defined** (Trent VI can.
  19). The three tiers are **common teaching (rung 4)**.
- The moral/ceremonial/judicial division is
  [`old-testament` 2.6](../old-testament/lessons/02-06-covenant-and-law.md)'s.

*Introduced:* [1.3](lessons/01-03-natural-law-in-light-of-revelation.md)

### The darkening of reason

**Sin does not delete the moral sense; it miswires the detail, and whole cultures
can share the miswiring.** Aquinas, I-II q.99 a.2 ad 2: human reason "could not go
astray in the abstract, as to the universal principles of the natural law; but
through being habituated to sin, it became obscured in the point of things to be
done in detail." The secondary precepts can be blotted out "by evil persuasions" or
"vicious customs and corrupt habits" (q.94 a.6). Theft was once not thought wrong
among the Germans (q.94 **a.4**, from Caesar). Paul: "their foolish heart was
darkened" (Rom 1:21).

- Failure is "as to knowledge", not rectitude: the general principles are the same
  for all.
- The tension with q.100 a.1 (theft is tier 1) dissolves once you see that q.100
  describes reason working as made, q.94 reason "perverted by passion, or evil
  habit".
- **Level.** Aquinas's analysis, **rung 4**.

*Introduced:* [1.3](lessons/01-03-natural-law-in-light-of-revelation.md); the invincible-ignorance use is [2.4](lessons/02-04-conscience-its-nature-and-its-errors.md)

### Moral necessity of revelation

**Reason can know the moral law, but after sin it knows it late, patchily and with
error; revelation lets all know it with ease, certainty and no error.** Aquinas's
second reason for a divine law is the uncertainty of human judgment on particulars
(q.91 a.4); the same pattern governs truths about God (ST I q.1 a.1).

- **Vatican I, *Dei Filius* ch. 2**: revelation lets such truths be known by all
  "with facility, with firm assurance, and with no admixture of error". Its subject
  is "things divine"; it is a conciliar **chapter, not a canon**.
- **Extension to moral truths**: Pius XII, *Humani Generis* (1950); CCC 1960
  (citing both) and 2071. **At least rung 3.**
- *Moral* necessity is not *absolute*: without revelation the precepts stay
  knowable, but not reliably known.
- The natural knowledge of God behind this is
  [`fundamental-theology` 1.2](../fundamental-theology/lessons/01-02-natural-knowledge-of-god.md).

*Introduced:* [1.3](lessons/01-03-natural-law-in-light-of-revelation.md)

### Magisterial competence over natural law

**The Church claims to interpret the natural law, not because it knows ethics
better, but because the natural law is part of what God wills for salvation.**
*Humanae Vitae* 4 (1968), paraphrased: no member of the faithful could deny it, and
his predecessors often claimed it; in sending the apostles to teach his commandments
Christ made them guardians and interpreters of the *whole* moral law, natural law
included, because that law too declares God's will and keeping it is necessary for
salvation. CCC 2036 gives the same reason.

- **What is disputed.** Whether the Magisterium can teach a specific,
  non-revealed natural-law norm *infallibly*, as part of infallibility's secondary
  object ([`fundamental-theology` 4.2](../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-02-infallibility-and-its-conditions.md)).
  That question decides *Humanae Vitae*'s level and belongs to [6.3](lessons/06-03-humanae-vitae-its-authority.md).
- The level of the competence claim itself is reserved to Boss 1(c): see
  [1.3](lessons/01-03-natural-law-in-light-of-revelation.md).

*Introduced:* [1.3](lessons/01-03-natural-law-in-light-of-revelation.md)

### The New Law

**The new thing in the New Law is a gift, not a text: chiefly the grace of the Holy
Spirit, given through faith in Christ and working through charity; secondarily a
written law.** ST I-II q.106 a.1: a thing is named from what preponderates in it,
and what predominates here, "whereon all its efficacy is based", is grace. Augustine
calls the divine laws written on the heart "the very presence of the Holy Spirit"
(*On the Spirit and the Letter* ch. 36). The written element (what to believe, the
sacraments, what to do) disposes to grace and directs its use.

- **The letter kills** (q.106 a.2): as to the written element the New Law does not
  justify; the "letter" is any writing external to man, "even that of the moral
  precepts such as are contained in the Gospel" (2 Cor 3:6).
- **Not natural law renamed** (a.1 ad 2): it is added to nature by grace, and helps
  as well as shows.
- **Fulfils the Old** (q.107 aa.1-2): one end, so not different in kind; perfect to
  imperfect. The moral precepts are fulfilled three ways: their true sense (interior
  acts), the safest way to keep them, added counsels.
- **The Sermon on the Mount** contains "the whole process of forming the life of a
  Christian" (q.108 a.3); Augustine called it a perfect standard of the Christian
  life (*On the Sermon on the Mount* I.1).
- **Lighter outwardly, heavier inwardly** (q.107 a.4).
- **Level.** Aquinas's analysis is **common teaching (rung 4)**; CCC 1965-1974
  adopts it almost verbatim, which does not raise the rung.

*Introduced:* [1.4](lessons/01-04-the-new-law.md)

### Precepts and counsels

**A precept obliges because it concerns what is necessary for beatitude; a counsel
is left to one's option and makes the end "more assured and expeditious" (q.108
a.4).** A person can use the world's goods and still reach the end, provided he does
not make them his end. The three counsels (poverty, chastity, obedience) answer the
three concupiscences of 1 Jn 2:16; they are kept absolutely in religious life and in
particular cases by anyone.

- **Law of liberty** (q.108 a.1): outward works that necessarily fit or contradict
  "faith that worketh through love" are commanded or forbidden; the rest are left to
  each person or to lawful superiors.
- The rich young man: "If thou wilt be perfect" (Mt 19:21) proposes a counsel;
  clinging to wealth *as one's end* is what the precepts forbid.
- Perfection consists essentially in the precepts of charity (CCC 1973-1974). The
  counsels are means, not a higher tier of obligation.

*Introduced:* [1.4](lessons/01-04-the-new-law.md)

### Legalism and antinomianism

**Two mirror errors, each keeping half of "chiefly grace, secondarily written".**
The legalist keeps the written element and loses grace; the antinomian keeps the
Spirit and drops the precepts.

- **Legalism**: the Pelagian is the classic case
  ([`patristics` 4.5](../patristics/lessons/04-05-augustine-against-the-pelagians.md));
  Trent VI can. 1 anathematizes justification by works done without grace.
  Pinckaers's charge against the manuals is a milder institutional form.
- **Antinomianism**: named from Luther's dispute with Johann Agricola in the late
  1530s, so the Reformers fought it too. Trent VI cann. 19-21 condemn saying that
  only faith is commanded in the Gospel, that the Decalogue does not concern
  Christians, that the justified are bound "only to believe", and that Christ was
  given as a redeemer to trust "and not also as a legislator whom to obey".
- **Level.** Cann. 1 and 19-21 are **defined (rung 1)**.
- **Keepable, not trivial.** Trent VI (13 January 1547) ch. 11 takes over
  Augustine's formula (*On Nature and Grace* 43.50): "God commands not
  impossibilities, but, by commanding, both admonishes thee to do what thou are
  able, and to pray for what thou art not able" (Waterworth); can. 18 is the matching
  canon. Its level is reserved to Boss 1(c): see [1.4](lessons/01-04-the-new-law.md). The same chapter grants
  that the just fall into light and daily sins without ceasing to be just.
- James on faith and works is
  [`new-testament` 6.3](../new-testament/lessons/06-03-james-faith-and-works.md).

*Introduced:* [1.4](lessons/01-04-the-new-law.md)

### Universal call to holiness

**Perfection is for everyone; the counsels are one road to it.** *Lumen Gentium* 40
(1964): the faithful of every state are called to the fullness of Christian life and
the perfection of charity (Mt 5:48); LG 42 presents the counsels as one means (CCC
2013, 1973-1974).

- **Level.** Conciliar teaching in a dogmatic constitution that defines nothing new:
  **at least rung 3**.
- It answers the manual split between moral theology (for everyone) and ascetical
  theology (for the keen).

*Introduced:* [1.4](lessons/01-04-the-new-law.md)

## Human acts and conscience

Three questions, in order: is the act *his* (the voluntary)? What *kind* of act is
it (the three fonts)? And did he judge it rightly (conscience)?

### Human act and act of a man

**Only acts a person is master of are praised or blamed.** A *human act* proceeds
from deliberate reason and will; an *act of a man* is anything else that happens in
a human body, like stroking one's beard while thinking of something else (ST I-II
q.1 a.1 and ad 3). Moral theology judges only the first kind.

*Introduced:* [2.1](lessons/02-01-the-voluntary.md)

### The voluntary

**An act is voluntary when it comes from within, with knowledge of what it is
for.** ST I-II q.6: the voluntary proceeds from an intrinsic principle with
knowledge of the end (a.1); animals have an imperfect voluntary, reason the perfect
one that deserves praise or blame (a.2). **Omissions** are voluntary when one could
and ought to act: the helmsman who stops steering (a.3). Four things seem to reduce
the voluntary, and they do so differently:

| Factor | Effect (q.6) | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Violence | causes involuntariness simply, but only in commanded acts; the will's own act cannot be forced (aa.4-5) | physical force, will concurring "not at all" |
| Fear | the [mixed voluntary](#the-mixed-voluntary): voluntary simply (a.6) | grave fear can diminish imputability (CCC 1735) |
| Concupiscence | makes the act *more* voluntary (a.7) | antecedent passion diminishes (q.77 a.6); passion removing reason excuses unless willed in its cause (q.77 a.7) |
| Ignorance | depends on its relation to the will (a.8) | see [Kinds of ignorance](#kinds-of-ignorance) |

- **Level.** Free will is real and was not destroyed by Adam's sin: **defined**
  (Trent VI can. 5). That ignorance, inadvertence, duress, fear, habit and similar
  factors can diminish or remove imputability: **rung 3** (CCC 1734-1735, 1860).
  The q.6 machinery: **common teaching (rung 4)**.
- Freedom as a metaphysical question (determinism, compatibilism, libertarianism)
  is [`metaphysics` 6.1](../metaphysics/lessons/06-01-determinism-and-the-consequence-argument.md)-[6.4](../metaphysics/lessons/06-04-libertarianism-and-its-critics.md).

*Introduced:* [2.1](lessons/02-01-the-voluntary.md)

### The mixed voluntary

**A threat changes what you choose among, but you still choose.** The captain in the
storm throws the cargo overboard to save the ship: the act is voluntary *simply*,
because it is what he actually chooses here and now, and involuntary only "in a
certain respect", compared with a calm sea that is not there. So, with Aristotle,
Aquinas calls it "voluntary rather than involuntary" (q.6 a.6).

- Grave fear can *diminish* imputability (CCC 1735); it does not turn a chosen act
  into something that happened to you. The apostate under torture has chosen,
  however much his guilt is lessened.
- Duress and cooperation: fear can supply the proportionate reason for remote
  material cooperation, never make close help with grave evil light
  ([Formal and material cooperation](#formal-and-material-cooperation)).

*Introduced:* [2.1](lessons/02-01-the-voluntary.md)

### Kinds of ignorance

**Ignorance excuses only insofar as it removes knowledge the act needed, and only if
the ignorance itself was not yours.** ST I-II q.6 a.8 sorts ignorance by its
relation to the will, not by when it occurs:

| Kind | Relation to the will | Aquinas's case | Effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| Concomitant | he would have acted anyway | shooting the enemy he wanted dead, taking him for a stag | non-voluntary; excuses nothing (q.76 aa.3-4) |
| Consequent: affected | ignorance willed on purpose, to have an excuse | | aggravates (q.76 a.4; CCC 1859) |
| Consequent: evil choice | passion or habit keeps him from considering what he knows | | diminishes, does not excuse |
| Consequent: negligence | he fails to learn what he ought to know | | diminishes, does not excuse |
| Antecedent | not voluntary, and the cause of the act | the archer who took "proper precaution" and killed an unseen passer-by | involuntary simply; excuses |

- "Consequent" means *following from the will*, not "arising after the act".
- The manuals compress this as vincible/invincible: see
  [Invincible ignorance](#invincible-ignorance).
- **Level: common teaching (rung 4)**; the Catechism uses parts of it (CCC 1736's
  "indirectly voluntary") without canonizing the whole.

*Introduced:* [2.1](lessons/02-01-the-voluntary.md)

### Invincible ignorance

**Ask whether he could, and should, have known.** Ignorance "entirely involuntary,
either through being invincible, or through being of matters one is not bound to
know" excuses altogether (q.76 a.3); vincible ignorance does not. "Vincible /
invincible" is the manuals' shorthand for consequent / antecedent; Aquinas uses
"invincible" here once.

- **In conscience** ([2.4](lessons/02-04-conscience-its-nature-and-its-errors.md)): it is the test for whether an erring conscience
  excuses (q.19 a.6). Where ignorance is invincible, the evil is not imputed but
  "remains no less an evil", and one must work to correct the error (CCC 1793; VS
  62-63).
- **In mortal sin** ([3.4](lessons/03-04-sin-mortal-and-venial.md)): non-culpable ignorance defeats "full knowledge",
  so grave matter done in invincible ignorance is not mortal sin (CCC 1860).
- **Its reach into the moral law.** No one is deemed ignorant of the *principles*
  of the moral law (CCC 1860). The manuals commonly held that *remote* conclusions
  could be invincibly unknown, for a time and in a given person (common teaching).
  No text draws the line between near and remote; it is judged case by case.
- **Level.** That invincible ignorance removes imputability while the act stays
  objectively disordered: **rung 3** (GS 16; VS 62-63; CCC 1790-1793).

*Introduced:* [2.1](lessons/02-01-the-voluntary.md); used in [2.4](lessons/02-04-conscience-its-nature-and-its-errors.md), [3.4](lessons/03-04-sin-mortal-and-venial.md)

### Voluntary in cause

**The will reaches an effect by reaching its cause.** A man "who wilfully gets drunk"
is "considered to do voluntarily whatever he does through being drunk" (q.77 a.7).
Passion that removes reason excuses only if it was not voluntary in its cause
(sickness excuses; wilful drunkenness does not). The Catechism's drunk driver: a bad
effect is imputable if it was foreseeable and avoidable (CCC 1737).

- **Drunkenness can also lessen the resulting sin**, sometimes by more than its own
  gravity adds (q.76 a.4 ad 2).
- **Habit strains it.** "Voluntary in cause" was built for a single wilful binge;
  habit laid down over years stretches it. The first acts are imputed as what they
  were; a later lapse is voluntary but diminished to the degree habit outruns
  reason (q.77 a.6); effort to resist counts as the will resisting. The tools give
  a structure, not a number ([2.1](lessons/02-01-the-voluntary.md) Example 2; [3.4](lessons/03-04-sin-mortal-and-venial.md) Example 2).

*Introduced:* [2.1](lessons/02-01-the-voluntary.md)

### The three fonts

**An act is good only if all three are good, and evil if any one fails**, like a
bridge that stands only if every span holds. The sources of morality are the
**object** chosen, the **end** or intention, and the **circumstances** (CCC 1750).
Aquinas takes the asymmetry from Dionysius: good needs the whole cause, evil
follows from any single defect (I-II q.18 a.4 ad 3; q.20 a.2; CCC 1755).

| Case | Object | End | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| alms to be admired | good | vainglory | evil (q.20 a.1; CCC 1753) |
| a lie for a children's hospital | disordered in itself | good | evil; the end lowers gravity only (CCC 1753) |
| stealing a chalice from a church | theft | | theft *and* sacrilege (q.18 a.10) |
| stealing 20 dollars or 20,000 dollars | theft | | same species, different gravity (q.18 a.11) |

- **Object first** (q.18 a.2); [the end is the form](#the-end-as-form-of-the-act)
  but not the whole; [some circumstances specify](#circumstances-that-change-species).
- **Diminished responsibility is not a changed act.** Fear and pressure bear on
  culpability ([2.1](lessons/02-01-the-voluntary.md)); they do not change the object.
- **Level.** That morality comes from the three together, the object first, and
  that a good intention cannot make an evil object good: **rung 3** (VS 77-82; CCC
  1750-1756), presented as constant teaching. The Thomist machinery behind it:
  **rung 4**.

*Introduced:* [2.2](lessons/02-02-the-moral-object.md); developed [2.3](lessons/02-03-intention-and-circumstances.md); applied to abortion [5.2](lessons/05-02-abortion.md)

### The moral object

**What you chose to do, as reason describes it: neither the bare motion nor the
motive behind it.** The object is the proximate end of the deliberate choice. Take a
coat off a rack: if it is yours, you use what is yours; if another's, you steal. The
arm does the same thing; what separates the acts is a feature only reason registers.
Steal it for a freezing beggar and you have still stolen it: the beggar is the end.

- ST I-II q.18 a.2: "an action has its species from its object, as movement from
  its term"; the primary goodness of a moral act comes from its suitable object
  ("to make use of what is one's own"), the primary evil from its object ("to take
  what belongs to another").
- **VS 78** (paraphrase): the object must be grasped from [the perspective of the
  acting person](#the-perspective-of-the-acting-person); it is a freely chosen kind
  of behaviour, the proximate end of a deliberate decision, not a process of the
  merely physical order judged by the state of affairs it produces.
- **Two errors.** The *physicalist* reads the object off the movement
  ([Physicalism](#physicalism)). The *intention-only* error lets any further purpose
  redescribe the choice.
- **Two senses of "intention".** VS 78 calls the object itself the proximate end;
  the "intention" among the three fonts is the *further* end. Confuse them and you
  can intend any object out of existence.
- **Used in** double effect's condition 2, the end-of-life line, direct abortion, the lie, GIFT, and the post-1993 dispute (lessons below).
- **Level.** The object as primary font: **rung 3**. *How* to describe it in hard
  cases: **rung 5** (VS 29).

*Introduced:* [2.2](lessons/02-02-the-moral-object.md); also [2.3](lessons/02-03-intention-and-circumstances.md), [4.2](lessons/04-02-double-effect-and-cooperation-as-catholic-method.md), [4.4](lessons/04-04-veritatis-splendor.md), [4.5](lessons/04-05-describing-the-object.md), [5.1](lessons/05-01-the-inviolability-of-innocent-life.md), [5.2](lessons/05-02-abortion.md), [5.3](lessons/05-03-euthanasia-and-the-end-of-life.md), [5.5](lessons/05-05-assisted-reproduction-and-the-embryo.md), [6.4](lessons/06-04-truthfulness-and-the-lie.md)

### Natural and moral species

**One bodily event can be two acts to reason.** "To kill a man" is one natural
species, which can be ordered to "the safeguarding of justice" or to "the satisfying
of anger" (I-II q.1 a.3 ad 3): the judge's act and the angry man's. The conjugal act
and adultery are one species to the generative power and two to reason (q.18 a.5 ad
3). Rule: "the species of moral actions are constituted by forms as conceived by the
reason" (q.18 a.10).

- A condition bearing specially on reason stops being an add-on and becomes a
  condition of the object ([Circumstances that change species](#circumstances-that-change-species)).
- **Level: rung 4**, Aquinas's analysis, never defined.

*Introduced:* [2.2](lessons/02-02-the-moral-object.md)

### Physicalism

**The charge that the manuals let biology settle morality.** After 1968 revisionist
moralists (Charles Curran popularized the word; Josef Fuchs and others made the
argument) said the manuals defined acts by physical structure: contraception as
frustrating a faculty, "direct" killing by what the hand did. Their remedy: treat
the bodily act and its harms as premoral evil and let the moral description emerge
only with intention and proportion ([4.3](lessons/04-03-proportionalism-and-the-fundamental-option.md)).

- **VS accepts the diagnosis, rejects the remedy.** VS 78: the object is not "a
  process or an event of the merely physical order"; VS 79 rejects the thesis that
  no chosen kind of behaviour can be judged evil by its object apart from intention
  and foreseeable consequences.
- Some of the encyclical's defenders (Rhonheimer most prominently) grant that some
  manual analyses read the object too physically.
- **Against *Humanae Vitae*.** The majority's charge that an act-by-act norm reads
  morality off the cycle ([6.2](lessons/06-02-humanae-vitae-the-argument.md)); the reply is that the chosen act, not the goal
  alone, is the object, and the body is the person, not his instrument.

*Introduced:* [2.2](lessons/02-02-the-moral-object.md); also [6.2](lessons/06-02-humanae-vitae-the-argument.md)

### The end as form of the act

**What you are really doing is what you are doing it *for* — but the end adds a
species; it never deletes one.** The interior act of will has the end as its object;
the exterior act has the thing it bears on. The will is formal, the deed material,
so the act takes its species formally from the end and materially from the object
(I-II q.18 a.6). Aristotle's example: "he who steals that he may commit adultery, is
strictly speaking, more adulterer than thief" (*Ethics* V.2, at q.18 a.6).

- **Double malice** (q.18 a.7): theft does not of itself lead to adultery, so the
  act falls under two unrelated species at once. Theft for alms is theft *and*
  almsgiving, which makes it evil.
- **Reused for charity** ([3.2](lessons/03-02-faith-hope-and-charity.md)): the premise of II-II q.23 a.8 is that in
  morals the form of an act is taken chiefly from the end.

*Introduced:* [2.3](lessons/02-03-intention-and-circumstances.md); also [3.2](lessons/03-02-faith-hope-and-charity.md)

### Circumstances that change species

**Most circumstances make an act better or worse by degree; a few change what kind
of act it is.** The classical seven (who, what, where, by what aids, why, how, when;
I-II q.7 a.3) are accidents of the act. The amount of a theft aggravates but leaves it
theft (q.18 a.11). A circumstance with a special relation to reason becomes the
principal condition of the object and specifies it: theft from a holy place is also
sacrilege (a.10).

- **CCC 1754** says circumstances cannot of themselves change the moral quality of
  acts; its next clause says what it means: they cannot make an evil act good.
  Aquinas's specifying circumstance makes it *worse*, and is folded into the object.
- **Consequences count as circumstances** (CCC 1754; VS 77): the seed of the
  argument against proportionalism.
- **The bread in extreme need** (II-II q.66 a.7 and ad 2): in manifest and urgent
  need with no other remedy, what a man takes to live becomes his by that need; the
  *object* changes. Whether that is redescription or weighing in disguise is
  [4.5](lessons/04-05-describing-the-object.md)'s fight.

*Introduced:* [2.3](lessons/02-03-intention-and-circumstances.md)

### The end does not justify the means

**A good end cannot rescue a bad object; a bad end spoils a good one.** From the
three fonts and the end-as-form: no end makes good an act whose object is evil.

- **Scripture.** Paul rejects the slander that he teaches "let us do evil that there
  may come good" (Rom 3:8, Douay-Rheims); the tradition's anchor (Augustine, *Against
  Lying* 1; VS 78; CCC 1756). Context: [`new-testament` 5.2](../new-testament/lessons/05-02-romans-1-4-the-righteousness-of-god.md).
- **Augustine, *Against Lying* 18** (NPNF 1.3), quoted at VS 81: it makes "very
  much difference, for what cause, with what end, with what intention a thing be
  done: but those things which are clearly sins, are upon no plea of a good cause
  ... to be done." Context: Consentius wanted to pose as a heretic to catch
  Priscillianists.
- **Level: rung 3** (CCC 1753-1756, 1759; VS 78-82), presented as constant
  teaching. Calling it "a prudential rule of thumb" understates it.
- **Not a ban on weighing.** Tolerating a lesser evil can be lawful; *doing* evil
  that good may come is not (VS 80, quoting HV 14).

*Introduced:* [2.3](lessons/02-03-intention-and-circumstances.md)

### Synderesis and conscientia

**The compass and the pilot's judgment.** *Synderesis* is a natural habit of the
first practical principles ("good is to be done, evil avoided"), about which no one
errs (ST I q.79 a.12). *Conscientia*, conscience proper, is an **act**: knowledge
*applied* to something (a.13). It does three jobs: it witnesses (I did or did not do
this); it binds or incites (this is to be done); it excuses, accuses or torments
(this was well or ill done). The name is sometimes stretched to *synderesis*, the
habit the acts come from.

- **Disputed historically.** Aquinas puts *synderesis* in the intellect; the
  Franciscan school (Bonaventure among them) placed it on the side of the will's
  affection. CCC 1780 uses the word without settling this.
- Ratzinger renamed it [anamnesis](#anamnesis).
- The philosophical first principle is [`ethics` 4.4](../ethics/lessons/04-04-natural-law-aquinas.md).

*Introduced:* [2.4](lessons/02-04-conscience-its-nature-and-its-errors.md)

### Conscience

**A judgment of reason about the moral quality of a concrete act of mine, not a
feeling and not a faculty.** CCC 1778; ST I q.79 a.13. Prudence immediately guides
its judgment (CCC 1806). A **certain** conscience must always be obeyed (CCC 1790;
VS 60, which calls it the proximate norm); acting in real doubt about whether an act
is wrong is itself condemned (VS 60), and resolving the doubt is [4.1](lessons/04-01-casuistry-and-the-probabilism-controversy.md)'s job.

| Conscience | Binds? | Excuses the one who follows it? | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| certain and true | yes | nothing to excuse: good act | q.19 a.5 |
| certain, erring, error invincible | yes | yes: not imputed, but the act stays objectively evil and the error is to be corrected | q.19 a.6; CCC 1793; VS 62-63 |
| certain, erring through negligence | yes: acting against it is sin | no | q.19 a.6; CCC 1791 |
| certain, erring through affected ignorance | yes | no; guilt increased | q.19 a.6; q.76 a.4 |
| doubtful | no rule for action | do not act; resolve the doubt first | VS 60; [4.1](lessons/04-01-casuistry-and-the-probabilism-controversy.md) |

- **Formation** (CCC 1783-1785): lifelong; by the Word of God, prayer, the gifts of
  the Spirit, the advice of others and the Church's authoritative teaching.
- **Aquinas's pair of cases** (q.19 a.6): reason telling a man to go to another's
  wife errs about divine law, "which he is bound to know" (no excuse); a man who
  takes another woman for his wife without negligence errs about a circumstance
  (excused).
- **Level.** Duty to follow a certain conscience; non-imputability of invincible
  error: **rung 3** (GS 16; VS 60-63; CCC 1790-1793). The Thomist machinery:
  **rung 4**.

*Introduced:* [2.4](lessons/02-04-conscience-its-nature-and-its-errors.md); also [2.5](lessons/02-05-the-primacy-of-conscience-rightly-understood.md), [3.3](lessons/03-03-the-cardinal-virtues-and-the-gifts.md)

### Erroneous conscience

**A certain but false conscience binds, and excuses only if the error was not your
fault.** It binds (I-II q.19 a.5): the will tends to nothing except as reason
proposes it, so acting against even an erring judgment wills what one takes to be
evil; "absolutely speaking, every will at variance with reason, whether right or
erring, is always evil". Aquinas rejects the view that an erring conscience binds
only in indifferent matters. Reply 2: erring reason presents its judgment *as God's*.
It excuses only when the error makes the act involuntary (a.6): error "voluntary,
either directly, or through negligence ... does not excuse"; error from ignorance of
a circumstance "without any negligence" does.

- **Binding is about the agent's will, not the act's goodness.** Following an
  invincible error does not give the act the moral value of one done with a true
  conscience (VS 63).
- **Sincerity is necessary, not sufficient.** Affected ignorance and a conscience
  "almost blinded" by habitual sin are sincere and culpable (CCC 1791; GS 16).

*Introduced:* [2.4](lessons/02-04-conscience-its-nature-and-its-errors.md); also [2.5](lessons/02-05-the-primacy-of-conscience-rightly-understood.md)

### The perplexed conscience

**The apparent trap: following a vincibly erring conscience is sin, and so is going
against it.** Aquinas: it is no true dilemma, since one "can lay aside his error,
since his ignorance is vincible and voluntary" (I-II q.19 a.6 ad 3). The English
Dominican text does not use the word "perplexed" there; the later tradition names
the case. Aquinas does use the distinction elsewhere: a priest in mortal sin bound
by office to administer a sacrament is perplexed only on the supposition that he
stays in sin, since he can repent first (III q.64 a.6 ad 3).

- The way out is to fix the error, not to pick one of the two sins.

*Introduced:* [2.4](lessons/02-04-conscience-its-nature-and-its-errors.md)

### Primacy of conscience

**Conscience is supreme as the place where the law reaches me, never as the author
of the law.** A last court, not a legislature.

- **Primacy of obligation (taught):** never act against a certain conscience. In
  conscience a person finds a law he did not give himself; it is his "sanctuary",
  where God's voice echoes (GS 16, paraphrased; CCC 1776). CCC 1790; VS 60; I-II q.19
  a.5. **Rung 3**, long held as common teaching.
- **Primacy of authorship (refused):** conscience decides what is good, so no norm
  can override it. See [Creative conscience](#creative-conscience).
- **Conscience governs acts, not doctrines.** It is a practical judgment about what
  to do, so appeal to conscience cannot justify dissent from doctrine, and a
  "supreme magisterium of conscience" set against the Church's is ruled out
  (*Donum Veritatis* 38; see 28). **Rung 3.** A theologian who cannot assent has a
  question about truth, which [`fundamental-theology` 4.3](../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-03-religious-submission.md)
  governs.
- **Conscience draws its authority from the truth it witnesses** (VS 60-61); the
  Magisterium helps it see what it ought already to hold (VS 64).
- **The live dispute: *Amoris Laetitia* ch. 8.** AL 301-302 build on the
  distinction between an objective situation and imputability (CCC 1735). AL 303
  says conscience can recognize what is, for now, the most generous response one
  can give God, with "a certain moral security", though it is not yet the full
  objective ideal. One reading: culpability and growth, the "law of gradualness"
  *Familiaris Consortio* 34 distinguished from a "gradualness of the law" (AL 295
  repeats it). The other: conscience resting in an objectively disordered act as
  God's will for now, in tension with VS 56. The 2016 *dubia*'s fifth question asked
  whether VS 56 still held; no public reply came. The text is **rung 3**; its
  reading is **freely disputed**. Sacramental questions go to
  [`sacraments-and-liturgy`](../sacraments-and-liturgy/syllabus.md).

*Introduced:* [2.5](lessons/02-05-the-primacy-of-conscience-rightly-understood.md)

### Creative conscience

**Conscience as maker of exceptions, rejected by *Veritatis Splendor*.** VS 54-56
(paraphrased) describe a view on which norms give only a general orientation,
conscience makes "decisions" rather than "judgments", and a "pastoral" solution may
allow in practice what the moral law calls intrinsically evil. VS 56 rejects this
"double status of moral truth": conscience applies the law; it does not grant
exceptions to negative precepts.

- **Level: at least rung 3**, tied to the existence of intrinsically evil acts,
  whose definitive status is itself disputed ([4.4](lessons/04-04-veritatis-splendor.md)).

*Introduced:* [2.5](lessons/02-05-the-primacy-of-conscience-rightly-understood.md)

### Newman's toast

**Newman ranks conscience first because it is Christ's oldest deputy, not his
rival.** *Letter to the Duke of Norfolk* (1875) §5, answering Gladstone's charge
that after 1870 no Catholic's loyalty could be relied on: "Conscience is the
aboriginal Vicar of Christ, a prophet in its informations, a monarch in its
peremptoriness, a priest in its blessings and anathemas". The section ends: "I shall
drink—to the Pope, if you please,—still, to Conscience first, and to the Pope
afterwards."

- **Read the noun.** A *vicar* acts in another's name. Newman calls conscience a
  messenger from God and contrasts it with a "counterfeit", the right of self-will.
- **Why a clash is rare.** Conscience judges particular acts, not doctrines; papal
  infallibility covers general teaching, not commands or policy; so a collision is
  possible only over a practical order, and even then the presumption lies with
  obedience. Newman then sets conditions a conscience must meet before it can stand
  against a papal command; that list is Boss 2's close reading, so it is not
  reproduced here.
- **Level.** Theology, no magisterial level. CCC 1778 quotes the first sentence,
  adopting that sentence, not Newman's whole theory.
- Newman on minimism (§9) is [The level of Humanae Vitae](#the-level-of-humanae-vitae)'s.

*Introduced:* [2.5](lessons/02-05-the-primacy-of-conscience-rightly-understood.md)

### Anamnesis

**Conscience's first level as an implanted memory of the good.** Joseph Ratzinger,
"Conscience and Truth" (Dallas, February 1991, as cardinal), paraphrased: keep the
two scholastic levels but rename *synderesis* as *anamnesis*, an original memory of
the good, the law "written on their hearts" (Rom 2:14-15). The pope's authority is
the authority of that memory: he is the advocate of the Christian memory, so the
toast to conscience rightly comes first.

- **Guilt moves earlier.** Rejecting the view that the SS were justified by
  certainty of conscience, he places the guilt of an untroubled wrong conscience in
  the earlier stifling of the memory: [2.4](lessons/02-04-conscience-its-nature-and-its-errors.md)'s vincible ignorance with a
  phenomenology.
- **Level.** A cardinal's lecture: theology, no magisterial level.

*Introduced:* [2.5](lessons/02-05-the-primacy-of-conscience-rightly-understood.md)

## Virtue and sin

Habits that make good action connatural, the gifts by which the Spirit moves beyond
them, and the acts and habits that turn a person from the end.

### The passions

**Movements of the sense appetite toward good and away from evil: the raw material
virtue orders, not vices.** Aquinas counts eleven (I-II q.23 a.4):

| Appetite | Regards | Passions |
|---|---|---|
| concupiscible | good or evil simply | love / hatred; desire / aversion; joy / sadness |
| irascible | good or evil as arduous | hope / despair; fear / daring; anger (no contrary) |

Temperance orders the first family, fortitude the second. A passion that *precedes*
choice and pushes it diminishes sin (q.77 a.6); concupiscence as such makes an act
more voluntary (q.6 a.7).

*Introduced:* [3.1](lessons/03-01-virtue-acquired-and-infused.md)

### The definition of virtue

**A virtue is a good operative habit; the classical definition is really a
definition of infused virtue.** ST I-II q.55 a.4 gives the definition "usually given",
gathered from Augustine: "Virtue is a good quality of the mind, by which we live
righteously, of which no one can make bad use, which God works in us, without us."
Aquinas reads it by the four causes:

| Clause | Cause |
|---|---|
| good quality (better: habit) | formal |
| of the mind | material (the subject) |
| by which we live righteously | final |
| which God works in us, without us | efficient, of *infused* virtue only |

- **Remove the last clause** and the rest defines virtue in general, acquired or
  infused.
- **"Without us" is not "without our consent"** (ad 6): God causes the habit
  without our action, not over our refusal. Objection 6 quotes Augustine: "He who
  created thee without thee, will not justify thee without thee."
- **A habit** is a quality disposing a thing well or ill, hard to lose (q.49 aa.1-2),
  needed where a power is open to many acts (q.49 a.4). God needs none; a stone can
  have none.
- CCC 1803 has its own wording; the four-cause reading is **rung 4**.
- Aristotle's version is [`ethics` 4.2](../ethics/lessons/04-02-aristotle-virtue-and-practical-wisdom.md).

*Introduced:* [3.1](lessons/03-01-virtue-acquired-and-infused.md)

### Infused virtue

**Practice builds virtues fitted to the human city; grace gives, whole and together
with charity, different virtues fitted to the household of God.**

1. Acts caused by reason can cause virtue measured by reason; nature supplies the
   "seeds" (first principles, inclinations) and repetition grows them (q.63 a.2 and
   ad 3).
2. Effects must be proportionate to causes; our end exceeds nature, so God gives
   the theological virtues and moral virtues proportioned to them (q.63 a.3; q.51
   a.4).
3. Habits differ in species by the formal aspect of their object and by what they
   are directed to: the mean fixed by human reason or by divine rule; citizens of
   the earthly city or "fellow-citizens with the saints" (Eph 2:19). So acquired and
   infused virtue differ **in species**, not degree (q.63 a.4).

**Corollaries.** One mortal sin destroys the infused virtues but not an acquired
habit, which only a contrary habit destroys (q.63 a.2 ad 2). Acts from an infused
habit strengthen it but do not generate a new one (q.51 a.4 ad 3). Infused virtue can
coexist with difficulty from "dispositions remaining from previous acts" (q.65 a.3 ad
2); Aristotle's *continent* person lacks the virtue, so the same struggle gets a
different diagnosis. When grace returns, all the infused virtues return at once (q.65
a.3), though the ease may not.

| Claim | Level |
|---|---|
| The charity of justification is poured forth and inheres | 1 (Trent VI can. 11) |
| Faith, hope, charity infused at once | conciliar chapter (Trent VI ch. 7) |
| The moral virtues are infused too | 4: Vienne (1311-12) chose it only as the *more probable* opinion; CCC 1266 |
| Infused and acquired differ in species | 5: Thomist thesis; Scotists usually reported as denying separate infused moral virtues (hedged) |

- Each cardinal virtue exists twice in the Christian, acquired and infused
  ([3.3](lessons/03-03-the-cardinal-virtues-and-the-gifts.md)); faith, hope and charity are infused only ([3.2](lessons/03-02-faith-hope-and-charity.md)).

*Introduced:* [3.1](lessons/03-01-virtue-acquired-and-infused.md); also [3.2](lessons/03-02-faith-hope-and-charity.md), [3.3](lessons/03-03-the-cardinal-virtues-and-the-gifts.md)

### The connection of the virtues

**Imperfect virtues come singly; perfect moral virtues come all together, through
prudence.** An imperfect virtue is only an inclination: a man can be generous and
unchaste. A perfect virtue needs prudence to choose well, and prudence needs every
moral virtue to supply its ends: all or none (q.65 a.1).

- **Without charity** (q.65 a.2): acquired virtues can exist, "even as they were in
  many of the Gentiles": true virtue, imperfect relative to the last end. Infused
  moral virtues cannot, because infused prudence needs a will rightly ordered to the
  last end; all are infused together with charity (a.3).
- The knowing coward who fails in *command* lacks prudence, not only fortitude
  ([3.3](lessons/03-03-the-cardinal-virtues-and-the-gifts.md) Example 1).
- **Level: common teaching (rung 4)**, shared with Aristotle.

*Introduced:* [3.1](lessons/03-01-virtue-acquired-and-infused.md)

### Charity as form of the virtues

**Charity directs every virtue's act to the last end, and so stamps each with its
final character.** II-II q.23 a.8: "In morals the form of an act is taken chiefly from
the end" ([The end as form of the act](#the-end-as-form-of-the-act)); only charity
orders acts to the *last* end (a.7); so charity "gives the form to all other acts of
virtue". Reply 1: not as their exemplar or essential form, "but rather by way of
efficient cause" — a commander deploying soldiers, not a pattern they copy. Justice
stays justice.

| Act | End | Verdict (q.23 a.7) |
|---|---|---|
| giving to see one's name on a donor wall | apparent good | counterfeit virtue |
| giving because the town's hungry deserve help, without faith | true particular good | true but imperfect virtue |
| the same gift out of love of God | the last end | virtue informed by charity, meritorious |

- **Level.** CCC 1827 calls charity the form of the virtues; the Thomist analysis
  (efficient causality) is **rung 4**.
- Infused moral virtues depend on charity through infused prudence (q.65 a.2),
  which is where [3.1](lessons/03-01-virtue-acquired-and-infused.md) points forward.

*Introduced:* [3.1](lessons/03-01-virtue-acquired-and-infused.md), [3.2](lessons/03-02-faith-hope-and-charity.md)

### Charity as friendship

**God shares his own happiness with us, and on that sharing a friendship between man
and God is founded.** II-II q.23 a.1: friendship needs benevolence, mutual love and a
*communication*, a shared life. "I will not now call you servants ... But I have
called you friends" (Jn 15:15, Douay-Rheims). It reaches enemies and sinners as love
of a friend reaches all who belong to him (ad 2-3).

- **A created habit** (a.2), against Peter Lombard, who held that charity in us is
  the Holy Spirit himself: such love would be neither voluntary nor connatural.
- **Theological virtues** (I-II q.62): God as object, infused by God alone, known
  only by revelation; three because the intellect must know the end (faith), the
  will tend to it as attainable (hope) and be united to it (charity) (a.3). Faith
  first in generation, charity first in perfection (a.4). The act of faith is
  [`fundamental-theology` 2.1](../fundamental-theology/lessons/02-01-the-act-of-faith.md)'s.
- Whether charity is distinct from sanctifying grace (Thomists) or identical with
  it (Scotists) is **rung 5**.

*Introduced:* [3.2](lessons/03-02-faith-hope-and-charity.md)

### Living and lifeless faith

**Faith without charity is still true faith, but lifeless, and not a virtue
simply.** Faith formed by charity is a virtue; lifeless faith is true faith lacking
its form (II-II q.4 aa.3-5).

- **Level: defined** that faith is not always lost with grace and that the faith
  that remains is true faith (Trent VI can. 28, with ch. 15).
- **Pagan virtue.** "Augustine called pagan virtues splendid vices" is not in the
  text: *City of God* XIX.25 says virtues without reference to God are rather vices
  than virtues, and Aquinas is milder still (true but imperfect, q.23 a.7). The
  thesis that every act of the unredeemed is a vice was Baius's, among propositions
  Pius V's *Ex omnibus afflictionibus* (1 October 1567) censured together, not each
  with its own note.

*Introduced:* [3.2](lessons/03-02-faith-hope-and-charity.md)

### Despair and presumption

**The two sins against hope: one gives up on God's mercy, the other presumes on it
or on oneself.** Hope's object is eternal happiness, an arduous good possible only by
God's help (II-II q.17); its certainty rests on God's omnipotence and mercy, not on
grace one already holds (q.18 a.4 ad 2).

- **Despair** conforms the will to a false estimate that God refuses pardon to the
  repentant (q.20 a.1); it can coexist with right faith in general (a.2). In
  species, unbelief and hatred of God are graver, but despair is more dangerous,
  cutting the nerve of effort (a.3).
- **Presumption**, two kinds (q.21 a.1): relying on one's own power for what exceeds
  it; relying on God's power for what he does not give (pardon without repentance,
  glory without merits), which Aquinas calls properly a sin against the Holy Ghost.
  Less grave than despair, since mercy suits God more than punishment (a.2).
- **Trent**: no absolute certainty of final perseverance without special
  revelation (VI can. 16, **defined**), yet all ought to repose "a most firm hope in
  God's help" (ch. 13).
- **Level.** Despair and presumption as sins against hope: **at least rung 3** (CCC
  2091-2092).
- Despair as sin is a voluntary movement, not a mood; an unconsented state falls
  under [2.1](lessons/02-01-the-voluntary.md).

*Introduced:* [3.2](lessons/03-02-faith-hope-and-charity.md)

### The order of charity

**Charity loves in an order: God, oneself spiritually, the neighbour's soul, then
bodies, and the nearer more intensely.** II-II q.26:

1. God first, as source of the shared happiness (a.2).
2. Oneself spiritually: one may never sin even to free a neighbour from sin (a.4).
3. A neighbour's soul before one's own body (a.5).
4. Among neighbours, the nearer loved more intensely, each in the sphere of the tie
   (aa.7-8).

- **Risking one's body** for a neighbour is obligatory only under a duty (office);
  otherwise it belongs to the perfection of charity (a.5 ad 3). Bearing bodily harm
  for a friend is itself loving oneself more in the spiritual sense (a.4 ad 2).
- **Closeness vs need**: no general rule; prudence decides (q.31 a.3 ad 1). A
  stranger in extreme necessity can come before a father not in such need.
- **Level: rung 4**, the fine grain of q.26.

*Introduced:* [3.2](lessons/03-02-faith-hope-and-charity.md)

### The cardinal virtues

**Four hinges, derived rather than listed: reason's order in its own act (prudence),
in operations (justice), in passions that push (temperance), in passions that pull
back (fortitude).** ST I-II q.61 a.2; the same four fall out a second way from their
subjects: reason, will, concupiscible, irascible. Scripture names them together at
Wisdom 8:7 (CCC 1805).

| Virtue | Orders | Chief act or matter |
|---|---|---|
| [Prudence](#prudence) | reason's own act | command (II-II q.47 a.8) |
| Justice | operations toward others | rendering each his due ([The parts of justice](#the-parts-of-justice)) |
| Fortitude | passions withdrawing us: fear, above all of death (q.123 a.4) | endurance, not attack (q.123 a.6); martyrdom is its act (q.124 a.2) |
| Temperance | passions inciting us: pleasures of touch, food, drink, sex (q.141 a.4) | the mean set by reason |

- "Cardinal" means hinge of the *moral* life, not highest: the theological virtues
  and the gifts outrank them (q.68 a.8). A hinge virtue makes you good, not just
  skilled (q.61 a.1).
- **Level.** The four as foundation of the moral life: **rung 3** (CCC 1805-1809),
  not defined.

*Introduced:* [3.3](lessons/03-03-the-cardinal-virtues-and-the-gifts.md)

### Prudence

**Right reason applied to action; not finished until it commands.** II-II q.47 a.2.

| Act | Its failure (q.53 a.5) |
|---|---|
| to take counsel | precipitation |
| to judge | thoughtlessness |
| to command (chief, nearest the deed, q.47 a.8) | inconstancy |

- **Eight integral parts** (q.48 a.1, each treated in q.49): memory, understanding,
  docility, shrewdness, reasoning, foresight, circumspection, caution. Shrewdness is
  "a quick conjecture of the middle term".
- It is *more* imprudent to sin knowingly than in ignorance (q.47 a.8).
- Prudence immediately guides the judgment of conscience (CCC 1806), and is not
  timidity or duplicity (CCC 1806).
- What counts as "solidly probable" in [4.1](lessons/04-01-casuistry-and-the-probabilism-controversy.md) is a prudential judgment.
- Phronesis is [`ethics` 4.2](../ethics/lessons/04-02-aristotle-virtue-and-practical-wisdom.md).

*Introduced:* [3.3](lessons/03-03-the-cardinal-virtues-and-the-gifts.md)

### The parts of justice

**Rendering each his due with a constant and perpetual will (II-II q.58 a.1), and the
virtues that fall short of strict due.**

- **Commutative** justice (between individuals, on equality) and **distributive**
  (the community to its members, in proportion) (q.61 a.1).
- **Respect of persons** opposes distributive justice: distributing by who someone
  *is* rather than by what makes the thing due (q.63 a.1).
- **Potential parts** (q.80): religion, piety toward parents and country,
  observance, truth, gratitude, vindication, liberality, friendliness. Truth is the
  virtue behind [6.4](lessons/06-04-truthfulness-and-the-lie.md); restitution (q.62) carries the cooperation list of
  [3.5](lessons/03-05-social-sin-and-cooperation-in-the-sins-of-others.md).
- Economic applications are ceded to
  [`catholic-social-teaching`](../catholic-social-teaching/syllabus.md).

*Introduced:* [3.3](lessons/03-03-the-cardinal-virtues-and-the-gifts.md)

### The gifts of the Holy Spirit

**The virtues are oars; the gifts are sails: they add a new mover, not a new kind of
act.** I-II q.68: the moral virtues dispose the appetite to obey reason; the gifts
dispose the person to obey the Holy Spirit readily (aa.1, 3). They surpass the
virtues "as regards the manner of working", being moved by a higher principle (a.2 ad
1). Reason holds the theological virtues imperfectly, so in matters of the
supernatural end the gifts are needed for salvation (a.2; Rom 8:14). They are in
everyone in grace (a.3 ad 1), unlike the charisms of 1 Corinthians 12, given to some
for others.

**The count.** Isaiah 11:2-3 (Douay-Rheims): "the spirit of wisdom, and of
understanding, the spirit of counsel, and of fortitude, the spirit of knowledge, and
of godliness. And he shall be filled with the spirit of the fear of the Lord." The
Hebrew of 11:2 names **six**, ending with the fear of the Lord, and 11:3 repeats
"fear". The Septuagint renders the first "fear" as *eusebeia* (piety); the Vulgate
follows with *pietas* (Douay "godliness"); so the Latin tradition counts **seven**.

| Gift | Perfects | Where |
|---|---|---|
| understanding, wisdom | speculative reason (with faith; wisdom with charity) | II-II qq.8, 45 |
| counsel, knowledge | practical reason (counsel perfects prudence) | II-II qq.52, 9 |
| piety | the appetite toward others (perfects justice: worship of God as Father) | II-II q.121 |
| fortitude | against fear (confidence of reaching eternal life) | II-II q.139 |
| fear of the Lord | against disordered pleasure (corresponds to temperance) | I-II q.68 a.4 ad 1 |

- **Order** (a.8): below the theological virtues, which regulate them; above the
  intellectual and moral virtues. Four perfect reason, three the appetite (a.4).
- "The Spirit prompted me" is no licence against reason: what opposes the gifts
  opposes reason too (a.1 ad 2).
- **Level.** Seven gifts as permanent dispositions of docility: **rung 3** (CCC
  1830-1831), not defined. Aquinas's theory of *how* they differ from virtues:
  **rung 4**; theologians reported in a.1 held them indistinguishable from the
  virtues, and Scotus is commonly read as their heir.

*Introduced:* [3.3](lessons/03-03-the-cardinal-virtues-and-the-gifts.md)

### Beatitudes and fruits

**Both are acts, not habits.** The Beatitudes differ from virtues and gifts "as act
from habit" (I-II q.69 a.1). The fruits are acts done under the Spirit's movement
that end in delight (q.70 a.1).

**The count of the fruits.** Galatians 5:22-23 lists **nine** in the Greek (love,
joy, peace, patience, kindness, goodness, faithfulness, gentleness, self-control).
The Vulgate, which the Douay follows, lists **twelve**: "charity, joy, peace,
patience, benignity, goodness, longanimity, mildness, faith, modesty, continency,
chastity". CCC 1832 gives twelve and reports the number as a tradition following the
Vulgate. Aquinas cites Augustine that the Apostle was not teaching a count (q.70 a.3
ad 4).

- **Level.** The twelve carries **no doctrinal weight**.
- The Beatitudes as the Gospel's statement of the last end: see
  [Perfect and imperfect happiness](#perfect-and-imperfect-happiness).

*Introduced:* [3.3](lessons/03-03-the-cardinal-virtues-and-the-gifts.md)

### The definition of sin

**Sin is a voluntary act against God's own reason, the eternal law.** Augustine,
*Against Faustus* XXII.27 (NPNF 1.4): sin is "any transgression in deed, or word, or
desire, of the eternal law". Aquinas accepts it (I-II q.71 a.6), reading the act
(word, deed, desire) as matter and the contrariety to eternal law as form. The
theologian considers sin "chiefly as an offense against God", the philosopher as
something against reason (ad 5).

- No voluntariness, no sin ([2.1](lessons/02-01-the-voluntary.md)); the measure is God's reason reached through
  our own ([Eternal law and natural law](#eternal-law-and-natural-law)).

*Introduced:* [3.4](lessons/03-04-sin-mortal-and-venial.md)

### Mortal and venial sin

**Venial sin sickens charity; mortal sin kills it.** I-II q.72 a.5: the last end is
the principle of the moral order, and charity unites the soul to it; a sin that turns
the soul from that end is mortal, one that disorders it without turning away is
venial. As in the body, death is "irreparable according to nature" (no principle left
within to repair it; repair comes from God), while sickness can be repaired. The
difference is *consequent to* the species of sins, so one species can contain both:
in adultery "the first movement is a venial sin" (ad 1).

**By genus, and by the agent** (q.88 a.2, quoted CCC 1856):

- *Mortal by genus*: an object of itself contrary to charity: against God
  (blasphemy, perjury) or neighbour (murder, adultery).
- *Venial by genus*: disordered but compatible with charity (the idle word,
  excessive laughter).
- A venial act becomes mortal if the agent makes it his last end or directs it to a
  mortal sin (the idle word spoken to seduce). A mortal act is venial "by reason of
  the act being imperfect", undeliberated. Adding a venial deformity never demotes a
  mortal sin (q.88 a.6).

**The three conditions**, all together (CCC 1857, quoting *Reconciliatio et
Paenitentia* 17): **grave matter**, **full knowledge**, **deliberate consent**.

- **Grave matter is not mortal sin.** It is one condition of three. RP 17 notes that
  grave sin is *in practice* identified with mortal sin in the Church's language,
  presupposing the other two.
- **Binary.** RP 17 declines a threefold venial / grave / mortal division: degrees
  among grave sins, but no middle between killing charity and not.
- **Not reduced to an option.** Mortal sin is not only an explicit, formal
  [fundamental option](#fundamental-option) against God; knowingly and freely
  choosing grave disorder is already the turn (RP 17; *Persona Humana* 10).
- **Judge acts, not persons** (CCC 1861).
- **Knowledge and consent** can be defeated by invincible ignorance, passions,
  external pressure, pathological disorders (CCC 1860); habit shifts the question
  to the history of the will ([Voluntary in cause](#voluntary-in-cause)).
- **The lie** is venial in itself unless against charity (II-II q.110 a.4; [6.4](lessons/06-04-truthfulness-and-the-lie.md)).
- **Shared sin** still needs the three conditions ([3.5](lessons/03-05-social-sin-and-cooperation-in-the-sins-of-others.md)).
- **1 John 5:16-17.** CCC 1854 cites it with "cf."; it does not map one-to-one. RP
  17 reads "sin unto death" chiefly as apostasy and idolatry; Challoner's note says
  the difference "can not be the same as betwixt sins that are called venial and
  mortal". The Douay's 5:17 reads "And there is a sin unto death", following the
  Clementine Vulgate; the Greek has "a sin *not* unto death".

- **Levels.** Grace lost by any mortal sin: **1** ([Trent on losing grace](#trent-on-losing-grace)). The three conditions and the binary: **3**. Aversion and sorting by genus: **4**. A manual's grave-matter list: **4 at most**.

*Introduced:* [3.4](lessons/03-04-sin-mortal-and-venial.md); also [3.5](lessons/03-05-social-sin-and-cooperation-in-the-sins-of-others.md), [4.3](lessons/04-03-proportionalism-and-the-fundamental-option.md), [6.4](lessons/06-04-truthfulness-and-the-lie.md)

### Trent on losing grace

**Trent defined *that* mortal sin loses grace, not *which* acts are mortal.**
Session VI (1547) ch. 15 (Waterworth), against those who held that only unbelief
forfeits justification: grace is lost "not only by infidelity ... but also by any
other mortal sin whatever, though faith be not lost". Canon 27 anathematizes the
contrary; canon 28 that faith is always lost with grace. Canon 23: no one can avoid
all sins, even venial, through a whole life without a special privilege, as the
Church holds of the Blessed Virgin. Trent thereby presupposes both kinds of sin.

- **Level: defined (rung 1)** for cann. 23, 27, 28. The three conditions are not
  here: they are RP 17 (1984), **rung 3**.
- How grace is regained and what justification is belong to
  [`creation-grace-last-things`](../creation-grace-last-things/syllabus.md) and
  [`sacraments-and-liturgy`](../sacraments-and-liturgy/syllabus.md). Trent as an
  event: [`church-history` 6.4](../church-history/lessons/06-04-trent-and-catholic-reform.md).

*Introduced:* [3.4](lessons/03-04-sin-mortal-and-venial.md)

### The capital sins

**Sources that give rise to other sins, not the seven mortal sins.** Anger,
gluttony and sloth are venial in countless instances.

| Aquinas, I-II q.84 a.4 (after Gregory, *Moralia* 31) | CCC 1866 (after Cassian and Gregory) |
|---|---|
| vainglory, envy, anger, sloth, covetousness, gluttony, lust; **pride** set above all seven as their "queen" | **pride**, avarice, envy, wrath, lust, gluttony, sloth or acedia |

Same tradition, two bookkeeping choices.

*Introduced:* [3.4](lessons/03-04-sin-mortal-and-venial.md)

### Social sin

**Only persons sin; "social" names how far sin reaches, never who else is guilty.**
*Reconciliatio et Paenitentia* 16 (John Paul II, 2 December 1984), paraphrased: sin in
the proper sense is always a personal act. A situation, institution or structure is
not the subject of moral acts. Responsibility cannot be shifted onto structures or
others; pressures can reduce freedom, never remove the person as subject. "The son
shall not bear the iniquity of the father" (Ezek 18:20).

| Sense (RP 16) | Meaning | Status |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | every sin affects others, by human solidarity (as holiness raises the communion of saints) | real; a personal sin |
| 2 | sins directly against the neighbour (justice, life, freedom, reputation, the common good; leaders' failures) | fully personal; "social" by their matter |
| 3 | relations between communities (class struggle, blocs, nations) | "social sin" **analogically** |
| rejected | social sin set *against* personal sin, blaming "the system" | not legitimate |

- What the Church condemns as social sin is the accumulation of many personal sins:
  of those who cause or exploit evil and of those who could limit it but hold back
  from fear, laziness or complicit silence.
- **Only sense 3 is analogical**; sense 2 is real personal sin.
- **Original sin** is sin only analogically, contracted not committed (CCC 404): not
  a model for collective guilt. Its transmission is defined (Trent V); the doctrine is
  ceded to [`creation-grace-last-things`](../creation-grace-last-things/syllabus.md).
- **Level: rung 3** (a post-synodal exhortation); not a definition. Freedom beneath
  it is defined (Trent VI can. 5).

*Introduced:* [3.5](lessons/03-05-social-sin-and-cooperation-in-the-sins-of-others.md)

### Structures of sin

**Many personal sins frozen into customs, incentives and institutions that make the
next sin easier.** *Sollicitudo Rei Socialis* 36 (encyclical, 30 December 1987):
rooted in personal sin, linked to the concrete acts of those who build and reinforce
them; they grow and become sources of further sins. CCC 1869: they express and result
from personal sins, lead their victims to do evil in turn, and are "social sin" only
analogically.

- Every arrow runs *through* persons: no arrow goes from a structure straight to
  guilt. Those who build them are guilty of [scandal](#scandal) (CCC 2286-2287).
- **Level: rung 3.** SRS's application to development, blocs and the economy is
  ceded to [`catholic-social-teaching`](../catholic-social-teaching/syllabus.md).

*Introduced:* [3.5](lessons/03-05-social-sin-and-cooperation-in-the-sins-of-others.md)

### Cooperation in the sins of others

**One person's free act can cause another's sin: by moving him, helping him, or
failing to stop him when bound to.** Aquinas, asking who must restore stolen goods
(II-II q.62 a.7), gives the first systematic list: *directly* (inducing the taking,
helping the taker, sharing the thing) and *indirectly*, by not preventing evil
"provided he be able and bound to prevent him". His memory verse: command, counsel,
consent, flattery, receiving, participation, silence, not preventing, not denouncing.
Reply 1 generalizes: whoever causes the sin in any way shares it (Rom 1:32).

| CCC 1868 | Aquinas's verse | Duty needed? |
|---|---|---|
| participating directly and voluntarily | participation | no |
| ordering, advising, praising, approving | command, counsel, flattery, consent | no |
| not disclosing or not hindering, when obliged | silence, not preventing, not denouncing | **yes** |
| protecting evil-doers | receiving (shelter, assistance) | no |

- The commander is first in line for restitution (ad 2); counsellors and flatterers
  are bound only when their influence probably caused the wrong.
- **What 1868 lacks**: how *close* a contribution must be, and when fear excuses it.
  That needs [Formal and material cooperation](#formal-and-material-cooperation).
- **Level.** That commanders, counsellors and shelterers share the guilt is **rung
  4** (Scripture, Aquinas); CCC 1868's wording carries only the weight of what it
  summarizes; the verse is a teaching device.

*Introduced:* [3.5](lessons/03-05-social-sin-and-cooperation-in-the-sins-of-others.md)

### Scandal

**Leading another into sin by what one does or says.** Aquinas: "something less
rightly done or said, that occasions another's spiritual downfall" (II-II q.43 a.1).
"Less rightly" covers what has the appearance of evil (ad 2; 1 Cor 8:10).

- **Active and passive.** Active: my disordered act occasions your fall. Passive:
  you fall. Where I act rightly and you fall through malice, the scandal is yours
  alone (a.1 ad 4). Aquinas also distinguishes the scandal of the "little ones",
  the weak, from Pharisaical scandal, taken from malice (aa.7-8).
- **CCC 2284-2287**: grave if it deliberately leads another into grave sin;
  aggravated by the offender's authority and the weakness of the one led; can be
  given by laws, institutions, fashion and opinion (2286), which is where
  structures of sin and scandal meet.
- **In cooperation**: the risk of scandal is one weight in the proportionate reason
  for material cooperation ([4.2](lessons/04-02-double-effect-and-cooperation-as-catholic-method.md)).

*Introduced:* [3.5](lessons/03-05-social-sin-and-cooperation-in-the-sins-of-others.md)

## Method

How the tradition reasons about doubtful and hard cases, the post-conciliar revolt
against that method, and the magisterial response.

### The moral systems

**Reflex principles for acting when a doubt about obligation will not go away; they
differ only on how strong the opinion favouring liberty must be.** The common
starting rule: you may not act while in *practical* doubt whether the act is sinful,
since accepting the risk of sinning is already a fault. Either settle the doubt
directly (study, advice) or use a reflex principle that turns speculative uncertainty
into practical certainty.

| System (strict to lax) | You may follow the opinion favouring liberty when it is... | Status |
|---|---|---|
| Absolute tutiorism | never, unless certain | **condemned** (Alexander VIII, 1690) |
| Mitigated tutiorism | most probable | not condemned; rarely defended |
| Probabiliorism | more probable than the opinion for the law | freely disputed |
| Equiprobabilism (Alphonsus, from c.1762) | equally or nearly equally probable, if the doubt is whether a law *exists*; more probable, if whether a known law has *ceased* (the law keeps possession) | freely disputed |
| Probabilism (Medina, 1577) | solidly probable, even if less probable | freely disputed |
| Laxism | slightly or tenuously probable, one reason or one author | **condemned** (Alexander VII, 1665-66; Innocent XI, 1679) |

Compensationism, a further variant, weighs the doubt against a proportionate reason.
Both ends of the table are condemned; the middle is where Catholic moralists may
disagree.

- **Level.** The exclusion of laxism and absolute tutiorism: **papal censures**
  (authentic magisterium, global, not definitions). The practical-doubt rule, *lex
  dubia non obligat*, and the limits in [Doubt of law and doubt of fact](#doubt-of-law-and-doubt-of-fact):
  **rung 4**. The choice among the middle systems: **rung 5**.
- **Alphonsus.** The Holy See sanctioned his moral works (decree 1803; Sacred
  Penitentiary reply 1831), canonized him (1839) and named him Doctor (1871). That
  marks his system as *safe to follow*, not as *the Church's system*.

*Introduced:* [4.1](lessons/04-01-casuistry-and-the-probabilism-controversy.md)

### Probabilism

**A doubtful law does not bind, so a solidly probable opinion for liberty may be
followed even when the opposite is more probable.** Bartolomé de Medina OP,
commentary on the *Prima Secundae* (Salamanca, 1577):

1. A law binds only if sufficiently promulgated, that is, made known.
2. A law whose existence or scope is seriously and objectively doubtful is not
   sufficiently promulgated: *lex dubia non obligat*.
3. A solidly probable opinion that the law does not bind makes it seriously
   doubtful, even if the opposite is more probable.
4. So one may follow it.

The rivals deny premise 3 at different points ([The moral systems](#the-moral-systems)).

- **"Probable"** means resting on serious intrinsic (reasons) or extrinsic
  (competent authors) grounds, so a prudent person could hold it: not "over 50
  percent".
- **Pascal**, *Provincial Letters* V (20 March 1656): his Jesuit says an opinion is
  probable "when it is founded upon reasons of some consideration. Hence it may
  sometimes happen that a single *very grave doctor* may render an opinion
  probable." The damage is in "some consideration" and "single". The *Letters* were
  put on the Index (1657); Rome condemned laxism *and* rigorism.
- **Jonsen and Toulmin**, *The Abuse of Casuistry* (1988), paraphrased: case-based
  reasoning is how practical ethics actually reaches agreement (their bioethics
  commission agreed on cases while disagreeing on principles); laxist abuse, not the
  method, discredited it. Usury is the classic case of casuistry developing a
  prohibition ([`theology-of-debt`](../theology-of-debt/syllabus.md)).
- **In the abortion argument** ([5.2](lessons/05-02-abortion.md)): probabilism covers doubts of law;
  where the doubt is whether a person is present, an innocent life is at stake and
  the safer course binds.
- **Level: rung 5** as against probabiliorism and equiprobabilism.

*Introduced:* [4.1](lessons/04-01-casuistry-and-the-probabilism-controversy.md); also [5.2](lessons/05-02-abortion.md)

### Doubt of law and doubt of fact

**Reflex principles settle doubts about obligation; they cannot settle whether you
might kill someone.** When a *certain* end must be secured (the validity of a
sacrament, another's life or established rights, one's own salvation), take the safer
means however the law question is resolved. The hunter unsure whether the movement
in the bushes is a deer or a man may not shoot on a "probable opinion" that it is a
deer. A doubt of fact that bears only on whether an act is lawful can be reduced to a
doubt of law.

Four limits every moderate system accepts:

- no acting in practical doubt;
- doubt of fact with a certain end at stake: the safer course;
- an opinion contrary to a clear magisterial decision is not probable, whoever holds
  it (so a single dissenting theologian cannot make a rung-3 teaching "doubtful";
  [4.1](lessons/04-01-casuistry-and-the-probabilism-controversy.md) Example 2);
- casuistry works only where the law is uncertain (VS 76).

Innocent XI (1679) condemned following a merely probable opinion on the validity of
a sacrament one administers.

- **Level: common teaching (rung 4).**

*Introduced:* [4.1](lessons/04-01-casuistry-and-the-probabilism-controversy.md)

### The seventeenth-century condemnations

**Rome struck at both ends: laxism in 1665-66 and 1679, rigorism in 1690.**

| Date | Act | Condemned |
|---|---|---|
| 1665-66 | Alexander VII | 45 laxist propositions |
| 2 March 1679 | Innocent XI, decree *Sanctissimus Dominus* | 65 laxist propositions, among them: a probable opinion on sacramental validity; excusing abortion before animation; the servant who helps his master through a window to rape a girl, for fear of notable harm, not sinning mortally; strict mental reservation (props 26-27, as Sanchez formulated it) |
| 26 June 1680 | Innocent XI, decree for Tirso González SJ | nothing: it authorized him to teach probabiliorism; whether it was meant against probabilism was argued later |
| 1690 | Alexander VIII | rigorist propositions, including absolute tutiorism: *non licet sequi opinionem vel inter probabiles probabilissimam* ("it is not lawful to follow even the most probable among probable opinions") |

- **Level.** Papal acts of the authentic magisterium with **global censures**: they
  do not call each proposition heretical. Do not upgrade them to "defined dogma";
  do not treat the excluded positions as open.

### Double effect

**You may cause a foreseen evil you do not choose, as end or as means, for a
proportionately grave reason; some acts are shut out before any weighing begins.**
The root is II-II q.64 a.7: "moral acts take their species according to what is
intended, and not according to what is beside the intention". Two further sentences
carry the Catholic form. The private defender may not *intend* the killing ("it is
not lawful for a man to intend killing a man in self-defense, except for such as have
public authority"); and adultery is not "necessarily directed to the preservation of
one's own life, as is the act whence sometimes results the taking of a man's life"
(ad 4) — the answer comes from the *kind of act*, not the weight of the end.

**Gury's four conditions** (*Compendium theologiae moralis*, 1850), in his order,
paraphrased:

1. the agent's end is good;
2. the act that causes the effects is good or indifferent in itself;
3. the evil effect is not the means to the good effect;
4. there is a proportionately grave reason.

Order and wording vary across manuals; [`ethics` 3.2](../ethics/lessons/03-02-the-doctrine-of-double-effect.md)
uses a later four-part form after Mangan's 1949 history, with the same content.

- **Condition 4 is not proportionalism.** Proportion enters only after condition 2
  has cleared the object. Knauer collapsed the four into the fourth
  ([Proportionalism](#proportionalism)).
- **Applications in the course.** Legitimate defense, the root case ([5.1](lessons/05-01-the-inviolability-of-innocent-life.md));
  the cancerous gravid uterus, indirect by every school ([4.2](lessons/04-02-double-effect-and-cooperation-as-catholic-method.md), [4.5](lessons/04-05-describing-the-object.md));
  indirect cases of fetal death routed away from the abortion norm ([5.2](lessons/05-02-abortion.md));
  pain relief and palliative sedation ([5.3](lessons/05-03-euthanasia-and-the-end-of-life.md)); therapeutic drugs with a
  foreseen contraceptive effect (HV 15; [6.2](lessons/06-02-humanae-vitae-the-argument.md)).
- **Level.** The formula is **common teaching (rung 4)**; magisterial documents use
  it without defining it. The norm it serves (no direct killing of the innocent)
  has the weight of *Evangelium Vitae*; keep the two apart. Particular verdicts are
  rung 4 where the manuals agreed and rung 5 where they did not ([4.5](lessons/04-05-describing-the-object.md)).
- The secular principle and the closeness problem:
  [`ethics` 3.2](../ethics/lessons/03-02-the-doctrine-of-double-effect.md),
  [3.3](../ethics/lessons/03-03-testing-the-principles-on-trolleys.md).

*Introduced:* [4.2](lessons/04-02-double-effect-and-cooperation-as-catholic-method.md); also [4.3](lessons/04-03-proportionalism-and-the-fundamental-option.md), [4.5](lessons/04-05-describing-the-object.md), [5.1](lessons/05-01-the-inviolability-of-innocent-life.md), [5.2](lessons/05-02-abortion.md), [5.3](lessons/05-03-euthanasia-and-the-end-of-life.md), [6.2](lessons/06-02-humanae-vitae-the-argument.md)

### Direct and indirect

**"Direct" names the will's structure, not the scalpel's distance.** An evil is
direct when it is willed as an end *or as a means*; indirect when it is the foreseen
side effect of an act whose object is something else. CCC 2271 defines direct
abortion in these terms; EV 62 does too.

- **The manual test**: where the act lands. Coppens, *Catholic Encyclopedia*
  (1907), reporting the Holy Office: a treatment "applied to her organism" to save
  the mother's life, even if the child's death follows, "should not be maintained"
  to attack fetal life directly. A physical-target criterion, and a clean one.
- **The minimal pair** ([4.2](lessons/04-02-double-effect-and-cooperation-as-catholic-method.md)): removing a cancerous uterus saves the mother
  by removing the cancer (indirect); emptying the uterus because the pregnancy
  itself threatens her heart saves her *through* ending the pregnancy (direct,
  fails condition 3). Same stakes, different structure of the will.
- **Post-1993 accounts** of what makes a killing direct: Long's natural *per se*
  ordering; Rhonheimer's vital-conflict account; Finnis, Grisez and Boyle's
  description of craniotomy (*The Thomist*, 2001, hedged) as possibly chosen under a
  description that leaves the death unintended — the furthest point to which
  intention has been asked to redescribe an act. See
  [The perspective of the acting person](#the-perspective-of-the-acting-person).

*Introduced:* [4.2](lessons/04-02-double-effect-and-cooperation-as-catholic-method.md); also [4.5](lessons/04-05-describing-the-object.md)

### Formal and material cooperation

**Am I sharing his evil will, or contributing something of my own that he uses?**

| Kind | Test | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| Formal, explicit | I want the principal's evil | always wrong |
| Formal, implicit | I say I disapprove, but what I choose to do *is* the evil act, or is ordered to it and nothing else | always wrong |
| Material, immediate | my act is part of the evil act itself | excluded when that act is intrinsically evil |
| Material, mediate | I supply a condition or a means, before or after | licit **only for a proportionate reason** |

Mediate material cooperation also divides **proximate / remote** by causal and moral
distance. The proportionate reason must grow with the gravity of the evil, the
nearness of my contribution, whether the evil would happen without it, any duty I
have to prevent it, and the risk of scandal. **Duress** can supply the reason for
remote cooperation; it does not make close help with grave evil light (the 1679
servant was a material cooperator).

- **The 2020 vaccine note** (CDF, 21 December 2020, approved at an audience on 17
  December), paraphrased: where ethically unproblematic vaccines are unavailable,
  receiving vaccines whose research or production used cell lines from two
  abortions in the last century is morally acceptable; the cooperation is *passive*
  material and *remote*; the duty to avoid it lapses in grave danger; it is not
  formal cooperation and presupposes opposition to abortion; vaccination is not as a
  rule a moral obligation, and objectors must avoid spreading infection. It borrows
  *Dignitas Personae* 35: those who *decide* to use such lines bear more
  responsibility. **Rung 3, and conditional.** Some moralists prefer "appropriation
  of evil" to "cooperation" here, since one cannot contribute to an act finished
  decades ago.
- **Euthanasia**: *Samaritanus Bonus* V.1 calls formal or immediate material
  cooperation in it grave; V.11 says chaplains are not to remain present until it is
  performed ([5.3](lessons/05-03-euthanasia-and-the-end-of-life.md)).
- **Level.** CCC 2272 (formal cooperation in abortion a grave offence): **rung 3**.
  The taxonomy: **rung 4**, used by DP 35 and the 2020 note, never defined.
- "I disapprove, so it's material" fails if what you choose to do *is* the evil act
  (the surgeon "under protest" is a principal agent).

*Introduced:* [4.2](lessons/04-02-double-effect-and-cooperation-as-catholic-method.md); previewed [3.5](lessons/03-05-social-sin-and-cooperation-in-the-sins-of-others.md); also [5.3](lessons/05-03-euthanasia-and-the-end-of-life.md)

### Proportionalism

**An act is wrong only if it causes a premoral evil without proportionate reason**,
stated as its defenders would:

1. Goods and evils in the world are premoral; moral good and evil belong to the will
   that causes them. Killing is always an evil, not always a wrong.
2. **Peter Knauer** (*Nouvelle Revue Théologique* 87, 1965; English "The Hermeneutic
   Function of the Principle of Double Effect", *Natural Law Forum* 12, 1967): double
   effect is the key to all moral judgment. An evil is "directly intended", morally,
   exactly when there is no *commensurate reason* for causing it; commensurate means
   the act does not in the long run undermine the value it pursues.
3. **The appeal to Aquinas**: species from "what is intended" (q.64 a.7), read
   morally; conclusions of natural law hold only "in the majority of cases" (the
   deposit not returned to one who would fight his country, q.94 a.4).
4. **Josef Fuchs** ("The Absoluteness of Moral Terms", 1971): formal norms ("do not
   murder") are absolute because "murder" means unjust killing; material norms
   describing physical behaviour cannot be, since no physical description foresees
   every relevant circumstance. **Bruno Schüller**: the tradition already reasons
   teleologically nearly everywhere, so its deontological islands owe a
   justification.
5. **Richard McCormick** (*Ambiguity in Moral Choice*, 1973; *Doing Evil to Achieve
   Good*, ed. with Paul Ramsey, 1978), in a common summary: a reason is proportionate
   when (a) the value sought is at least equal to the value sacrificed, (b) there is
   no less harmful way to protect it here and now, (c) this way will not undermine it
   in the long run.
6. **Not consequentialism**, its defenders insisted: the weighing includes the kind
   of act and its meaning, incommensurable goods and long-run effects on the value
   at stake; McCormick held some norms virtually exceptionless. Many disliked the
   label, which came largely from critics.

Conclusion: no act can be judged morally wrong by its physical description alone,
before its reasons are weighed. Contraception after 1968 was the case everyone had in
mind ([`church-history` 9.3](../church-history/lessons/09-03-after-the-council.md)).

- **The encyclical's verdict**: VS 75 names it, with consequentialism, as a form of
  "teleologism"; VS 79 rejects the thesis that no chosen kind of behaviour can be
  judged evil by its object apart from intention and total foreseeable
  consequences. See [What Veritatis Splendor rejects](#what-veritatis-splendor-rejects).
- **The pressure point**: the bread in extreme need ([2.3](lessons/02-03-intention-and-circumstances.md)) — redescription of
  the object, or weighing in disguise?
- **Level.** A theological theory, not a magisterial position.

*Introduced:* [2.3](lessons/02-03-intention-and-circumstances.md) (preview); stated [4.3](lessons/04-03-proportionalism-and-the-fundamental-option.md); assessed [4.4](lessons/04-04-veritatis-splendor.md)

### Premoral and moral evil

**Death, pain, mutilation and falsehood are real evils, but not yet moral evils.**
Premoral (ontic) evil is the evil in the world an act causes; moral evil is the will
causing it without proportionate reason ([Proportionalism](#proportionalism)).

- "Premoral" does not mean "morally neutral, so anything goes": a premoral evil is
  real and always needs a reason.
- Theological vocabulary, not the Church's; **no magisterial level**.

*Introduced:* [4.3](lessons/04-03-proportionalism-and-the-fundamental-option.md)

### Fundamental option

**Beneath particular choices lies a basic self-disposal toward God or away from him,
and mortal sin is a reversal of that.** Stated at strength:

- **Karl Rahner** (essays of the 1960s; *Foundations of Christian Faith*, 1976):
  *categorical* freedom chooses among particular objects; *transcendental* freedom is
  the subject's disposal of himself as a whole before God, realized in and through
  categorical choices but never identical with one and never fully visible to
  reflection.
- **Josef Fuchs**'s "basic freedom" (c. 1970) applies this to morals: particular acts
  express the core commitment more or less deeply.
- **So** mortal sin, a turning from the last end, is a reversal of the option; it
  engages the whole person and cannot happen lightly. An act of grave matter under
  passion, habit or immaturity may leave basic love of God intact: the theory's
  pastoral appeal.
- **Claimed ancestor**: I-II q.89 a.6: at the use of reason "the first thing that
  occurs to a man to think about then, is to deliberate about himself", and if he
  directs himself to the due end he receives remission of original sin. The text
  does not say later acts cannot overturn it.

**The Magisterium's response.** *Persona Humana* 10 (CDF, 29 December 1975) *uses*
the notion and grants that the option defines a person's moral disposition, but
teaches that any free, knowing choice of something seriously disordered turns a
person from God. RP 17 (1984) repeats this. VS 65-66 affirm a fundamental choice (the
decision of faith, lived in particular choices); VS 67-70 reject separating it from
particular acts: one deliberate choice in grave matter can revoke it, and mortal sin
keeps its classical definition (VS 70, citing RP 17).

- **Level.** The theory: theology, **no level**; the notion is **not condemned**.
  *Persona Humana*, RP and VS on it: **rung 3**.
- Radical and moderate versions can diverge on a planned act of adultery; the
  documents let the act decide, the moderate theory needs the act to reveal a depth
  no one can inspect ([4.3](lessons/04-03-proportionalism-and-the-fundamental-option.md) Example 2).

*Introduced:* [3.4](lessons/03-04-sin-mortal-and-venial.md); stated [4.3](lessons/04-03-proportionalism-and-the-fundamental-option.md); also [4.4](lessons/04-04-veritatis-splendor.md)

### Intrinsically evil acts

**Some kinds of act are evil whatever the intention and circumstances, because of
what they are.** VS 80: objects that cannot be ordered to God because they radically
contradict the good of the person made in his image; the tradition calls them
*intrinsece malum*, evil always and *per se*, apart from further intentions and
circumstances. Intention and circumstances can lessen the evil, not remove it (VS 78,
81).

- **The lists.** VS 80 takes its list from *Gaudium et Spes* 27 and adds
  contraceptive practices, quoting HV 14. CCC 1756 names blasphemy, perjury, murder
  and adultery; CCC 1753 lying and calumny; CCC 1755 fornication; CCC 2370 calls
  contraception intrinsically evil; *Samaritanus Bonus* V.1 euthanasia; EV 62 applies
  the phrase "intrinsically illicit" to direct abortion. RP 17 (1984) already taught
  that some acts are always gravely wrong by their object.
- **Augustine, *Against Lying* 18** (quoted VS 81): works "in themselves evil, such
  as thefts, fornications, blasphemies" cannot on good causes become "no sins, or,
  what is more absurd, just sins". Note "clearly": recognizing the class is where
  [4.5](lessons/04-05-describing-the-object.md) happens.
- **The death penalty**: before 2018 the tradition held it not intrinsically evil;
  the 2018 text does not use the phrase; whether "inadmissible" implies it is half
  the live dispute ([5.4](lessons/05-04-the-death-penalty-and-ccc-2267.md)).
- **Level (a standing test case).** **At least rung 3**: VS presents it as constant
  teaching and restates it "with the authority of the Successor of Peter" (VS 115).
  **Whether also rung 2 is disputed.** The 1998 CDF commentary does not list the
  general thesis; it lists particular norms. Nothing in VS is defined (can. 749 §3).
- **Separate the thesis from the list and from descriptions.** That the class
  exists; that a given kind is in it; and that *this* act is of that kind are three
  claims with three levels.

*Introduced:* [2.2](lessons/02-02-the-moral-object.md); owned [4.4](lessons/04-04-veritatis-splendor.md); also [2.3](lessons/02-03-intention-and-circumstances.md), [4.2](lessons/04-02-double-effect-and-cooperation-as-catholic-method.md), [4.3](lessons/04-03-proportionalism-and-the-fundamental-option.md), [5.2](lessons/05-02-abortion.md), [5.4](lessons/05-04-the-death-penalty-and-ccc-2267.md), [6.1](lessons/06-01-the-theology-of-the-body-and-chastity.md), [6.2](lessons/06-02-humanae-vitae-the-argument.md), [6.3](lessons/06-03-humanae-vitae-its-authority.md), [6.4](lessons/06-04-truthfulness-and-the-lie.md)

### Negative and positive precepts

**Prohibitions of intrinsically evil acts bind always and in every case; positive
commands bind universally, but what they require here and now is prudence's to
say.** Negative precepts bind *semper et pro semper*, for everyone, without exception
(VS 52, 82). Positive precepts (Sunday worship, honouring parents) also bind
universally, but their application depends on circumstances (VS 52, 67).

- **Martyrdom** is the proof case (VS 90-94): if a negative precept has no exception,
  keeping it can cost one's life. Susanna chose death rather than sin (Dan 13:22-23,
  VS 91).
- "Every moral rule is exceptionless" overstates: only the negative precepts
  against intrinsically evil acts are.
- **Level: at least rung 3**, standing or falling with intrinsically evil acts.

*Introduced:* [4.4](lessons/04-04-veritatis-splendor.md)

### What Veritatis Splendor rejects

**The encyclical (6 August 1993) rules on how moral questions are answered, not on
one question; it says this is the first time the Magisterium has set out the
foundations in detail (VS 115).** Chapters: I (6-27) the rich young man; II (28-83)
discernment of recent tendencies under four heads (freedom and law, conscience and
truth, fundamental choice and specific behaviour, the moral act); III (84-117)
martyrdom, witness and the bishops' duty. The thread: no freedom apart from or
against the truth (VS 96).

| Rejects | Does not reject |
|---|---|
| teleologism as the test of the object: no kind of act evil by its object apart from intention and total foreseeable consequences (VS 75, 79) | attention to intention and consequences, which responsibility requires (VS 77) |
| consequences changing an act's species (they are circumstances, VS 77) | weighing goods in prudence and in applying positive precepts (VS 67) |
| exceptions to negative precepts, including "pastoral" ones (VS 56, 67, 82) | tolerating a lesser evil (VS 80, with HV 14); only *doing* evil for good is excluded |
| a fundamental option separable from particular acts (VS 65-70) | a fundamental choice lived in particular choices (VS 65-66) |
| "creative" conscience (VS 54-56) | the duty to follow a certain conscience (VS 60) |
| a merely physical object (VS 78) | any particular theological system for describing objects (VS 29) |

- **The fair test.** McCormick and others answered that the encyclical attacked a
  position none of them held (paraphrased); check whether the target is really
  theirs. On the judge-and-riot case both condemn the hanging; they disagree about
  why ([4.4](lessons/04-04-veritatis-splendor.md) Example 1).
- **Level.** An encyclical, authentic papal magisterium; it defines nothing. The
  rejection of teleologism: **at least rung 3**, standing with the thesis it
  protects.

*Introduced:* [4.4](lessons/04-04-veritatis-splendor.md)

### The perspective of the acting person

**The object is what I choose, not what a camera records — but not whatever I say I
chose.** VS 78 (paraphrase): the object is the proximate end of a deliberate choice,
grasped from the agent's perspective; not a merely physical event; yet some kinds of
behaviour are always wrong to choose, and a good intention cannot rescue them. The
real question is narrower: **how much of the act's physical structure enters the
description of what I chose?**

| Account | The object is... | Direct killing means... |
|---|---|---|
| Manual rule (Holy Office replies, Coppens 1907) | read from where the act lands | the act lands on the child rather than the mother's organism |
| Steven Long, *The Teleological Grammar of the Moral Act* (2007) | the chosen act *with* its natural *per se* ordering, which the agent chooses in choosing the act | the act is *per se* ordered to death |
| Martin Rhonheimer, *Vital Conflicts in Medical Ethics* (2009) | the basic intentional content as practical reason structures it; the physical is its matter | choosing against a life owed respect: a norm of justice, so in a true vital conflict no injustice, no "murder" |

- **The crux**: either the natural ordering of the behaviour fixes the species
  regardless of situation, or a situation where no good can be saved for the victim
  changes what the choice is.
- Rhonheimer is a sharp critic of proportionalism and denies weighing lives; critics
  say he re-imports proportion under "justice". Whether it collapses is an
  evaluative question, not a label.
- Aquinas's self-defense (q.64 a.7) is the shared paradigm of *praeter intentionem*.
- **Level.** VS 78: **rung 3**. Long's and Rhonheimer's accounts: **rung 5**,
  neither taught nor condemned. VS 29: the Magisterium imposes no theological
  system.

*Introduced:* [4.5](lessons/04-05-describing-the-object.md)

### Ectopic pregnancy positions

**Where the post-1993 accounts of the object are tested hardest; the Church has
ruled on the principle, not on the procedures.** In a tubal pregnancy the embryo
implants in the fallopian tube, cannot develop to viability, and threatens rupture
and haemorrhage. Three interventions are discussed:

- **salpingectomy**: removing the tube (or part) with the embryo inside;
- **salpingostomy**: opening the tube and removing the embryo;
- **methotrexate**: a drug that stops cell division in the trophoblast, the
  embryo's own tissue that would form the placenta. Whether it "attacks the embryo"
  or "treats the invading tissue" is the very description in dispute.

**The documents.** The shared norm: no direct killing of the innocent (EV 57); direct
means willed as end or means (EV 62). A Holy Office reply of 20 March 1902 (reported
by Coppens) refused the early extraction of ectopic embryos. The later manual
acceptance of removing the tube was defended above all by T. Lincoln Bouscaren,
*Ethics of Ectopic Operations* (1933, date hedged), by relocating the act onto the
pathological organ. The US bishops' *Ethical and Religious Directives* forbid any
intervention in an extrauterine pregnancy that is a direct abortion, without naming
which procedures are.

**How the schools divide.** Each account in
[The perspective of the acting person](#the-perspective-of-the-acting-person) sorts
the three procedures by its own criterion (where the act lands; natural ordering;
vital conflict). [4.5](lessons/04-05-describing-the-object.md)'s map gives how each school is *usually* applied, with the
caution that neither Long nor Rhonheimer is quoted procedure by procedure.

- **Levels.** Reserved to Boss 4(c): the lesson grades each procedure. What the
  card will say: no universal magisterial act names any of the three.
- Phoenix (November 2009) is the same fight on a different organ: see
  [4.5](lessons/04-05-describing-the-object.md) Example 2.

*Introduced:* [4.5](lessons/04-05-describing-the-object.md)

## Life

The inviolability of innocent life, from the *Didache* to *Evangelium Vitae*, and the
questions where teaching developed or where Catholics still argue. Every entry here
runs on one question from [4.5](lessons/04-05-describing-the-object.md): **is the death chosen, as end or as means?**

### The inviolability of innocent life

**Never choose the death of someone who is not unjustly attacking.** "Thou shalt not
kill" (Ex 20:13) cannot mean "never cause a death": the same Law acquits the
householder who kills a night burglar (Ex 22:2) and commands executions. The verb,
*ratsach*, names homicide within the community; it is used of the accidental
manslayer (Num 35:11) and once of executing a murderer (Num 35:30), so the word alone
does not fix the scope. The tradition read it from Ex 23:7: "The innocent and just
person thou shalt not put to death". (Fifth commandment in the Catholic and Lutheran
numbering, sixth in the Jewish and Reformed.)

- **Innocent** = not engaged in unjust aggression (*in-nocens*, not harming), not
  "sinless": a psychotic attacker is blameless and still not innocent in this sense
  (EV 55). Aquinas's own "innocent" in q.64 a.6 means righteous as against guilty,
  because his exception there is punishment.
- **Direct** = chosen as end or means; a foreseen, unchosen death falls under
  [double effect](#double-effect).
- **Aquinas**, II-II q.64 a.6: "in every man though he be sinful, we ought to love
  the nature which God has made"; the only exception runs through the common good
  (a.2), reserved to public authority (a.3); the innocent supply nothing to trigger
  it, so "it is in no way lawful to slay the innocent". God alone is Lord of life and
  death (a.6 ad 1; EV 55).
- **Self-defense is outside the norm, not an exception to it**: see
  [Legitimate defense](#legitimate-defense).
- **Its reach to the embryo** ([5.5](lessons/05-05-assisted-reproduction-and-the-embryo.md)): EV 63, DV I.5, CCC 2274-2275; **at least rung 3** as an application. See [Assist and replace](#assist-and-replace).
- **The norm's level** is a standing test case reserved to Boss 5(b): see
  [Evangelium Vitae 57](#evangelium-vitae-57) and [5.1](lessons/05-01-the-inviolability-of-innocent-life.md).
- **Norm and application.** "Direct killing of the innocent is always wrong" and
  "this procedure is a direct killing" are separate claims; the second is often
  freely disputed ([4.5](lessons/04-05-describing-the-object.md)).

*Introduced:* [5.1](lessons/05-01-the-inviolability-of-innocent-life.md); also [5.5](lessons/05-05-assisted-reproduction-and-the-embryo.md)

### Evangelium Vitae 57

**The pope *confirms*, he does not define.** John Paul II, *Evangelium Vitae* (25
March 1995). Background: the 1991 consistory of cardinals asked him to reaffirm the
inviolability of life with Petrine authority, and at Pentecost 1991 he wrote to every
bishop for their cooperation (EV 5). Paragraph 57, paraphrased, in three steps:

1. **The evidence.** Scripture teaches it, Tradition upholds it, the Magisterium
   proposes it; its constancy shows the supernatural sense of the faith (citing LG
   12).
2. **The formula.** By the authority Christ conferred on Peter and his successors,
   and in communion with the bishops, the pope confirms that the direct and voluntary
   killing of an innocent human being is always gravely immoral, grounded in natural
   law (Rom 2:14-15), Scripture, Tradition and the teaching of the ordinary and
   universal magisterium (citing LG 25).
3. **The scope.** Never licit, as end or as means; no one may ask for it or consent
   to it; no authority may permit it.

- **The evidence for its level.** The route is the ordinary and universal
  magisterium attested, not an *ex cathedra* definition. The 1998 CDF commentary
  §11 lists this doctrine, citing EV 57 (note 31), among its **first**-paragraph
  examples (truths to be believed as divinely revealed). The grading is reserved to
  Boss 5(b): see [5.1](lessons/05-01-the-inviolability-of-innocent-life.md).
- **Contrast.** EV 56 (the death penalty) does not fall under this formula; EV 62
  and 65 use parallel ones ([Evangelium Vitae 62](#evangelium-vitae-62),
  [Euthanasia](#euthanasia)).
- How the ordinary universal magisterium works:
  [`fundamental-theology` 4.2](../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-02-infallibility-and-its-conditions.md).

*Introduced:* [5.1](lessons/05-01-the-inviolability-of-innocent-life.md)

### Legitimate defense

**The defender chooses to stop the attack; the attacker's death is beside the
intention.** II-II q.64 a.7: "moral acts take their species according to what is
intended, and not according to what is beside the intention"; the act is lawful if
the force is proportionate, unlawful if excessive. A private person may not intend
the killing; public authority may, referring it to the public good.

- **CCC 2263**: legitimate defense is **not an exception** to the prohibition of
  murdering the innocent; 2263-2264 quote a.7; 2265: defense can be a grave duty for
  one responsible for others. EV 55: the aggressor need not be culpable.
- **When the attack ends, so does defense.** Shooting a fleeing attacker "so he
  never comes back" chooses his death, which a private person may not do (q.64 a.3)
  ([5.1](lessons/05-01-the-inviolability-of-innocent-life.md) Example 1).
- **Defense vs punishment** ([5.4](lessons/05-04-the-death-penalty-and-ccc-2267.md)): Brugger can allow killing an escaping
  aggressor in *defense* while denying execution as *punishment*.
- **Level.** Lawful, and can be a duty: **at least rung 3** (CCC 2263-2265; EV 55).
  Whether a private defender may ever intend the death: a **theologians' question**.
- The secular principle: [`ethics` 3.2](../ethics/lessons/03-02-the-doctrine-of-double-effect.md);
  war is [`catholic-social-teaching`](../catholic-social-teaching/syllabus.md)'s.

*Introduced:* [5.1](lessons/05-01-the-inviolability-of-innocent-life.md); also [5.4](lessons/05-04-the-death-penalty-and-ccc-2267.md)

### Evangelium Vitae 62

**Direct abortion, willed as end or means, is always gravely wrong as the deliberate
killing of an innocent human being.** EV 62 (1995), paraphrased: after reviewing the
tradition, the pope, by the authority conferred on Peter and his successors and in
communion with the bishops (who, though dispersed, have shown unanimous agreement),
declares that direct abortion is always a grave moral disorder; the teaching rests on
natural law and the written Word of God, is transmitted by Tradition and taught by
the ordinary and universal magisterium. No circumstance, purpose or law can make
licit an act intrinsically illicit.

- **The evidence the lessons weigh.** A confirming formula like EV 57's, not an *ex
  cathedra* definition. It defines direct abortion as a case of killing the innocent,
  whose norm the 1998 commentary places under its first paragraph; the commentary
  itself **does not name abortion**, so any placement from it is an inference, and
  must be said to be one. The grading is reserved to Boss 4(c): see [5.2](lessons/05-02-abortion.md).
- **Understating** it as "a prudential judgment" or "authentic but reformable" is an
  error the lesson names.
- **Discipline is not doctrine.** EV 62 mentions the present automatic
  excommunication; penalties are canonical and changeable.

*Introduced:* [5.2](lessons/05-02-abortion.md)

### Delayed animation

**Aquinas held the rational soul arrives weeks after conception; the teaching on
abortion never depended on the answer.** ST I q.118 a.2 ad 2: the embryo has "the
nutritive soul from the beginning, then the sensitive, lastly the intellectual soul",
the last "created by God at the end of human generation". II-II q.64 a.8 ad 2: one who
strikes a pregnant woman is guilty of homicide if death results to her "or of the
animated fetus".

- **Modern forms.** Joseph Donceel (1970): on hylomorphic grounds a rational soul
  needs a body organized enough to receive it. Norman Ford (1988): an embryo that can
  still twin is not yet one individual.
- **The Church's reply, in three parts.** (1) *History*: medieval authors who held
  delayed animation still judged abortion a grave sin at every stage; they graded the
  *penalty*, not the permission (*Declaration on Procured Abortion* 7, citing
  Aquinas on the *Sentences* IV d.31); Innocent XI (1679) condemned a proposition
  excusing abortion before animation. (2) *Independence*: the *Declaration* (1974)
  note 19 leaves ensoulment unresolved and gives two reasons the norm stands either
  way: the embryo is a human life preparing for and calling for a soul; and the
  soul's presence is at least *probable*, so to kill is to accept the risk of killing
  a man (EV 60 restates the second). (3) *The practical criterion*: *Donum Vitae* I.1
  (1987) declines to commit to a philosophical thesis, asks how a human individual
  could fail to be a human person, and fixes the norm: from conception the embryo is
  to be respected and **treated as a person**.
- **The two tracks** ([5.2](lessons/05-02-abortion.md)): penal discipline moved (Elvira, Ancyra c.21 of 314, Sixtus V 1588, Gregory XIV, Pius IX 1869, the codes); the moral judgment did not, from the *Didache* ch. 2 ("thou shalt not murder a child by abortion", ANF 7) on.
- **Level.** Embryo treated as a person from conception: **rung 3** (DV I.1; EV 60).
  When the soul is infused: **freely disputed**; "the Church teaches immediate
  ensoulment" overstates.
- The *Didache* as a text: [`patristics` 1.2](../patristics/lessons/01-02-first-clement-and-the-didache.md).

*Introduced:* [5.2](lessons/05-02-abortion.md)

### Euthanasia

**An act or omission that of itself and by intention causes death, to eliminate
suffering.** EV 65, taking the definition from the CDF's *Declaration on Euthanasia*
(5 May 1980) part II. The 1980 English text and CCC 2277 read "of itself *or* by
intention"; EV 65's English reads "and". Build nothing on the conjunction. The method
and the intention define it, not the medical setting.

- **The line is not act vs omission.** An omission chosen to cause death is
  euthanasia; a positive act (a dose that risks death) can be licit. The line runs
  through **what is chosen** ([The moral object](#the-moral-object)).
- **Aquinas**, II-II q.64 a.5 ad 3: man is "master of himself" in what pertains to
  this life, "but the passage from this life to another and happier one is subject not
  to man's free-will but to the power of God"; so not to escape "any unhappiness
  whatsoever". Hence EV 65: the malice of suicide or of murder, depending on the
  circumstances.
- **The acts and the evidence.** EV 65 uses the confirming formula of EV 57. The
  1998 CDF commentary §11 discusses euthanasia among its **second**-paragraph
  examples (definitive, connected with revelation by logical necessity), adding at
  once that Scripture excludes the self-determination euthanasia presupposes — a
  sentence some theologians read as leaving room for the first paragraph.
  *Samaritanus Bonus* (CDF, dated 14 July 2020, approved 25 June 2020, made public
  September 2020) V.1 calls the teaching definitive and euthanasia intrinsically evil.
  The grading is reserved to Boss 5(b): see [5.3](lessons/05-03-euthanasia-and-the-end-of-life.md).
- **Cooperation**: SB V.1 calls formal or immediate material cooperation grave; V.11
  on chaplains.

*Introduced:* [5.3](lessons/05-03-euthanasia-and-the-end-of-life.md)

### Proportionate and disproportionate means

**You may never choose death, but you need not choose every means of postponing
it.** The duty to preserve life binds only to **ordinary** means, those imposing no
extraordinary burden on oneself or others: Pius XII, address to anesthesiologists,
**24 November 1957** (the distinction usually traced to sixteenth-century Dominican
moralists). The *Declaration on Euthanasia* (1980) part IV prefers
**proportionate / disproportionate** and gives the test: weigh the treatment's type,
complexity, risk, cost and availability against the expected result, given the
patient's state and resources.

- Forgoing disproportionate means is not suicide or euthanasia; it accepts the human
  condition before death (EV 65; CCC 2278).
- **Who decides**: the patient if competent, otherwise those entitled to speak for
  him (CCC 2278; Declaration IV). The normal care due to the sick is never
  interrupted (Declaration IV; CCC 2279).
- **"Extraordinary" is not "high-tech"**: a simple treatment can be disproportionate
  for one patient and a complex one ordinary for another.
- **The minimal pair** ([5.3](lessons/05-03-euthanasia-and-the-end-of-life.md)): withdrawing a ventilator a patient judges
  gravely burdensome is licit; the same withdrawal requested *so that he will die*
  before his savings run out is euthanasia by omission.
- **Level.** The principle: **rung 3**, constant since Pius XII. Whether *this* means
  is disproportionate for *this* patient: **no magisterial level**; the patient's
  judgment, formed by the criteria.

*Introduced:* [5.3](lessons/05-03-euthanasia-and-the-end-of-life.md)

### Pain relief and palliative sedation

**Relief may be chosen even at the risk of shortening life, so long as death is
neither the end nor the means.** CCC 2279; EV 65. Pius XII said so in a *different*
1957 address, of **24 February** (cited at EV 65, note 79); the *Declaration* (1980)
part III.

- **Consciousness**: the dying are not to be deprived of it without serious reason,
  since they must be able to prepare to meet God (EV 65).
- ***Samaritanus Bonus* V.7** extends the permission to deep palliative sedation
  even where it hastens death, provided the intention to kill is excluded.
- This is [double effect](#double-effect) at the end of life.
- **Level: rung 3.**

*Introduced:* [5.3](lessons/05-03-euthanasia-and-the-end-of-life.md)

### Artificial nutrition and hydration

**Food and water, even by tube, are care, not treatment.** John Paul II, address of
20 March 2004 (§4): a natural means of preserving life, not a medical act; "in
principle, ordinary and proportionate", and so obligatory as long as it attains its
end of nourishing the patient; withdrawing it knowingly to end life is euthanasia by
omission. CDF responses to the US bishops (1 August 2007, approved by Benedict XVI in
ordinary form) apply this to the "permanent vegetative state".

- **Exceptions** (the CDF's accompanying commentary): feeding physically impossible
  (remote places, extreme poverty); the body can no longer assimilate it; in rare
  cases, excessive burden or significant discomfort. *Samaritanus Bonus* V.8 repeats
  that such measures can become disproportionate in some cases.
- **The pre-2004 argument at strength**: Pius XII grounded the duty in life's
  subordination to spiritual ends; tube-feeding is placed surgically and managed
  clinically. The 2004/2007 reply denies the premises, not the method.
- **Level: rung 3.** Before 2004 the application was argued; after 2007 it is not a
  free question (the *Humani Generis* principle). "Defined" or "rung 2" overstates.

*Introduced:* [5.3](lessons/05-03-euthanasia-and-the-end-of-life.md)

### The death penalty and CCC 2267

**For eight centuries the tradition said the state may execute; from 1995 the popes
said it almost never should; in 2018 the Catechism called it "inadmissible".** Keep
two questions apart: *principle* (can the state ever rightly take a criminal's life?)
and *application* (should this state execute anyone now?).

| Date | Act | Content (paraphrased unless public domain) |
|---|---|---|
| 18 December 1208 | Innocent III, letter *Eius exemplo*: a profession of faith for Durand of Huesca and his Waldensian companions | the secular power can exercise a "judgment of blood" without mortal sin, if it proceeds by judgment and not in hatred |
| c. 1271 | ST II-II q.64 a.2 | [the diseased limb](#the-diseased-limb-argument) |
| 1566 | *Roman Catechism*, issued under Pius V at Trent's direction | the civil sword wielded by justice is "an act of paramount obedience to this commandment which prohibits murder" (Donovan, 1829) |
| 1992 | CCC 2266, first edition | proportionate penalties, "not excluding, in cases of extreme gravity, the death penalty" (as reported in secondary sources); bloodless means to be preferred when sufficient |
| 1995 | [EV 56](#evangelium-vitae-56) | only when absolutely necessary; such cases "very rare, if not practically non-existent" |
| 1997 | CCC 2267, Latin typical edition | writes EV 56 into the Catechism |
| 11 May 2018 | Francis approves a new CCC 2267 | |
| 1 August 2018 | CDF rescript (published 2 August) and letter to bishops | "inadmissible" |

**The 2018 text**, paraphrased: execution was long considered an appropriate, if
extreme, response; awareness has grown that dignity is not lost even after very
serious crimes; there is a new understanding of penal sanctions; effective detention
protects citizens without removing the chance of redemption; so, "in the light of the
Gospel", the death penalty is inadmissible as an attack on the inviolability and
dignity of the person, and the Church works for its abolition. **The CDF letter**
(§§7-8): an authentic development, "not in contradiction" with prior teaching (citing
Vincent of Lérins); earlier teaching is explained by its social context; the duty of
authority to defend citizens stands (CCC 2265-2266).

**The dispute** (both sides agree on the history):

- **Contradiction worry**: Edward Feser and Joseph Bessette, *By Man Shall His Blood
  Be Shed* (2017), paraphrased: legitimacy in principle is taught by Scripture (Gen
  9:6; Rom 13:4), the Fathers and Doctors and popes without a break, so it is
  irreformable; the use is prudential. Written before 2018, it implies "inadmissible"
  keeps continuity only as a judgment about present conditions.
- **Development case**: E. Christian Brugger, *Capital Punishment and Roman Catholic
  Moral Tradition* (2003), paraphrased in outline: the acceptance was authoritative
  but never definitive; EV and 1997 had already moved the justification from
  retribution to defense, on whose logic killing someone no longer a threat can be
  excluded in principle.
- **They disagree about** the level of the old teaching and what "inadmissible"
  asserts (a principle, or present conditions). Canon 749 §3 is the default-down rule
  both must engage.
- **Newman's notes** ([`fundamental-theology` 5.2](../fundamental-theology/lessons/05-02-newmans-theory-of-development.md)) are evidence each side cites (anticipation, conservative action, continuity of principles), not proof.
- **Levels.** The level of the 2018 text and whether execution is intrinsically evil
  are reserved to Boss 5(b): see [5.4](lessons/05-04-the-death-penalty-and-ccc-2267.md). Settled around them: no council or *ex
  cathedra* act ever defined licitness; the *Roman Catechism* is not a conciliar
  decree; EV 56 and 1997 are **rung 3**, a prudential judgment of fact; the duty to
  defend and punish proportionately (CCC 2265-2266) is **rung 3** and unchanged.

*Introduced:* [5.4](lessons/05-04-the-death-penalty-and-ccc-2267.md)

### The diseased limb argument

**The part exists for the whole, so a member dangerous to the body may be cut off.**
II-II q.64 a.2: "if the health of the whole body demands the excision of a member,
through its being decayed or infectious to the other members, it will be both
praiseworthy and advantageous to have it cut away"; each person is compared to the
community as part to whole; so a man "dangerous and infectious to the community, on
account of some sin" may be killed for the common good (1 Cor 5:6). Article 3 reserves
this to public authority.

- **The justification is harm to the community**, not simply guilt.
- **The limit of the analogy**: a person is not *only* a part; a.6 insists every man
  "in himself" is to be loved.
- **Reply 3**: "although it be evil in itself to kill a man so long as he preserve
  his dignity, yet it may be good to kill a man who has sinned, even as it is to kill
  a beast"; by grave sin a man "falls away from the dignity of his manhood". The 2018
  CCC 2267 affirms that dignity is not lost even after very serious crimes.
- The argument's structure is Boss 5(a)'s to reconstruct.

*Introduced:* [5.4](lessons/05-04-the-death-penalty-and-ccc-2267.md)

### Evangelium Vitae 56

**Keep the principle, confine its use almost to nothing.** EV 56 (1995), paraphrased:
punishment's primary purpose is to redress the disorder caused by the offence;
authority should not go to the extreme of execution except in cases of absolute
necessity, when it would not otherwise be possible to defend society; because penal
systems have steadily improved, such cases today are "very rare, if not practically
non-existent". It keeps the 1992 Catechism's bloodless-means principle.

- **Not under EV 57's formula**: EV 57 concerns the *innocent*; EV 56 and 57 carry
  different levels.
- **Level: rung 3**, a prudential judgment about facts; written into CCC 2267 in the
  1997 typical edition.
- Answering "the popes say it is never needed now" answers the *application*
  question, not the principle.

*Introduced:* [5.4](lessons/05-04-the-death-penalty-and-ccc-2267.md)

### Donum Vitae and Dignitas Personae

**The two CDF instructions on procreation and the embryo.**

| | *Donum Vitae* | *Dignitas Personae* |
|---|---|---|
| Date | 22 February 1987 | dated 8 September 2008; approved by Benedict XVI 20 June 2008 |
| Signed / approved | Cardinal Ratzinger; approved by John Paul II, who ordered publication | Cardinal Levada; calls itself a *doctrinal* instruction (DP 1) |
| Key sections | I.1 (treat as person from conception); I.5-6 (embryo research, freezing); II.A.2 (heterologous); II.B.4-7 (inseparability; the simple case; assist vs replace) | 1 (DV fully valid); 12-13 (licit aids); 14-15 (IVF in practice); 17 (ICSI); 18 (freezing); 19 (frozen embryos, adoption); 21 (embryo reduction); 22 (PGD); 35 (use of illicit biological material); 37 (religious assent) |

- **Level.** CDF documents approved by the pope share in his ordinary magisterium
  (*Donum Veritatis* 18); neither was approved *in forma specifica*; DP 37 asks
  religious assent. **Rung 3.** Their appeal to the norm against killing the innocent
  carries that norm's own weight.
- **On ensoulment** both deliberately decline a philosophical commitment (DV I.1; EV
  60).
- **Augustine's triad** behind DV II.B.4: the goods of marriage are "offspring,
  faith, sacrament" (*On the Good of Marriage* 32, NPNF 1.3); fidelity as the spouses'
  right to become parents only through each other (DV II.A.2; 1 Cor 7:4).

*Introduced:* [5.5](lessons/05-05-assisted-reproduction-and-the-embryo.md)

### Assist and replace

**A technique that removes an obstacle so the spouses' own act can conceive is
licit; one that makes conception the result of a laboratory act is not.** DV
II.B.6-7; DP 12, drawing the line from Pius XII's 1949 address to Catholic doctors.

| Technique | Verdict | Source |
|---|---|---|
| hormonal treatment, endometriosis surgery, tubal repair | licit: assists | DP 13 |
| restorative programmes (e.g. NaProTechnology) | of this class, though no document names them | [5.5](lessons/05-05-assisted-reproduction-and-the-embryo.md) |
| homologous IVF, even the "simple case" (own gametes, one embryo, none frozen, no masturbation) | illicit: replaces | DV II.B.5; CCC 2377 |
| ICSI | illicit | DP 17 |
| donor gametes (heterologous) | gravely immoral: fails fidelity too | DV II.A.2; CCC 2376 |
| GIFT | no ruling: disputed whether it assists or replaces | rung 5 |

- **Two grounds.** Ground A: what happens to embryos (selection, freezing, discard,
  research). Ground B: the [inseparability principle](#the-inseparability-principle)
  — procreation apart from union, "an analogous separation" to contraception (DV
  II.B.4). The simple case fails on Ground B alone.
- **Totality answered**: the couple's whole married life cannot lend the laboratory
  procedure its moral quality; it is judged in itself (DV II.B.5). McCormick and
  others defended the simple case on the majority report's totality logic.
- **The child** so conceived is to be loved as God's gift; the judgment falls on the
  procedure, never on the child. A child is a gift, not something owed (CCC 2378).
- **Level.** The aid/substitute principle and the IVF, ICSI and heterologous
  verdicts: **rung 3**. GIFT: **rung 5**.

*Introduced:* [5.5](lessons/05-05-assisted-reproduction-and-the-embryo.md)

### Embryo adoption

**Left open: may a woman gestate a frozen embryo so that it can be born?**
*Dignitas Personae* 19, paraphrased: using frozen embryos for research, or giving them
to infertile couples as *treatment*, is unacceptable; "prenatal adoption" undertaken
solely so that they may be born is praiseworthy in intention but presents "problems
not dissimilar" to those already mentioned; the situation is an injustice that cannot
be resolved. It gives **no verdict of illicitness**.

- **For** (Grisez among others): a rescue; the woman gestates a child already in
  existence, and gestation is not the conjugal act.
- **Against** (Mary Geach, Nicholas Tonti-Filippini among others): becoming pregnant
  other than through one's husband violates what fidelity reserves to the spouses.
- DP 19 is read by some as a tilt against, by others as a deliberate abstention.
- **Level: rung 5.** "Condemned" and "endorsed" both overstate.

*Introduced:* [5.5](lessons/05-05-assisted-reproduction-and-the-embryo.md)

## Sexuality

The theological vision behind Catholic sexual ethics, *Humanae Vitae*'s argument,
and the teaching whose authority is most argued about.

### The theology of the body

**John Paul II's catechesis on "the beginning": creation, not Moses's concession, is
the norm.** Asked about divorce, Jesus answers that "from the beginning it was not
so" (Mt 19:4-8, Douay-Rheims). Wednesday general audiences, **5 September 1979 to 28
November 1984**, roughly 130, read three words of Christ:

1. "the beginning" (Mt 19): original solitude, unity, nakedness; the
   [nuptial meaning of the body](#the-nuptial-meaning-of-the-body);
2. adultery "in the heart" (Mt 5:27-28): concupiscence, the look that uses, the
   redemption of the body;
3. the resurrection, where "they shall neither marry nor be married" (Mt 22:30): the
   body's final meaning, celibacy for the kingdom;

then marriage as sacrament (Eph 5) and a rereading of *Humanae Vitae* (1984), the
"language of the body". In the last audience he called "theology of the body" a
working term and the cycle, in effect, an extended commentary on HV.

- **The heart, not only the act.** Audience of 8 October 1980: the man of Mt 5:28
  commits adultery in the heart because of *how* he looks; he would do the same
  looking so at his own wife. No act-type rule captures this; it is exegesis in a
  catechesis and grades no marital act.
- **Level.** An audience catechesis: **rung 3 at its lightest grade**. Where it
  restates doctrine it carries that doctrine's level; its own categories are papal
  theology no act proposes as doctrine. Fame does not raise weight.

*Introduced:* [6.1](lessons/06-01-the-theology-of-the-body-and-chastity.md)

### The nuptial meaning of the body

**The sexed body can express love in which the person becomes a gift, and in giving
fulfils the meaning of his existence.** Audience of 16 January 1980 (paraphrase),
anchored in *Gaudium et Spes* 24: man, the one creature God willed for its own sake,
finds himself only through a sincere gift of himself. *Familiaris Consortio* 11
(1981): physical self-giving without total personal self-giving would be a lie.

- **The personalist route to the norm** ([6.1](lessons/06-01-the-theology-of-the-body-and-chastity.md)): the body expresses the person
  (CCC 2332); a person finds himself only in gift and may never be merely used; the
  sexual act is by nature a bodily act of total self-giving, open to new life; a
  bodily gift is true only if the personal gift is total (exclusive, definitive); so
  genital acts outside marriage say with the body what the persons have not given.
- **The Thomist route** (II-II q.154 a.2): the upbringing of a child needs a father
  bound to a determinate woman; fornication is an "indeterminate union"; a matter
  under law "is judged according to what happens in general". Different premises,
  same conclusion; **rung 4 at most** as an argument.
- **Level.** Papal theology, not a defined thesis.

*Introduced:* [6.1](lessons/06-01-the-theology-of-the-body-and-chastity.md)

### The personalistic norm

**A person may never be merely used; the only fitting attitude to a person is
love.** Karol Wojtyła, *Love and Responsibility* (1960), paraphrased: the negative
form (never merely a means) and the positive form (love). Assumed in [6.1](lessons/06-01-the-theology-of-the-body-and-chastity.md) as a
premise of the argument for chastity, and owned by
[`phenomenology-and-personalism`](../phenomenology-and-personalism/syllabus.md)
(5.2-5.3 there).

- The Kantian ancestor is [`ethics` 2.3](../ethics/lessons/02-03-humanity-autonomy-and-the-lie.md).
- **Level.** Philosophy, no magisterial level.

*Introduced:* [6.1](lessons/06-01-the-theology-of-the-body-and-chastity.md)

### Chastity

**The virtue that makes the gift of self true: self-mastery ordered to gift.** The
Catechism defines it as the successful integration of sexuality within the person
(CCC 2337, paraphrased); a part of temperance (CCC 2341; II-II q.151); self-mastery is
ordered to the gift of self (CCC 2346): you cannot give what you do not possess.

- **Everyone, by state of life**: conjugal chastity for the married, continence for
  others (CCC 2348-2350). Engagement is a time of continence (CCC 2350). Chastity is
  not celibacy.
- **Not prudishness.** II-II q.153 a.2: the sexual act can be without sin, "provided
  they be performed in due manner and order, in keeping with the end of human
  procreation", as food can be taken rightly. Intense pleasure in an ordered act "is
  not opposed to the mean of virtue" (ad 2). The passion's resistance to reason is a
  *penalty* of the first sin, a wound not a guilt (ad 2-3).
- **Offences** (CCC 2351-2356): fornication (CCC 2353); adultery adds injustice to
  spouse and covenant (CCC 2380-2381).
- ***Persona Humana* 7**: the engaged couple — however firm the intention, union is
  legitimate only once a definitive community of life exists.
- **The case against, at strength.** Margaret Farley, *Just Love* (2006): judge
  sexual acts by norms of justice (no unjust harm, free consent, mutuality,
  equality, commitment, fruitfulness, social justice); committed non-marital or
  same-sex relationships can meet them. The Catholic reply grants the demand for
  justice and denies that consent and mutuality settle what the act *means*. The
  CDF's *Notification* (30 March 2012, approved by Benedict XVI) judged several of
  its positions contrary to Catholic teaching.
- **Levels.** The Catechism's account of chastity: **rung 3**. Fornication and
  prostitution: **rung 2 on the 1998 commentary's sorting** (§11, citing CCC 2353).
  Adultery: **at least rung 3** (CCC 1756, 2380), commonly held revealed as a
  Decalogue precept (Ex 20:14), which you must cite to claim. *Persona Humana*
  (approved 7 November 1975, dated 29 December 1975): **rung 3** as an act.

*Introduced:* [6.1](lessons/06-01-the-theology-of-the-body-and-chastity.md)

### Responsible parenthood

**Generosity or, for serious reasons, spacing births — discerned within God's order,
not simply decided.** HV 10, paraphrased: responsible parenthood is exercised either
by the deliberate and generous decision to raise a large family or, for serious
reasons and with respect for the moral law, by deciding to avoid a new birth for a
time or indefinitely. HV 16 requires well-grounded reasons even for recourse to the
infertile periods.

- Common ground in 1968: married love is good; couples may have serious reasons to
  space or limit births; intercourse in infertile times is legitimate (HV 11; Pius
  XII's 1951 address to midwives, cited GS 51 note 14).
- **Level: rung 3**, the same document and rung as the norm.

*Introduced:* [6.2](lessons/06-02-humanae-vitae-the-argument.md)

### The inseparability principle

**One act, two meanings: God has joined the unitive and the procreative meanings of
the conjugal act, and man may not break the connection on his own initiative** (HV 12,
paraphrased). Hence HV 11: each and every marital act must retain its intrinsic
relationship to procreation; infertile acts keep it, because their unitive adaptation
is not suppressed by the couple. Man is not master of the sources of life (HV 13).

- **Two directions of separation.** Contraception keeps union and excludes
  procreation; IVF seeks procreation apart from union, "an analogous separation"
  (DV II.B.4, quoting HV 12) ([5.5](lessons/05-05-assisted-reproduction-and-the-embryo.md)).
- **What it excludes** (HV 14): every action specifically intended to prevent
  procreation, before, during or after intercourse; direct sterilization; abortion.
  HV 14 rejects the [totality](#the-principle-of-totality) reply by name.
- **What it allows**: therapeutic means with a foreseen, unintended impediment (HV
  15); recourse to infertile periods for well-grounded reasons (HV 16), because the
  couple use the faculty as nature provides it and abstain, rather than obstruct the
  generative process — HV 16 concedes the intention to avoid a child may be the same.
- **"Artificial" is not the criterion**: HV 16 praises intelligence directing
  nature, and HV 15 permits drugs with contraceptive effects.
- **Earlier reasons were different.** Augustine condemned contraception by "wrong
  desire or evil appliance" (*On Marriage and Concupiscence* I.15.17, NPNF 1.5) but on
  premises that made intercourse not sought for offspring a venial sin, and he
  condemned the Manichees' use of infertile times too (*On the Morals of the
  Manichaeans* 18.65). *Casti Connubii* (31 December 1930) restated the norm; it was
  not a definition.
- **Level.** **At least rung 3**; whether infallibly taught is [6.3](lessons/06-03-humanae-vitae-its-authority.md)'s dispute.

*Introduced:* [6.2](lessons/06-02-humanae-vitae-the-argument.md); used in [5.5](lessons/05-05-assisted-reproduction-and-the-embryo.md)

### The principle of totality

**A part may be sacrificed for the good of the whole.** Pius XII allowed a bodily
part to be sacrificed for the whole body (amputating a gangrenous limb). The birth
control commission's majority (papers leaked 1967) extended it from the body to the
marriage: particular acts may be made infertile for the good of a marriage fruitful
as a whole.

- **The majority's case at strength.** GS 50-51 order marriage to children and ask
  that methods be judged by objective standards drawn from the person and his acts,
  without saying each act must be procreative (GS 51 note 14 left the question to the
  commission). Once the Church approved infertile-period intercourse to avoid
  conception, it granted that couples may will intercourse and will that no child
  result; so only means remain, and means are judged by the whole. Reading morality
  off biology is [physicalism](#physicalism). *Casti Connubii* was not a definition
  and could develop, as usury judgments had.
- **The encyclical's answer.** HV 3 states the question fairly; HV 14 rejects
  totality by name (a contraceptive act cannot borrow goodness from the fertile acts
  around it; tolerating a lesser evil is sometimes lawful, doing evil that good may
  come is not); HV 17 insists on a *correct* understanding of Pius XII's principle.
- **Level.** The majority report: **no magisterial weight** (advice; HV 6).
- Caricaturing the majority as indifferent to children is a pitfall: it required
  marriages generously open to life. The dispute was over the unit.

*Introduced:* [6.2](lessons/06-02-humanae-vitae-the-argument.md)

### The contralife argument

**The wrong in contraception lies in the will against a possible child, not in
frustrating a faculty.** Germain Grisez, Joseph Boyle, John Finnis and William May,
"Every Marital Act Ought to Be Open to New Life", *The Thomist* 52 (1988),
paraphrased, building on the basic goods of
[`ethics` 4.5](../ethics/lessons/04-05-the-new-natural-law-theory.md): human life
is a basic good; one may never choose directly against a basic good; contraception is
a distinct act chosen because one foresees that a new life might begin and wants it
not to; abstaining is not an act against anything.

- **Its cost**: a couple using periodic continence with a will against the child
  would share the wrong, which its authors grant. It also condemns contraception
  outside marriage as contralife.
- **Level.** A theologians' argument, **freely disputed as an argument**. If it fails,
  the norm does not fall; if the norm binds, the argument is not thereby proven.

*Introduced:* [6.2](lessons/06-02-humanae-vitae-the-argument.md)

### The level of Humanae Vitae

**Keep three questions apart.** (1) Is the encyclical a definition? **No**, on every
reading. (2) What is the floor? **Rung 3**, religious submission of intellect and
will, at the heaviest end of that rung. (3) Was the norm *already* infallibly taught
before 1968 by the bishops dispersed through the world concurring in it *as
definitively to be held* (LG 25)? **Freely disputed** — a question about the norm's
history, not the document's genre.

- **The floor's markers.** *Humanae Vitae* (25 July 1968): Paul VI examined the
  question personally because the commission was not unanimous, and answered by the
  mandate entrusted to him by Christ (HV 6); he asks priests who teach moral theology
  for inward as well as outward obedience (HV 28). LG 25's signs (character of the
  document, frequent repetition, manner of speaking) are all strong. HV 14 calls
  deliberately contraceptive intercourse intrinsically wrong.
- **Why not a definition.** No defining formula; can. 749 §3 (1983 Code),
  paraphrased: no doctrine is understood as infallibly defined unless this is
  manifestly established. Paul VI used no formula of confirmation either (contrast
  EV 57).
- **The two readings**: [Ford and Grisez on infallibility](#ford-and-grisez-on-infallibility);
  [Sullivan on the burden of proof](#sullivan-on-the-burden-of-proof). No
  magisterial act has settled which is right (contrast the CDF's 1995 response on
  *Ordinatio Sacerdotalis*, which did settle its case).
- **Newman's rule**, *Letter to the Duke of Norfolk* §9: his "repugnance to impose
  upon the faith of others more than what the Church distinctly claims of them", met
  "with a wise and gentle minimism" — but owed to "a generous loyalty towards
  ecclesiastical authority". Minimism forbids inflating a level; it does not license
  hunting the lowest defensible level to escape the teaching.
- **Level is not truth; reception does not vote.** Rung 3 means not shown to be
  irreformable, not "probably wrong"; widespread non-practice lowers nothing
  ([`fundamental-theology` 5.3](../fundamental-theology/lessons/05-03-the-sensus-fidei.md)).
- The event and the dissent: [`church-history` 9.3](../church-history/lessons/09-03-after-the-council.md).

*Introduced:* [6.3](lessons/06-03-humanae-vitae-its-authority.md)

### Ford and Grisez on infallibility

**The norm was infallibly taught by the ordinary universal magisterium before 1968:
the bishops everywhere taught it as a grave law of salvation, and no formula was
needed.** John C. Ford and Germain Grisez, *Theological Studies* 39 (1978) 258-312.
Supporters also cite the 1998 CDF commentary's note 17 (diachronic ordinary universal
magisterium) and the 1997 *Vademecum for Confessors* (part 2, n. 4), which calls the
teaching definitive and irreformable.

*Introduced:* [6.3](lessons/06-03-humanae-vitae-its-authority.md)

### Sullivan on the burden of proof

**Authoritative, but not shown to be infallibly taught: can. 749 §3 puts the burden
on the one who claims infallibility, and teaching a grave obligation is not the same
as proposing it as irreformable.** Francis A. Sullivan SJ, *Magisterium* (1983)
143-52; Grisez replied in *The Thomist* 49 (1985). Supporters also note that the 1998
commentary's moral examples of definitive doctrine (§11) omit contraception, while
that list is not exhaustive.

*Introduced:* [6.3](lessons/06-03-humanae-vitae-its-authority.md)

### The Winnipeg Statement

**A bishops' conference cannot move a universal norm's level, and its statement is
evidence in the reception debate, not a ruling.** Canadian bishops, plenary assembly
at St Boniface, Winnipeg, 27 September 1968, paraphrased: some Catholics find the
teaching extremely difficult or impossible to make their own; confessors should be
understanding; one who, after honest effort, chooses the course that seems right to
him does so in good conscience (§26).

- **Genre**: a pastoral statement of a conference, **particular, not universal**
  magisterium. Its conscience clause is measured by [2.4](lessons/02-04-conscience-its-nature-and-its-errors.md)'s account of the
  erroneous conscience.
- **As evidence**: for one reading, the episcopal consensus fracturing; for the
  other, irrelevant, since the concurrence that counts came before 1968.
- Later Canadian statements (a 1969 clarification; a 2008 pastoral letter reported as
  reaffirming HV) are **not verified** by the lesson.

## Truthfulness

Silence, reservation and the lie look alike from outside. The tradition allows the
first two for good reason and never the third; the modern dispute is over the
boundary of the third.

### The definition of the lie

**To lie is to put forward as your mind what is not your mind.** Augustine, *On
Lying* (c. 395; NPNF 1.3) 3-5, paraphrased: the fault is in the speaker's mind, not the
facts; one who says what he believes, even falsely, does not lie; one who has one
thing in mind and utters another has a double heart; a false statement put forward
with the will to deceive is beyond doubt a lie. CCC 2482 opens with this formula.

**Aquinas sharpens it** (II-II q.110):

| Falsehood | Meaning | Role (a.1 and ad 3) |
|---|---|---|
| material | the words are false | not required |
| formal | the speaker wills to say what is false (asserts against his mind) | **gives the species** |
| effective | he wills to deceive | the lie's "perfection", not its species |

- **Every lie is a sin** (a.3): "as words are naturally signs of intellectual acts,
  it is unnatural and undue for anyone to signify by words something that is not in
  his mind"; evil "in respect of its genus", so no end rescues it. The objection that
  a lie does less harm than a killing is refused (ad 4). The midwives of Exodus 1
  were rewarded "not for their lie" but for fear of God (ad 2). Augustine forbids all
  eight of his kinds of lie (*On Lying* 25, 42).
- **Not every lie is mortal** (a.4; CCC 2484): mortal when contrary to charity
  (corrupting divine truth, gravely injuring a neighbour or intending to, grave
  scandal); jocose and officious lies are venial in themselves.
- **Concealment is not lying.** Hiding the truth by silence is not uttering a lie
  (*Against Lying* 23); "it is lawful to hide the truth prudently, by keeping it back"
  (q.110 a.3 ad 4). Firmus of Thagaste told imperial officers he could neither lie
  nor betray the man he hid, and bore torture (*On Lying* 23). But refusing to say
  whether someone is in a place can betray him like a pointing finger (*On Lying*
  24): reserve must be prudent, not merely literal.
- **Truthfulness** is a part of justice: showing oneself as one is (II-II q.109; CCC
  2468-2469).
- **Level.** Lying intrinsically disordered: **at least rung 3** (CCC 1753, 2485);
  definitive status goes with [intrinsically evil acts](#intrinsically-evil-acts).
  Venial unless against charity: **rung 4** (CCC 2484).
- Kant and Constant: [`ethics` 2.3](../ethics/lessons/02-03-humanity-autonomy-and-the-lie.md).

*Introduced:* [6.4](lessons/06-04-truthfulness-and-the-lie.md)

### Mental reservation

**Broad reservation says something true that the hearer can read; strict reservation
hides the true sense in the speaker's head.** In a **broad** reservation the
qualifying sense is really available in the words or the setting: a confessor's "I do
not know" means "I have no knowledge I can communicate", readable by anyone who knows
what a confessor is; the butler's "He is not at home" where the phrase conventionally
means "not receiving". In a **strict** reservation the qualifier exists only in the
mind.

- **History.** Navarrus defended strict reservation and Sanchez formulated it;
  Laymann answered that it is simply a lie, since the words as spoken say what the
  speaker knows to be false. **Innocent XI, 2 March 1679**, condemned strict
  reservation as Sanchez put it (props 26-27, among the sixty-five).
- **A social fact decides it.** Move the butler's words to a culture without the
  convention and the qualifier becomes private: licitness depends on whether hearers
  can actually read the true sense. One side calls much broad reservation "a lie with
  better manners"; the other says the line between permitting self-deception and
  false signification is thinner than Augustine made it.
- **Level.** The 1679 condemnation: a **papal censure**, binding, not a definition,
  and of the *strict* kind only. Broad reservation with just cause: **rung 4**.

*Introduced:* [6.4](lessons/06-04-truthfulness-and-the-lie.md)

### The right to the truth

**The rival definition: a lie is a falsehood that wrongs someone with a right to the
truth.** Grotius, *De iure belli ac pacis* (1625) III.1, on ruses and falsehood in war:
a culpable falsehood violates a right of the person addressed. Constant's "right to
the truth" against Kant is the secular form.

**CCC 2483, the wording history.**

| Text | Wording of the definition of lying |
|---|---|
| 1994 English Catechism | to lead into error "someone who has the right to know the truth" |
| 1997 Latin *editio typica* | *ad inducendum in errorem*, "to lead into error": the right-to-know clause **dropped** |
| vatican.va English, at the time of writing | still prints the 1994 wording (though CCC 2267 there is updated); check the Latin or the second English edition |
| CCC 2482 | opens with Augustine's formula, with the intention of deceiving |
| CCC 2488-2489 | unchanged: the right to receive the truth is not unconditional; no one is bound to reveal the truth to someone without the right to know it |

**The modern dispute** (after the Live Action videos of early 2011, in which actors
posing as sex traffickers sought help from clinics):

- **Christopher Tollefsen** (with Alexander Pruss; *Lying and Christian Ethics*,
  2014), paraphrased: every assertion contrary to one's mind is a lie whoever hears
  it; it divides the inner self from the presented self and damages the trust
  assertion depends on; the 1997 revision confirms Augustine and Aquinas.
- **Janet Smith** (*First Things*, June 2011), paraphrased: the purpose of speech is
  read in a fallen world; one who seeks grave evil forfeits the right to truth as an
  aggressor forfeits immunity from force, so a false statement to him is not a lie in
  the morally relevant sense; the 1997 change clarified wording, and John Paul II said
  the revisions changed no doctrine.
- **Both** hold lying intrinsically evil; they divide over **what the object "lie"
  includes**.
- **Level: rung 5.** The 1997 text removed a clause; it added no condemnation of the
  right-to-truth view, and does not settle the dispute by itself.

*Introduced:* [6.4](lessons/06-04-truthfulness-and-the-lie.md)

## Pitfalls

### Level errors

- **Overstating.** "Vienne defined the infused moral virtues" (it chose the *more
  probable* opinion); "Trent defined the three conditions" or "which acts are mortal";
  "the Church defined seven gifts and twelve fruits"; the antecedent/concomitant
  scheme, the three fonts as a formula, or double effect "as dogma"; "*Veritatis
  Splendor* infallibly defined intrinsically evil acts"; "*Humanae Vitae* was defined
  *ex cathedra*"; "*Persona Humana* / RP 17 are infallible"; "the 2007 responses are
  definitive"; "IVF is infallibly condemned"; "the Church teaches immediate
  ensoulment"; "the theology of the body is the Church's official doctrine of
  sexuality"; "CCC 1778 makes Newman's theory Church teaching"; "the Church teaches
  that Ockham corrupted moral theology"; the 1679 or 1690 censures as defined dogma;
  a school's verdict on an ectopic procedure as Church teaching, either way. **Find
  the act.** *([1.1](lessons/01-01-what-moral-theology-is.md), [1.2](lessons/01-02-beatitude-the-last-end.md), [1.4](lessons/01-04-the-new-law.md), [2.1](lessons/02-01-the-voluntary.md) and others)*
- **Understating.** "Free will is a Thomist opinion" (Trent VI can. 5); "losing
  grace by mortal sin is a medieval opinion" (can. 27); "charity's infusion is
  theological opinion" (can. 11); "the end does not justify the means is just
  Thomism"; "the duty to follow conscience is a suggestion"; "the Magisterium's
  competence over natural law is one encyclical's opinion"; "direct abortion is only
  a manual category" or "a prudential judgment"; "an encyclical, so non-binding";
  "a CDF instruction is only guidance"; "premarital sex falls short of an ideal";
  "the gifts are medieval decoration"; "lying is a pious ideal"; "social sin is not
  a Catholic idea"; "it's all opinion, rigorist or laxist". Non-definitive is not
  optional. *([1.3](lessons/01-03-natural-law-in-light-of-revelation.md), [2.1](lessons/02-01-the-voluntary.md), [2.3](lessons/02-03-intention-and-circumstances.md), [2.4](lessons/02-04-conscience-its-nature-and-its-errors.md) and others)*
- **Promoting the application with the norm.** "This procedure is a direct killing"
  or "dialysis is disproportionate for Mrs K." does not inherit the norm's rung.
  *([4.5](lessons/04-05-describing-the-object.md), [5.1](lessons/05-01-the-inviolability-of-innocent-life.md), [5.3](lessons/05-03-euthanasia-and-the-end-of-life.md), [5.5](lessons/05-05-assisted-reproduction-and-the-embryo.md))*
- **Tying a norm to one argument.** A weak argument does not lower a norm; a strong
  one does not raise it (the contralife argument; Aquinas's upbringing argument).
  *([6.1](lessons/06-01-the-theology-of-the-body-and-chastity.md), [6.2](lessons/06-02-humanae-vitae-the-argument.md))*
- **Reading level off repetition, reception, penalties or a conference.** Repetition
  weighs within rung 3; non-practice lowers nothing; a changed penalty is not a
  changed teaching; the Winnipeg Statement moves nothing. *([5.2](lessons/05-02-abortion.md), [6.3](lessons/06-03-humanae-vitae-its-authority.md))*
- **Settling a free question.** The middle moral systems; the intellect/will essence
  of beatitude; the seat of *synderesis*; the specific difference of infused virtue;
  embryo adoption; GIFT; the reading of AL 303; the aggressor-and-lie case. Calling
  either side "heresy" or "dissent" overstates. *([1.2](lessons/01-02-beatitude-the-last-end.md), [2.4](lessons/02-04-conscience-its-nature-and-its-errors.md), [2.5](lessons/02-05-the-primacy-of-conscience-rightly-understood.md), [3.1](lessons/03-01-virtue-acquired-and-infused.md) and others)*

### The act and its fonts

- **"Same physical act, same moral act"** is physicalism; **"the object is whatever
  the agent says he intended"** is the intention-only error. VS 78 excludes both.
  *([2.2](lessons/02-02-the-moral-object.md), [4.5](lessons/04-05-describing-the-object.md))*
- **"The end is the form, so intention decides everything."** The end adds a
  species, never deletes one; theft for alms is theft *and* almsgiving. *([2.3](lessons/02-03-intention-and-circumstances.md))*
- **"Circumstances never change the species."** Some do (q.18 a.10); CCC 1754 denies
  only that they make an evil act good. *([2.3](lessons/02-03-intention-and-circumstances.md))*
- **Confusing the two senses of "intention"** (proximate end = object; further end =
  the font). *([2.3](lessons/02-03-intention-and-circumstances.md))*
- **Diminished responsibility is not a changed act.** Fear lowers imputability; it
  does not make the object good. *([2.3](lessons/02-03-intention-and-circumstances.md), [5.2](lessons/05-02-abortion.md))*
- **"Letting die is always fine, killing always wrong."** The line is what is chosen,
  not act vs omission. *([5.3](lessons/05-03-euthanasia-and-the-end-of-life.md))*
- **"Direct" means physically immediate.** It means willed as end or means. *([4.2](lessons/04-02-double-effect-and-cooperation-as-catholic-method.md), [5.2](lessons/05-02-abortion.md))*

### The voluntary and conscience

- **Fear makes an act involuntary.** It is "voluntary rather than involuntary"; grave
  fear diminishes. *([2.1](lessons/02-01-the-voluntary.md))*
- **"Consequent" ignorance means ignorance after the act.** It means ignorance
  following from the will. *([2.1](lessons/02-01-the-voluntary.md))*
- **Any ignorance excuses a little.** Concomitant excuses nothing; affected
  aggravates. *([2.1](lessons/02-01-the-voluntary.md))*
- **Reading CCC 1860 as abolishing invincible ignorance in morals.** It concerns
  *principles*. *([2.1](lessons/02-01-the-voluntary.md), [2.4](lessons/02-04-conscience-its-nature-and-its-errors.md))*
- **"An erring conscience binds, so the act is right."** Binding concerns the will;
  following a vincible error can still be sin, and invincible error leaves the act
  disordered. *([2.4](lessons/02-04-conscience-its-nature-and-its-errors.md))*
- **Sincerity excuses.** Necessary, not sufficient. *([2.4](lessons/02-04-conscience-its-nature-and-its-errors.md), [2.5](lessons/02-05-the-primacy-of-conscience-rightly-understood.md))*
- **Conscience is a feeling.** It is a judgment of reason. *([2.4](lessons/02-04-conscience-its-nature-and-its-errors.md))*
- **Primacy of conscience as primacy of authorship**; conscience as judge of
  doctrine. *([2.5](lessons/02-05-the-primacy-of-conscience-rightly-understood.md))*

### Traps in virtue and sin

- **Infused virtue as acquired virtue with a boost** (a difference of degree); a
  pagan's virtue as fake; "without us" as "without consent"; continence mistaken for
  infused virtue with lingering difficulty. *([3.1](lessons/03-01-virtue-acquired-and-infused.md))*
- **"Augustine called pagan virtues splendid vices."** Not in the text. *([3.2](lessons/03-02-faith-hope-and-charity.md))*
- **Firm hope as certainty of salvation**; presumption as merely "too much hope";
  despair as depression. *([3.2](lessons/03-02-faith-hope-and-charity.md))*
- **"Cardinal" as "highest"; prudence as caution; the gifts as the charisms of 1 Cor
  12.** *([3.3](lessons/03-03-the-cardinal-virtues-and-the-gifts.md))*
- **Grave matter = mortal sin**; the seven capital sins as the seven mortal sins; 1
  John 5:16-17 as the mortal/venial distinction verbatim. *([3.4](lessons/03-04-sin-mortal-and-venial.md))*
- **"Every silence is complicity."** The omission ways need duty and ability; scandal
  is not causing offence. *([3.5](lessons/03-05-social-sin-and-cooperation-in-the-sins-of-others.md))*
- **All three senses of social sin as analogical**; original sin as a model for
  collective guilt. *([3.5](lessons/03-05-social-sin-and-cooperation-in-the-sins-of-others.md))*

### Traps in method

- **"Probable" means over 50 percent**; probabilism used on a doubt of fact with a
  certain end at stake; Pascal as the verdict of history. *([4.1](lessons/04-01-casuistry-and-the-probabilism-controversy.md))*
- **"Material cooperation is always fine"**; "I disapprove, so it's material"; the
  2020 note as making vaccination obligatory or condemning objectors. *([4.2](lessons/04-02-double-effect-and-cooperation-as-catholic-method.md))*
- **Condition (iv) of double effect as proportionalism.** Proportion enters after
  the object is cleared. *([4.2](lessons/04-02-double-effect-and-cooperation-as-catholic-method.md))*
- **"Proportionalism is utilitarianism in a cassock"**; "premoral" as "morally
  neutral"; "the Church condemned the fundamental option". *([4.3](lessons/04-03-proportionalism-and-the-fundamental-option.md))*
- **"VS forbids weighing consequences"; "every moral rule is exceptionless"; "VS
  abolished the fundamental option".** *([4.4](lessons/04-04-veritatis-splendor.md))*
- **Rhonheimer as a proportionalist** by label rather than argument. *([4.5](lessons/04-05-describing-the-object.md))*

### Traps about life

- **"The Church teaches you may never kill"**; "innocent means sinless". *([5.1](lessons/05-01-the-inviolability-of-innocent-life.md))*
- **Naming the wrong route**: a confirmation of the ordinary universal magisterium is
  not an *ex cathedra* definition, even where the rung is high. *([5.1](lessons/05-01-the-inviolability-of-innocent-life.md), [5.2](lessons/05-02-abortion.md), [5.3](lessons/05-03-euthanasia-and-the-end-of-life.md))*
- **"Extraordinary" as "high-tech."** Burden against benefit, for this patient.
  *([5.3](lessons/05-03-euthanasia-and-the-end-of-life.md))*
- **Collapsing principle and application** on the death penalty; confusing EV 56 with
  EV 57; "Trent defined the death penalty" (the *Roman Catechism* is not a conciliar

- **"The problem with IVF is the lost embryos"** (the simple case fails on
  inseparability alone); "embryo adoption is condemned" (or endorsed). *([5.5](lessons/05-05-assisted-reproduction-and-the-embryo.md))*

## Conventions

- **One card per course**, covering every lesson. The linter checks that every lesson
  file is cited somewhere on this card.
- **Intuition first**, as in the lessons: a plain-English line before any text or
  Latin.
- **Headings are anchors.** Renaming a `###` breaks inbound lesson links. Headings are
  ASCII only (no parentheses, dashes or diacritics).
- **No prose dollar signs** (see CLAUDE.md).
- **Boss problems are reserved.** Level items set in Boss 1(c), 4(c) and 5(b) are
  marked "see lesson" in the level tables; no premise-by-premise reconstruction of
  ST I-II q.91 a.2, II-II q.64 a.2 or HV 11-14 appears here; the Ford-Grisez and
  Sullivan readings are one line each; no worked mortal/venial case set.
- **Modern authors and post-1930 documents are paraphrased**, cited by work, year and
  (only where the lessons verified it) section. Quotations are from public-domain
  translations only.

| Kind of text | How this course cites it | Example |
|---|---|---|
| *Summa* | part, question, article, reply; English Dominican Province | ST I-II q.19 a.6 ad 3; II-II q.64 a.7 |
| Council | session, chapter or canon, and which of the two it is (Waterworth for Trent) | Trent VI ch. 15 (chapter); VI can. 27 (canon) |
| Papal and curial documents | title, date, section; paraphrased | EV 57; DV II.B.5; *Samaritanus Bonus* V.7 |
| Catechism | paragraph, checked, paraphrased; the edition when wording changed | CCC 2483 (1994 vs 1997) |
| Fathers | work, section, public-domain translation (NPNF/ANF) | Augustine, *On Lying* 3-5 (NPNF 1.3) |
| Scripture | book, chapter, verse, Douay-Rheims | Rom 3:8 |
| Modern moralists | paraphrase, work and year | McCormick, *Ambiguity in Moral Choice* (1973) |
| Canon law | canon and paragraph, only non-penal canons checked by the lessons | can. 749 §3 |
