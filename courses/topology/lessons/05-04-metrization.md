# Topology · Lesson 5.4: Metrization

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 5: Separation, countability, and metrization · Builds on: [5.2](05-02-normal-spaces-urysohn.md), [5.3](05-03-countability-separability.md) · Unlocks: Module 6 — [6.1](06-01-homotopy-of-paths.md)

## Why this matters

Back in [1.3](01-03-topological-spaces-axioms.md) we threw the metric away and kept only open sets. It bought enormous generality — but it also raised a debt: was the metric ever really *gone*, or just hidden? For many spaces you meet, the topology you defined by open sets is secretly the metric topology of some distance you never wrote down. Knowing when that happens is worth real money: a metric hands you sequences, $\varepsilon$-balls, completeness, uniform continuity — the whole analytic toolkit — for free. This lesson closes the point-set arc by answering exactly *when* a topology comes from a metric, and the answer braids together every strand of Module 5.

## The idea

A topology is a rule for which sets are open. A metric is a rule for measuring distance, which *also* produces open sets (the ones you can fill with $\varepsilon$-balls). So the question "is this space really a metric space?" is not "does someone hand me a distance" — it's "**does there exist any distance whatsoever whose open sets are exactly the ones I already have?**"

That reframing matters. There is never *one* metric — the same topology on $\mathbb{R}$ comes from $|x-y|$, from $\min(1,|x-y|)$, from a dozen others (Lesson 1.2 called these equivalent metrics). Metrizability asks only whether the *family* is non-empty.

Here's the strategy for saying yes. A metric space is very well-behaved: points are closed, disjoint closed sets sit in disjoint open cushions, and every point has a shrinking sequence of neighborhoods. If your abstract space *fails* any of those, it can't be metric — instant disqualification. And the deep theorem (Urysohn) says: if your space has those good manners **and** isn't too big — a countable basis suffices — then you can actually manufacture a metric, by using continuous functions as coordinates and dropping the space into an infinite-dimensional cube that already has one.

## The formal version

**Metrizable.** A topological space $(X,\mathcal{T})$ is **metrizable** if there is a metric $d$ on $X$ whose metric topology equals $\mathcal{T}$ — i.e. a set is $\mathcal{T}$-open iff it is a union of $d$-balls.

> In words: the open sets you were handed are exactly the open sets some distance would produce.

Metrizability is a **topological property**: if $X$ is metrizable and $Y\cong X$ (homeomorphic), then $Y$ is metrizable too — push the metric across by the homeomorphism. So "is metric" survives any renaming of the space.

**Necessary conditions.** Every metric space is:

- **Hausdorff ($T_2$):** distinct points $x\ne y$ get disjoint balls of radius $\tfrac12 d(x,y)$.
- **Normal ($T_4$):** disjoint closed sets are separated by disjoint open sets (via the distance-to-a-set function — [5.2](05-02-normal-spaces-urysohn.md)).
- **First countable:** the balls $B(x,\tfrac1n)$, $n\in\mathbb{N}$, are a countable neighborhood basis at $x$.

> In words: a genuine metric space always separates points, separates closed sets, and has shrinking countable neighborhoods. Fail even one and you are **not** metrizable — a fast disqualifier.

**Urysohn metrization theorem.** *Every second-countable regular Hausdorff space is metrizable.*

> In words: if a space can separate points from closed sets (regular + $T_2$) **and** its whole topology is generated by a countable basis, then a compatible metric exists — you never need to exhibit it, existence is guaranteed.

**Proof idea (a genuine sketch).** Let $\{B_n\}_{n\in\mathbb N}$ be a countable basis. First, regular + second countable $\Rightarrow$ **normal**, so [5.2](05-02-normal-spaces-urysohn.md)'s Urysohn lemma is available. For each pair of basis elements with $\overline{B_m}\subseteq B_n$, Urysohn's lemma gives a continuous $f:X\to[0,1]$ equal to $0$ on $\overline{B_m}$ and $1$ off $B_n$. Countably many such pairs give a **countable family** $\{f_n:X\to[0,1]\}$ that *separates points from closed sets*: for any point $x$ and closed $C\not\ni x$, some $f_n$ is $0$ at $x$ and $1$ on $C$. Now use these functions as **coordinates**,
$$F(x) = \big(f_1(x),\,f_2(x),\,f_3(x),\dots\big)\in[0,1]^{\omega},$$
where $[0,1]^{\omega}$ is the countable product of copies of $[0,1]$, the **Hilbert cube**. The separation property makes $F$ a homeomorphism onto its image (an *embedding*). And $[0,1]^{\omega}$ is metrizable — e.g. by
$$d(\mathbf a,\mathbf b) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} 2^{-n}\,|a_n - b_n|,$$
which converges since each $|a_n-b_n|\le 1$ and $\sum 2^{-n}=1$. A subspace of a metric space is metric (restrict $d$), so $X\cong F(X)$ is metrizable. $\blacksquare$

**The Hilbert cube is universal.** The proof shows more than metrizability: *every* second-countable metric space embeds in the single space $[0,1]^{\omega}$. It is the one universal home for this whole class — one metric space to contain them all.

**The full characterization (name only).** Dropping second countability costs you: the **Nagata–Smirnov (and Bing) metrization theorem** says a space is metrizable iff it is regular Hausdorff and has a *$\sigma$-locally-finite* basis. Urysohn's theorem is the clean special case; we state the general one and move on.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (mechanical — disqualify by a failed necessary condition).** Take an infinite set $X$ with the **cofinite topology**: open sets are $\varnothing$ and the complements of finite sets ([1.3](01-03-topological-spaces-axioms.md)). Is it metrizable? Check Hausdorff. Two nonempty open sets are complements of finite sets, so each is *cofinite*; two cofinite sets always intersect (their union misses only finitely many points, but $X$ is infinite, so they can't be disjoint). Hence no two distinct points have disjoint neighborhoods — **not Hausdorff**. A metric space is always Hausdorff, so cofinite-on-an-infinite-set is **not metrizable**. (It is $T_1$ — points are closed — which shows $T_1$ alone is far too weak.)

**Example 2 (why you'd care — a "nice-looking" space that still fails).** Consider $\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{R}}$: functions $\mathbb{R}\to\{0,1\}$ with the product topology, i.e. an **uncountable** product of the two-point discrete space. Each factor is compact Hausdorff and lovely; by Tychonoff (Lesson 4.4) the whole product is compact Hausdorff, hence normal. So it passes *two* of the three necessary tests. But it is **not first countable**: no countable collection of basic (finitely-supported) neighborhoods can pin down a point across uncountably many coordinates. Since every metric space is first countable, $\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{R}}$ is **not metrizable** — a striking case where separation is perfect but the space is simply "too wide" in independent directions. This is the metrization face of the cardinality gap from `real-analysis` (Lesson 1.4): uncountably many coordinates defeat any countable neighborhood basis.

## Watch out

- You might think metrizability names a *metric*, but it names a *topology*. There are always many compatible metrics (bounded and unbounded versions, rescalings — Lesson 1.2) and no canonical one; "metrizable" asserts the family is non-empty, nothing more. Don't ask "what is *the* distance" — ask "does one exist."
- You might think Urysohn's theorem is the whole story, but it is **sufficient, not necessary**: second-countable + regular $\Rightarrow$ metrizable, yet metrizable spaces need not be second countable (an uncountable discrete space is metrizable by the discrete metric but has no countable basis). The honest iff is Nagata–Smirnov.
- You might think "Hausdorff + normal" guarantees a metric. It doesn't — you also need the countability muscle. The **Sorgenfrey plane** $\mathbb{R}_\ell^2$ (square of the lower-limit line, [5.3](05-03-countability-separability.md)) is Hausdorff and even separable, yet **not normal** (the anti-diagonal defeats it), so it fails the *normal* test and is non-metrizable. And the wide product above is normal but not first countable. Each non-example dies of a *different* missing axiom — that's the diagnostic skill.

## One-liner

> A topology is secretly a metric exactly when it separates well and stays countably generated — Urysohn proves it by using continuous functions as coordinates to slot the space into the Hilbert cube.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢)** The **indiscrete topology** on a set $X$ with $\ge 2$ points has only $\varnothing$ and $X$ open. Name a necessary condition it fails and conclude it is not metrizable. (Which separation axiom dies first?)

**P2 (🟡)** Suppose $X$ is second countable and regular Hausdorff, so by Urysohn it embeds in the Hilbert cube $[0,1]^{\omega}$. Use the given metric $d(\mathbf a,\mathbf b)=\sum_{n\ge1}2^{-n}|a_n-b_n|$ to show that convergence in $[0,1]^{\omega}$ is exactly **coordinatewise** convergence: $\mathbf x^{(k)}\to\mathbf x$ in $d$ iff $x^{(k)}_n\to x_n$ for every fixed $n$. (This is *why* the product topology, not the box topology, is the right target — Lesson 2.3.)

**P3 (🔴, optional)** Show that any metric space $(X,d)$ is normal, directly from the metric — no Urysohn lemma. (Hint: for disjoint closed $A,B$, use the distance function $\rho_A(x)=\inf_{a\in A}d(x,a)$ and the open sets where a point is strictly closer to one set than the other.) Then note in one line why this makes "normal" a *necessary* condition for metrizability, matching the lesson.

<details>
<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** It fails **$T_0$** (the weakest separation axiom) — and hence Hausdorff. With only $\varnothing$ and $X$ open, any two distinct points $x\ne y$ have the *same* neighborhoods (only $X$ itself), so no open set contains one but not the other; you cannot even topologically tell them apart, let alone separate them into disjoint opens. A metric space is Hausdorff (radius-$\tfrac12 d(x,y)$ balls are disjoint), so the indiscrete space on $\ge2$ points is not Hausdorff and therefore **not metrizable**. (Equivalently: any metric would give $d(x,y)>0$, forcing an open ball around $x$ missing $y$ — but no such open set exists here.)

**P2** ($\Rightarrow$) Fix a coordinate $n$. From the sum, every term is nonnegative, so $2^{-n}|x^{(k)}_n-x_n|\le d(\mathbf x^{(k)},\mathbf x)$. If $d(\mathbf x^{(k)},\mathbf x)\to0$ then $|x^{(k)}_n-x_n|\le 2^{n}\,d(\mathbf x^{(k)},\mathbf x)\to0$, so $x^{(k)}_n\to x_n$. Coordinatewise convergence is necessary.

($\Leftarrow$) Suppose $x^{(k)}_n\to x_n$ for every $n$. Let $\varepsilon>0$. Choose $N$ with $\sum_{n>N}2^{-n}<\varepsilon/2$ (the tail of a convergent geometric series); since each $|a_n-b_n|\le1$, the tail of the distance sum is at most $\varepsilon/2$ regardless of the points. For the finitely many coordinates $n\le N$, pick $K$ so that for all $k\ge K$ and each such $n$, $|x^{(k)}_n-x_n|<\varepsilon/2$ (finitely many limits, take the max index). Then for $k\ge K$,
$$d(\mathbf x^{(k)},\mathbf x)=\sum_{n\le N}2^{-n}|x^{(k)}_n-x_n|+\sum_{n>N}2^{-n}|x^{(k)}_n-x_n| < \frac{\varepsilon}{2}\sum_{n\le N}2^{-n} + \frac{\varepsilon}{2} \le \frac{\varepsilon}{2}+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}=\varepsilon.$$
So $\mathbf x^{(k)}\to\mathbf x$ in $d$. The two notions coincide. The point: the metric weights far coordinates by $2^{-n}$ so their *bounded* wobble can't spoil convergence — that damping is exactly the product topology's "eventually only finitely many coordinates matter," which the box topology lacks.

**P3** Let $A,B\subseteq X$ be disjoint, closed, nonempty. Define $\rho_A(x)=\inf_{a\in A}d(x,a)$ and $\rho_B(x)=\inf_{b\in B}d(x,b)$; both are continuous (indeed $1$-Lipschitz). Key fact: for closed $A$, $\rho_A(x)=0$ iff $x\in\overline A=A$. So on $B$ (disjoint from $A$) we have $\rho_A>0$, and on $A$ we have $\rho_B>0$. Set
$$U=\{x: \rho_A(x)<\rho_B(x)\},\qquad V=\{x:\rho_B(x)<\rho_A(x)\}.$$
Both are open (preimages of the open set $\{s<t\}$ under the continuous map $x\mapsto(\rho_A(x),\rho_B(x))$), and disjoint (the strict inequalities are mutually exclusive). If $x\in A$ then $\rho_A(x)=0<\rho_B(x)$, so $A\subseteq U$; symmetrically $B\subseteq V$. Thus disjoint closed sets sit in disjoint opens — $X$ is normal. Since metrizability *requires* the topology to be a metric topology and every metric topology is normal, **normal is a necessary condition** for a space to be metrizable — which is why the Sorgenfrey plane, being non-normal, cannot be metrized.

</details>

## Flashback

**From Lesson 5.2 (Normal spaces and Urysohn's lemma):** Prove that a **compact Hausdorff space** $X$ is normal. (You may use: in a Hausdorff space, a point and a disjoint compact set can be separated by disjoint open sets — first separate a point from a compact set, then upgrade to two compact sets.)

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Step 1 — point vs. compact set.** Let $K\subseteq X$ be compact and $x\notin K$. For each $k\in K$, Hausdorff gives disjoint opens $U_k\ni x$ and $V_k\ni k$. The $\{V_k\}_{k\in K}$ cover $K$; by compactness finitely many suffice, $K\subseteq V_{k_1}\cup\dots\cup V_{k_m}=:V$. Set $U=U_{k_1}\cap\dots\cap U_{k_m}$, a finite intersection of opens, so open, and $x\in U$. Then $U$ and $V$ are disjoint: any point of $V$ lies in some $V_{k_i}$, which is disjoint from $U_{k_i}\supseteq U$. So $x$ and $K$ are separated by disjoint opens.

**Step 2 — compact vs. compact.** Now let $A,B\subseteq X$ be disjoint closed sets. In a compact space, closed subsets are compact, so $A,B$ are compact. For each $a\in A$, Step 1 (with $K=B$) gives disjoint opens $U_a\ni a$ and $W_a\supseteq B$. The $\{U_a\}_{a\in A}$ cover the compact set $A$; take a finite subcover $A\subseteq U_{a_1}\cup\dots\cup U_{a_n}=:U$, and set $W=W_{a_1}\cap\dots\cap W_{a_n}$, open with $B\subseteq W$. They are disjoint: a point of $U$ lies in some $U_{a_j}$, disjoint from $W_{a_j}\supseteq W$. So the disjoint closed sets $A,B$ sit in disjoint opens — $X$ is normal. $\blacksquare$

(This is why compact Hausdorff spaces are such comfortable places to work: Step 1's "compactness turns pointwise Hausdorff separation into set-wise separation" is the same finite-subcover move that powers the whole module.)

</details>

## Connections

- **Backward:** this is the payoff of the module. Normality and Urysohn's lemma ([5.2](05-02-normal-spaces-urysohn.md)) *manufacture* the coordinate functions; second countability ([5.3](05-03-countability-separability.md)) *bounds* how many you need; the necessary conditions are just the separation ladder (Lesson 5.1) plus first countability read backwards. And the metric we recover is one of the many equivalent metrics from Lesson 1.2 — the topology never changed.
- **Forward:** metrizability is the safety net under Module 6. When we build homotopies and loops in [6.1](06-01-homotopy-of-paths.md) the spaces of interest ($S^1$, $D^2$, the torus) are all metric — sequences, uniform continuity, and $\varepsilon$-arguments are legal there precisely because of this lesson.
- **Sideways (`real-analysis`):** the failure of $\{0,1\}^{\mathbb R}$ to be first countable is the cardinality gap of `real-analysis` (Lesson 1.4) wearing a topological uniform — uncountably many independent coordinates defeat any countable neighborhood basis, exactly as an uncountable set defeats any list. And functional analysis will lean on the Hilbert-cube embedding when it metrizes weak-* topologies on bounded sets of a separable dual.
