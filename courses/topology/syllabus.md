# Topology — Syllabus

> Tier 1 · 27 lessons · Prereqs: [proofs-primer](../proofs-primer/syllabus.md), [real-analysis](../real-analysis/syllabus.md) · Roadmap id: `topology`

## Goal

Learn to think about "shape" and "nearness" without distance. Topology strips geometry down to the single idea of an open set and rebuilds continuity, limits, connectedness, and compactness on that foundation — so the same theorems cover the real line, function spaces, and the surface of a doughnut at once. This course does the point-set core in full (spaces, continuity, the constructions that build new spaces, connectedness, compactness, and the separation/countability hierarchy up to metrization), then spends its last module on a genuine taste of algebraic topology: homotopy, the fundamental group, the computation $\pi_1(S^1)=\mathbb{Z}$, and the fixed-point theorems it proves.

Deliberately scoped: this is a first course. We build on `real-analysis` (ε–δ, open/closed sets, compactness on $\mathbb{R}$, sequences) and generalize it rather than re-deriving it. Point-set topology gets the rigor; algebraic topology gets an honest but bounded introduction — the fundamental group and covering-space idea for $S^1$, but not higher homotopy or homology, and not the general classification of surfaces or manifold theory (those live downstream, and differential geometry is folded into `relativity`). Tychonoff, Urysohn, and Tietze are proved or carefully sketched; the deepest metrization results are stated and used.

## Dangerous Checklist

When you finish, you can:

- [ ] Check the axioms for a metric or a topology, and generate a topology from a basis
- [ ] Compute the interior, closure, boundary, and limit points of a set in any space
- [ ] Prove a function continuous from the open-set definition, and prove two spaces homeomorphic (or not)
- [ ] Build the subspace, product, and quotient topologies, and recognize a torus, Möbius band, or circle as a quotient
- [ ] Prove a space connected or path-connected, and use it (generalized IVT, counting components)
- [ ] Prove compactness from open covers, and translate between the covering, sequential, and closed-and-bounded pictures
- [ ] State and apply the Heine–Borel and Tychonoff theorems, and know exactly where each fails
- [ ] Place a space in the separation hierarchy ($T_0$ through normal), and say why Hausdorff makes limits unique
- [ ] State Urysohn's lemma, the Tietze extension theorem, and the Urysohn metrization theorem, and explain what each buys
- [ ] Define homotopy and the fundamental group, and explain why $\pi_1(S^1)=\mathbb{Z}$
- [ ] Use $\pi_1$ to prove the Brouwer fixed-point theorem (2-D), the no-retraction theorem, and the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra topologically

## Modules

### Module 1: Spaces

From "distance" to "open set" — the abstraction that makes topology, and the vocabulary for everything after.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1.1 | Metric spaces | Axiomatize distance and see how many different "distances" fit | metric axioms, Euclidean/taxicab/discrete/sup metrics, open ball |
| 1.2 | Open sets and the metric topology | See that continuity and limits depend only on which sets are open | open set in a metric space, the metric topology, equivalent metrics |
| 1.3 | Topological spaces: the axioms | Drop the metric — take open sets as primitive and keep only three axioms | topology, open sets axioms, discrete/indiscrete/cofinite/standard topologies |
| 1.4 | Bases, subbases, and comparing topologies | Generate a topology from a small family, and compare two topologies | basis, basis criterion, subbasis, finer/coarser topologies |
| 1.5 | Interior, closure, boundary, and limit points | Compute the topological "anatomy" of a set in any space | interior, closure, boundary, limit point, dense set, $G_\delta$ (brief) |

**Boss problem 1:** On $\mathbb{R}$, compare the standard topology, the lower-limit ("Sorgenfrey") topology generated by half-open intervals $[a,b)$, and the cofinite topology: for each, decide whether $[0,1)$ is open, closed, both, or neither, and compute its closure and boundary. Then prove one topology is strictly finer than another by exhibiting a set open in one but not the other.

### Module 2: Continuity and new spaces from old

The morphisms of topology, and the three machines (subspace, product, quotient) that build almost every space you'll meet.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2.1 | Continuous functions and homeomorphisms | Define continuity by "preimage of open is open" and recognize when two spaces are the same | continuity (open-set def), recovering ε–δ, homeomorphism, topological equivalence |
| 2.2 | The subspace topology | Give a subset its inherited topology and handle the "open in the subspace" trap | subspace topology, relative openness, embeddings |
| 2.3 | The product topology | Topologize a product and see why the product beats the box for infinite products | product topology, projections, box vs. product topology, universal property |
| 2.4 | The quotient topology and gluing | Glue a space by an equivalence relation and build the circle, torus, and Möbius band | quotient topology, identification, cylinder/torus/Möbius/projective plane |
| 2.5 | Topological properties and telling spaces apart | Assemble invariants to prove two spaces are not homeomorphic | topological property/invariant, the homeomorphism problem, cut-point/cardinality arguments |

**Boss problem 2:** Prove that the interval $[0,1]$ and the circle $S^1$ are not homeomorphic (removing a point disconnects one but not the other), and that $\mathbb{R}$ and $\mathbb{R}^2$ are not homeomorphic by the same style of argument. Then realize the torus as a quotient of the square $[0,1]^2$, writing down the identifications explicitly and checking the result is what you expect near a corner.

### Module 3: Connectedness

The precise meaning of "one piece," and the theorem it turns into.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3.1 | Connectedness | Define connected via the absence of a separation, and prove intervals are connected | connected space, separation, connectedness of intervals, continuous image |
| 3.2 | Path-connectedness and components | Relate paths to connectedness and decompose a space into pieces | path-connectedness, path components, connected components, topologist's sine curve |
| 3.3 | Connectedness at work | Deploy connectedness: the generalized IVT and invariance arguments | generalized Intermediate Value Theorem, connectedness as an invariant, applications |

**Boss problem 3:** Prove the general Intermediate Value Theorem (a continuous map from a connected space into $\mathbb{R}$ hits every value between two it attains) and use it to show any continuous $f:S^1\to\mathbb{R}$ takes some pair of antipodal points to the same value (1-D Borsuk–Ulam). Then show the "topologist's sine curve" is connected but not path-connected, pinpointing where a path would have to fail.

### Module 4: Compactness

The single most important topological property — "finiteness for infinite spaces" — in full generality.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4.1 | Compactness: open covers | Define compactness by finite subcovers and prove the basic closure properties | open cover, finite subcover, compactness, closed subset of compact, compact subset of Hausdorff |
| 4.2 | Compactness in metric spaces | Reconcile the covering picture with sequences and completeness | sequential compactness, total boundedness, completeness, Lebesgue number lemma |
| 4.3 | Heine–Borel and maps on compact spaces | Prove the payoff theorems: continuous images, extreme values, uniform continuity | Heine–Borel, continuous image of compact, Extreme Value Theorem, uniform continuity |
| 4.4 | Tychonoff's theorem | State and use "any product of compact spaces is compact" | Tychonoff's theorem, finite-product case, the role of the axiom of choice |
| 4.5 | Local compactness and compactification | Handle spaces that are compact only nearby, and add a point at infinity | local compactness, one-point (Alexandroff) compactification, $S^n$ as a compactification of $\mathbb{R}^n$ |

**Boss problem 4:** Prove that a continuous bijection from a compact space to a Hausdorff space is a homeomorphism, and use it to conclude that the quotient map realizing the circle as $[0,1]/(0\sim1)$ is a homeomorphism onto $S^1$. Then show $[0,1]$ is compact directly from the open-cover definition (via the least-upper-bound property from `real-analysis`), naming exactly where completeness enters.

### Module 5: Separation, countability, and metrization

How much can a topology "tell points apart," how big it needs to be, and when it secretly comes from a metric.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5.1 | The separation axioms: Hausdorff and friends | Climb the $T_0$–$T_1$–$T_2$ ladder and see why Hausdorff makes limits unique | $T_0$, $T_1$, Hausdorff ($T_2$), uniqueness of limits, closed points |
| 5.2 | Normal spaces, Urysohn's lemma, and Tietze | Separate closed sets by functions and extend continuous functions | regular/normal spaces, Urysohn's lemma, Tietze extension theorem |
| 5.3 | Countability and separability | Bound a topology's size and see where sequences still suffice | first/second countable, separable, Lindelöf, sequences vs. nets (brief) |
| 5.4 | Metrization | Decide when a topological space is really a metric space | metrizable space, Urysohn metrization theorem, why some spaces can't be metrized |

**Boss problem 5:** Prove that a compact Hausdorff space is normal, then state Urysohn's lemma and explain in words how it "manufactures" a continuous function from a purely set-theoretic separation. Separately, show that the lower-limit (Sorgenfrey) line is separable and first countable but not second countable, and use that to argue its square is not normal (or explain the obstruction) — a canonical failure of good behavior.

### Module 6: A first taste of algebraic topology

Attach an algebraic fingerprint — a group — to a space, and let algebra prove theorems geometry can't reach.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 6.1 | Homotopy and path homotopy | Make "continuous deformation" precise for maps and for paths | homotopy, path homotopy, homotopy equivalence, contractible spaces |
| 6.2 | The fundamental group | Turn loops at a basepoint into a group | loop concatenation, $\pi_1(X,x_0)$, group axioms, basepoint dependence |
| 6.3 | The fundamental group of the circle | Compute the first nontrivial example: $\pi_1(S^1)=\mathbb{Z}$ | covering map $\mathbb{R}\to S^1$, path/homotopy lifting, the winding-number isomorphism |
| 6.4 | Induced homomorphisms and invariance | Make $\pi_1$ a functor and prove it's a homotopy invariant | induced homomorphism, functoriality, homotopy invariance, simply connected spaces |
| 6.5 | Fixed points and applications | Cash out $\pi_1$: Brouwer, no-retraction, and the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra | no-retraction theorem, Brouwer fixed-point theorem (2-D), topological proof of the FTA |

**Boss problem 6:** Assuming $\pi_1(S^1)=\mathbb{Z}$, prove the no-retraction theorem (there is no continuous retraction of the disk $D^2$ onto its boundary $S^1$) and deduce the 2-D Brouwer fixed-point theorem (every continuous $f:D^2\to D^2$ has a fixed point). Then sketch how the same circle-of-ideas gives a topological proof that every non-constant complex polynomial has a root, naming the bridge to `complex-analysis`'s two other proofs (Liouville and the argument principle).
