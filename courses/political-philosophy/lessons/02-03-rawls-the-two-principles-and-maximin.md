# Political Philosophy · Lesson 2.3: Rawls II: the two principles and maximin

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 2: Justice and distribution · Builds on: [2.2 Rawls I: the original position](02-02-rawls-the-original-position.md), [2.1 Utilitarianism as a principle for institutions](02-01-utilitarianism-as-a-principle-for-institutions.md), [`decision-theory` 3.4](../../decision-theory/lessons/03-04-choosing-behind-the-veil-rawls-vs-harsanyi.md) · Unlocks: [2.4 Nozick](02-04-nozick-entitlement-and-the-challenge-to-patterns.md), [2.6 Luck, relations, priority and sufficiency](02-06-luck-relations-priority-and-sufficiency.md)

## Why this matters

[2.2](02-02-rawls-the-original-position.md) built the machine: free and equal parties choosing rules for the [basic structure](../reference.md#basic-structure) behind a veil. This lesson is what comes out of it, and why. The output is the most argued-over sentence pair in modern political philosophy: equal basic liberties first, then inequalities allowed only if they work for the worst off. Every later lesson in this module (Nozick, equality of what, luck and priority) is an attack on one of these two principles or on the route to them.

## The idea

Start from equal shares. Behind the veil no one can claim more than anyone else, so an equal split of [primary goods](../reference.md#primary-goods) is the natural benchmark. Now suppose some inequality, say higher pay for scarce skills, makes the pie larger, and everyone, including the person at the bottom, ends up with more than the equal share. Refusing it would be refusing a gain to everybody out of pure spite. So rational parties accept *some* inequality. Which ones? Only those that leave the worst-off position better than it would be under any alternative. That is the difference principle in one breath.

Rawls also gives a route that doesn't use the veil at all. *A Theory of Justice* (1971) §12 lays out four readings of its own first, vague formulation (positions open to all, inequalities to everyone's advantage):

| | Efficiency alone | Difference principle |
|---|---|---|
| **Careers open to talents** | Natural liberty | Natural aristocracy |
| **Fair equality of opportunity** | Liberal equality | Democratic equality |

*Natural liberty* (formal equality plus markets) lets shares track your family's wealth, which Rawls calls arbitrary from a moral point of view. *Liberal equality* fixes that with fair equality of opportunity, but still lets shares track native talent, which is just as unearned. Only *democratic equality* stops rewarding either lottery for its own sake: talent may earn more, but only when that works for everyone, so the distribution of talent is treated, in effect, as a common asset. This is the root of the luck-egalitarian line that [`philosophy-of-economics` 5.4](../../philosophy-of-economics/lessons/05-04-markets-and-desert.md) cited back to this course.

## The argument

**The two principles** (final statement in *Theory* §46, paraphrased):

1. **Equal basic liberties.** Each person has an equal right to the most extensive scheme of basic liberties compatible with a like scheme for all. The list is fixed: political liberty, speech and assembly, conscience and thought, freedom of the person with the right to hold personal property, freedom from arbitrary arrest.
2. Social and economic inequalities must be (a) to the greatest benefit of the least advantaged (the [difference principle](../reference.md#difference-principle)) and (b) attached to positions open to all under [fair equality of opportunity](../reference.md#fair-equality-of-opportunity): equal prospects for equal talent and willingness, whatever one's class of origin.

*In words:* liberties are equal and not for sale; jobs are fairly open; and the gaps that remain must pay their way at the bottom.

**Priority rules.** The ordering is *lexical*: principle 1 before 2, and 2(b) before 2(a). A basic liberty can be restricted only for the sake of liberty itself, never to raise income; fair opportunity cannot be traded for gains to the worst off. The [priority of liberty](../reference.md#priority-of-liberty) has a condition: it holds once a society is rich enough for the basic liberties to be effectively exercised. Below that threshold Rawls falls back on a "general conception" in which all primary goods are distributed to everyone's advantage. (The later *Political Liberalism*, 1993, and *Justice as Fairness: A Restatement*, 2001, say "fully adequate scheme" in place of "most extensive", and list fair opportunity first.)

**Two refinements in §13.** *Chain connection:* if raising the top's prospects raises the bottom's, it raises everyone's in between. *Close-knitness:* no position can move without all the others moving. Under both, the difference principle and "everyone gains" coincide.

**From the veil to the difference principle,** reconstructed:

- **P1.** The parties choose principles for the basic structure, knowing general facts but not their own position, and care about their share of primary goods ([2.2](02-02-rawls-the-original-position.md)).
- **P2.** Equal division is the benchmark: no party can claim more.
- **P3.** An inequality that leaves every position above the benchmark is one rational, mutually disinterested parties accept.
- **P4.** Among such arrangements, they rank by the worst position: either because maximin is rational here, or because they could not honour any other choice.
- **∴ C.** They choose the difference principle (with equal liberty and fair opportunity already secured).

**Maximin, reloaded.** [`decision-theory` 3.4](../../decision-theory/lessons/03-04-choosing-behind-the-veil-rawls-vs-harsanyi.md) owns this dispute. Rawls's [three conditions](../reference.md#rawls-conditions-for-maximin) (§26: no basis for probabilities, little care for gains above a guaranteed floor, unacceptable worst outcomes elsewhere) make maximin rational under the veil. Harsanyi's [equiprobability model](../reference.md#equiprobability-model) gives each person equal chances and so picks the highest average; Rawls gives up vNM continuity, Harsanyi gives up the claim that ignorance differs from risk. One more point: maximin applies to primary goods, not utility.

**What decision theory leaves out.** §29 argues for the two principles over average utility on grounds that are not about gambling at all:

- **Strains of commitment.** Parties may only agree to what they could keep. Utilitarianism can ask the losers to accept less for others' larger gains, a promise someone at the bottom may be unable to honour; the two principles never ask that.
- **Publicity.** The principles must work when everyone knows them. A public rule saying "your prospects were lowered to raise the total" corrodes; one saying "inequalities exist only because they help you" does not.
- **Stability and self-respect.** Equal liberty publicly affirms each citizen's equal standing, and Rawls counts the social bases of self-respect among the most important primary goods.

In the *Restatement* Rawls says maximin carries the comparison with average utility; against a "mixed" view (average utility plus a guaranteed minimum) he leans on reciprocity, publicity and stability instead.

**Where the argument is weakest.** P4. The arbitrariness argument shows that talent shouldn't be rewarded *for its own sake*; it does not show that the floor is all that matters. A prioritarian weights the worst off more but not infinitely ([2.6](02-06-luck-relations-priority-and-sufficiency.md)); Harsanyi says the averaging chooser is the impartial one. The critic says maximin turns a reasonable concern for the bottom into a lexical absolute: a tiny gain at the bottom outweighs any loss to everyone else. The Rawlsian answers that the conditions of the veil, and the strains of commitment, are exactly what license an absolute.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean): an incentive scheme.** Invented numbers. The Republic of Corvel has four positions by population share: unskilled 40%, semi-skilled 30%, skilled 20%, professional 10%. Entries are a primary-goods index. Under flat pay E everyone gets 20. Under incentive pay I, higher pay draws people into training and long hours, and output rises enough that the index becomes (22, 26, 34, 70).

- Worst-off position: 20 under E, 22 under I. The difference principle **endorses I**.
- Average: $0.4(22)+0.3(26)+0.2(34)+0.1(70)=30.4$, against 20.
- Every position is higher under I, so chain connection holds here.

The point Rawls wants: the principle is not equality. It *requires* the 70, because refusing it would cost the unskilled 2 points.

**Example 2 (hard): Cohen's incentives critique.** G. A. Cohen (*If You're an Egalitarian, How Come You're So Rich?*, 2000; *Rescuing Justice and Equality*, 2008) asks *why* the professionals need 70. Not because the work is harder (Cohen allows pay for burdensome work), but because they will not work as hard for less. Suppose they would do the same work at 40. The freed resources are $0.1\times(70-40)=3$ per head. Lifting the two bottom positions to 28 costs

$$0.4\times(28-22)+0.3\times(28-26)=2.4+0.6=3.$$

So scheme C = (28, 28, 34, 40): same average, 30.4, but a floor of 28 rather than 22. The inequality in I is "necessary" only given the professionals' own choice to withhold effort. Cohen's test: the argument "pay us more or the poor lose" sounds fine from a third party, but said by the professionals to the poor themselves it is closer to a demand than a justification. People who really accepted the difference principle, he says, would carry it into their choices as an egalitarian ethos.

Here the principle strains, because which scheme it selects depends on what counts as "feasible". Two Rawlsian replies, stated at full strength. **The basic-structure reply:** the principles govern institutions, not personal choices within them; justice leaves people free to pursue their ends inside fair rules. **The liberty reply:** an ethos requiring the talented to work at their most socially productive job for modest pay threatens free choice of occupation, itself a basic liberty with priority. Cohen answers that the basic structure must include informal social norms, since Rawls already counts the family in it.

## Watch out

- **You might think the difference principle maximizes equality.** It maximizes the floor, and can require large gaps (Example 1). A flat scheme that lowers the floor is ruled out.
- **You might think a just basic structure settles authority or obligation.** The two principles say which institutions are *just*. Whether such a state is [legitimate](../reference.md#power-legitimacy-authority-obligation), and whether citizens are obliged to obey it, are further questions; Rawls answers the last with the natural duty of justice ([1.4](01-04-natural-duty-and-philosophical-anarchism.md)).
- **You might think the priority of liberty forbids any rule about speech or assembly.** It forbids trading liberty for *income*. Regulating liberties so that the whole scheme works (time slots for rallies, procedures for debate) is restricting liberty for liberty's sake, which it permits.

## One-liner

> Equal liberties first, fair opportunity second, and only then inequalities that pay their way at the bottom; maximin is one argument for that ordering, the strains of commitment and publicity are others, and Cohen asks whether "pay their way" lets the talented set the price.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢)** *(Formal (a)–(b) · Exegetical (c).)* Invented numbers. A society has four positions with population shares 10%, 30%, 40% and 20%, listed worst to best. Two basic structures give the primary-goods indices P = (12, 20, 30, 50) and Q = (14, 18, 26, 40).

(a) Which structure does the difference principle select, and which has the higher average? Give the cost of the difference principle's choice in average index per head.
(b) Let P's lowest entry be $x$ instead of 12, all else fixed. For which $x$ does the difference principle, with Rawls's leximin tie-break, select P?
(c) If the worst-off position were only 1% of the population, would the difference principle's verdict change? One sentence on why.

**P2 (🟡)** *(Exegetical (a)–(c).)* Invented cases. For each policy, name the principle or priority rule that decides it under Rawls's two principles, and give the verdict. Two sentences each.

(a) The Republic of Varden, a prosperous country, proposes suspending contested elections for ten years under an appointed development council, projected to raise the least advantaged's income index by 40%.
(b) Varden's state universities propose reserving a fifth of places for children of alumni, who pay a levy that funds larger cash transfers to the least advantaged.
(c) Varden's capital limits rallies in its main square to booked two-hour slots, allocated by lottery among all groups that apply.

**P3 (🔴, optional)** *(Evaluative.)* Steelman and reply. An invented association of Varden surgeons tells the finance ministry: "Cut the top tax rate or many of us will cut our hours, and the poorest will wait longer for operations." Give Cohen's strongest objection to treating this as a difference-principle justification for the cut, then the strongest Rawlsian reply. 150 words or fewer. Any verdict passes.

<details>
<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Formal (a)–(b) · Exegetical (c))*

(a) Minima: P 12, Q 14, so the difference principle selects **Q**. Averages:

$$\bar P = 0.1(12)+0.3(20)+0.4(30)+0.2(50)=1.2+6+12+10=29.2,$$

$$\bar Q = 0.1(14)+0.3(18)+0.4(26)+0.2(40)=1.4+5.4+10.4+8=25.2.$$

P has the higher average. Cost of the difference principle's choice: $29.2-25.2=4.0$ per head.

(b) For $x<14$ the worst entry of P is below Q's 14, so Q. For $x>14$ P's worst entry ($\min(x,20)$) beats 14, so P. At $x=14$ the worst positions tie and leximin compares the next-worst: P's 20 against Q's 18, so P. Answer: **P for every $x\ge 14$.**

(c) No: the difference principle looks only at the level of the worst-off position, not at how many occupy it, whereas the average does depend on the shares (with a 1% bottom position the averages become 29.92 and 25.56).

**Must hit, strict (a)–(b):** Q by the floor, P by the average, cost 4.0 with population weights; threshold $x\ge14$ including the tie broken by the second position (20 vs 18).

**Must hit, strict (c):** insensitivity to the size of the worst-off group.

**Wrong turns:** taking plain (unweighted) averages, 28 and 24.5, which gives the right ranking by the wrong rule; answering $x>14$ and forgetting leximin's tie-break.

---

**P2** *(Exegetical (a)–(c))*

**Must hit, strict (a):** the priority of liberty (principle 1 lexically before 2): political liberty may not be traded for income gains to anyone, the worst off included. Forbidden; and since Varden is prosperous, the condition for the priority holds, so the general conception does not apply.

**Must hit, strict (b):** fair equality of opportunity is lexically prior to the difference principle: legacy places give equal talent unequal prospects by family origin, and transfers to the least advantaged cannot buy that back. Forbidden.

**Must hit, strict (c):** restricting a liberty for the sake of the liberty scheme itself (every group gets a fair chance to be heard) is permitted under principle 1; no trade with income is involved. Permitted.

**Wrong turns:** in (a), weighing the 40% gain against the loss, which is exactly what lexical priority excludes; in (b), approving because the levy helps the worst off (that is the difference principle answering a question fair opportunity has already settled); in (c), treating any regulation of assembly as a violation.

**Model answer:** (a) Priority of liberty: basic liberties cannot be exchanged for economic gains, and in a prosperous society the priority applies in full, so the proposal is ruled out. (b) Fair equality of opportunity, prior to the difference principle: places allocated by parentage violate it, and the transfers cannot compensate. (c) Principle 1 itself: the slots regulate assembly so all can use it, restricting liberty only for liberty, so it is permitted.

---

**P3** *(Evaluative)*

**Must hit, any verdict:**

- State Cohen's objection precisely: the "necessity" of the cut depends on the surgeons' own choice to withhold effort, so the argument, said by them to the poor, is a threat dressed as a justification (the interpersonal test); people who accept the difference principle should act on it.
- State a Rawlsian reply at full strength: the principles apply to the basic structure, not to individual choices within it; or an ethos requiring work at a set level for set pay threatens free choice of occupation, a basic liberty.
- Name the premise in dispute: whether justice applies to personal choices (Cohen) or only to institutions (Rawls), and say what the reply costs if it succeeds.

**Wrong turns:** reading Cohen as rejecting all pay differences (he accepts compensation for burdensome work); answering with Nozick's entitlement theory, which rejects the difference principle rather than defending this use of it.

**Model answer, one of several:** Cohen: the surgeons do not say operations become harder; they say they will choose to do fewer unless paid more. The inequality is "needed" only because of their own decision, so offered by them to the poorest patients it is a demand, not a justification. A society that really accepted the difference principle would expect people to act on it. Rawlsian reply: justice is a property of institutions. Fair rules leave citizens free to pursue their own ends, including fewer hours; a duty to work as much as the worst off need would conscript the talented and override free choice of occupation, which the first principle protects. The crux is whether the site of justice includes personal choices. If it does not, the cut may be justified; if it does, the surgeons fail Cohen's test.

</details>

## Flashback

**From Lesson [2.1](02-01-utilitarianism-as-a-principle-for-institutions.md) (Utilitarianism as a principle for institutions):** *(Formal (a)-(b) · Exegetical (c).)* Invented numbers. Household R earns 100 and household P earns 20 (thousand dollars), and both have $u=\ln c$. (a) A fifth of every dollar transferred leaks away ($\lambda=0.2$). Find the utilitarian transfer $t^*$, both final incomes, and $c_P/c_R$. Then find the smallest leak at which the utilitarian transfers nothing. (b) Lerner's argument, with new numbers: 60 is to be split between two people, one with $u=3\ln c$ and the other with $u=\ln c$. Find the utilitarian split if the state knows who is who. Then find it if the state does not know, each assignment being equally likely. (c) One sentence: which premise of 2.1's argument does (b) turn on, and what does Sen's disabled-person case add?

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

(a) Using $t^*=\dfrac{(1-\lambda)y_R-y_P}{2(1-\lambda)}$:

$$t^*=\frac{0.8\times100-20}{2\times0.8}=\frac{60}{1.6}=37.5.$$

R keeps $100-37.5=62.5$. P receives $0.8\times37.5=30$ and ends with $50$. The ratio is $50/62.5=0.8=1-\lambda$. Check the first-order condition: $1/62.5=0.016$ and $0.8/50=0.016$. Nothing is transferred once $\lambda\ge1-20/100=0.8$, a leak of 80 percent.

(b) *Known:* maximize $3\ln x+\ln(60-x)$. Then $3/x=1/(60-x)$, so $x=45$: 45 to the $3\ln c$ person and 15 to the other. *Unknown:* the expected sum is $\tfrac12[3\ln x+\ln(60-x)]+\tfrac12[\ln x+3\ln(60-x)]=2[\ln x+\ln(60-x)]$, which is maximized at $x=30$: 30 each.

**Must hit, strict (a)-(b):**

- $t^*=37.5$, final incomes $62.5$ and $50$, ratio $0.8$, and no transfer at a leak of 80 percent or more.
- A split of 45/15 when the state knows who is who, and 30/30 when it does not.

**Must hit, strict (c):**

- P3, which treats everyone's utility functions as the same, here because the state does not know them. The equal split comes from the state's ignorance, not from concern for equality, and it disappears once the state knows who converts income into well-being better.
- Sen's case adds that knowing can cut against the badly off: if a disabled person gains less well-being from each dollar, the sum gives him *less*.

**Wrong turns:** giving P $20+37.5$ and forgetting the leak. Giving the larger share to the $\ln c$ person in the known case. Reading the 30/30 split as the utilitarian valuing equality.

</details>
