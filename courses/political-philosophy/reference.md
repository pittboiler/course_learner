# Political Philosophy · Reference Card

> Open book. This card is meant to be open while you work — including during
> quizzes. Nothing here is something you're expected to recall cold.

The course asks what, if anything, justifies the state: why anyone owes it obedience, who should
get what, what it may forbid, on what terms people who disagree about the good may coerce one
another, why the majority should decide, and what is owed across borders and to conscience. It
takes no side. Every position below is stated as its defenders state it, with the premise its
critics attack, and no entry records a verdict; religious and secular positions get the same
treatment. Use the card mid-problem to find an argument's skeleton and weakest premise, a
formula with its symbols defined, who said what and when, or the slide between two concepts. Two
habits carry the course: ask which of the four things an argument actually establishes
([power, legitimacy, authority, obligation](#power-legitimacy-authority-obligation)), and sort
every claim into empirical, conceptual or normative ([three kinds of claim](#three-kinds-of-claim)).
Where the build's checks against the sources corrected the syllabus, the card follows the
lessons ([Conventions](#conventions)).

## The discipline

### Power legitimacy authority obligation

**Four things a justification of the state could establish; most bad arguments prove one and
claim another.** Kept apart for the whole course from [1.1](lessons/01-01-the-problem-of-political-authority.md).

| Concept | Means | Kind | An argument for it must show |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Power** | the ability to make people comply, by force, threat or habit | descriptive | only that they do comply (a gang has it) |
| **[Legitimacy](#legitimacy)** | the moral right to rule: to make law and enforce it coercively without wronging those coerced | normative | that coercing *these* people wrongs none of them |
| **[Authority](#political-authority)** | the moral power to impose duties by directive: "do X" itself gives a duty to do X | normative | that the directive, as such, creates a [content-independent](#content-independence) reason |
| **[Political obligation](#political-obligation)** | the citizen's moral duty to obey the law of her own state | normative | a duty that is content-independent, general and particular (owed to *this* state) |

**What entails what** (the argument map in [1.1](lessons/01-01-the-problem-of-political-authority.md)):

- **Authority entails obligation, by definition**, and authority entails legitimacy.
- **Power does not entail legitimacy**, nor the reverse (a government in exile).
- **Justification does not entail legitimacy** (Simmons: [justification and legitimacy](#justification-and-legitimacy)).
- **Legitimacy entails obligation?** Disputed: Edmundson (1998) and Applbaum (2019) deny it. Most
  of Module 1 lives on this arrow.

### Three kinds of claim

**Sort every claim before you argue with it.** An empirical claim is settled by evidence; a
conceptual claim by analysis of what something is; a normative claim by argument about what ought
to be done. The course's premise labels (Normative, Empirical, Conceptual) mark which kind each
premise is, and most Watch-out traps are slides between them (method:
[`philosophical-method` 3.2](../philosophical-method/lessons/03-02-distinctions-and-verbal-disputes.md)).

| **Empirical** | evidence | Citizens accept their government (Weber's legitimacy as belief, [1.1](lessons/01-01-the-problem-of-political-authority.md)). Emigration is ruinous for a day labourer (Hume's ship, [1.2](lessons/01-02-consent-actual-tacit-and-hypothetical.md)). Political culture is the main cause of a people's wealth ([6.1](lessons/06-01-global-justice-cosmopolitans-and-statists.md)). |
| **Conceptual** | analysis | Authority is a right to be obeyed ([1.1](lessons/01-01-the-problem-of-political-authority.md)). ### Legitimacy

**The moral right to rule: to make law and enforce it coercively without thereby wronging the
people coerced.** A normative property, not a belief. Weber's legitimacy is descriptive (people
take the order to be rightful, which stabilizes domination), owned by
[`social-theory`](../social-theory/syllabus.md) [4.3](../social-theory/lessons/04-03-domination-and-the-three-types-of-authority.md); a regime can be believed legitimate and not
be, and the reverse.

- **Does it entail a duty to obey?** Disputed. Edmundson (1998): a legitimate state may issue and
  enforce directives without its subjects having even a prima facie duty to obey. Applbaum (2019):
  legitimate authority makes you *liable* to be put under a duty, not already under one. Others
  hold a right to rule just is a right to be obeyed.
- **Consent may not be its whole basis:** even a valid consent scheme leaves the coercion of
  dissenters needing another ground ([1.2](lessons/01-02-consent-actual-tacit-and-hypothetical.md) Example 1).
- **Later uses.** The original position justifies principles, not legitimacy ([2.2](lessons/02-02-rawls-the-original-position.md)); Dworkin:
  silencing a view weakens the state's legitimacy to coerce its holders ([3.4](lessons/03-04-free-speech.md)); Rawls's
  [liberal principle](#liberal-principle-of-legitimacy) governs legitimacy at the level of
  constitutional essentials, and legitimate laws may be unjust ([4.1](lessons/04-01-political-liberalism.md)); communitarian claims
  yield no legitimacy verdict without a normative premise ([4.3](lessons/04-03-perfectionism-and-the-common-good.md)); "the family is a site of
  justice" vs "the state may coerce to correct it" ([4.4](lessons/04-04-feminist-political-philosophy.md)); the counter-majoritarian difficulty
  as a question of legitimacy ([5.2](lessons/05-02-majority-rule-vs-rights-judicial-review.md)); deliberative legitimacy as justifiability to the coerced
  vs Schumpeter's authorization by a fair contest ([5.3](lessons/05-03-deliberative-vs-aggregative-democracy.md)); the proceduralist grounds it in fair
  procedure, not a prior popular will ([5.4](lessons/05-04-does-social-choice-wound-democracy.md)); Nagel's trigger is coercion claiming legitimacy
  in the coerced's name ([6.1](lessons/06-01-global-justice-cosmopolitans-and-statists.md)); a state's right to exclude ([6.2](lessons/06-02-immigration-may-a-state-exclude.md)); an exemption claim
  concedes it ([6.3](lessons/06-03-conscience-and-exemptions.md)).

### Political authority

**The moral power to bind by directive: the fact that the state said "do X" itself gives you a
duty to do X.** Wolff's definition: the right to command, and correlatively the right to be
obeyed. Authority entails obligation by definition, which is why Wolff's argument against it is
an argument against obligation too. Raz's alternative account: the
[service conception](#service-conception-of-authority).

*Lessons:* [1.1](lessons/01-01-the-problem-of-political-authority.md)

### Political obligation

**A moral duty to obey the law of your own state because it is its law.** As the literature uses
the term it has three features:

| Feature | Means |
|---|---|
| [Content-independent](#content-independence) | the reason comes from the directive, not the merits of what it directs |
| General | covers the body of law, not only the laws you like |
| Particular | owed to your own state, not every just state (Simmons 1979's particularity requirement; see [particularity problem](#particularity-problem)) |

- **Each Module 1 lesson tests one ground:** consent ([1.2](lessons/01-02-consent-actual-tacit-and-hypothetical.md)), fair play, gratitude, association
  ([1.3](lessons/01-03-fair-play-gratitude-and-associative-obligation.md)), natural duty ([1.4](lessons/01-04-natural-duty-and-philosophical-anarchism.md)). See the [map](#map-of-grounds-of-obligation).
- **Distinct from legitimacy and authority:** the liberal principle of legitimacy ([4.1](lessons/04-01-political-liberalism.md)), a
  deliberative outcome ([5.3](lessons/05-03-deliberative-vs-aggregative-democracy.md)) and the original position ([2.2](lessons/02-02-rawls-the-original-position.md)) do not by themselves
  establish it; Rawls's bridge from just institutions to a duty to obey is the
  [natural duty of justice](#natural-duty-of-justice). A Condorcet cycle bears on whether outcomes
  express a will, not directly on obligation ([5.4](lessons/05-04-does-social-choice-wound-democracy.md)).

*Lessons:* [1.1](lessons/01-01-the-problem-of-political-authority.md), [1.2](lessons/01-02-consent-actual-tacit-and-hypothetical.md), [1.3](lessons/01-03-fair-play-gratitude-and-associative-obligation.md), [1.4](lessons/01-04-natural-duty-and-philosophical-anarchism.md), [2.2](lessons/02-02-rawls-the-original-position.md), [4.1](lessons/04-01-political-liberalism.md), [5.3](lessons/05-03-deliberative-vs-aggregative-democracy.md), [5.4](lessons/05-04-does-social-choice-wound-democracy.md)

### Content-independence

**A reason that comes from the fact of the directive, not from the merits of what it directs.**
The law against dumping solvent in a river gives you no new reason (you had decisive reason
already); a speed limit of 50 where 55 is equally safe does, if anything does.

- **Test a claim of obligation on a law like the speed limit,** whose content gives no prior
  reason, not on one that forbids a crime: there obligation is idle.
- **A duty to obey only the laws you judge just is no duty to obey law at all**, so obligation must
  survive *some* disagreement on the merits ([1.1](lessons/01-01-the-problem-of-political-authority.md) Example 2).

*Lessons:* [1.1](lessons/01-01-the-problem-of-political-authority.md)

### Justification and legitimacy

**A state can be justified (morally defensible, better than the alternatives, permissibly
enforcing laws against murder) without being legitimate over a particular person.** Simmons,
"Justification and Legitimacy" (1999): legitimacy over a person needs a special, transactional
relation between that person and that state, such as her actual consent. In words: a good argument
that states like ours are worth having is not yet an argument that this state may rule *you*.

- **What survives if every ground of obligation fails** ([1.4](lessons/01-04-natural-duty-and-philosophical-anarchism.md)): justification, and the
  permissible enforcement of laws against serious wrongs.
- **Later uses:** welfare maximization justifies an arrangement, it does not establish the right
  to rule ([2.1](lessons/02-01-utilitarianism-as-a-principle-for-institutions.md)); a correct court ruling is an outcome reason (justification), the court's
  right to impose it over the legislature is legitimacy ([5.2](lessons/05-02-majority-rule-vs-rights-judicial-review.md)).

*Lessons:* [1.1](lessons/01-01-the-problem-of-political-authority.md), [1.4](lessons/01-04-natural-duty-and-philosophical-anarchism.md), [2.1](lessons/02-01-utilitarianism-as-a-principle-for-institutions.md), [5.2](lessons/05-02-majority-rule-vs-rights-judicial-review.md)

### Philosophical anarchism

**No (or almost no) actual state has authority over its residents: there is no general,
content-independent duty to obey.** Not a programme of disobedience: the anarchist has strong
reasons to keep most laws, which forbid what is wrong anyway or coordinate in ways that protect
people, and has no duty to resist.

| Version | Who | Argument |
|---|---|---|
| *A priori* | Wolff, *In Defense of Anarchism* (1970) | authority (a right to be obeyed) conflicts with autonomy, our primary duty; so no state could have it, except possibly a unanimous direct democracy |
| *A posteriori* | Simmons (1979) | legitimate authority is possible (unanimous genuine consent would supply it), but every account fails for actual states |

**Wolff's argument**, paraphrased in [1.1](lessons/01-01-the-problem-of-political-authority.md):

1. (Conceptual) Authority is the right to command and correlatively to be obeyed.
2. (Normative) Each person has a primary duty of autonomy: to decide for herself what she ought to do.
3. (Conceptual) Doing X because you were told to is forfeiting that judgment.
4. So an autonomous person can never acknowledge a duty to obey a command as such.
5. C. No state (bar unanimous direct democracy) has authority over anyone.

- **Premise critics attack: 3.** Raz's camp: judgment can itself tell you to defer, as to a pilot
  in turbulence. The Wolffian reply: such deference is conditional on your continuing judgment of
  the source, so it is advice taken, not authority obeyed.
- **Simmons's route** keeps actual consent ([1.2](lessons/01-02-consent-actual-tacit-and-hypothetical.md)) and accepts the conclusion; the state may
  still be [justified](#justification-and-legitimacy).
- **On civil disobedience** the anarchist needs no special justification for lawbreaking as such;
  she judges the act by its content and harms ([1.4](lessons/01-04-natural-duty-and-philosophical-anarchism.md) Example 2).

*Lessons:* [1.1](lessons/01-01-the-problem-of-political-authority.md), [1.2](lessons/01-02-consent-actual-tacit-and-hypothetical.md), [1.4](lessons/01-04-natural-duty-and-philosophical-anarchism.md)

### Service conception of authority

**An authority is legitimate over you when you would better conform to the reasons that already
apply to you by following its directives than by working things out yourself.** Raz's normal
justification thesis (*The Morality of Freedom*, 1986; also *The Authority of Law*, 1979).

- **Piecemeal:** authority holds person by person and domain by domain. The traffic code binds
  nearly everyone; a pharmacologist may owe no deference to a drug-safety rule she understands
  better than its drafters. Real authority, but no *general* obligation to obey.
- **The leading non-consent account;** it answers Wolff's premise 3 ([1.1](lessons/01-01-the-problem-of-political-authority.md)).
- **In 5.1** it is one attempt to bridge from a procedure's reliability to authority ([5.1](lessons/05-01-why-democracy.md)).

*Lessons:* [1.1](lessons/01-01-the-problem-of-political-authority.md), [1.4](lessons/01-04-natural-duty-and-philosophical-anarchism.md), [5.1](lessons/05-01-why-democracy.md)

### Express and tacit consent

**Express consent is an explicit act (an oath, a naturalization ceremony); tacit consent is given
by conduct.** Consent is a normative power and voluntarist: the obligation exists because you
chose it, which squares the state's claims with the premise that people are born free (Locke,
*Second Treatise* §119).

| Form | Act | Effect for Locke |
|---|---|---|
| Express | oath, naturalization | makes you a member of the society, bound for life (§119, §121) |
| Tacit | owning land, lodging for a week, travelling the highway | binds you to obey *while* you enjoy them; no membership (§119-122) |

- **The state-of-nature device has two uses:** read historically (the state was founded by an
  agreement that still binds) or as a test (a state is legitimate if free and equal people *would*
  agree to it). Confusing them is the commonest error. Hobbes's, Locke's and Rousseau's own
  versions are [`history-of-political-thought`](../history-of-political-thought/lessons/04-02-locke-limited-government-and-toleration.md).
- **The consent theorist's dilemma** ([1.2](lessons/01-02-consent-actual-tacit-and-hypothetical.md)): (1) obligation requires consent; (2) only a
  deliberate, informed act made when refusal was a real option at tolerable cost counts as
  consent; (3) express consent is given by almost no native-born citizens; (4) residence is
  given by nearly everyone but refusal means emigrating. So few citizens are bound by consent.
  The forms that are real are rare, and the forms that are common are not real consent.
- **Premise critics attack: 1.** Every exit after voting quietly gives it up; see
  [hypothetical](#hypothetical-consent) and [normative consent](#normative-consent).
- **Voting as consent** (Plamenatz, *Consent, Freedom and Political Obligation*, 2nd ed. 1968,
  postscript): deliberate, informed, costless to decline. Replies: silent on abstainers; a vote for
  the loser is hard to read as consent to the winner; the voter is bound whether she votes or not.

*Lessons:* [1.2](lessons/01-02-consent-actual-tacit-and-hypothetical.md)

### Simmonss conditions for tacit consent

**When silence or inaction counts as consent.** Simmons (*Moral Principles and Political
Obligations*, 1979, ch. IV), from a chairman who announces a new meeting time and asks for
objections; silence there is consent because:

1. everyone knows consent is being asked for;
2. there is a definite, reasonable period for dissent, by an understood means;
3. it is clear when that period ends;
4. dissent is easy;
5. the consequences of dissent are not extremely detrimental.

- **Residence fails (1) for most people and (5) for nearly all.**
- **A "consent day"** with a dissent form can meet all five, but only if dissenters may stay; and
  then the dissenters are still coerced, so consent is not the whole basis of legitimacy ([1.2](lessons/01-02-consent-actual-tacit-and-hypothetical.md)
  Example 1).

*Lessons:* [1.2](lessons/01-02-consent-actual-tacit-and-hypothetical.md)

### Humes ship objection

**Staying is no consent when leaving is ruinous.** Hume, "Of the Original Contract" (1748, public
domain): a poor peasant or artisan who knows no foreign language and lives on daily wages is no
freer to leave than a man carried aboard a ship asleep, who must leap into the ocean and perish if
he leaves.

| Strand | Kind | Depends on |
|---|---|---|
| The ship | premise 4 of the consent dilemma: the cost of exit | an empirical premise that changes with passports and wages |
| The paragraph before it | conceptual: implied consent can only have place where a man imagines the matter depends on his choice | the empirical premise that people believe they are born into allegiance |

- **Hume's wider charge is historical:** almost all governments were founded on usurpation or
  conquest; he grounds allegiance in its usefulness instead.
- **Change what people believe** and the conceptual objection weakens, though the ship does not.

*Lessons:* [1.2](lessons/01-02-consent-actual-tacit-and-hypothetical.md)

### Hypothetical consent

**What free and equal people would agree to under fair conditions.** Dworkin ("The Original
Position", 1973; *Taking Rights Seriously*, 1977): a hypothetical contract is not a weak form of a
real one, it is "no contract at all"; that I would have sold you my painting yesterday gives you
no claim to it today.

- **The Kantian and contractualist reply**
  ([`ethics` 5.2](../ethics/lessons/05-02-contractualism-what-no-one-could-reasonably-reject.md)):
  the agreement was never meant to bind *as an agreement*. It is a test that filters out arbitrary
  and self-serving reasons. If the reply works, it gives up voluntarism: the reasons bind, not any
  choice.
- **Rawls's version** ([2.2](lessons/02-02-rawls-the-original-position.md)): the [original position](#original-position) is a device of
  justification, its force coming from the conditions it models, adjusted in reflective
  equilibrium.

*Lessons:* [1.2](lessons/01-02-consent-actual-tacit-and-hypothetical.md), [2.2](lessons/02-02-rawls-the-original-position.md)

### Normative consent

**If you would be wrong to refuse consent to an authority, your refusal is void and you are bound
as if you had agreed.** Estlund (*Democratic Authority*, 2008) runs consent in reverse: a "yes" at
gunpoint is null, so a "no" can be null too.

- **Limits:** some refusals are never void (refusing sex); and it covers only authority acceptable
  from every qualified point of view ([qualified acceptability](#epistemic-proceduralism)).
- **The critic:** what binds is the reason you ought to agree, so "consent" labels a natural-duty
  theory ([1.4](lessons/01-04-natural-duty-and-philosophical-anarchism.md)). **The reply:** the consent structure explains why the duty runs to *this*
  authority and its *directives*, not to justice in general.

*Lessons:* [1.2](lessons/01-02-consent-actual-tacit-and-hypothetical.md)

### Principle of fairness

**Those who benefit from others' rule-governed restraint in a cooperative scheme owe a similar
restraint; the wrong is free riding.** Hart, "Are There Any Natural Rights?" (*Philosophical
Review*, 1955); named the duty of fair play by Rawls ("Legal Obligation and the Duty of Fair
Play", 1964).

1. (Normative) When people run a mutually beneficial scheme by rules that restrict their liberty,
   those who benefit from others' compliance owe similar compliance.
2. (Conceptual + empirical) A reasonably just legal order is such a scheme.
3. (Empirical) Each citizen benefits from it.
4. C. Each citizen owes compliance with the law to fellow citizens.

- **Strength:** the duty is owed to the cooperators of *this* scheme, so fair play meets the
  particularity requirement more easily than its rivals.
- **Premise critics attack: 1,** read as covering benefits merely received
  ([Nozick](#nozicks-public-address-objection), [Simmons](#accepting-and-receiving-benefits)).
  Klosko narrows it to [presumptive public goods](#presumptive-public-goods); the critic then
  attacks 2, since the state does far more than supply those goods.
- **Rawls in *A Theory of Justice*** (1971) restricts fair play to those who voluntarily accept
  benefits such as office; citizens generally fall under the natural duty ([1.4](lessons/01-04-natural-duty-and-philosophical-anarchism.md)).
- **Keep the grounds apart:** refusing a vaccine may be wrong by a natural duty not to harm,
  whether or not the refuser benefits from anyone ([1.3](lessons/01-03-fair-play-gratitude-and-associative-obligation.md) Watch out).
- **The free rider's incentive** is [`public-economics` 1.2](../public-economics/lessons/01-02-voluntary-provision-and-crowding-out.md); fairness says the free rider also *wrongs* someone.

*Lessons:* [1.3](lessons/01-03-fair-play-gratitude-and-associative-obligation.md)

### Nozicks public address objection

**Others cannot bind you merely by benefiting you and then sending the bill.** Nozick, *Anarchy,
State, and Utopia* (1974, pp. 90-95): 364 neighbours set up a public address entertainment scheme,
each running it one day a year. After 138 days of enjoying the broadcasts your day comes; you need
not give it up. Likewise if neighbours take turns sweeping the street.

- **Attacks premise 1 of the [fairness argument](#principle-of-fairness)** where benefits are
  thrust on you.
- **Run against a debt to the country that trained you** in
  [`philosophy-of-debt` 6.4](../philosophy-of-debt/lessons/06-04-debts-of-gratitude.md).

*Lessons:* [1.3](lessons/01-03-fair-play-gratitude-and-associative-obligation.md)

### Accepting and receiving benefits

**Fairness binds only for benefits you accept, not benefits you merely receive.** Simmons (1979)
concedes Hart's principle but restricts it.

| | Means | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Accepted | sought out, or taken willingly and knowingly as products of others' cooperation | joining a club's facilities |
| Received | arrives whether you want it or not | clean air, national defence |

- **Most state benefits are only received,** and most citizens think of them as bought with taxes
  from an authority, not accepted from fellow citizens' cooperation. So fairness binds few to the
  state.

*Lessons:* [1.3](lessons/01-03-fair-play-gratitude-and-associative-obligation.md)

### Presumptive public goods

**Goods anyone needs for an acceptable life, whatever else they want: defence, law and order,
protection from environmental hazards.** Klosko (*The Principle of Fairness and Political
Obligation*, 1992): if a scheme provides such goods, they are worth their cost and burdens are
fairly shared, then everyone who receives them owes a share. You cannot decline what no one can
live decently without.

- **The critic's next move:** the state also funds museums, zoning and subsidies (discretionary
  goods), which presumptive goods do not cover. Klosko (*Political Obligations*, 2005) adds
  natural duty and the common good in a **multiple principle** account; critics call that a
  concession that fairness alone cannot ground a *general* obligation.

*Lessons:* [1.3](lessons/01-03-fair-play-gratitude-and-associative-obligation.md)

### Gratitude account

**Benefited citizens must not act against the interests of their benefactor, the state.** The
Laws' speech in Plato's *Crito*; revived by Walker ("Political Obligation and the Argument from
Gratitude", *Philosophy & Public Affairs*, 1988): whoever benefits from a benefactor should not act
against the benefactor's interests; every citizen has benefited from the state; disobedience acts
against its interests; so citizens should comply.

- **Objections:** gratitude is owed to a benefactor acting from goodwill at some cost to itself,
  while the state's benefits are paid for by your taxes and given from duty or policy; and
  gratitude fixes no content ("do whatever it says" is rarely the fitting response). Gratitude is
  owed but not collectible; law is collectible
  ([`philosophy-of-debt` 6.4](../philosophy-of-debt/lessons/06-04-debts-of-gratitude.md)).

*Lessons:* [1.3](lessons/01-03-fair-play-gratitude-and-associative-obligation.md)

### Associative obligations

**Membership in a political community, like membership in a family, generates obligations as part
of what the relationship is.** Dworkin (*Law's Empire*, 1986, ch. 6) and Horton (*Political
Obligation*, 1992). Dworkin limits it to a "true" community, whose practices show equal concern for
every member.

- **Objections (Simmons):** unjust associations (a racist family, a mafia "family", an oppressive
  state) would bind members to injustice; if only just ones bind, the obligation comes from
  justice, not membership. The family analogy is thin: love, knowledge and interaction are absent
  among millions of strangers (also Jeske).
- **Reply:** Dworkin's conditions already exclude unjust communities, and belonging to a polity is
  real.
- **"Citizens feel bound as members"** is an empirical report; the normative bridge must be argued,
  not assumed.

*Lessons:* [1.3](lessons/01-03-fair-play-gratitude-and-associative-obligation.md)

### Natural duty of justice

**A duty owed by everyone, without any act of consent, to support and comply with just
institutions that exist and apply to us, and to help bring about just arrangements when that costs
little.** Rawls, *A Theory of Justice* (1971), §19.

1. (Normative) Everyone has a natural duty to support and comply with just institutions that
   apply to them.
2. (Empirical/normative) Varden's institutions are just, or nearly just.
3. (Conceptual) Varden's institutions apply to me, and Norrland's do not.
4. C. I have a political obligation to Varden in particular.

- **Premise critics attack: 3** (the [particularity problem](#particularity-problem)).
- **Conditional:** it inherits premise 2's limits. In a nearly just society we must comply even
  with some unjust laws, within limits; where institutions are seriously unjust, premise 1 binds
  no one to them. That leaves room for [civil disobedience](#civil-disobedience).
- **The bridge Rawls uses** from just institutions ([2.2](lessons/02-02-rawls-the-original-position.md), [2.3](lessons/02-03-rawls-the-two-principles-and-maximin.md)) to a duty to obey.

*Lessons:* [1.4](lessons/01-04-natural-duty-and-philosophical-anarchism.md)

### Particularity problem

**An account of political obligation must bind me to one particular state, my own, not to justice
in general.** Simmons (1979). Consent passes easily and fails on coverage; natural duty is the
mirror image, since justice is not located anywhere: if Norrland is as just as Varden, why support
Varden?

- **Waldron's answer** ("Special Ties and Natural Duties", *Philosophy & Public Affairs*, 1993):
  institutions of justice are range-limited (a court or land registry works only by settling
  matters for a definite population and territory). Insiders owe compliance and support; outsiders
  owe only non-undermining. Particularity comes from range, not from any act of yours.
- **Simmons's reply:** "applies to me" is either thin (any just body could bind me by claiming
  jurisdiction) or borrowed from consent. **The Waldronian rejoinder:** range is fixed by the moral
  need for one settlement among people who cannot avoid one another. The dispute: does that need
  pick out *one* institution, or only show that *some* must exist?
- **6.2 runs it in reverse:** a general duty to admit with no particular bearer ([6.2](lessons/06-02-immigration-may-a-state-exclude.md)).

*Lessons:* [1.4](lessons/01-04-natural-duty-and-philosophical-anarchism.md), [6.2](lessons/06-02-immigration-may-a-state-exclude.md)

### Civil disobedience

**A public, nonviolent, conscientious yet political act contrary to law, usually aimed at changing
a law or policy, addressed to the majority's sense of justice, within fidelity to law (shown by
openness and willingness to accept the penalty).** Rawls, *A Theory of Justice* §55.

| | Civil disobedience (§55) | Conscientious refusal (§56) | Exemption claim ([6.3](lessons/06-03-conscience-and-exemptions.md)) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Aim | change the law for all | personal noncompliance with a direct order | relief for the claimant, law kept for others |
| Appeal to majority | yes | need not | asks the lawmaker for a carve-out |

- **Rawls's conditions for justification** (beyond meeting the definition): clear and substantial
  injustice, normal political appeals tried and failed, no threat to the order of a nearly just
  scheme.
- **Three verdicts on the same act** ([1.4](lessons/01-04-natural-duty-and-philosophical-anarchism.md) Example 2): natural-duty theorist (justified if the
  conditions hold; fidelity to law matters morally); philosophical anarchist (lawbreaking needs no
  special justification, judge the content); natural law (Aquinas, ST I-II q.96 a.4: an unjust law
  does not bind in conscience, though one may keep it to avoid scandal or disturbance; King's
  "Letter from Birmingham Jail", 1963, drew on this while insisting on open disobedience).
- **Covert, violent and purely personal acts** fall outside the definition; meeting the definition
  is not being justified.

*Lessons:* [1.4](lessons/01-04-natural-duty-and-philosophical-anarchism.md), [6.3](lessons/06-03-conscience-and-exemptions.md)

### Basic structure

**The major social institutions (the constitution, property and contract law, markets, taxes, the
family) that assign rights and duties and divide the gains of cooperation; for Rawls the primary
subject of justice.** Rawls, *A Theory of Justice* (1971) §2: its effects are profound and present
from birth, fixing which starting places exist and what each leads to. A theory of justice is a
standard for this structure, not a rule for individual choices.

- **Utilitarian version:** the just basic structure maximizes the sum of well-being ([2.1](lessons/02-01-utilitarianism-as-a-principle-for-institutions.md)).
- **What the parties choose for** in the original position ([2.2](lessons/02-02-rawls-the-original-position.md)); what the two principles
  govern ([2.3](lessons/02-03-rawls-the-two-principles-and-maximin.md)).
- **Background-justice reply to Nozick** ([2.4](lessons/02-04-nozick-entitlement-and-the-challenge-to-patterns.md)): fair transactions can erode the fair
  background that made them fair, so justice applies first to the rules (a tax schedule set in
  advance is a rule, not a raid).
- **The basic-structure reply to Cohen** ([2.3](lessons/02-03-rawls-the-two-principles-and-maximin.md)): principles govern institutions, not choices
  within them; Cohen answers that it must include informal norms, since Rawls counts the family.
- **The family** ([4.4](lessons/04-04-feminist-political-philosophy.md)): Okin puts it in; Rawls ("The Idea of Public Reason Revisited", 1997, §5)
  agrees but applies the principles to the family's internal life only indirectly, as to churches.
- **Political liberalism:** the freestanding political conception's subject ([4.1](lessons/04-01-political-liberalism.md)).
- **Global:** whether a global basic structure exists is Beitz's interdependence question ([6.1](lessons/06-01-global-justice-cosmopolitans-and-statists.md)).
- **Cited from 1.4:** the natural duty binds only if institutions are just ([1.4](lessons/01-04-natural-duty-and-philosophical-anarchism.md)).

### Utilitarian case for redistribution

**As a principle for institutions, utilitarianism redistributes because a dollar does more for the
poor, and stops where the leak eats the gain.** Mill, *Utilitarianism* (1863) ch. V: equal concern
involves an equal claim to all the means of happiness, except where the conditions of life and the
general interest set limits. Goodin (*Utilitarianism as a Public Philosophy*, 1995): what makes
utilitarianism a poor guide to private life makes it a good guide for officials, who choose
general, public, repeated policies; the classic counterexamples (the framing sheriff) are one-off
secret acts, aimed at the wrong level.

1. (Normative) The basic structure should maximize $W=\sum_i u(c_i)$.
2. (Empirical) [Diminishing marginal utility](#diminishing-marginal-utility): $u$ increasing and concave.
3. (Epistemic) Treat people's $u$ as the same: they are similar, or (Lerner, *The Economics of
   Control*, 1944) the state cannot tell who converts income into well-being better.
4. (Formal) So moving income from richer to poorer raises $W$.
5. (Empirical) Transfers leak ([leaky bucket](#leaky-bucket)).
6. C. Redistribute until the recipient's marginal gain net of leakage equals the payer's marginal
   loss, and no further.

- **Premise critics attack: 3,** and with it the equality conclusion. Sen ("Equality of What?",
  1979): a disabled person who gets less well-being per dollar must get *less*. The egalitarian
  conclusion came from a contingent fact about utility functions, not concern for persons. Reply:
  such cases are rare at policy level, and disability often *raises* marginal need.
- **Equality is a by-product,** not a value in premise 1. A rise in the sum is not a Pareto
  improvement (R loses).
- **Separateness at the institutional level** ([separateness of persons](#separateness-of-persons)):
  weakened when losers are statistical and risks spread ex ante; strengthened when losers are an
  identifiable group (a region) in an inescapable structure. Rule-level reply (Mill: security is
  "the most vital of all interests"): guarantees such as minimum access standards; the critic says
  protection that rises and falls with the numbers is not what a separate person is owed.
- **Formula:** [log utility with a leak](#utilitarian-transfer-with-a-leak); Lerner in
  [miniature](#lerners-equal-division-under-ignorance).

*Lessons:* [2.1](lessons/02-01-utilitarianism-as-a-principle-for-institutions.md)

### Diminishing marginal utility

**An extra dollar buys less well-being the richer you already are: $u$ is increasing and concave.**
With identical $u$, the utilitarian sum favours redistribution, since the poorer person's marginal
utility $u'(c)$ is higher. With $u=\ln c$ and leak $\lambda$, the transfer stops where
$c_P/c_R=1-\lambda$ ([formula](#utilitarian-transfer-with-a-leak)).

*Lessons:* [2.1](lessons/02-01-utilitarianism-as-a-principle-for-institutions.md)

### Leaky bucket

**A fraction of each transferred dollar is lost to blunted incentives and administration.** Okun,
*Equality and Efficiency* (1975). With leak $\lambda$ the recipient gets $(1-\lambda)t$ of a
transfer $t$. It sets where utilitarian redistribution stops short of equality, and at
$\lambda\ge 1-y_P/y_R$ the utilitarian transfers nothing. Also in
[`philosophy-of-economics` 3.1](../philosophy-of-economics/lessons/03-01-the-pareto-principle.md)
as the price of equality.

*Lessons:* [2.1](lessons/02-01-utilitarianism-as-a-principle-for-institutions.md)

### Separateness of persons

**A cost to one person is not compensated by a gain to another; a sum treats them as one ledger.**
Owned by [`ethics` 1.3](../ethics/lessons/01-03-justice-and-the-separateness-of-persons.md); not
re-run here. Rawls (TJ §5): classical utilitarianism extends one person's principle of rational
choice to society.

- **At the institutional level** ([2.1](lessons/02-01-utilitarianism-as-a-principle-for-institutions.md)): weakened when no victim is identified in advance and
  risks are spread ex ante (everyone may gain in prospect, Goodin's point made precise);
  strengthened when losers know who they are and cannot leave the structure.
- **The original position** is built so each party knows she has one life only ([2.2](lessons/02-02-rawls-the-original-position.md)).

*Lessons:* [2.1](lessons/02-01-utilitarianism-as-a-principle-for-institutions.md), [2.2](lessons/02-02-rawls-the-original-position.md)

### Original position

**Choose principles for society as if you did not know who you would be in it.** Rawls's
hypothetical choice situation (*A Theory of Justice*, 1971; a "device of representation" in
*Political Liberalism*, 1993): rational, mutually disinterested parties behind a
[veil of ignorance](#veil-of-ignorance) choose principles for the
[basic structure](#basic-structure), caring about [primary goods](#primary-goods). The cake-cutting
rule generalized: ignorance does the moral work. "Justice as fairness": the principles inherit
their justice from the fairness of the choice (pure procedural justice).

| Design choice | Its job |
|---|---|
| Veil of ignorance (§24) | strip out every fact you could use to tailor principles to yourself, leaving the choice informed |
| Primary goods (§15) | a currency for comparing prospects without knowing anyone's conception of the good |
| Mutual disinterest (and no envy) | keep moral motives out of the parties so fairness sits in the constraints; a modelling assumption, not a claim that people are selfish |
| Circumstances of justice (§22) | say when justice is needed at all |
| Representation | nobody was ever in it; it models conditions we accept on reflection as fair |

**The route, reconstructed** ([2.2](lessons/02-02-rawls-the-original-position.md)):

1. (Normative) Principles for the basic structure are justified if chosen in a situation fair
   between persons regarded as free and equal.
2. (Normative) A situation is fair only if no one can shape principles using facts arbitrary from
   a moral point of view (social position, talents, bargaining strength, conception of the good).
3. (Stipulation) The veil hides exactly those facts.
4. (Stipulation) The parties are rational, mutually disinterested, want more primary goods.
5. (Conceptual) Identically situated, so what one would choose, all would choose: nothing to
   bargain over.
6. C. The principles one rational party would choose are the principles of justice.

- **Premises critics attack: 1 and 2 together.** Dworkin: a [hypothetical](#hypothetical-consent)
  agreement binds no one, so the force comes entirely from premise 2 and the contract is idle.
  Rawls largely accepts this: it is a device of justification, adjusted in reflective equilibrium.
  Then: if the conditions are tuned to the judgments, what independent support has premise 2?
  Communitarians: the self behind the veil is no one we could be (Sandel, [4.3](lessons/04-03-perfectionism-and-the-common-good.md)).
- **Neighbours:** Hobbesian contractarianism (parties know their strength; threat advantage) and
  Scanlonian contractualism (fully informed; what no one could reasonably reject),
  [`ethics` 5.1](../ethics/lessons/05-01-contractarianism-morality-as-mutual-advantage.md) and
  [5.2](../ethics/lessons/05-02-contractualism-what-no-one-could-reasonably-reject.md).
- **It justifies principles, not a duty to obey** ([2.2](lessons/02-02-rawls-the-original-position.md) Watch out; bridge: natural duty, [1.4](lessons/01-04-natural-duty-and-philosophical-anarchism.md)).
- **Hiding the conception of the good** yields equal liberty of conscience; the believer's
  non-neutrality worry is answered by reasonable pluralism ([4.1](lessons/04-01-political-liberalism.md)); *Dignitatis humanae*
  reaches the same verdict without a veil ([religious liberty](#religious-liberty-as-immunity-from-coercion)).

*Lessons:* [2.2](lessons/02-02-rawls-the-original-position.md), [2.3](lessons/02-03-rawls-the-two-principles-and-maximin.md)

### Veil of ignorance

**What the parties in the original position do not know.** TJ §24.

| Hidden | Known |
|---|---|
| social position and class | general facts of economics, psychology, political sociology |
| natural talents and strength | that their society faces the circumstances of justice |
| conception of the good (religion, idea of a worthwhile life) | |
| special psychology, including attitude to risk | |
| the society's particular circumstances (economy, development) | |
| generation | |

- **Its first job is to model fairness,** not to set up a gamble; how parties handle the ignorance
  (maximin vs expected utility) is a separate question ([2.3](lessons/02-03-rawls-the-two-principles-and-maximin.md),
  [`decision-theory` 3.4](../decision-theory/lessons/03-04-choosing-behind-the-veil-rawls-vs-harsanyi.md)).
- **What it filters:** every argument whose force depends on who is making it ([2.2](lessons/02-02-rawls-the-original-position.md) Example 1).

*Lessons:* [2.2](lessons/02-02-rawls-the-original-position.md)

### Primary goods

**Things any rational person wants whatever else she wants.** TJ §15: basic rights and liberties;
freedom of movement and choice of occupation; powers of offices and positions; income and wealth;
the social bases of self-respect. Natural goods (health, talents) are affected by the basic
structure but not directly distributed by it.

- **Why this currency** ([2.5](lessons/02-05-equality-of-what.md)): what free and equal citizens need whatever their conception of
  the good, and an index of it is *public*, so citizens can check whether the basic structure
  delivers. Rawls addresses citizens within the normal range of capacities, leaving severe
  impairment to a later, legislative stage.
- **Sen's objection: fetishism** (mistaking means for ends) and conversion: same income, very
  different functionings ([2.5](lessons/02-05-equality-of-what.md)).
- **The difference principle ranks positions by a primary-goods index** ([2.3](lessons/02-03-rawls-the-two-principles-and-maximin.md)), which faces the
  [indexing problem](#indexing-problem) too.

*Lessons:* [2.2](lessons/02-02-rawls-the-original-position.md), [2.3](lessons/02-03-rawls-the-two-principles-and-maximin.md), [2.5](lessons/02-05-equality-of-what.md)

### Circumstances of justice

**The conditions under which justice is needed at all: moderate scarcity and limited altruism.**
TJ §22, from Hume, *Treatise* III.ii.2 "Of the Origin of Justice and Property" (public domain):
justice answers selfishness and limited generosity joined to scarcity; increase benevolence or the
bounty of nature enough and justice is useless.

- **A conditional:** remove either ingredient and justice goes with the problem. Rawls softens it to
  *moderate* scarcity (enough to make cooperation worthwhile, not so much that conflicting claims
  vanish).
- **Hume explains why justice exists;** Rawls uses the conditions to set up a choice of principles,
  a further step. Waldron's "circumstances of politics" is modelled on it ([5.2](lessons/05-02-majority-rule-vs-rights-judicial-review.md)).

*Lessons:* [2.2](lessons/02-02-rawls-the-original-position.md)

### Two principles of justice

**Equal liberties first, fair opportunity second, and only then inequalities that pay their way at
the bottom.** TJ §46, paraphrased:

1. **Equal basic liberties:** each has an equal right to the most extensive scheme of basic
   liberties compatible with a like scheme for all (political liberty, speech and assembly,
   conscience and thought, freedom of the person with personal property, freedom from arbitrary
   arrest).
2. Social and economic inequalities must be (a) to the greatest benefit of the least advantaged
   (the [difference principle](#difference-principle)) and (b) attached to positions open to all
   under [fair equality of opportunity](#fair-equality-of-opportunity).

| Rule | Content |
|---|---|
| Lexical order | 1 before 2; 2(b) before 2(a) |
| Later wording | *Political Liberalism* (1993) and *Restatement* (2001): "fully adequate scheme", FEO listed first |
| General conception | below the threshold where liberties can be effectively exercised, all primary goods to everyone's advantage ([priority of liberty](#priority-of-liberty)) |

**The argument from the veil** ([2.3](lessons/02-03-rawls-the-two-principles-and-maximin.md)): parties care about primary goods (P1); equal division
is the benchmark (P2); an inequality leaving every position above it is accepted (P3); among
such arrangements they rank by the worst position, because maximin is rational here or because
they could not honour any other choice (P4); so the difference principle. **Premise critics
attack: P4:** arbitrariness shows talent should not be rewarded *for its own sake*, not that only
the floor matters (prioritarians; Harsanyi). Reply: the veil's conditions and the strains of
commitment license the absolute.

**Non-gambling arguments (§29):**

| Argument | Point |
|---|---|
| Strains of commitment | parties may agree only to what they could keep; utilitarianism may ask the bottom to accept less for others' gain |
| Publicity | principles must work when known; "inequalities exist only because they help you" does not corrode |
| Stability and self-respect | equal liberty publicly affirms equal standing |

In the *Restatement*, maximin carries the comparison with average utility; against a mixed view
(average plus a floor) Rawls argues from reciprocity, publicity and stability.

*Lessons:* [2.3](lessons/02-03-rawls-the-two-principles-and-maximin.md)

### Difference principle

**Inequalities are just only if they make the worst-off position better than it would be under any
alternative.** Maximize the floor: leximin over a primary-goods index. Not equality: it *requires*
inequality when that raises the floor (Corvel: incentive pay (22, 26, 34, 70) beats flat 20).

**Rawls's 2x2** (TJ §12), four readings of "positions open to all, inequalities to everyone's
advantage":

| | Efficiency alone | Difference principle |
|---|---|---|
| Careers open to talents | Natural liberty | Natural aristocracy |
| Fair equality of opportunity | Liberal equality | Democratic equality |

Natural liberty lets shares track family wealth; liberal equality still lets them track native
talent; only democratic equality rewards neither lottery for its own sake, treating the
distribution of talent in effect as a common asset (the root of the luck-egalitarian line).

- **Chain connection** (raising the top raises everyone between) and **close-knitness** (no position
  moves alone), TJ §13: under both, the difference principle and "everyone gains" coincide.
- **Insensitive to how many** occupy the worst position; the average is not ([2.3](lessons/02-03-rawls-the-two-principles-and-maximin.md) P1).
- **Cohen's incentives critique** (*If You're an Egalitarian, How Come You're So Rich?*, 2000;
  *Rescuing Justice and Equality*, 2008): if the talented need more only because they will not work
  as hard for less, the inequality is "necessary" only given their choice; said to the poor, "pay
  us more or you lose" is a demand, not a justification; true acceptance would carry an egalitarian
  ethos into choices. Rawlsian replies: the basic-structure reply; the liberty reply (an ethos
  dictating occupation threatens free choice of occupation).
- **Formula:** [maximin, average and sum on a table](#maximin-expected-utility-and-sum-on-a-table).

*Lessons:* [2.3](lessons/02-03-rawls-the-two-principles-and-maximin.md)

### Fair equality of opportunity

**Equal prospects for equal talent and willingness, whatever one's class of origin.** Stronger
than formal "careers open to talents"; lexically prior to the difference principle, so it cannot be
traded for gains to the worst off (a legacy-admissions levy funding transfers fails it, [2.3](lessons/02-03-rawls-the-two-principles-and-maximin.md) P2).
The residue of the civil-service case behind the veil ([2.2](lessons/02-02-rawls-the-original-position.md) Example 1).

*Lessons:* [2.3](lessons/02-03-rawls-the-two-principles-and-maximin.md)

### Priority of liberty

**A basic liberty may be restricted only for the sake of liberty itself, never traded for income.**
Holds once a society is rich enough for the basic liberties to be effectively exercised; below that
Rawls falls back on the general conception.

- **It does not forbid regulating liberties** so the scheme works (booked time slots for rallies):
  that is restricting liberty for liberty's sake.
- **Suspending elections** to raise the least advantaged's income in a prosperous country fails it.

*Lessons:* [2.3](lessons/02-03-rawls-the-two-principles-and-maximin.md)

### Rawls conditions for maximin

**Three features of a choice that make maximin (pick the option with the best worst outcome)
rational.** TJ §26: (1) no basis for estimating probabilities; (2) little care for gains above the
guaranteed floor; (3) the worst outcomes of other options are unacceptable. Owned by
[`decision-theory` 3.4](../decision-theory/lessons/03-04-choosing-behind-the-veil-rawls-vs-harsanyi.md),
where Rawls is read as giving up vNM continuity. Maximin applies to primary goods, not utility.

*Lessons:* [2.3](lessons/02-03-rawls-the-two-principles-and-maximin.md)

### Equiprobability model

**Harsanyi: behind the veil, assign an equal chance of being each person; the expected-utility
chooser then maximizes average utility.** Harsanyi gives up the claim that ignorance differs from
risk. Owned by
[`decision-theory` 3.4](../decision-theory/lessons/03-04-choosing-behind-the-veil-rawls-vs-harsanyi.md)
and [5.1](../decision-theory/lessons/05-01-harsanyis-aggregation-theorem.md). Lerner's
equal-division argument is the same move with unknown utility functions
([formula](#lerners-equal-division-under-ignorance)).

*Lessons:* [2.3](lessons/02-03-rawls-the-two-principles-and-maximin.md)

### Patterned and historical principles

**Nozick's two cuts.** End-state (judge the current time-slice: who has what now) vs historical
(judge how the holdings came about: identical snapshots differ if one arose by theft). Patterned
("to each according to ___": need, effort, merit, contribution) vs unpatterned. Almost every
principle defended is patterned or end-state; the [entitlement theory](#entitlement-theory) is
historical and unpatterned. See the table under the
[map of distributive views](#map-of-distributive-views).

*Lessons:* [2.4](lessons/02-04-nozick-entitlement-and-the-challenge-to-patterns.md)

### Entitlement theory

**A distribution is just if it arose from a just position by just steps, whatever it looks like.**
Nozick, *Anarchy, State, and Utopia* (1974) ch. 7. Like a fairly played poker game: check that no
one cheated, not the final stacks. Background: rights as side constraints and the separateness of
persons ([`ethics` 2.4](../ethics/lessons/02-04-constraints-and-options.md)).

1. (Normative) Whoever acquires an unowned thing by the principle of justice in acquisition is
   entitled to it.
2. (Normative) Whoever acquires a thing by just transfer (voluntary exchange, gift) from someone
   entitled to it is entitled to it.
3. (Normative) No one is entitled except by repeated applications of 1 and 2.
4. (Normative) Holdings from violations (theft, fraud, enslavement) fall under rectification.
5. C. A distribution is just iff everyone is entitled to what they hold under it.

- **Premise critics attack:** the step from "entitled to my share" to "entitled to transfer it on
  any terms, free of later claims": what a just pattern assigns may be a bundle of rights already
  including the tax. Nozick's side: then the pattern decides in advance which uses are allowed,
  the interference he predicted.
- **Not desert:** Wilt's fortune is just because the transfers were free, not because he earned it
  ([`philosophy-of-economics` 5.4](../philosophy-of-economics/lessons/05-04-markets-and-desert.md)).
- **Rectification:** Nozick allows an end-state rule as a rough guide, failing a full theory;
  applied in [`philosophy-of-debt` 5.4](../philosophy-of-debt/lessons/05-04-jubilee-as-a-moral-principle.md).

*Lessons:* [2.4](lessons/02-04-nozick-entitlement-and-the-challenge-to-patterns.md)

### Liberty upsets patterns

**No pattern survives free people using what the pattern gave them without continual
interference.** Nozick's Wilt Chamberlain case, in outline: realize your favourite pattern (D1);
fans freely pay 25 cents a ticket into Wilt's box; a million fans later he has 250,000 dollars and
the pattern is broken (D2). If everyone was entitled to their D1 shares and third parties still
hold theirs, where is the injustice? Keeping the pattern means forbidding such exchanges or
continually taking from Wilt.

- **A conceptual and normative claim,** not a prediction that redistribution shrinks output.
- **Replies:** background justice (Rawls, see [basic structure](#basic-structure)); Cohen on the
  third parties, who never consented to the power Wilt's fortune gives him over them; the
  bundle-of-rights reply under [entitlement theory](#entitlement-theory).
- **Village and fiddler** ([2.4](lessons/02-04-nozick-entitlement-and-the-challenge-to-patterns.md) Example 1): even maximin on holdings ranks D2 below D1 though
  every payer chose it, since the dances bought are not counted as holdings.

*Lessons:* [2.4](lessons/02-04-nozick-entitlement-and-the-challenge-to-patterns.md)

### Self-ownership

**Each person has full rights over her own body, labour and powers.** Mainly the critics' label
(G. A. Cohen's) for the root of Nozick's view; Nozick himself argues mostly from side constraints.

- **Cohen** (*Self-Ownership, Freedom, and Equality*, 1995): self-ownership is hard to refute but
  does not deliver unequal *world*-ownership. That needs a further premise: that the external world
  starts unowned and open to appropriation under a weak proviso. Start it jointly owned and
  self-owners get very different results.
- **Grounds [taxation as forced labor](#taxation-as-forced-labor).**

*Lessons:* [2.4](lessons/02-04-nozick-entitlement-and-the-challenge-to-patterns.md)

### Lockean proviso

**An appropriation does not create ownership if it leaves others, who can no longer use the thing,
worse off than they would otherwise have been.** Nozick's weak version of Locke's "enough and as
good" (*Second Treatise* §33, public domain: no one is injured by another's drink who has a whole
river left; the image is silent on a single spring).

| Feature | Content |
|---|---|
| Comparison | net welfare; losing the *chance* to appropriate does not count |
| Remedy | compensation, not surrender (the appropriator keeps the well) |
| Baseline | never pinned down, so a market economy passes easily |
| Nozick's cases | no seizing the only water hole, nor charging anything if all others dry up; a researcher synthesizing a new drug takes nothing |

- **The baseline is a normative choice the proviso cannot make for itself:** against a state of
  nature nearly everything passes; against an equal share or another property regime many fail
  ([2.4](lessons/02-04-nozick-entitlement-and-the-challenge-to-patterns.md) Example 2, the aquifer).
- **Locke's own labour-mixing theory** is [`history-of-political-thought` 4.1](../history-of-political-thought/lessons/04-01-locke-against-filmer-and-property.md).

*Lessons:* [2.4](lessons/02-04-nozick-entitlement-and-the-challenge-to-patterns.md)

### Taxation as forced labor

**Taking a person's earnings is "on a par with forced labor":** the state makes you work those
hours for others' purposes. Nozick, ASU ch. 7, leaning on [self-ownership](#self-ownership).

- **A claim about legitimacy, not obligation:** a redistributive state exceeds its legitimate
  authority. Whether a citizen must still comply is the separate question of [1.4](lessons/01-04-natural-duty-and-philosophical-anarchism.md).
- **In 6.4** it is the libertarian's objection to welfare provision and to basic income ([6.4](lessons/06-04-welfare-property-and-subsidiarity.md)).

*Lessons:* [2.4](lessons/02-04-nozick-entitlement-and-the-challenge-to-patterns.md), [6.4](lessons/06-04-welfare-property-and-subsidiarity.md)

### Currency of justice

**The metric a theory equalizes, maximizes or guarantees.** Sen's "Equality of What?" (Tanner
Lecture, delivered 1979, published 1980). The debate runs by counterexample.

| Currency | Proponent | Objection |
|---|---|---|
| Welfare (happiness, satisfied preference) | utilitarians, welfarists | expensive tastes (Dworkin); adapted contentment (Sen) |
| Primary goods | Rawls | fetishism of means; ignores conversion (Sen) |
| Resources | Dworkin | insurance may under-compensate handicaps |
| Capabilities | Sen, Nussbaum | the [indexing problem](#indexing-problem) |
| Opportunity for welfare | Arneson (1989) | screens out choice, keeps welfare |
| Access to advantage | Cohen (1989) | the line is choice vs luck, not preferences vs resources |

**Sen's argument for capabilities** ([2.5](lessons/02-05-equality-of-what.md)): (1) the currency should measure real advantage;
(2) welfare adapts to deprivation and inflates with cultivated tastes; (3) people differ in
converting resources into functionings; (4) so equal resources can leave unequal advantage;
(5) the capability set is not distorted by adaptation and tracks conversion. C: capabilities.
**Premise critics attack: 5 into C** (indexing).

- **Nussbaum** (*Women and Human Development*, 2000; *Creating Capabilities*, 2011): ten central
  capabilities, each owed up to a threshold; Sen refuses a fixed list. Capabilities as a welfare
  metric: [`philosophy-of-economics` 1.3](../philosophy-of-economics/lessons/01-03-capabilities-as-a-welfare-metric.md).
- **The question is normative,** not which measure is easiest to collect (though measurability is
  one of Rawls's reasons).

*Lessons:* [2.5](lessons/02-05-equality-of-what.md)

### Equality of resources

**Equalize the means each person has to pursue her plans, and let her bear the costs of what she
chooses: ambition-sensitive, endowment-insensitive.** Dworkin, "What is Equality?" Parts 1-2
(*Philosophy & Public Affairs*, 1981).

- **The island auction:** shipwreck survivors each get equal clamshells and bid for the island's
  resources until the result passes the [envy test](#envy-test). Tastes are charged to their
  owners: wanting scarce things costs more.
- **Handicaps** cannot be auctioned: [hypothetical insurance](#hypothetical-insurance). Talents get a
  parallel insurance against low earning power. Bodily and mental powers count as internal
  resources.
- **Expensive tastes:** a person identifies with her tastes, so is responsible for them, but not
  for her handicaps. Needs no theory of the good: each judges by her own lights.
- **Cohen's revision:** the right line is choice vs luck, not preferences vs resources; unchosen
  expensive tastes split him from Dworkin.

*Lessons:* [2.5](lessons/02-05-equality-of-what.md)

### Envy test

**An allocation is envy-free if no one prefers anyone else's bundle to her own, each judging by her
own valuation.** Dworkin's criterion for an equal division of resources. It does not mean equal
amounts: an envy-free allocation can give one person more units than another, and equal welfare can
fail it ([2.5](lessons/02-05-equality-of-what.md) Example 1). Formula: [envy-freeness](#envy-freeness).

*Lessons:* [2.5](lessons/02-05-equality-of-what.md)

### Hypothetical insurance

**Compensate a handicap at the cover an average person would buy against it if everyone faced the
same risk, funded by tax.** Dworkin. Fair premium = probability times cover
([formula](#fair-insurance-premium)).

- **It can under-compensate:** if money does less for you once disabled, buying less than full
  cover can be rational, and the scheme inherits that choice (Maya is still 10,000 dollars short).
  The resource egalitarian: the shortfall is no injustice; the capability theorist: it *is* the
  injustice.
- **In 6.4:** the welfare state read as the cover people would buy facing equal risk; ex post
  pay-outs, insuring brute luck only ([6.4](lessons/06-04-welfare-property-and-subsidiarity.md)).

*Lessons:* [2.5](lessons/02-05-equality-of-what.md), [6.4](lessons/06-04-welfare-property-and-subsidiarity.md)

### Indexing problem

**Comparing multi-dimensional bundles needs weights, and critics say the weights embed a contested
view of the good.** How much mobility trades for literacy or affiliation. Pressed against
capabilities by Dworkin and Rawlsians; Sen replies that a partial ordering suffices (where one
person is worse in every dimension the verdict is clear; public reasoning settles more); Nussbaum
that a short list of thresholds, not a full ranking, is all justice needs. Primary goods face it
too (income vs opportunity vs self-respect).

*Lessons:* [2.5](lessons/02-05-equality-of-what.md)

### Brute and option luck

**Option luck is the outcome of a deliberate gamble you chose and could have declined; brute luck is
everything else.** Dworkin. Taught in [2.5](lessons/02-05-equality-of-what.md), used in [2.6](lessons/02-06-luck-relations-priority-and-sufficiency.md).

- **Degrees:** "was it a choice?" comes in degrees, and Arneson holds the capacity for responsible
  choice is partly luck; the more is credited to luck, the less harsh the view ([2.6](lessons/02-06-luck-relations-priority-and-sufficiency.md)).
- **Birthplace as brute luck** in luck-egalitarian cosmopolitanism (Caney, Tan; [6.1](lessons/06-01-global-justice-cosmopolitans-and-statists.md)).
- **The welfare state as insurance** covers brute luck only ([6.4](lessons/06-04-welfare-property-and-subsidiarity.md)).

*Lessons:* [2.5](lessons/02-05-equality-of-what.md), [2.6](lessons/02-06-luck-relations-priority-and-sufficiency.md), [6.1](lessons/06-01-global-justice-cosmopolitans-and-statists.md)

### Luck egalitarianism

**Inequality is unjust when it reflects luck rather than choice: neutralize brute luck, let people
bear their option luck.** Dworkin; Arneson (1989); Cohen ("On the Currency of Egalitarian
Justice", *Ethics*, 1989). The label is Anderson's (1999), coined to attack it. Comparative.

- **Objections:** [harshness](#harshness-objection) and [disrespect](#disrespect-objection).
- **Its best replies to the negligent driver** ([2.6](lessons/02-06-luck-relations-priority-and-sufficiency.md) Example 2), each with a cost: pluralism
  (compassion overrides; concedes an obvious requirement is not justice); a sufficiency floor
  (borrowed, not derived); mandatory insurance (Anderson: paternalism).

*Lessons:* [2.6](lessons/02-06-luck-relations-priority-and-sufficiency.md)

### Harshness objection

**Luck egalitarianism abandons the imprudent.** Anderson ("What Is the Point of Equality?",
*Ethics*, 1999), against the hard-line version she finds in Rakowski: an uninsured driver who
negligently makes an illegal turn is owed no emergency care, and no accommodation if disabled.
Extended to people in dangerous jobs and to caregivers who become dependent by doing their duty.

**Anderson's argument, reconstructed** ([2.6](lessons/02-06-luck-relations-priority-and-sufficiency.md)): (P1) an egalitarian theory must express equal
respect and concern; (P2) luck egalitarianism withholds aid from victims of bad option luck;
(P3) it aids victims of brute luck because their endowments are inferior; (P4) abandoning
citizens to their mistakes and certifying some as inferior both fail equal respect; C: it fails
P1, so equality should be relational. **Premise critics attack: P3** (see
[disrespect objection](#disrespect-objection)).

- **Reversed in 6.4:** the reciprocity objection to basic income ([6.4](lessons/06-04-welfare-property-and-subsidiarity.md)).

*Lessons:* [2.6](lessons/02-06-luck-relations-priority-and-sufficiency.md), [6.4](lessons/06-04-welfare-property-and-subsidiarity.md)

### Disrespect objection

**Compensating brute luck has the state certify citizens' inferiority.** Anderson imagines
compensation cheques arriving with letters from a "State Equality Board" explaining to the
disabled, the untalented and the unattractive that they are paid for their deficiencies. A
normative claim about what the state's reasons express, not a prediction that recipients will
feel insulted. Reply: the theory says what justice requires, not what the state must announce;
compensation can flow through general schemes. Rejoinder: the theory's real reasons still rank
citizens' worth.

*Lessons:* [2.6](lessons/02-06-luck-relations-priority-and-sufficiency.md)

### Democratic equality

**The point of equality is a society of equals: no one dominated, exploited or marginalized.**
Anderson (1999). Each citizen is guaranteed, over a whole life and whatever her choices, effective
access to the capabilities needed to function as a human being, as a participant in cooperative
production, and as an equal citizen. It guarantees *enough* to stand as an equal, so in practice
it has a sufficientarian floor. (Rawls's "democratic equality" in the 2x2 under
[difference principle](#difference-principle) is a different use of the phrase.)

*Lessons:* [2.6](lessons/02-06-luck-relations-priority-and-sufficiency.md)

### Telic and deontic egalitarianism

**Telic: inequality makes an outcome worse in itself. Deontic: inequality matters only when someone
wrongs someone by producing it.** Parfit's distinction; the
[leveling-down objection](#leveling-down-objection) targets the telic view only.

*Lessons:* [2.6](lessons/02-06-luck-relations-priority-and-sufficiency.md)

### Leveling-down objection

**If inequality is bad in itself, then destroying the better-off's surplus, benefiting no one,
improves things in one respect, which seems absurd.** Parfit, "Equality or Priority?" (Lindley
Lecture 1991, published 1995).

1. (L1) If inequality is bad in itself, removing it makes an outcome better in at least one respect.
2. (L2) Inequality can be removed by making the better-off worse off and no one better off.
3. (L3, person-affecting) An outcome cannot be better in any respect if it is better for no one.
4. So telic egalitarianism is false.

- **Premise critics attack: L3.** Temkin (*Inequality*, 1993) denies it: leveling down is better in
  one respect, usually worse all things considered; L3 begs the question against the egalitarian.
- **Parfit's exit:** [prioritarianism](#prioritarianism), which leveling down cannot touch.
- **On numbers** ([2.6](lessons/02-06-luck-relations-priority-and-sufficiency.md) Example 1): see [rankings](#prioritarian-and-sufficientarian-rankings).

*Lessons:* [2.6](lessons/02-06-luck-relations-priority-and-sufficiency.md)

### Prioritarianism

**Benefits matter more the worse off the recipient; equality as such does not matter at all.**
Defined in [`ethics` 1.3](../ethics/lessons/01-03-justice-and-the-separateness-of-persons.md) as
maximizing $W=\sum_i f(u_i)$ with $f$ increasing and concave. Only the priority weight $f'(u_i)$
matters here ([formula](#prioritarian-and-sufficientarian-rankings)).

- **Non-comparative:** a benefit to someone at 9 weighs the same whether others are at 9 or 900.
  Hence immune to leveling down, and silent against inequality as such.
- **Against maximin:** weights the worst off more, but not infinitely ([2.3](lessons/02-03-rawls-the-two-principles-and-maximin.md)).

*Lessons:* [2.3](lessons/02-03-rawls-the-two-principles-and-maximin.md), [2.6](lessons/02-06-luck-relations-priority-and-sufficiency.md)

### Sufficientarianism

**What matters is that everyone has enough.** Frankfurt ("Equality as a Moral Ideal", *Ethics*,
1987): equality distracts from this. Crisp ("Equality, Priority, and Compassion", *Ethics*, 2003): a
refined version giving priority below a threshold $T$. Strict version: exclusive concern below $T$,
no distributive concern for gaps above it. Non-comparative.

- **Objection:** indifference above the line (Casal, "Why Sufficiency Is Not Enough", *Ethics*,
  2007): everyone clears $T$ but a few hold almost everything.
- **Anderson's democratic equality** guarantees a sufficiency floor in practice.

*Lessons:* [2.6](lessons/02-06-luck-relations-priority-and-sufficiency.md)

## Liberty and its limits

Module 3. What freedom is, and what the state may forbid.

### Map of liberty and its limits

**Three concepts of liberty** ([3.1](lessons/03-01-three-concepts-of-liberty.md)):

| Concept | Question it answers | Unfree when | Test case |
|---|---|---|---|
| [Negative](#negative-liberty) (Berlin) | how large is the area in which I am left alone? | others deliberately interfere | every law reduces someone's liberty |
| [Positive](#positive-liberty) (Berlin, Taylor) | who governs me? | ruled by compulsion, addiction, manipulation | the addict; the self-governing people |
| [Non-domination](#freedom-as-non-domination) (Pettit, Skinner) | could anyone interfere with me at will? | another has the capacity to interfere arbitrarily | the benevolent master |

| | Interference | No interference |
|---|---|---|
| **Domination** | a permit official who refuses on a whim (unfree on both views) | the kind master who never commands (unfree only for the republican) |
| **No domination** | a speed limit made and reviewable under constitutional checks (unfree only for the negative theorist) | lawful business in a contestable, rule-bound state |

**Grounds for restricting conduct** ([3.2](lessons/03-02-mills-harm-principle.md), [3.3](lessons/03-03-paternalism-and-legal-moralism.md)):

| The conduct | Ground in play | Position |
|---|---|---|
| harms non-consenting others | [harm principle](#harm-principle) | Mill: eligible, then weigh |
| offends others, harms none | [offense principle](#offense-principle) | Feinberg: only after balancing |
| harms only the agent, not substantially voluntary | soft paternalism | Feinberg: no real departure from liberalism |
| harms only the agent, voluntary | hard paternalism | Mill's real rival |
| harms and offends no one, held wrong | [legal moralism](#legal-moralism) | impure (Devlin) or pure |

Feinberg's liberal accepts the harm principle, the offense principle and soft paternalism, and
rejects hard paternalism and both moralisms; critics of liberalism defend one or more of those.

### Negative liberty

**You are free to the extent that no other person interferes with what you could otherwise do.**
Berlin, "Two Concepts of Liberty" (Oxford inaugural lecture, 1958). Unfreedom needs *human*
prevention: being unable to fly, or to afford a flight, is inability, not unfreedom. Hobbes and
Bentham held versions; every law, even a good one, reduces someone's liberty.

- **A legitimate law still reduces negative liberty;** legitimacy asks whether the state *wrongs*
  you, not whether you lose liberty ([3.1](lessons/03-01-three-concepts-of-liberty.md) Watch out).
- **Cohen's challenge** turns poverty into negative unfreedom ([worth of liberty](#worth-of-liberty)).

*Lessons:* [3.1](lessons/03-01-three-concepts-of-liberty.md)

### Positive liberty

**You are free to the extent that you are your own master: your choices come from your own reason,
not compulsion, addiction or manipulation.** Collective form: a people is free when it makes its
own laws. Taylor (1979): negative liberty is an *opportunity* concept (the options open to you),
positive an *exercise* concept (you actually direct your life). Positive liberty is self-mastery,
not having resources (those belong to the [worth of liberty](#worth-of-liberty) or
[capabilities](#currency-of-justice)).

**Berlin's totalitarian-risk argument** ([3.1](lessons/03-01-three-concepts-of-liberty.md), paraphrased):

1. (Conceptual) Positive liberty is self-mastery: rule by one's rational self.
2. (Conceptual) This splits the self into a "higher" rational and a "lower" empirical self.
3. (Empirical-epistemic) Others (a party, a church, a state) may claim to know what my rational
   self would choose better than I do.
4. (Normative) Then making me do what that self would choose realizes my freedom rather than
   violating it.
5. C. Positive liberty can license unlimited coercion described as liberation. Chief exhibit:
   Rousseau's "forced to be free"
   ([`history-of-political-thought` 4.4](../history-of-political-thought/lessons/04-04-rousseau-the-social-contract.md)).

- **Premise critics attack: the move from 3 to 4.** Taylor accepts 1 and 2 but denies anyone's
  *claim* to know my rational will gives a right to act on it: self-mastery must be achieved by me.
  Berlin: once freedom sits in a "true" self, someone will speak for it. Whether that is conceptual
  or a prediction about history is the dispute; premise 3 is empirical, not a logical consequence.
- **The retreat to the inner citadel:** I can make myself free by ceasing to want what I cannot
  get (Stoic, ascetic). Berlin: then a tyrant could free his subjects by teaching them to want
  nothing.

*Lessons:* [3.1](lessons/03-01-three-concepts-of-liberty.md)

### Freedom as non-domination

**You are unfree when someone has the capacity to interfere in your choices on an arbitrary basis,
whether or not they use it.** Pettit, *Republicanism* (1997); Skinner, *Liberty before Liberalism*
(1998). Interference is arbitrary when the interferer is not forced to track the interests and
judgments of the person interfered with (not "arbitrary" in a moralized sense).

- **The benevolent master:** a slave whose kind master never commands suffers no interference, yet
  lives at another's mercy and checks the master's mood before speaking. Unfree; on strict negative
  liberty, free.
- **The republican argument:** (P1) the kindly-mastered slave is unfree; (P2) he suffers no
  interference; (P3) his unfreedom need not involve any failure of self-mastery; so freedom is
  neither non-interference nor self-mastery. The missing ingredient is *status*: being nobody's
  subject.
- **Conditioning vs compromising:** a just, contestable law limits options but only *conditions*
  freedom; the kind master *compromises* it without lifting a finger.
- **Reply** (Carter, Kramer, 2008): non-domination is just security from interference, reducible to
  the negative view.
- **Republican basic income** ([6.4](lessons/06-04-welfare-property-and-subsidiarity.md)): an unconditional grant gives the power to refuse, removing
  domination by employers and officials (Pettit, 2007).

*Lessons:* [3.1](lessons/03-01-three-concepts-of-liberty.md), [6.4](lessons/06-04-welfare-property-and-subsidiarity.md)

### Triadic analysis of freedom

**Every freedom claim has three places: agent $x$ is free from constraint $y$ to do or become $z$.**
MacCallum, "Negative and Positive Freedom" (*Philosophical Review*, 1967). "Freedom from" and
"freedom to" are two slots in one relation, not two kinds of freedom. The camps disagree about the
variables: who is the agent (empirical or "true" self)? what counts as a constraint (deliberate
interference only, or inner compulsion, poverty, a master's power)? which $z$ matters?

*Lessons:* [3.1](lessons/03-01-three-concepts-of-liberty.md)

### Worth of liberty

**Rawls: lacking money does not shrink your liberty, it lowers the worth of your liberty to you.**
*A Theory of Justice* §32 keeps liberty narrow. Cohen, "Freedom and Money" (2001), replies on
negative liberty's own terms: board the train to Glasgow without a ticket and the conductor, with
the state behind him, removes you; lacking money exposes you to interference, so poverty is
negative unfreedom. The analytic form of the old charge that formal rights coexist with real
unfreedom ([Marx](../history-of-political-thought/lessons/06-05-marx-from-hegel-to-historical-materialism.md)).
Also: fair value of political liberties in [property-owning democracy](#property-owning-democracy).

*Lessons:* [3.1](lessons/03-01-three-concepts-of-liberty.md)

### Harm principle

**Society may coerce a capable adult, by law or by public opinion, only to prevent harm to others;
his own good, and the majority's dislike, are never sufficient.** Mill, *On Liberty* ch. 1 (1859,
public domain). A filter on reasons, not a verdict on laws: "even right" means not even a *correct*
moral judgment about self-regarding conduct licenses coercion.

| Feature | Content |
|---|---|
| Scope | "compulsion and control" by anyone: legal penalties and the moral coercion of opinion; not advice, argument or avoidance |
| Necessary, not sufficient | harm makes interference *eligible*; then weigh general welfare (examination losers are harmed and rightly left so, ch. 5) |
| Positive acts | some may be compelled: giving evidence, a share of common defence, an obvious rescue (ch. 1), with more caution |
| Exclusions | children, and "backward states of society" (despotism legitimate if aimed at and achieving their improvement); the criterion is capacity for improvement by free and equal discussion |

**Mill's argument, reconstructed** ([3.2](lessons/03-02-mills-harm-principle.md)):

1. (Normative) Utility "in the largest sense, grounded on the permanent interests of man as a
   progressive being" is the ultimate appeal.
2. (Empirical) Each adult is generally the best judge and most interested guardian of his own good;
   society overruling him usually acts on its likes and dislikes, and errs.
3. (Normative, ch. 3) The [argument from individuality](#argument-from-individuality).
4. (Empirical, ch. 3) Experiments of living produce the ideas everyone's progress depends on.
5. C. Adopt the harm principle as a rule held *absolutely*.

- **Premise critics attack: the step from 1 to an absolute rule.** Stephen (*Liberty, Equality,
  Fraternity*, 1873): if utility rules, wherever coercion does more good than harm utility
  requires it, so the principle is a rule of thumb. Indirect-utilitarian reply (Gray, *Mill on
  Liberty: A Defence*, 1983): a firm rule beats calculation by biased majorities. Rejoinder: then
  it is only as strong as that empirical bet. Alternative reading: premise 3 does the work, and
  Mill defends a perfectionist ideal of autonomy.
- **The exclusions:** Mehta (*Liberalism and Empire*, 1999) argues the universality was conditional
  on a civilizational verdict applied by an East India Company official; defenders detach the
  capacity test from its application. The exclusion is in the text.
- **Not about authority:** which grounds justify an exercise of power, not whether the enactor
  has the right to rule ([3.2](lessons/03-02-mills-harm-principle.md) Watch out).
- **Raz's rereading** ([4.3](lessons/04-03-perfectionism-and-the-common-good.md)): an autonomy-based limit on coercion, in which failing to secure
  autonomy's conditions can itself count as harm.
- **Free speech** is not the harm principle applied to words ([3.4](lessons/03-04-free-speech.md)).

*Lessons:* [3.2](lessons/03-02-mills-harm-principle.md), [3.3](lessons/03-03-paternalism-and-legal-moralism.md), [3.4](lessons/03-04-free-speech.md), [4.3](lessons/04-03-perfectionism-and-the-common-good.md)

### Self and other-regarding conduct

**Self-regarding conduct affects only the agent, or others only with their free, voluntary and
undeceived consent; it is the agent's own business.** Mill, *On Liberty* ch. 1 and 4.

- **Ch. 4's working test:** once conduct "affects prejudicially the interests of others", society
  has jurisdiction, and whether to interfere is a question of general welfare.
- **A "distinct and assignable obligation"** to others, once breached, moves conduct into the
  other-regarding class: punish the breach (the drunk parent's neglect), not its cause (the
  drinking). Harm short of that, "constructive injury", society can bear for the sake of liberty.
- **Interpretive disputes:** affecting others vs affecting their *interests* (Rees, "A Re-reading
  of Mill on Liberty", *Political Studies*, 1960: interests, not mere effects, which is why a
  neighbour's disgust does not count); interests vs rights (ch. 4: violating "constituted rights"
  is punishable by law; conduct merely "wanting in due consideration" only by opinion).
- **"Drug use harms families"** bundles an empirical claim (use leads to neglect), a conceptual one
  (is that harm or constructive injury?) and a normative one (coerce the user, or enforce the
  parent's duty?).

*Lessons:* [3.2](lessons/03-02-mills-harm-principle.md)

### Argument from individuality

**Choosing one's own plan of life exercises and develops the distinctive human faculties
(judgment, discrimination, self-control), and that development is itself a principal part of
well-being.** *On Liberty* ch. 3. Those who let custom choose need only the "ape-like" faculty of
imitation; experiments of living drive progress. The premise critics call perfectionist: if it
carries the harm principle, Mill defends an ideal of autonomy and "utility" does little work.

*Lessons:* [3.2](lessons/03-02-mills-harm-principle.md)

### Soft and hard paternalism

**Paternalism is interference with a person's liberty for that person's own good** (G. Dworkin,
"Paternalism", *The Monist*, 1972: the justifying reasons refer to the coerced person's welfare).
Feinberg (*Harm to Self*, 1986):

| Kind | Interferes | In words |
|---|---|---|
| **Soft** | only when self-regarding conduct is not substantially voluntary (ignorance, compulsion, delirium, panic), or to establish whether it is | protects your will from defects |
| **Hard** | even with a fully informed, voluntary choice, because it is bad for the chooser | protects you from your will |

| Other axis | Means |
|---|---|
| Pure / impure | impure restricts more people than it protects (a ban on *selling* cigarettes restricts sellers to protect smokers) |
| Weak / strong (Dworkin's later usage) | interferes with chosen means / with chosen ends; older writers used weak/strong for soft/hard |

- **Mill's unsafe bridge** (*On Liberty* ch. 5, public domain): seize a man about to cross a bridge
  known to be unsafe when there is no time to warn him, with no real infringement of liberty, since
  he does not desire to fall in; where there is only danger, warn and leave him alone (unless a
  child, delirious or too agitated to reflect). Soft paternalism's founding case.
- **The slavery contract** (ch. 5): void, since "the principle of freedom cannot require that he
  should be free not to be free". Soft, hard, or a separate inalienability principle? Disputed:
  the seller may be fully informed (pushes toward hard), and Mill's reason concerns the value of
  future liberty.
- **"Soft" concerns voluntariness, not gentleness:** a mild fine on informed adults is hard;
  forcibly stopping the man on the bridge is soft. Nudges sit off this axis (not coercive);
  [`philosophy-of-economics` 2.3](../philosophy-of-economics/lessons/02-03-nudges-and-behavioural-welfare-economics.md).
- **Hart** accepted some paternalism while rejecting moralism (consent is no defence to murder).

*Lessons:* [3.3](lessons/03-03-paternalism-and-legal-moralism.md)

### Offense principle

**Serious offense, not harm, may be prohibited, but only after balancing.** Feinberg, *Offense to
Others* (1985), motivated by imagined episodes on a crowded bus you cannot leave, each disgusting,
shocking or embarrassing but harmless.

| Seriousness of the offense | Reasonableness of the conduct |
|---|---|
| intensity, duration, extent | importance to the actor |
| reasonable avoidability | availability of alternatives (another place or time) |
| consent of the offended | malice |
| abnormal susceptibility (discounts) | character of the neighbourhood |

- **Pressure on Hart:** if offense counts for something, why does distress at mere knowledge of
  hidden wrongdoing count for exactly nothing? ([Hart-Devlin debate](#hart-devlin-debate)).

*Lessons:* [3.3](lessons/03-03-paternalism-and-legal-moralism.md)

### Legal moralism

**Criminalizing conduct because it is immoral, though it harms and offends no one.**

| Version | Ground | Who |
|---|---|---|
| Impure (Hart's moderate thesis) | enforcement justified by a further harm, such as social disintegration | Devlin |
| Pure (Hart's extreme thesis) | wrongness as such, harm or no harm | |

- **Mill denies it** even for a correct moral judgment ("wise, or even right") about self-regarding
  conduct ([3.2](lessons/03-02-mills-harm-principle.md)).
- **Devlin's argument is impure,** which is why its empirical premise carries the weight.
- **Natural-law defence of morals legislation** for the [common good](#common-good)
  ([natural-law perfectionism](#natural-law-perfectionism), [4.3](lessons/04-03-perfectionism-and-the-common-good.md)); George's moral-ecology
  argument is arguably impure.
- **"Most people are disgusted"** is empirical, never by itself normative.

*Lessons:* [3.2](lessons/03-02-mills-harm-principle.md), [3.3](lessons/03-03-paternalism-and-legal-moralism.md)

### Hart-Devlin debate

**May the criminal law enforce a society's shared morality against private conduct?** Wolfenden
Report (1957): private homosexual conduct between consenting adults should stop being a crime;
there is a realm of private morality that is not the law's business. Devlin replied (Maccabaean
Lecture 1959; *The Enforcement of Morals*, 1965).

**Devlin, reconstructed** ([3.3](lessons/03-03-paternalism-and-legal-moralism.md)):

1. (Conceptual) A society is constituted by a community of ideas, including a shared morality.
2. (Empirical) Tolerated deviation loosens it, and a loosening morality tends to disintegrate
   society.
3. (Normative) A society may use law to preserve itself, as against treason.
4. C1. It may use criminal law to enforce its shared morality, including against private conduct.
5. (Normative-procedural) The morality is what the reasonable person (the juror; the law's stock
   man in the Clapham omnibus) would condemn after reflection, signalled by real intolerance,
   indignation and disgust.

Premises 1 and 2 are the **disintegration thesis**. Devlin's limits: maximum freedom consistent
with integrity, respect for privacy, caution where moral feeling is shifting.

**Hart** (*Law, Liberty, and Morality*, 1963), three moves:

| Move | Point |
|---|---|
| Against 1 and 2 | either "society" is *defined* by its current morality (then any change "destroys" it, trivially) or the claim is empirical and unsupported |
| Positive vs critical morality | what a society accepts vs the standards for judging any society's morality; disgust reports the first |
| Distress is not harm | distress at the bare knowledge that others act wrongly cannot count, or no disapproved liberty survives |

- **Premises critics attack:** Devlin's 2 (empirical claim stated as if conceptual); defenders say
  it concerns a society's capacity to hold *any* common standards. Hart's third move (see
  [offense principle](#offense-principle)).

*Lessons:* [3.3](lessons/03-03-paternalism-and-legal-moralism.md)

### Four grounds for free speech

**A free-speech principle says some harms of expression do not count as reasons to restrict it, or
count for less than the same harm caused another way.** Not the harm principle applied to words.

**Mill's four grounds** (*On Liberty* ch. 2 recap, public domain):

1. A silenced opinion may be true; to deny it is to assume infallibility.
2. A false opinion often holds part of the truth, supplied only by collision with rivals.
3. A wholly true opinion, never contested, is held as a prejudice.
4. Its meaning then fades into a formula that no longer shapes conduct.

Grounds 1-2 concern *getting* the truth, 3-4 *holding* it well. Aimed at social pressure as much as
law ("tyranny of the majority", shared with Tocqueville,
[`history-of-political-thought` 6.3](../history-of-political-thought/lessons/06-03-tocqueville-tyranny-of-the-majority-and-soft-despotism.md)).

| Family | Who | Protects |
|---|---|---|
| Truth and discovery | Mill, ch. 2 | false and partly false opinions, dissent |
| Autonomy | Scanlon, "A Theory of Freedom of Expression" (*Philosophy & Public Affairs*, 1972) | anything whose harm passes through persuasion |
| Democracy and legitimacy | Dworkin, Foreword to *Extreme Speech and Democracy* (2009) | input into public opinion, hateful views included |
| Dignity (restricting) | Waldron, *The Harm in Hate Speech* (2012) | permits restricting visible attacks on a group's standing |

All four permit restricting [incitement](#incitement-and-advocacy).

**The autonomy argument** ([3.4](lessons/03-04-free-speech.md)): (1) coercion is justified only by reasons a citizen could
accept as an equal, autonomous, rational agent; (2) some harms of expression are
persuasion-mediated (they pass through a listener's belief or decision); (3) an autonomous agent
could not accept a state that keeps her from hearing a view lest she be persuaded; C1:
persuasion-mediated harms cannot justify restriction. **Premise critics attack: 3,** and Scanlon
was first: in "Freedom of Expression and Categories of Expression" (*University of Pittsburgh Law
Review*) he made autonomy one interest to weigh. Defender: weighing gives officials the power to
rank opinions.

- **Dworkin's legitimacy argument:** a majority may enforce laws against a dissenter only if he had
  his say in forming the opinion behind them; silence him upstream and you lose part of the right
  to coerce him downstream.
- **Empirical vs normative:** whether counter-speech works or bans make martyrs is empirical;
  whether a harm passing through judgment may count is normative. Scanlon 1972 and Dworkin do not
  depend on the empirical premise; Mill's grounds 1-2 lean on it more.

*Lessons:* [3.4](lessons/03-04-free-speech.md)

### Incitement and advocacy

**Arguing that an act would be good differs from setting a crowd to do it now.** The line every view
draws; the harm of incitement does not pass through the listener's judgment. Mill's corn-dealer
(*On Liberty* ch. 3): the opinion that corn-dealers starve the poor may circulate in the press but
is punishable when delivered to an excited mob outside a corn-dealer's house. The line is about
circumstances, not how dangerous the opinion is. US doctrine: *Brandenburg v. Ohio* (1969)
protects advocacy unless aimed at producing imminent lawless action and likely to produce it
(doctrine owned by [`constitutional-law`](../constitutional-law/syllabus.md) 6.1).

*Lessons:* [3.4](lessons/03-04-free-speech.md)

### Dignity argument for hate-speech laws

**A well-ordered society gives each member a public assurance of equal standing; hate speech in the
visible environment attacks it, a harm not mediated by persuasion.** Waldron, *The Harm in Hate
Speech* (2012). Dignity, not offense; attacks on doctrines are not covered.

4. (Conceptual) The harm of a public poster saying a group is unfit for citizenship is the members'
   lost assurance of equal treatment, whether or not anyone is persuaded.
5. (Normative) That assurance is a public good, part of the shared environment, owed to everyone.
6. C2. The autonomy argument's C1 does not protect hate speech.

(Numbered as continuing the [autonomy argument](#four-grounds-for-free-speech), whose C1 it
accepts.)

- **Dworkin's counter:** grant the harm; banning expression upstream costs the state legitimacy to
  enforce anti-discrimination law downstream. The cost falls on the state's right to rule.

*Lessons:* [3.4](lessons/03-04-free-speech.md)

### Reasonable pluralism

**Under free institutions, people who reason well and in good faith come to hold different and
incompatible religious, philosophical and moral doctrines, lastingly.** Rawls, *Political
Liberalism* (1993). Paired with the **fact of oppression**: continued agreement on one doctrine
could be kept only by state coercion (the Inquisition kept medieval Christendom Christian).
Pluralism is what freedom produces.

- **Why Rawls changed course:** *A Theory of Justice* Part III argued a just society would be
  stable because citizens would affirm justice as fairness within one shared (Kantian) outlook; by
  1993 he judged that unrealistic (PL Introduction).
- **Not relativism:** partly empirical (free institutions produce lasting diversity), partly moral
  (reasonable citizens will not coerce over it); it says nothing about which doctrine is true.
- **Explained by** the [burdens of judgment](#burdens-of-judgment).

*Lessons:* [4.1](lessons/04-01-political-liberalism.md), [4.2](lessons/04-02-public-reason-and-religious-argument.md)

### Burdens of judgment

**Six sources of disagreement among fully rational people.** PL Lecture II:

1. The evidence is conflicting and complex.
2. Agreeing on which considerations matter, we weigh them differently.
3. Concepts are vague and have hard cases.
4. Assessment is shaped by differing life experience.
5. Normative considerations of different force bear on both sides.
6. Limited social space: no institutions admit every value, so some are selected and ranked.

**A reasonable person** (a moral idea, not an epistemic grade) proposes fair terms of cooperation
and abides by them if others do, and accepts the burdens of judgment and what follows.

**The argument to toleration, in outline** ([4.1](lessons/04-01-political-liberalism.md)): free institutions produce lasting
diversity; the burdens explain it without convicting anyone of irrationality; a reasonable person
applies that to her own doctrine too (she may hold it true but cannot expect good reasoners to see
it so); it is unreasonable to repress a reasonably held view on grounds others could reasonably
reject (close to [Scanlon](../ethics/lessons/05-02-contractualism-what-no-one-could-reasonably-reject.md));
so reasonable persons will not use state power to repress reasonable doctrines. Rawls generalizes
the conclusion into the [liberal principle of legitimacy](#liberal-principle-of-legitimacy).

- **Premises critics attack: the second and third steps.** Raz ("Facing Diversity: The Case of
  Epistemic Abstinence", 1990): the burdens either imply scepticism (itself a contested
  comprehensive claim) or give a believer no reason to hold back. Rawls: no scepticism asked, only
  recognition that others cannot be expected to share the belief.
- **The asymmetry objection** (Waldron, *Law and Disagreement*, 1999; Sandel): we disagree about
  justice as reasonably as about the good, so why may the political conception take sides on
  justice? Rawls's partial answer: legitimacy needs only reasonable acceptability of constitutional
  essentials, leaving a family of liberal conceptions in play.

*Lessons:* [4.1](lessons/04-01-political-liberalism.md), [4.2](lessons/04-02-public-reason-and-religious-argument.md)

### Comprehensive and political doctrines

**A comprehensive doctrine covers the whole of life; a political conception covers only the basic
structure.** Comprehensive: Catholicism, Islam, Kantian autonomy, Mill's individuality,
utilitarianism. A political conception is **freestanding** (stated without relying on any
comprehensive doctrine; a "module" that fits into many) and built from ideas implicit in democratic
public culture: citizens as free and equal, society as fair cooperation. Public reason excludes
*every* comprehensive doctrine, secular ones included ([4.2](lessons/04-02-public-reason-and-religious-argument.md)).

*Lessons:* [4.1](lessons/04-01-political-liberalism.md), [4.2](lessons/04-02-public-reason-and-religious-argument.md)

### Overlapping consensus

**Each reasonable comprehensive doctrine endorses the same political conception from inside, for its
own reasons.** PL Lecture IV. The Catholic endorses religious liberty because faith must be free; the
Kantian because persons are autonomous; the utilitarian because liberty serves welfare; the
pluralist because political values usually outweigh others (Rawls's model case). Agreement on the
conclusion, not the grounds. An unreasonable doctrine does not join and is contained without its
agreement. Affirmed on moral grounds internal to each doctrine, so it survives a shift in the
balance of power: "stability for the right reasons". Contrast [modus vivendi](#modus-vivendi).

*Lessons:* [4.1](lessons/04-01-political-liberalism.md)

### Modus vivendi

**A treaty-like settlement each side accepts because it cannot win, and would abandon if the balance
of power shifted.**

- **Same laws and behaviour today** as an overlapping consensus; the difference is counterfactual
  (what each side would do as the majority). Rawls's history: post-Reformation toleration began as
  a modus vivendi, deepening into a constitutional consensus and then an overlapping one, so "is it
  a consensus?" may have no answer at one date.
- **Rawls:** fragile, and does not treat citizens as endorsing their own terms. **Gray** (*Two Faces
  of Liberalism*, 2000): peaceful coexistence among rival ways of life is the most politics should
  aim at.
- **The crux** between them is a premise about legitimacy: whether full legitimacy requires that
  citizens can endorse the terms from within their own reasonable views ([4.1](lessons/04-01-political-liberalism.md) P3).

*Lessons:* [4.1](lessons/04-01-political-liberalism.md)

### Liberal principle of legitimacy

**Coercive political power is properly exercised only in accordance with a constitution whose
essentials all citizens, as free and equal, may reasonably be expected to endorse in the light of
principles and ideals acceptable to their common human reason.** PL Lecture IV (p. 137),
paraphrased.

| It governs | It does not |
|---|---|
| legitimacy (1.1's right to coerce) | justice: a legitimate law can be unjust |
| constitutional essentials, first | every bylaw |
| exercises of power | political obligation, by itself |

- **Root:** Locke's *Letter Concerning Toleration*
  ([`history-of-political-thought` 4.2](../history-of-political-thought/lessons/04-02-locke-limited-government-and-toleration.md)),
  extended by Rawls to secular philosophies, Kant's and Mill's included.
- **First premise of the duty of civility** ([public reason](#public-reason)).
- **Is it self-defeating?** See [self-defeat objection](#self-defeat-objection).

*Lessons:* [4.1](lessons/04-01-political-liberalism.md), [4.2](lessons/04-02-public-reason-and-religious-argument.md)

### Neutrality of aim and effect

**Rawls requires that the state not aim at favouring any comprehensive doctrine; he does not require
that its policies affect all doctrines equally.** PL Lecture V:

| Kind | Means | Rawls |
|---|---|---|
| Procedural | a procedure justified without appeal to any moral values | rejected: political liberalism rests on values such as fairness |
| Of aim | the state does nothing *intended* to favour a comprehensive doctrine | required |
| Of effect or influence | policies make no difference to which doctrines flourish | abandoned as impracticable |

- **An uneven effect does not show a law illegitimate** ([4.1](lessons/04-01-political-liberalism.md)).
- **Perfectionists deny neutrality of aim** ([4.3](lessons/04-03-perfectionism-and-the-common-good.md)).

*Lessons:* [4.1](lessons/04-01-political-liberalism.md), [4.3](lessons/04-03-perfectionism-and-the-common-good.md)

### Public reason

**The reasons citizens can offer one another as free and equal despite reasonable pluralism:
political values (equal liberty, fair opportunity, the common good of citizens, public health,
security) plus methods nobody reasonably disputes (common sense, settled science).** Rawls, PL
Lecture VI (1993); "The Idea of Public Reason Revisited" (*University of Chicago Law Review*, 1997).

| Limit | Content |
|---|---|
| Topic | constitutional essentials and matters of basic justice |
| Forum | the public political forum: judges (most strictly), officials, candidates, citizens voting on fundamentals; not the background culture (churches, universities, families, associations) |
| Kind of duty | the **duty of civility** (be ready to explain positions on fundamentals by public reasons) is moral, not legal; no one is censored |

**The duty of civility, reconstructed** ([4.2](lessons/04-02-public-reason-and-religious-argument.md)):

1. (Normative) The [liberal principle of legitimacy](#liberal-principle-of-legitimacy).
2. (Conceptual) Reasonable citizens reasonably reject one another's comprehensive doctrines.
3. So a reason whose force depends on a comprehensive doctrine cannot be one all could be expected
   to endorse.
4. (Normative: reciprocity) Citizens exercising power over one another owe a justification meeting
   premise 1.
5. C. On fundamentals, in the public political forum, citizens have a moral duty to be able to
   justify their positions by public reasons.

- **Premise critics attack: 4,** the step from a test for *laws* to a duty on *citizens' reasons*.
  Eberle: legitimacy needs a public case to exist, not that each voter be moved by it. Defender:
  respect is owed in the act of coercing, not only in the result.
- **The line is public vs comprehensive, not secular vs religious.**
- **Not a speech law** ([3.4](lessons/03-04-free-speech.md)'s questions are separate). A senator breaching the duty commits a
  moral fault; whether the law is illegitimate depends on whether a public case for it exists.
- **In 5.3:** Cohen's "reasoned" condition needs an account of which reasons count; public reason
  moves from constitutional essentials to everyday deliberation ([5.3](lessons/05-03-deliberative-vs-aggregative-democracy.md)).

*Lessons:* [4.2](lessons/04-02-public-reason-and-religious-argument.md), [5.3](lessons/05-03-deliberative-vs-aggregative-democracy.md)

### Inclusive and exclusive public reason

**When may comprehensive reasons enter public political argument?**

| View | Answer | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Exclusive | never, on fundamental questions | |
| Inclusive | where doing so strengthens the ideal of public reason, especially in a society not well-ordered (the abolitionists, King) | PL (1993) |
| Wide | at any time, subject to [the proviso](#the-proviso) | "Public Reason Revisited" (1997) |

All three treat secular comprehensive reasons (Kantian, utilitarian) exactly like scriptural ones.

*Lessons:* [4.2](lessons/04-02-public-reason-and-religious-argument.md)

### The proviso

**Comprehensive reasons may be introduced provided that, in due course, proper public reasons
sufficient to support the same conclusion are given.** Rawls's wide view (1997). He leaves the
details ("in due course", by whom) to be worked out in practice.

*Lessons:* [4.2](lessons/04-02-public-reason-and-religious-argument.md)

### Consensus and convergence

**Consensus: justification by reasons all reasonable citizens can share (Rawls). Convergence: a law is
publicly justified when each citizen has sufficient reason to endorse it from her own point of view,
even if the reasons differ** (Gaus, *The Order of Public Reason*, 2011; Vallier, *Liberal Politics
and Public Faith*, 2014).

| Vallier's grade | A reason is |
|---|---|
| Shareable | accepted by all |
| Accessible | assessable by all by common standards |
| Intelligible | seen by others as justified for her by her own standards |

Consensus views demand one of the first two; convergence needs only intelligibility, so a religious
citizen's intelligible reason can defeat a law's justification.

*Lessons:* [4.2](lessons/04-02-public-reason-and-religious-argument.md)

### Integrity objection

**Restraint asks religious citizens to divide themselves.** Wolterstorff (in Audi and Wolterstorff,
*Religion in the Public Square*, 1997): many believers hold faith as the frame through which they
see justice, not one input among others. A normative claim, not a report of how believers feel.

- **The fairness objection** (Eberle, *Religious Conviction in Liberal Politics*, 2002): accept
  *conscientious engagement* (seek public justification, weigh critics, listen), deny *restraint*:
  a citizen who has done all that and still has only a religious reason may vote on it; requiring
  more singles out religious citizens' deepest commitments. (Not the "asymmetry objection", which
  names the justice-vs-good point, [burdens of judgment](#burdens-of-judgment).)

*Lessons:* [4.2](lessons/04-02-public-reason-and-religious-argument.md)

### Self-defeat objection

**The principle of public reason is itself reasonably contested, so by its own standard it may not
justify anything coercive.** Pressed by Wall (2002) and Raz, among others. Quong's reply
(*Liberalism without Perfection*, 2011), in outline: an *internal* conception, justifying only to
citizens idealized as already accepting freedom, equality and fair cooperation, for whom the
principle is a presupposition, not a contested conclusion. Cost: the "reasonable" are partly
defined by the theory, which critics call circular.

*Lessons:* [4.2](lessons/04-02-public-reason-and-religious-argument.md)

### Perfectionism

**The state may act on judgments of what makes a life go well: promote valuable options, discourage
worthless ones.** Its denial is anti-perfectionism, or [neutrality of aim](#neutrality-of-aim-and-effect).
Three questions sort the positions: may the state promote the good at all? what grounds the answer
(autonomy, or substantive goods)? by what means (coercion, or subsidies, taxes, public provision)?

**Raz's liberal perfectionism** (*The Morality of Freedom*, 1986), reconstructed in [4.3](lessons/04-03-perfectionism-and-the-common-good.md):

1. (Normative) Personal autonomy is a central ingredient of well-being in a society like ours.
2. (Conceptual) Autonomy requires an adequate range of options.
3. (Normative) Autonomy is valuable only in pursuit of valuable options.
4. (Empirical) Many valuable options exist only if publicly supported.
5. (Normative) Coercion invades autonomy and is justified only to prevent harm, where failing to
   secure others' autonomy can count as harm (Raz's [harm principle](#harm-principle)).
6. C. The state may promote valuable options, mainly non-coercively; it may not coerce people into
   the good life.

- **Premise critics attack: 5 against "mainly non-coercive".** Anti-perfectionist: a subsidy is
  coerced from taxpayers and a punitive tax works like a ban. Natural-law critic: if worthless
  options add nothing, why tax a degrading pastime but never prohibit it? Raz: coercion expresses
  distinctive disrespect and removes options wholesale.
- **Quong's paternalism objection:** subsidizing the "better" option treats citizens as unable to
  judge their good, denying equal standing even without coercion.
- **Rivals:** [natural-law perfectionism](#natural-law-perfectionism), the CST
  [common good](#common-good), the [communitarian critique](#communitarian-critique).
- **Museums but not casinos:** every state funds some things and not others. Political liberalism
  asks that such choices, where coercive and basic, rest on reasons every reasonable citizen can
  accept, not on a ranking of ways of life; the perfectionist may cite the value of the option.

*Lessons:* [4.3](lessons/04-03-perfectionism-and-the-common-good.md)

### Communitarian critique

**Liberal neutrality rests on a false picture of the self and of what sustains justice.** None of the
three accepted the label.

| Author | Claim |
|---|---|
| Sandel, *Liberalism and the Limits of Justice* (1982) | the original position presupposes an "unencumbered self" prior to its ends; but some attachments (family, people, faith) are constitutive |
| MacIntyre, *After Virtue* (1981) | virtues are defined within practices and traditions; a life makes sense as a narrative within one; liberal neutrality is a tradition that does not know it is one |
| Taylor, "Atomism" (1979) | the social thesis: the capacities that make rights worth having develop only in a certain society, which whoever affirms rights must sustain |

- **Rawls's reply:** the self of the original position is a *political* conception of the citizen,
  not a metaphysical claim; one may hold constitutive attachments and still not impose them.
- **The claims are conceptual and empirical** and yield no legitimacy verdict without a normative
  premise ([4.3](lessons/04-03-perfectionism-and-the-common-good.md) Watch out).
- **Sandel's placement is contested:** usually read as a civic republican.

*Lessons:* [2.2](lessons/02-02-rawls-the-original-position.md), [4.3](lessons/04-03-perfectionism-and-the-common-good.md)

### Natural-law perfectionism

**Law cannot make people virtuous directly, but it may protect the moral ecology in which they make
character-forming choices, by suppressing inducements to vice.** Robert P. George, *Making Men
Moral* (1993), defending the central tradition of morals legislation from Aristotle and Aquinas.

- **A pluralistic perfectionism:** a plurality of incommensurable basic goods (the new natural law,
  [`ethics` 4.5](../ethics/lessons/04-05-the-new-natural-law-theory.md)).
- **Against Raz:** autonomy is valuable as a condition for realizing basic goods, so it cannot rule
  out all coercion for the good; prudence and the rights of persons limit coercion instead.
- **Morals legislation** for the [common good](#common-good) ([3.3](lessons/03-03-paternalism-and-legal-moralism.md)); arguably impure moralism.

*Lessons:* [3.3](lessons/03-03-paternalism-and-legal-moralism.md), [4.3](lessons/04-03-perfectionism-and-the-common-good.md)

### Common good

**The sum of the conditions of social life that let groups and their members reach their fulfilment
more fully and readily.** *Gaudium et spes* (1965), paraphrased; Catholic social teaching and
natural law. Includes moral and spiritual conditions; not an aggregate of welfare or utilities.

- **Two limits:** [subsidiarity](#subsidiarity) (reserve to families and smaller bodies what they
  can do) and religious liberty (*Dignitatis humanae*, 1965,
  [immunity from coercion](#religious-liberty-as-immunity-from-coercion)).
- **One position among several,** taught from within the tradition in
  [`catholic-social-teaching`](../catholic-social-teaching/syllabus.md).
- **Uses:** defence of morals legislation ([3.3](lessons/03-03-paternalism-and-legal-moralism.md)); perfectionism ([4.3](lessons/04-03-perfectionism-and-the-common-good.md)); welfare,
  property and subsidiarity ([6.4](lessons/06-04-welfare-property-and-subsidiarity.md)).

*Lessons:* [3.3](lessons/03-03-paternalism-and-legal-moralism.md), [4.3](lessons/04-03-perfectionism-and-the-common-good.md), [6.4](lessons/06-04-welfare-property-and-subsidiarity.md)

### Public and private

**"The personal is political": relations within marriage and the household involve power, so they
raise questions of justice; calling them "private" does not make them so.** Usually traced to Carol
Hanisch's 1970 essay of that title in *Notes from the Second Year* (Hanisch says the editors chose
the title). A *conceptual* claim about where justice applies; whether the state may coerce to
correct an injustice is a separate *normative* question about legitimacy.

*Lessons:* [4.4](lessons/04-04-feminist-political-philosophy.md)

### Okin on the family

**By Rawls's own standards the family is part of the basic structure, and the gendered family fails
them twice: as a distributor of prospects and as the school of justice.** Okin, *Justice, Gender,
and the Family* (1989).

1. (Normative, Rawls's own) The principles govern the basic structure.
2. (Empirical) The family profoundly shapes prospects from the start.
3. C1. The family is part of the basic structure.
4. (Normative, Rawls's own) Citizens first learn a sense of justice in the family.
5. (Empirical) A gender-hierarchical family teaches dependence and command, not reciprocity.
6. (Empirical) Gender-structured marriage makes women vulnerable at every stage ("vulnerability by
   marriage").
7. C2. The family's structure must become just; policy should make an egalitarian family feasible
   (shared earnings, parental leave, childcare).

- **Rawls's reply** ("Public Reason Revisited", 1997, §5): accepts C1; principles apply *directly*
  to the basic structure, *indirectly* to associations within it (as to churches). Wives have all
  citizens' rights, children are protected as future citizens; he endorsed a wife's equal share of
  income earned during the marriage. A "fully voluntary" gendered division may persist; involuntary
  division is to be reduced to zero; equal division may not be mandated.
- **Premise critics attack: the step from C1 to C2.** The dispute is over "voluntary": choices made
  inside a structure that rewards the gendered division vs fixing the background and respecting
  what people choose.
- **Pateman** (*The Sexual Contract*, 1988): the social contract presupposed a prior contract
  securing men's political right over women. Wollstonecraft is
  [`history-of-political-thought` 5.4](../history-of-political-thought/lessons/05-04-wollstonecraft-rights-virtue-and-education.md).

*Lessons:* [4.4](lessons/04-04-feminist-political-philosophy.md)

### Dependency work and doulia

**Dependency is inevitable, someone must care for dependents, and the carer becomes dependent in
turn; society must support those who care.** Kittay, *Love's Labor* (1999). *Dependency work* makes
the carer derivatively dependent on whoever provides for her. A theory modelling citizens as
independent cooperators over a whole life writes out both dependencies. **Doulia** (after the
*doula*, who cares for the new mother): as we were cared for, society must care for the carers.
Care counted as contribution returns in White's [reciprocity objection](#reciprocity-objection)
([6.4](lessons/06-04-welfare-property-and-subsidiarity.md)).

*Lessons:* [4.4](lessons/04-04-feminist-political-philosophy.md)

### Structural injustice

**The normal working of social processes, each action within the rules, puts some groups under
systematic threat of domination or deprivation: wrong without a wrongdoer.** Young, *Responsibility
for Justice* (2011). Shared with
[`philosophy-of-economics` 5.5](../philosophy-of-economics/lessons/05-05-hayek-knowledge-order-and-the-mirage-of-social-justice.md),
where it answers Hayek's claim that injustice needs a wrongdoer; here the question is responsibility
([social connection model](#social-connection-model)).

*Lessons:* [4.4](lessons/04-04-feminist-political-philosophy.md)

### Social connection model

**Responsibility for structural injustice falls on everyone who sustains the process, forward-looking
and shared.** Young (2011).

| | Liability model | Social connection model |
|---|---|---|
| Who | isolates the agents whose acts caused the harm | not isolating: all who sustain the process |
| When | backward-looking: blame for a past act | forward-looking: change what comes next |
| Background | treated as normal | judged itself |
| Distribution | shifts: blaming one excuses others | shared: no one is excused |
| Discharged by | punishment or compensation | collective action only |

- **Parameters** grading what each agent owes: **power** over the process, **privilege** from it,
  **interest** in changing it (victims have a strong one), **collective ability** to act.
- **It does not replace liability;** it blames no one for the structure (political responsibility,
  not guilt).

*Lessons:* [4.4](lessons/04-04-feminist-political-philosophy.md)

## Democracy

### Instrumental epistemic and intrinsic justifications

**Three ways to defend democracy: by its results, by its accuracy, or as valuable in itself.**

| Kind | Claim | Who |
|---|---|---|
| Instrumental | good because of what it produces (protection of interests, peace, accountable rulers) | Sen, *Development as Freedom* (1999): no substantial famines in functioning democracies with a relatively free press |
| Epistemic | a special instrumental case: there is a procedure-independent correct answer and democracy is good at finding it | [Condorcet's jury theorem](#condorcet-jury-theorem) (1785); Aristotle's summation argument ([`history-of-political-thought` 1.4](../history-of-political-thought/lessons/01-04-aristotle-constitutions-citizens-and-the-polity.md); different mechanism) |
| Intrinsic (procedural) | an equal say is what equal standing looks like in collective decisions, whatever it produces | Waldron, *Law and Disagreement* (1999): greatest equal weight to each view compatible with deciding; Christiano, [public equality](#public-equality) |

- **The first two are hostages to empirical fact;** the intrinsic view is not, which is its strength
  and the epistocrat's target.
- **Any of the three shows at most that democracy is justified;** authority and obligation need a
  further step ([5.1](lessons/05-01-why-democracy.md) Watch out).

*Lessons:* [5.1](lessons/05-01-why-democracy.md)

### Public equality

**Given disagreement, fallibility and self-interested bias, equal treatment must be visible to each
citizen; an equal say realizes it.** Christiano, *The Constitution of Equality* (2008). Even a
benevolent expert who chose well would wrong us by deciding for us. Democratic authority ends where
a decision violates public equality itself (stripping some citizens of basic rights); where those
limits sit, and who enforces them, is [5.2](lessons/05-02-majority-rule-vs-rights-judicial-review.md). A second vote for test-passers publicly declares some
judgment counts more, which wrongs others even if outcomes improve and pass rates are equal.

*Lessons:* [5.1](lessons/05-01-why-democracy.md)

### Condorcet jury theorem

**If independent voters are each more likely right than wrong on a yes/no question, the chance the
majority is right rises toward 1 as the group grows; if each is less likely right, it falls toward
0.** Condorcet (1785). Proof and failures owned by [`social-choice`](../social-choice/syllabus.md)
(Module 5); here it is one premise. Formula: [jury theorem](#jury-theorem-binomial).

| Premise it needs | Kind |
|---|---|
| a correct answer exists ("the dam is safe" vs "the best tax policy") | conceptual and normative |
| competence $p>1/2$ (Brennan's P2 denies it) | empirical |
| independence given the truth | empirical |

- **It cuts both ways:** a large electorate of slightly-worse-than-chance voters is almost certainly
  wrong.
- **Correlation caps it:** a shared error source (common shock) caps the limit below 1, even when
  average competence exceeds a half; shared information sources are the rule in politics.
- **Rousseau's general will** is often read epistemically through it
  ([`history-of-political-thought` 4.4](../history-of-political-thought/lessons/04-04-rousseau-the-social-contract.md)).

*Lessons:* [5.1](lessons/05-01-why-democracy.md)

### Epistocracy

**Rule by the knowledgeable: where some arrangement would decide more competently, it should replace
universal-suffrage democracy.** Brennan, *Against Democracy* (2016). Voters are **hobbits**
(uninformed, uninterested), **hooligans** (informed but partisan fans) or **vulcans** (informed and
impartial); most are the first two. Forms: restricted suffrage, plural votes, expert veto,
government by simulated oracle. Ancestors: Plato's ship of state
([`history-of-political-thought` 1.2](../history-of-political-thought/lessons/01-02-plato-against-democracy-and-the-second-best-city.md)),
Mill's plural voting ([6.4](../history-of-political-thought/lessons/06-04-mill-representative-government.md)).

1. (Normative, the competence principle) It is unjust to impose high-stakes decisions by force when
   made incompetently or by an incompetent body.
2. (Empirical) Universal-suffrage electorates are frequently incompetent.
3. (Empirical, comparative) Some feasible epistocracy would decide more competently without
   outweighing costs.
4. (Normative) An equal say has no intrinsic value weighty enough to override 1.
5. (Normative, implicit) A more competent arrangement may rightly hold the power to decide.
6. C. Where 3 holds, replace universal suffrage with that epistocracy.

- **Premise critics attack: 5** (Estlund's expert/boss fallacy, [epistemic proceduralism](#epistemic-proceduralism)).
  Christiano attacks 4.
- **Brennan is cautious about C:** premise 3 is empirical and may prove false.

*Lessons:* [5.1](lessons/05-01-why-democracy.md)

### Epistemic proceduralism

**Democracy has authority because it is better than random at getting things right and is the best
among procedures acceptable to all qualified points of view, even when it errs.** Estlund,
*Democratic Authority* (2008).

| Tenet of epistocracy | Estlund |
|---|---|
| Truth: there are correct political answers | accepts |
| Knowledge: some know them better | accepts |
| Authority: knowing better gives a right to rule | denies: the expert/boss fallacy |

- **Qualified acceptability requirement:** coercive authority needs a justification every qualified
  (reasonable) point of view could accept. Also limits [normative consent](#normative-consent).
- **Objection:** "qualified" does all the work; set the bar for reasonable suspicion low and nothing
  passes, democracy included.

*Lessons:* [1.2](lessons/01-02-consent-actual-tacit-and-hypothetical.md), [5.1](lessons/05-01-why-democracy.md)

### Demographic objection

**The knowledge-qualified group will over-represent some races, classes or regions, and a reasonable
citizen could suspect undetected shared biases; so epistocracy fails qualified acceptability even if
more competent.** Estlund. Brennan's reply: control for demographics (government by simulated
oracle). Make pass rates equal across groups and the objection loses its foothold, while
Christiano's public-equality objection is untouched ([5.1](lessons/05-01-why-democracy.md) Example 2). The objection rests on how
reasonable suspicion is defined.

*Lessons:* [5.1](lessons/05-01-why-democracy.md)

### Counter-majoritarian difficulty

**When a court strikes down a statute, it overrides the people's actual representatives.** Bickel,
*The Least Dangerous Branch* (1962), named it in order to answer it (he defended review) but insisted
the defence be made. A question of legitimacy and authority (who may settle rights disputes), not of
which rulings are correct.

**Three supports for entrenching rights:** tyranny of the majority (Madison's majority faction,
[`history-of-political-thought` 5.2](../history-of-political-thought/lessons/05-02-the-federalist-faction-and-the-extended-republic.md);
Tocqueville, [6.3](../history-of-political-thought/lessons/06-03-tocqueville-tyranny-of-the-majority-and-soft-despotism.md));
[precommitment](#precommitment); the [moral reading](#moral-reading). Against them:
[Waldron](#waldrons-case-against-judicial-review).

*Lessons:* [5.2](lessons/05-02-majority-rule-vs-rights-judicial-review.md)

### Precommitment

**A people binds itself in a calm moment against passions it expects to feel, as Ulysses has himself
tied to the mast.** Elster, *Ulysses and the Sirens* (1979); doubted in *Ulysses Unbound* (2000):
framers bind *other* people (later generations), and often write in crisis, not calm.

*Lessons:* [5.2](lessons/05-02-majority-rule-vs-rights-judicial-review.md)

### Moral reading

**A constitution's abstract clauses (equal protection, free speech) state moral principles, so
applying them requires moral judgment, and courts are one institution charged with it.** Dworkin,
*Freedom's Law* (1996).

- **Paired with the constitutional conception of democracy** (later renamed the **partnership
  conception**) against the **majoritarian premise** (an outcome is democratic only if a majority
  would endorse it): self-government is collective action by free and equal partners, with
  preconditions (equal concern, a real say, independence in conscience). A correct ruling protecting
  them costs democracy nothing; the only loss is one vote on one question, borne equally.
- **Dworkin closes the justification-legitimacy gap** this way: a correct ruling that protects
  democratic conditions is legitimate for that reason ([5.2](lessons/05-02-majority-rule-vs-rights-judicial-review.md) Watch out).

*Lessons:* [5.2](lessons/05-02-majority-rule-vs-rights-judicial-review.md)

### Waldrons case against judicial review

**Where democracy and rights-commitment are in good order and people disagree in good faith about
rights, the fair way to settle the disagreement is the one that counts everyone equally.** Waldron,
"The Core of the Case Against Judicial Review" (*Yale Law Journal*, 2006). Target: **strong-form**
review only.

Four assumptions (all empirical): (1) democratic institutions in reasonably good order, with a
representative legislature elected by universal adult suffrage; (2) courts in reasonably good order;
(3) a general commitment to individual and minority rights; (4) persistent, substantial,
good-faith disagreement about rights. Then:

5. (Normative) A rights-settling institution can be defended by **outcome-related reasons** (gets
   rights right more often) or **process-related reasons** (decides fairly, whatever it decides).
6. (Empirical and conceptual) Outcome reasons do not clearly favour courts.
7. (Normative) Process reasons clearly favour the legislature: equal say respects citizens as equals.
8. C. Under 1-4, strong judicial review is not legitimate.

- **Premises critics attack: 6 and assumption 1's "reasonably good order"** (an empirical claim from
  the armchair; "good order" may assume the protections whose absence makes review attractive).
  Waldron: the claim is comparative and modest; a broken legislature falls outside it. **Dworkin
  attacks 7** ([moral reading](#moral-reading)).
- **Where it stops:** a franchise excluding a minority fails assumption 1 (Ely, *Democracy and
  Distrust*, 1980: courts police the channels of political change, representation-reinforcement);
  a permanent minority that votes but never wins is where the framework gives no verdict.
- **"Circumstances of politics"** (*Law and Disagreement*, 1999): we need a common decision though we
  disagree; modelled on Rawls's [circumstances of justice](#circumstances-of-justice). Rights are among
  the things we disagree about.

*Lessons:* [5.2](lessons/05-02-majority-rule-vs-rights-judicial-review.md)

### Strong and weak judicial review

**Strong form: a court may decline to apply a statute or modify its effect, so the court has the last
word. Weak form (Tushnet): a court declares a statute incompatible with rights while the legislature
keeps the last word.** Examples of weak-form designs: declarations of incompatibility under the UK
Human Rights Act 1998, which leave the statute valid; Canada's Charter notwithstanding clause. Designs
compared in [`political-institutions`](../political-institutions/syllabus.md) [5.3](../political-institutions/lessons/05-03-judicial-review-compared.md)-[5.4](../political-institutions/lessons/05-04-weak-form-review-appointments-and-independence.md). Weak-form
review accepts Waldron's conclusion and offers a design that respects it; Dworkin's worry is that it
can leave the violation in place.

*Lessons:* [5.2](lessons/05-02-majority-rule-vs-rights-judicial-review.md)

### Aggregative democracy

**Preferences are fixed inputs and democracy is a fair machine for counting them equally.** Nobody asks
where preferences came from; fairness lies in counting each once. Labels used by Young, *Inclusion and
Democracy* (2000). A preference can be a moral judgment: the model is indifferent to content but does
not ask for or test reasons. Rousseau's *will of all* is the aggregative output, his *general will*
the deliberativist's hope ([`history-of-political-thought` 4.4](../history-of-political-thought/lessons/04-04-rousseau-the-social-contract.md)).

*Lessons:* [5.3](lessons/05-03-deliberative-vs-aggregative-democracy.md)

### Schumpeterian minimalism

**Democracy is a method in which would-be leaders win the power to decide through a competitive
struggle for votes; citizens choose, and can remove, who decides.** Schumpeter, *Capitalism, Socialism
and Democracy* (1942), against the "classical doctrine" (a definite popular will about the common
good). Often called **competitive elitism**. Deflationary, not anti-democratic: it values peaceful
turnover. Its reply to deliberativists: if citizens cannot form reasoned policy views (empirical),
legitimacy comes from authorization by a fair contested election (normative).

*Lessons:* [5.3](lessons/05-03-deliberative-vs-aggregative-democracy.md)

### Deliberative democracy

**Outcomes are democratically legitimate when they could be the object of free and reasoned agreement
among equals.** Joshua Cohen, "Deliberation and Democratic Legitimacy" (1989). The ideal procedure is:
**free** (bound only by the results of deliberation and its preconditions), **reasoned** (only the
force of reasons settles anything), **equal** (equal standing to propose, speak and vote), **aimed at
rational consensus** (majority vote as fallback). Habermas, *Between Facts and Norms* (1992; English
1996): the **discourse principle** (a norm is valid only if all affected could agree as participants
in rational discourse) and the two-track model (informal public sphere filtered into law by formal
bodies).

**Cohen's argument** ([5.3](lessons/05-03-deliberative-vs-aggregative-democracy.md)): (1) coercive power over free and equal citizens is legitimate only if
justifiable to them; (2) justifying means offering reasons she could accept, not showing more people
wanted it; (3) equals exchanging reasons and deciding by their force is the institutional expression
of mutual justification; (4) a bare count justifies nothing to the outvoted beyond "there were more
of them"; C: outcomes are legitimate to the extent they result from such deliberation, aggregation
only as fallback.

- **Premise critics attack: 3,** the bridge from ideal to actual: real deliberation is unequal and
  polarizing, and with majority fallback the verdicts collapse into aggregation. Reply: the ideal is
  a standard for criticizing institutions, and a vote after reasons were demanded is better justified
  to the outvoted. Schumpeter attacks 1 at the level of mass politics.
- **Consensus is the aim, not a requirement.**
- **Rawls** described a well-ordered constitutional democracy as deliberative (1997); Cohen's
  "reasoned" condition borrows [public reason](#public-reason).
- **It shows the decision's legitimacy, not an obligation to obey it** ([5.3](lessons/05-03-deliberative-vs-aggregative-democracy.md) Example 1).
- **Dryzek and List (2003):** deliberation may induce [single-peakedness](#domain-restriction).

*Lessons:* [5.3](lessons/05-03-deliberative-vs-aggregative-democracy.md), [5.4](lessons/05-04-does-social-choice-wound-democracy.md)

### Group polarization

**Members of a deliberating group predictably move toward a more extreme position in the direction
they already leaned.** Sunstein, "The Law of Group Polarization" (*Journal of Political Philosophy*,
2002). Mechanisms: a lopsided argument pool in a like-minded group, and social comparison (shifting
to keep standing among peers).

- **Mutz** (*Hearing the Other Side*, 2006): exposure to opposing views, which deliberation needs,
  dampens participation; like-minded networks spur it.
- **Fishkin's deliberative polls** (random sample, balanced materials, moderated small groups, opinion
  measured before and after) are the counter-exhibit; critics say such conditions are rare.
- **Empirical claims about actual groups;** they attack the bridge premise, not Cohen's normative
  thesis. A shift's direction says nothing about its accuracy, but calling it learning needs an
  independent standard of correctness (the epistemic question of [5.1](lessons/05-01-why-democracy.md)).

*Lessons:* [5.3](lessons/05-03-deliberative-vs-aggregative-democracy.md)

### Condorcet cycle

**Pairwise majority preference loops ($x$ beats $y$, $y$ beats $z$, $z$ beats $x$) though every voter's
ranking is transitive; then there is no Condorcet winner (an option beating every other pairwise).**
Each faction is rational; the majority is not. That cycles *can* occur is formal; how *often* is
empirical. Halden's council ([5.4](lessons/05-04-does-social-choice-wound-democracy.md)):

| Faction | Members | Ranking |
|---|---|---|
| Builders | 5 | $H \succ B \succ P \succ S$ |
| Commerce | 4 | $B \succ P \succ S \succ H$ |
| Greens | 3 | $P \succ S \succ H \succ B$ |

$H$ beats $B$ 8-4, $B$ beats $P$ 9-3, $P$ beats $H$ 7-5: a cycle. With two options May's theorem (1952,
[`social-choice`](../social-choice/syllabus.md)) singles out majority rule; trouble starts at three.
Counting: [Condorcet winner and Borda](#condorcet-winner-and-borda-count).

*Lessons:* [5.4](lessons/05-04-does-social-choice-wound-democracy.md)

### Arrows theorem

**With three or more options, no rule turning individual rankings into a social ranking satisfies all
five conditions.** Arrow, *Social Choice and Individual Values* (1951). Proof owned by
[`grad-game-theory` 5.1](../grad-game-theory/lessons/05-01-social-choice-impossibility.md); welfare
reading in [`grad-micro` 6.5](../grad-micro/lessons/06-05-social-choice-welfare.md).

| Condition | Means | Majority rule | Borda |
|---|---|---|---|
| Unrestricted domain | works for every profile | keeps | keeps |
| Weak Pareto | if all prefer $x$ to $y$, so does society | keeps | keeps |
| IIA | society's $x$ vs $y$ verdict depends only on individual $x$ vs $y$ rankings | keeps | **drops** |
| Non-dictatorship | no one's ranking always becomes society's | keeps | keeps |
| Transitivity | the social ranking never loops | **drops** | keeps |

- **Gibbard-Satterthwaite:** every non-dictatorial rule over three or more options can be gamed by
  strategic voting.
- **It shows no rule *guarantees* coherence on every profile,** not that voting usually produces
  incoherence.

*Lessons:* [5.4](lessons/05-04-does-social-choice-wound-democracy.md)

### Rikers argument

**If "the people" have no consistent preference to reveal, a democracy claiming to execute their will
executes nothing in particular.** Riker, *Liberalism against Populism* (1982). **Populism** (traced to
Rousseau): what the people want ought to be law, and voting reveals it. **Liberalism** (Madisonian):
periodic elections let voters remove officials.

1. (Conceptual) Populism needs a determinate collective preference prior to the vote.
2. (Formal) Majority preference can cycle; then the winner depends on procedure and agenda, not the
   profile alone.
3. (Formal) Every reasonable rule is open to strategic voting (Gibbard-Satterthwaite).
4. (Empirical) We see outcomes, not full profiles, so we cannot tell a Condorcet winner from an
   artefact of agenda control or manipulation; with two or more issue dimensions majority cycles can
   wander across the whole space (McKelvey, 1976).
5. (Conceptual) So no outcome can be identified as the popular will.
6. C. Populism is incoherent; liberal democracy survives, needing only an electorate able to say no.

| Reply | Attacks | Content |
|---|---|---|
| Mackie, *Democracy Defended* (2003) | premise 4 | Riker's case studies (a 1956 House school-aid vote, the run-up to the Civil War) rest on factual or interpretive mistakes; cycling, agenda control and manipulation not harmful, frequent or irremediable enough; Arrow's conditions, IIA especially, demand more than a democrat must |
| [Domain restriction](#domain-restriction) | reach of premise 2 | structured preferences cannot cycle |
| Proceduralism | premise 1 | outcomes are legitimate because the procedure was fair; democracy never claimed to reveal a prior will |

- **Weak points:** premise 4 and the move from "can" to "does" (Riker's defender: the premise is
  about what we can *know*); and the asymmetry: electing officials is also a choice among options, so
  why do the theorems spare liberalism?
- **Impartial culture risk:** [cycle probability](#condorcet-winner-and-borda-count).
- **A cycle bears on whether outcomes express a will,** not directly on legitimacy or obligation.

*Lessons:* [5.4](lessons/05-04-does-social-choice-wound-democracy.md)

### Domain restriction

**Dropping unrestricted domain: if preferences have structure, majority rule need not cycle.** If
options lie on one line and each voter's ranking has a single peak (Black, 1948), majority rule cannot
cycle and the median voter's favourite is the Condorcet winner; sufficient, not necessary, for one.
Dryzek and List (2003): deliberation can produce this structure by bringing voters to agree on the
issue's dimension while disagreeing about the answer. An empirical hypothesis about preferences, not a
rule excluding voters; if it fails, cycles return.

*Lessons:* [5.4](lessons/05-04-does-social-choice-wound-democracy.md)

### Agenda manipulation

**Under a cycle, sequential pairwise voting makes the winner depend on the order of votes, so whoever
sets the agenda can pick the outcome, even a Pareto-dominated one.** Halden ([5.4](lessons/05-04-does-social-choice-wound-democracy.md) Example 1):
order $B, P, H, S$ ends with the small park $S$ though all 12 members prefer the full park; across
all 24 orders each option wins under some agenda. Riker: the outcome is the chair's. Proceduralist:
was agenda power fairly allocated and checked? Every view counts a Pareto-dominated result as a
defect.

*Lessons:* [5.4](lessons/05-04-does-social-choice-wound-democracy.md)

### Cosmopolitanism

**Egalitarian distributive principles apply globally.** Everyone (statists included) accepts a duty
to aid (Singer, [`ethics` 1.4](../ethics/lessons/01-04-integrity-and-demandingness.md)); the dispute is
over *egalitarian* justice, which asks whether inequalities are justified, not only whether anyone is
destitute.

| Cosmopolitan | Argument |
|---|---|
| Beitz, *Political Theory and International Relations* (1979) | (1) natural resources are as arbitrarily distributed as talents, so a global original position adopts resource redistribution; (2) global trade and investment form a scheme of cooperation, so a global difference principle |
| Pogge, *World Poverty and Human Rights* (2002) | a *negative* duty not to impose an unjust institutional order; [resource and borrowing privileges](#resource-and-borrowing-privileges) |
| Caney, Tan (luck egalitarian) | birthplace is [brute luck](#brute-and-option-luck) par excellence |
| Catholic social teaching | universal destination of goods; solidarity (a firm commitment to the common good of all peoples) a duty under interdependence; *Populorum progressio* (1967), *Sollicitudo rei socialis* (1987) |

Placement is partly contested: Pogge's duty arises from a relation but protects rights everyone has;
Beitz's resource argument is closer to the non-relational side.

*Lessons:* [6.1](lessons/06-01-global-justice-cosmopolitans-and-statists.md)

### Statism

**Egalitarian distributive justice applies only within states; between them, a humanitarian minimum or
duty of assistance and fair bargaining.**

**Nagel's argument** ("The Problem of Global Justice", *Philosophy & Public Affairs*, 2005),
reconstructed in [6.1](lessons/06-01-global-justice-cosmopolitans-and-statists.md):

1. (Normative) Egalitarian standards are owed only within relations needing special justification to
   those inside them; beyond them, a humanitarian minimum.
2. (Normative) A sovereign state's coercive law is such a relation: imposed in the name of those it
   binds, who are treated as its co-authors.
3. (Empirical and conceptual) No global institution imposes coercive law in the name of all the
   world's people; treaties and trade bodies bind states, made by states bargaining.
4. C. Distributive justice stops at the border.

- **Blake** ("Distributive Justice, State Coercion, and Autonomy", *Philosophy & Public Affairs*,
  2001): drops co-authorship; coercion invades autonomy and only a state's legal system coerces across
  the whole of economic life (property, contract, tax).
- **Premises critics attack: 3** (Cohen and Sabel, 2006: the WTO makes rules binding people directly;
  Abizadeh: the border coerces outsiders) **and 1** (the non-relational cosmopolitan: why should a
  relation matter at all?).
- **The trigger is coercion claiming legitimacy** in the coerced's name, not raw power.
- **Rawls's version:** [duty of assistance](#duty-of-assistance).

*Lessons:* [6.1](lessons/06-01-global-justice-cosmopolitans-and-statists.md)

### Duty of assistance

**Well-ordered peoples owe help to burdened societies (lacking the institutions, culture or resources to
be well-ordered), with a target and a cut-off.** Rawls, *The Law of Peoples* (1999): principles between
societies are chosen by representatives of peoples, not individuals. Target: helping them manage their
own affairs justly or decently; once reached, the duty ends, however poor the society stays. Rawls
rejects a global difference principle because peoples decide differently about saving, education and
population, and transfers would override those decisions; and because (empirical premise) political
culture, more than resources, determines a people's wealth.

*Lessons:* [6.1](lessons/06-01-global-justice-cosmopolitans-and-statists.md)

### Resource and borrowing privileges

**Global rules that let whoever holds effective power over a territory sell its resources with good legal
title (the international resource privilege) and borrow in the country's name, binding later
governments (the borrowing privilege).** Pogge. They reward seizing power and foreseeably sustain severe
poverty; upholding them is taking part in harm, not failing to help. "The global order causes poverty"
is the empirical layer.

*Lessons:* [6.1](lessons/06-01-global-justice-cosmopolitans-and-statists.md)

### Relational and non-relational accounts

**What triggers duties of distributive justice: shared humanity, or a special relation that may or may not
exist globally?** The cosmopolitan-statist crux.

| Account | Trigger | Who |
|---|---|---|
| Non-relational | shared humanity; birthplace is morally arbitrary | Caney, Tan; Singer for the floor |
| Relational: cooperation | a scheme of social cooperation | Beitz |
| Relational: coercion | coercive law imposed in one's name | Nagel, Blake |
| Relational: imposition | an order imposed on others | Pogge |

- **A second dispute inside the relational family:** whether the relation in fact holds across borders.
  Beitz and Nagel are both relationalists who disagree about the facts and which relation counts.
- **Objections to statist relationalism:** Cohen and Sabel (2006, WTO rule-making); Abizadeh (border
  coercion).
- **Empirical and normative layers:** the facts decide how each view applies, not which trigger is right.

*Lessons:* [6.1](lessons/06-01-global-justice-cosmopolitans-and-statists.md)

### Feudal-privilege analogy

**Citizenship in a rich democracy is inherited status that fixes life chances and is enforced against
outsiders, like feudal privilege; so entry restrictions need the reasons any restriction of a basic
liberty needs (chiefly public order).** Carens, "Aliens and Citizens: The Case for Open Borders" (*Review
of Politics*, 1987).

1. (Normative) Persons are moral equals; a just order may not distribute life chances on morally
   arbitrary facts.
2. (Conceptual) Place of birth, and so citizenship, is morally arbitrary.
3. (Empirical) Citizenship in a rich democracy greatly enhances life chances.
4. (Normative) Inherited status fixing life chances and enforced against outsiders is feudal privilege.
5. C. Entry restrictions are justified only by public-order-type reasons.

- **Three routes to the same verdict:** Nozick (the state may not block a voluntary deal between a
  citizen and a foreigner, [2.4](lessons/02-04-nozick-entitlement-and-the-challenge-to-patterns.md)); a global original position ([2.2](lessons/02-02-rawls-the-original-position.md)); utilitarianism (migrants'
  gains weigh heavily).
- **Premise critics attack: 2 against 4:** unchosen membership is not thereby unjust; family ties are
  unchosen and still ground special duties.

*Lessons:* [6.2](lessons/06-02-immigration-may-a-state-exclude.md)

### Freedom-of-association argument

**A legitimate state may refuse to associate with would-be immigrants, as a person may refuse to marry.**
Wellman, "Immigration and Freedom of Association" (*Ethics*, 2008): (1) legitimate (rights-protecting)
states are entitled to self-determination; (2) freedom of association is part of it; (3) it includes the
right not to associate; C: a legitimate state may exclude, even those who would gain a great deal.

- **Refugees:** urgent claims, but a state may sometimes discharge the duty by helping where they are.
- **Premise critics attack: 3 as applied to states:** marriage is voluntary on both sides, citizens are
  born into the state, and exclusion also restricts insiders who want to hire, house or marry foreigners.
- **"Strong" only on the default:** Wellman still owes refugees rescue.

*Lessons:* [6.2](lessons/06-02-immigration-may-a-state-exclude.md)

### National self-determination

**A nation has a legitimate interest in shaping its public culture and future, including its size and
composition.** Miller, *Strangers in Our Midst* (2016).

- **Weak cosmopolitanism:** equal moral worth, not equal treatment; outsiders are owed their human
  rights, not membership.
- **No human right to immigrate:** free movement is protected as a human right only as far as needed for
  an adequate range of options, which a decent state can supply at home.
- **Refugees are special:** under the 1951 Refugee Convention, a person outside her country of
  nationality with a well-founded fear of persecution for race, religion, nationality, membership of a
  particular social group or political opinion. Her rights cannot be secured at home, so her claim to be
  taken in is strong, shared among states. No racist admission criteria.
- **Catholic social teaching** (qualified control): *Pacem in terris* (1963), a natural right to
  emigrate and settle elsewhere for just reasons; the Catechism pairs prosperous nations' duty to
  welcome, as far as able, with public authority's right to set conditions for the common good and the
  immigrant's duty to respect the host's heritage and laws. Numbers are a prudential judgment.

*Lessons:* [6.2](lessons/06-02-immigration-may-a-state-exclude.md)

### Border coercion argument

**If coercion must be justified to those it coerces, border control coerces outsiders, so a democratic state
has no right to set it unilaterally; outsiders are owed a say.** Abizadeh, "Democratic Theory and Border
Coercion" (*Political Theory*, 2008), turning 6.1's statist trigger around. A conclusion about *who
decides*, not how open the border must be; it presses that the state's legitimacy claim over outsiders
has not been justified to them.

*Lessons:* [6.1](lessons/06-01-global-justice-cosmopolitans-and-statists.md), [6.2](lessons/06-02-immigration-may-a-state-exclude.md)

### Exit and entry asymmetry

**The Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948, Art. 13) recognizes a right to leave any country, but
no right to enter any country but one's own.** Cole (*Philosophies of Exclusion*, 2000): incoherent, a
right to stand at the border. Wellman's reply: the marriage asymmetry; the right to exit is against your
own state's coercion (no Iron Curtain), satisfied if *some* state takes you. Where it strains: a
stateless person no state admits, the [particularity problem](#particularity-problem) in reverse (a
general duty with no particular bearer).

*Lessons:* [6.2](lessons/06-02-immigration-may-a-state-exclude.md)

### Rule and exemption

**A general law with a carve-out for those whose religion or culture it burdens, vs a uniform rule.** An
exemption claim usually concedes the law's legitimacy and denies only that authority reaches the
claimant (contrast [civil disobedience](#civil-disobedience)).

**Barry's case for the uniform rule** (*Culture and Equality*, 2001), reconstructed in [6.3](lessons/06-03-conscience-and-exemptions.md):

1. (Normative) Equal treatment means the same rules for all; fairness concerns opportunities, not
   willingness to use them.
2. (Conceptual) A law forbidding an act to everyone leaves the same opportunity set; beliefs affect
   willingness, not opportunity.
3. (Normative) People bear the consequences of their beliefs and practices.
4. Either the rule's justification outweighs the objectors' burden or it does not.
5. If it does, it should bind everyone.
6. If not, it is too restrictive for everyone and should be dropped for all.
7. C. Rule and exemption is never required by justice; exemptions at most from prudence or generosity.

- **Targets:** Sikhs exempted from motorcycle crash helmets; Jewish and Muslim slaughter exempted from
  stunning.
- **Premise critics attack: 2** (Kymlicka: "the same rules" are written around the majority's language,
  holidays and practices; the Sikh loses the road while the majority loses nothing it values). Barry's
  reply: every law frustrates someone's deep commitments; measuring costs by belief leaves no stable
  baseline, and supports Leiter's parity.
- **Barry grants unequal impact;** his claim is that unequal *impact* is not unequal *treatment*.

*Lessons:* [6.3](lessons/06-03-conscience-and-exemptions.md)

### External protections and internal restrictions

**External protections are a group's claims against the wider society that reduce its vulnerability to
outside decisions (endorsed); internal restrictions are its claims against its own members that limit
their liberty in the name of tradition (rejected).** Kymlicka, *Multicultural Citizenship* (1995).
*Polyethnic rights* include exemptions from generally applicable laws, offsetting majority-built rules.
The hard case is a claim that is both at once (the Ostra Brethren's early school-leaving: protection
against assimilation, restriction on children's knowledge of exit, [6.3](lessons/06-03-conscience-and-exemptions.md) Example 2).

*Lessons:* [6.3](lessons/06-03-conscience-and-exemptions.md)

### Internal minorities

**Members of a minority group (often women, girls, children) who bear the costs of its practices and of
group rights.** Okin, *Is Multiculturalism Bad for Women?* (1999 volume): group rights strengthen whoever
controls a group's norms, often older men, and costs fall on those least able to leave. Kymlicka's test
helps only if one can tell which claims restrict members. Rawls's reply to Okin ([4.4](lessons/04-04-feminist-political-philosophy.md)) relies on
exit, which is what the contested schooling teaches.

*Lessons:* [6.3](lessons/06-03-conscience-and-exemptions.md)

### No burden-shifting principle

**Exemptions for conscience, religious or secular alike, are permissible only if they shift no burdens
onto others.** Leiter, *Why Tolerate Religion?* (2013). Religion's distinctive features (categorical
demands; insulation from evidence) give the state no reason to respect it more than secular conscience,
which can also issue categorical demands; so toleration attaches to conscience generally, and since no
state can exempt every objector, the burden test limits it. Conscription: exemption passes only with
alternative service of comparable burden; it equalizes burden, not risk, and the principle does not say
whether that suffices.

*Lessons:* [6.3](lessons/06-03-conscience-and-exemptions.md)

### Integrity-protecting commitments

**Practices or refusals, religious or secular, that let a person act in line with her deepest
commitments.** Laborde, *Liberalism's Religion* (2017): disaggregate "religion" into the interests law
protects; for exemptions the interest is integrity. Exemption owed when such a commitment and a vital
life project are both at stake, limited by third-party costs and vulnerable members' rights. Sikh
turban: exemption from construction hard-hat rules (a livelihood), not from motorcycle helmets.
Leiter and Laborde both deny religion as such deserves special treatment; they differ in what they
protect and how much room they leave.

*Lessons:* [6.3](lessons/06-03-conscience-and-exemptions.md)

### Religious liberty as immunity from coercion

**Every person has a right to immunity from coercion in religious matters: no one may be forced to act
against his convictions, or prevented from acting on them, privately or publicly, alone or with others,
within due limits.** *Dignitatis humanae* (1965).

| Feature | Content |
|---|---|
| Ground | the dignity of the person, known by reason and revelation |
| Reach | persists in someone who does not seek the truth |
| Form | to be a civil right in constitutional law |
| Limit | just public order: the rights of all citizens, public peace, public morality |
| Not | a claim that all religions are equally true |

- **One position,** taught from within in [`catholic-social-teaching`](../catholic-social-teaching/syllabus.md);
  why conscience binds its holder is [`moral-theology` 2.5](../moral-theology/lessons/02-05-the-primacy-of-conscience-rightly-understood.md).
- **Same verdict as the veil** on liberty of conscience, by a different route ([2.2](lessons/02-02-rawls-the-original-position.md)).
- **Answers yes** to "is the interest special to religion?", a claim about religion's distinctive good,
  not that secular conscience counts for nothing.
- **A limit on the common good** ([4.3](lessons/04-03-perfectionism-and-the-common-good.md)).

*Lessons:* [2.2](lessons/02-02-rawls-the-original-position.md), [4.3](lessons/04-03-perfectionism-and-the-common-good.md), [6.3](lessons/06-03-conscience-and-exemptions.md)

### Property-owning democracy

**Disperse capital and human capital at the start of each period, so citizens manage their own affairs
rather than be cared for.** Rawls, *Justice as Fairness: A Restatement* (2001); idea and name taken from
James Meade.

| Regime | Realizes the two principles? |
|---|---|
| Laissez-faire capitalism | no |
| Welfare-state capitalism | no: a minimum after the fact, but concentrated ownership buys political influence and erodes the fair value of the political liberties |
| State socialism with a command economy | no |
| Property-owning democracy | yes |
| Liberal (democratic) socialism | yes |

*Lessons:* [6.4](lessons/06-04-welfare-property-and-subsidiarity.md)

### Basic income

**An unconditional individual grant, with no work test, at the highest sustainable level.**

| Version | Ground |
|---|---|
| Van Parijs, *Real Freedom for All* (1995) | real freedom (the means, not just the right); jobs are scarce assets paying rents, and taxing them for an equal grant shares a common asset |
| Van Parijs, "Why Surfers Should Be Fed" (*Philosophy & Public Affairs*, 1991) | against Rawls's Malibu surfers remark (repeated in *Political Liberalism*, 1993): a neutral state may not favour those who prefer work over the waves |
| Pettit, "A Republican Right to Basic Income?" (*Basic Income Studies*, 2007) | [non-domination](#freedom-as-non-domination): the power to say no removes an employer's arbitrary will |

**The argument** ([6.4](lessons/06-04-welfare-property-and-subsidiarity.md)): (1) a just society secures real freedom to pursue one's good, including to
refuse a job; (2) a liberal state must not favour one conception of the good; (3) any work test favours
some conceptions and leaves subsistence at employers' and officials' discretion; (4) a sustainable grant
is possible; C: an unconditional basic income at the highest sustainable level. **Premise critics
attack: 2 applied to work:** "contribute if you can" is fairness among cooperators, not a view of the good
([fair play](#principle-of-fairness)). Defender: heirs and landowners already live on unearned income.

- **Budget:** [budget identity](#basic-income-budget-identity); a linear tax *is* a basic income with a
  demogrant ([`public-economics` 5.1](../public-economics/lessons/05-01-the-linear-income-tax.md)). The
  identity settles what can be paid, not who deserves it.
- **Libertarian objection:** [taxation as forced labor](#taxation-as-forced-labor).

*Lessons:* [6.4](lessons/06-04-welfare-property-and-subsidiarity.md)

### Reciprocity objection

**Whoever willingly takes a share of the social product owes a matching contribution, if able, given a fair
background; an unconditional grant lets some live on others' labour.** Stuart White, *The Civic Minimum*
(2003): "fair reciprocity"; contribution includes care. [2.6](lessons/02-06-luck-relations-priority-and-sufficiency.md)'s harshness debate turned around: there
critics charged luck egalitarians with abandoning the imprudent; here they charge basic income with
ignoring a duty the able owe.

- **Odile and Rafe** ([6.4](lessons/06-04-welfare-property-and-subsidiarity.md) Example 2): care is civic labour, so the dispute moves to what counts.
- **Participation income** (a grant conditional on work, care, study or volunteering) is the compromise;
  critics: either so wide it excludes almost no one (an expensive work test) or narrow enough to bring
  back officials' discretion, the domination the republican wanted removed.

*Lessons:* [6.4](lessons/06-04-welfare-property-and-subsidiarity.md)

### Subsidiarity

**What smaller and lower bodies can do should not be absorbed by larger and higher ones, and the higher
body owes them help (*subsidium*).** *Quadragesimo Anno* (1931), paraphrased; taught from within at
[`catholic-social-teaching`](../catholic-social-teaching/syllabus.md) 3.3.

- **Binds both ways:** a restraint and an obligation on the state; not a presumption for small
  government. Withdrawing help in its name misreads it.
- **Family and intermediate bodies are first providers, the state the guarantor;** widely spread
  ownership is the tradition's long-standing preference.
- **Tocqueville's "tutelary" power** ([`history-of-political-thought` 6.3](../history-of-political-thought/lessons/06-03-tocqueville-tyranny-of-the-majority-and-soft-despotism.md))
  is a family resemblance, not the same doctrine.
- **A limit on the [common good](#common-good)** ([4.3](lessons/04-03-perfectionism-and-the-common-good.md)).

*Lessons:* [4.3](lessons/04-03-perfectionism-and-the-common-good.md), [6.4](lessons/06-04-welfare-property-and-subsidiarity.md)

### Universal destination of goods

**The earth's goods are meant for all; private property is the ordinary, legitimate means to that end, not

## Formulas and arithmetic

Every computation the lessons run, with symbols defined and a worked number to check against. The
formal parts are light; the arithmetic never settles the normative question it illustrates.

### Utilitarian transfer with a leak

**Two people, richer R and poorer P, with incomes $y_R > y_P$ and log utility $u(c)=\ln c$ (so marginal
utility $u'(c)=1/c$).** A transfer $t$ is taken from R; a fraction $\lambda$ (the
[leaky bucket](#leaky-bucket)) is lost, so P receives $(1-\lambda)t$. The utilitarian sum is

$$W(t)=\ln(y_R-t)+\ln\big(y_P+(1-\lambda)t\big).$$

Setting $W'(t)=0$:

$$t^*=\frac{(1-\lambda)\,y_R-y_P}{2(1-\lambda)}.$$

$$\frac{c_P}{c_R}=1-\lambda .$$

In words: the utilitarian stops when the poorer person has $1-\lambda$ times the richer person's
income, and transfers nothing once $\lambda \ge 1-y_P/y_R$.

| $y_R$, $y_P$ (thousands) | $\lambda$ | $t^*$ | Final incomes |
|---|---|---|---|
| 120, 30 | 0 | 45 | 75, 75 (full equality) |
| 120, 30 | 0.25 | $60/1.5=40$ | 80, 60 (ratio 0.75) |
| 120, 30 | $\ge 0.75$ | 0 | 120, 30 |

The first thousand moved gains about $1/30-1/120=0.025$ units. A rise in the sum is not a Pareto
improvement: R loses. *From* [2.1](lessons/02-01-utilitarianism-as-a-principle-for-institutions.md)

### Lerners equal division under ignorance

**If the state cannot tell who converts income into well-being better, the expected sum is maximized by
equal division.** Split 100 between two people; one has $u=2\ln c$, the other $u=\ln c$, each assignment
equally likely. For a split $(x, 100-x)$ the expected sum is

$$1.5\,[\ln x+\ln(100-x)],$$

maximized at $x=50$. Knowing who is who, the state maximizes $2\ln x+\ln(100-x)$: $2/x=1/(100-x)$, so the
better converter gets $x=66.67$. The same move as Harsanyi's
[equiprobability model](#equiprobability-model). *From* [2.1](lessons/02-01-utilitarianism-as-a-principle-for-institutions.md), [2.3](lessons/02-03-rawls-the-two-principles-and-maximin.md)

### Maximin expected utility and sum on a table

**A table of schemes (rows) by positions (columns), each position with population share $s_j$
($\sum_j s_j=1$) and a primary-goods index $x_j$.**

| Rule | Picks the scheme with the highest | Formula |
|---|---|---|
| Difference principle (maximin) | worst-off position's index | $\min_j x_j$ |
| Leximin tie-break | if minima tie, the next-worst position, and so on | |
| Average utility (Harsanyi's expected utility with equiprobability) | population-weighted average | $\bar x=\sum_j s_j x_j$ |
| Utilitarian sum | total; with $N$ people, $N\bar x$, so it ranks like the average for a fixed population | $\sum_i x_i$ |

Maximin is insensitive to how many occupy the worst position; the average is not. Weight by population
shares, not by columns (unweighted averages are a standard wrong turn).

Corvel, shares 40, 30, 20, 10 percent (worst to best) ([2.3](lessons/02-03-rawls-the-two-principles-and-maximin.md)):

| Scheme | Indices | Minimum | Average |
|---|---|---|---|
| E (flat) | 20, 20, 20, 20 | 20 | 20 |
| I (incentive) | 22, 26, 34, 70 | 22 | $0.4(22)+0.3(26)+0.2(34)+0.1(70)=30.4$ |
| C (Cohen) | 28, 28, 34, 40 | 28 | 30.4 |

The difference principle prefers I to E (it *requires* the 70), and C to I. Cohen's arithmetic: if
professionals would work at 40, freeing $0.1\times(70-40)=3$ per head lifts the bottom two to 28, since

$$0.4\times(28-22)+0.3\times(28-26)=3.$$

*From* [2.3](lessons/02-03-rawls-the-two-principles-and-maximin.md)

### Envy-freeness

**Allocation $(x_1,\dots,x_n)$ is envy-free if every person values her own bundle at least as highly as
anyone else's, by her own utility function $u_i$:**

$$u_i(x_i)\ \ge\ u_i(x_j)\quad\text{for all } i, j.$$

Ines $u_I=2g+w$ and Lucien $u_L=\tfrac12 g+w$ share 12 grain ($g$) and 12 wine ($w$) ([2.5](lessons/02-05-equality-of-what.md)):

| Allocation | Ines: own vs other's | Lucien: own vs other's | Envy-free? |
|---|---|---|---|
| A: Ines $(10,2)$, Lucien $(2,10)$ | 22 vs 14 | 11 vs 7 | yes |
| W (equal welfare): Ines $(7.2,0)$, Lucien $(4.8,12)$ | 14.4 vs 21.6 | 14.4 vs 3.6 | no: Ines envies |

W solves $2g=\tfrac12(12-g)+12$, so $5g=36$, $g=7.2$. Equal welfare costs Ines 7.6 points to fund a
cultivated taste. *From* [2.5](lessons/02-05-equality-of-what.md)

### Fair insurance premium

**The actuarially fair premium for cover $C$ against an event of probability $p$:**

$$\text{premium}=p\times C.$$

Dworkin's handicap insurance ([2.5](lessons/02-05-equality-of-what.md)): $p=1/20$, $C=30{,}000$ dollars a year, so the premium is 1,500
dollars. In a town of 1,000, the pool balances: $50\times 30{,}000=1{,}000\times 1{,}500$. Maya's extra
cost is 40,000, so she remains 10,000 short. *From* [2.5](lessons/02-05-equality-of-what.md)

### Prioritarian and sufficientarian rankings

**Prioritarian value $W=\sum_i f(u_i)$ with $f$ increasing and concave; the priority weight of one more
unit to person $i$ is $f'(u_i)$.** With $f(u)=\sqrt u$:

$$f'(u)=\frac{1}{2\sqrt u}.$$

A unit at $u=4$ weighs $0.25$; at $u=100$, $0.05$ (five times less). **Sufficientarian:** count (or
weight) only shortfalls below a threshold $T$.

Leveling down, $A=(25,64,100)$ to $L=(25,25,25)$, $T=20$ ([2.6](lessons/02-06-luck-relations-priority-and-sufficiency.md)):

| View | $A$ | $L$ | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| Strict equality (range) | 75 | 0 | $L$ better |
| Utilitarian sum | 189 | 75 | $A$ better |
| Prioritarian $\sum\sqrt u$ | $5+8+10=23$ | 15 | $A$ better |
| Sufficiency: number below $T$ | 0 | 0 | indifferent |

Leveling down lowers terms and raises none, so it can only lower $W$. *From* [2.6](lessons/02-06-luck-relations-priority-and-sufficiency.md)

### Jury theorem binomial

**$n$ voters ($n$ odd), each independently right with probability $p$ on a yes/no question; the majority
is right with probability**

$$P_n=\sum_{k=(n+1)/2}^{n}\binom{n}{k}p^k(1-p)^{n-k}.$$

Add the chances of every outcome with more than half right. If $p>1/2$, $P_n\to1$; if $p<1/2$, $P_n\to0$.
For $n=3$:

$$P_3=p^3+3p^2(1-p).$$

| $p$ | $P_3$ | $P_{11}$ | $P_{101}$ | $P_{1001}$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.55 | 0.575 | 0.633 | 0.844 | 0.9992 |
| 0.45 | 0.425 | 0.367 | 0.156 | 0.0008 |

**Common shock** (correlation): with probability $q$ everyone's competence drops below a half, otherwise
it is above. For large $n$ the majority is almost surely right in the good world and wrong in the bad,
so

$$P_\infty=1-q.$$

Brask ([5.1](lessons/05-01-why-democracy.md)): $q=0.1$, competence 0.45 or 0.55 (average 0.54), $P_\infty=0.9$; at $n=1001$, 0.899
against 0.994 for independent voters at $p=0.54$. *From* [5.1](lessons/05-01-why-democracy.md)

### Condorcet winner and Borda count

**A Condorcet winner beats every other option in pairwise majority votes; a cycle means there is none.**
To check: for each pair $(x,y)$, count the voters ranking $x$ above $y$.

**Borda count** with $m$ options: a voter's first place earns $m-1$ points, second $m-2$, down to 0 for
last; the highest total wins. Totals sum to (number of voters) $\times\, m(m-1)/2$. Borda keeps
transitivity and drops IIA: moving a third option between $x$ and $y$ on some ballots can reverse $x$
vs $y$ though no one's $x$-vs-$y$ ranking changed ([5.4](lessons/05-04-does-social-choice-wound-democracy.md) P1). A Borda winner need not be the
Condorcet winner, nor the majority's first choice.

| Case | Pairwise | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Halden (5, 4, 3 members; [cycle](#condorcet-cycle)) | $H$ beats $B$ 8-4; $B$ beats $P$ 9-3; $P$ beats $H$ 7-5 | no Condorcet winner; Borda (3, 2, 1, 0): $H$ 18, $B$ 22, $P$ 22, $S$ 10 (total $72=12\times6$) |
| Varden survey (1,000 voters) | $C$ beats $N$ 600-400; $C$ beats $I$ 650-350; $N$ beats $I$ 510-490 | Condorcet winner $C$; order $C\succ N\succ I$ |

**Impartial culture** (all six rankings of three options equally likely), probability of no Condorcet
winner: $12/216\approx5.6\%$ with three voters; in a large electorate

$$\tfrac14-\tfrac{3}{2\pi}\arcsin\tfrac13\approx 8.8\%,$$

rising toward certainty as options multiply. A model, not data. *From* [5.4](lessons/05-04-does-social-choice-wound-democracy.md)

### Basic income budget identity

**A flat tax at rate $t$ on every adult's income funds other spending $g$ per adult and an equal grant
$B$; with average income $\bar y$ the budget balances when**

$$B=t\bar y-g.$$

A person with income $y$ gains on net $B-ty$, so she gains exactly when

$$y<\frac{B}{t}.$$

Example ([6.4](lessons/06-04-welfare-property-and-subsidiarity.md)): $t=0.25$, $\bar y=48{,}000$, $g=4{,}000$ give $B=8{,}000$ dollars and break-even
income $32{,}000$. Holds earnings fixed; behavioural responses and the optimal rate are
[`public-economics` 5.1](../public-economics/lessons/05-01-the-linear-income-tax.md). The identity
settles what can be paid, not who deserves it. *From* [6.4](lessons/06-04-welfare-property-and-subsidiarity.md)

## Pitfalls

The Watch-out traps of every lesson, deduplicated and grouped by theme.

### Pitfalls on the four concepts

- **"Legitimate, so citizens must obey."** That is the disputed inference; Edmundson and Applbaum
  separate legitimacy from a duty to obey. *([1.1](lessons/01-01-the-problem-of-political-authority.md))*
- **"No consent, so no right to rule."** A failed consent theory shows at most that consent grounds no
  general obligation; the state may be justified or have a right to coerce on other grounds. *([1.2](lessons/01-02-consent-actual-tacit-and-hypothetical.md))*
- **"Fair play gives the state authority."** At most an obligation, owed to fellow cooperators, covering
  the scheme's rules. *([1.3](lessons/01-03-fair-play-gratitude-and-associative-obligation.md))*
- **"The original position (or a just basic structure, or the sum maximized) shows we must obey."** It
  justifies principles or arrangements; legitimacy and obligation are further steps (Rawls's bridge is
  the natural duty). *([2.1](lessons/02-01-utilitarianism-as-a-principle-for-institutions.md), [2.2](lessons/02-02-rawls-the-original-position.md), [2.3](lessons/02-03-rawls-the-two-principles-and-maximin.md), [2.5](lessons/02-05-equality-of-what.md), [2.6](lessons/02-06-luck-relations-priority-and-sufficiency.md))*
- **"Taxation is forced labor, so I need not pay."** A claim about legitimacy, not obligation.
  *([2.4](lessons/02-04-nozick-entitlement-and-the-challenge-to-patterns.md), [6.4](lessons/06-04-welfare-property-and-subsidiarity.md))*
- **"A legitimate law cannot reduce freedom" / "it reduces freedom, so it is illegitimate."** On
  negative liberty every law reduces someone's liberty; only the republican links the two (non-arbitrary
  law conditions freedom). *([3.1](lessons/03-01-three-concepts-of-liberty.md))*
- **"It passes the harm principle, so it has authority."** The principle filters grounds for power; it
  says nothing about the enactor's right to rule or your duty to obey. A wrong ground does not make the
  state illegitimate either. *([3.2](lessons/03-02-mills-harm-principle.md), [3.3](lessons/03-03-paternalism-and-legal-moralism.md))*
- **"A justified speech ban is legitimately enacted."** Dworkin's argument lives in the gap. *([3.4](lessons/03-04-free-speech.md))*
- **"The liberal principle of legitimacy says when a law is just" or "gives a duty to obey."** A weaker
  standard than justice, about the right to coerce on constitutional essentials. A breach of the duty of
  civility is a moral fault, not by itself illegitimacy. *([4.1](lessons/04-01-political-liberalism.md), [4.2](lessons/04-02-public-reason-and-religious-argument.md))*
- **"Democracy tracks the truth, so its verdicts bind."** Reliability is a reason to believe, not
  authority (Estlund's expert/boss fallacy turned on the majority). *([5.1](lessons/05-01-why-democracy.md))*
- **"The ruling was correct, so the court was entitled to make it."** Outcome reasons justify; the
  court's right to impose is legitimacy. *([5.2](lessons/05-02-majority-rule-vs-rights-judicial-review.md))*
- **"A better-justified deliberative outcome obliges obedience"; "a cycle makes the law illegitimate."**
  The first shows the decision's legitimacy only; Riker's conclusion is conceptual, about a popular
  will. *([5.3](lessons/05-03-deliberative-vs-aggregative-democracy.md), [5.4](lessons/05-04-does-social-choice-wound-democracy.md))*
- **"Nagel's trigger is raw power"; "a right to exclude settles whether outsiders must comply."** The
  trigger is coercion claiming legitimacy; excluding is a legitimacy claim, the migrant's duty a further
  one. *([6.1](lessons/06-01-global-justice-cosmopolitans-and-statists.md), [6.2](lessons/06-02-immigration-may-a-state-exclude.md))*
- **"An exemption claim attacks the law's legitimacy."** It usually concedes it and denies only that
  authority reaches the claimant. *([6.3](lessons/06-03-conscience-and-exemptions.md))*

### Pitfalls on kinds of claim

- **"Citizens accept the government, so it is legitimate."** Acceptance is Weber's empirical belief.
  *([1.1](lessons/01-01-the-problem-of-political-authority.md))*
- **"Hume's ship is about what people believe."** It is about the cost of exit; the conceptual objection
  (no sense of choosing) rests on a different empirical premise. *([1.2](lessons/01-02-consent-actual-tacit-and-hypothetical.md))*
- **"Citizens feel bound as members, so they are."** An empirical report; the normative bridge must be
  argued. *([1.3](lessons/01-03-fair-play-gratitude-and-associative-obligation.md))*
- **"Mutual disinterest says people are selfish."** A modelling assumption. *([2.2](lessons/02-02-rawls-the-original-position.md))*
- **"Wilt shows patterns are inefficient."** Its claim is conceptual and normative. *([2.4](lessons/02-04-nozick-entitlement-and-the-challenge-to-patterns.md))*
- **"The currency question is about measurability."** It is normative, though measurability is one of
  Rawls's reasons. *([2.5](lessons/02-05-equality-of-what.md))*
- **"The disrespect objection predicts recipients will feel insulted."** It is about what the state's
  reasons express. *([2.6](lessons/02-06-luck-relations-priority-and-sufficiency.md))*
- **"Berlin proved positive liberty leads to tyranny."** His premise 3 is empirical-historical, not a
  logical consequence. *([3.1](lessons/03-01-three-concepts-of-liberty.md))*
- **"Drug use harms families" settles it; "most people are disgusted" settles it.** Each bundles
  empirical, conceptual and normative claims. *([3.2](lessons/03-02-mills-harm-principle.md), [3.3](lessons/03-03-paternalism-and-legal-moralism.md))*
- **"Counter-speech works better than bans" is the core of the liberal case.** An empirical premise
  Scanlon 1972 and Dworkin do not need. *([3.4](lessons/03-04-free-speech.md))*
- **"Reasonable pluralism says no doctrine is true."** Partly empirical, partly moral; silent on truth.
  *([4.1](lessons/04-01-political-liberalism.md))*
- **"The integrity objection is about how believers feel."** Normative. *([4.2](lessons/04-02-public-reason-and-religious-argument.md))*
- **"The communitarians proved the liberal state illegitimate."** Their claims are conceptual and
  empirical. *([4.3](lessons/04-03-perfectionism-and-the-common-good.md))*
- **"The jury theorem shows big electorates are wise."** Conditional on $p>1/2$ and independence, both
  empirical. *([5.1](lessons/05-01-why-democracy.md))*
- **"Courts protect minorities better" is the normative core of review.** An empirical premise (Waldron's
  6). *([5.2](lessons/05-02-majority-rule-vs-rights-judicial-review.md))*
- **"Polarization findings refute deliberative democracy."** Empirical claims against the bridge
  premise, not the normative thesis. *([5.3](lessons/05-03-deliberative-vs-aggregative-democracy.md))*
- **"Arrow shows majority voting usually produces incoherence."** It shows no rule *guarantees*
  coherence; frequency is empirical. *([5.4](lessons/05-04-does-social-choice-wound-democracy.md))*
- **"Pogge's and Rawls's causal claims settle global justice"; "migration's wage effects settle
  borders."** The facts decide how each view applies, not which normative premise is right.
  *([6.1](lessons/06-01-global-justice-cosmopolitans-and-statists.md), [6.2](lessons/06-02-immigration-may-a-state-exclude.md))*
- **"The budget identity shows basic income is justified."** It is arithmetic. *([6.4](lessons/06-04-welfare-property-and-subsidiarity.md))*

### Pitfalls on authority and anarchism

- **"Philosophical anarchism counsels disobedience."** It denies only a general content-independent
  duty; the anarchist may obey nearly every law for its own reasons, with no duty to resist.
  *([1.1](lessons/01-01-the-problem-of-political-authority.md), [1.4](lessons/01-04-natural-duty-and-philosophical-anarchism.md))*
- **"Hypothetical consent is weak actual consent."** A different kind of argument: a test of reasons.
  *([1.2](lessons/01-02-consent-actual-tacit-and-hypothetical.md))*
- **"Writing 'that apply to us' into the natural duty solves particularity."** The account of
  application does the work, and is what critics attack. *([1.4](lessons/01-04-natural-duty-and-philosophical-anarchism.md))*
- **"Any principled lawbreaking is civil disobedience."** Rawls's definition excludes covert, violent
  and purely personal refusals, and meeting it is not being justified. *([1.4](lessons/01-04-natural-duty-and-philosophical-anarchism.md))*
- **"The case against a vaccine refuser is fair play."** Much of its force may be a natural duty not to
  harm. *([1.3](lessons/01-03-fair-play-gratitude-and-associative-obligation.md))*

### Pitfalls on distribution

- **"A utilitarian basic structure aims at equality."** It aims at the sum; equality is a by-product of
  identical (or unknown) utility and no leak. *([2.1](lessons/02-01-utilitarianism-as-a-principle-for-institutions.md))*
- **"Redistribution raises the sum, so everyone is better off."** A rise in the sum is not a Pareto
  improvement. *([2.1](lessons/02-01-utilitarianism-as-a-principle-for-institutions.md))*
- **"The veil is about choosing under risk."** Its first job is to model fairness. *([2.2](lessons/02-02-rawls-the-original-position.md))*
- **"The difference principle maximizes equality."** It maximizes the floor and can require large gaps.
  *([2.3](lessons/02-03-rawls-the-two-principles-and-maximin.md))*
- **"The priority of liberty forbids any rule about speech or assembly."** It forbids trading liberty
  for income, not regulating liberty for liberty's sake. *([2.3](lessons/02-03-rawls-the-two-principles-and-maximin.md))*
- **"Entitlement is desert."** Nozick needs no one to deserve anything; desert is a pattern he attacks.
  *([2.4](lessons/02-04-nozick-entitlement-and-the-challenge-to-patterns.md))*
- **"Equality of resources means equal amounts."** The envy test compares bundles by each person's own
  valuation. *([2.5](lessons/02-05-equality-of-what.md))*
- **"Prioritarianism is a kind of egalitarianism."** It is non-comparative: immune to leveling down,
  silent on inequality as such. *([2.6](lessons/02-06-luck-relations-priority-and-sufficiency.md))*

### Pitfalls on liberty and its limits

- **"Positive liberty means having resources."** Berlin's positive liberty is self-mastery; resources
  belong to the worth of liberty or capabilities; after MacCallum "from" vs "to" marks no difference.
  *([3.1](lessons/03-01-three-concepts-of-liberty.md))*
- **"The harm principle says preventing harm justifies coercion."** Harm is necessary, not sufficient.
  *([3.2](lessons/03-02-mills-harm-principle.md))*
- **"Devlin's argument is pure moralism."** It is impure: it runs through the harm of disintegration.
  *([3.3](lessons/03-03-paternalism-and-legal-moralism.md))*
- **"Soft paternalism means gentle intervention."** It concerns voluntariness, not gentleness; nudges
  are another axis. *([3.3](lessons/03-03-paternalism-and-legal-moralism.md))*
- **"The incitement line is about how dangerous an opinion is."** It is about circumstances. *([3.4](lessons/03-04-free-speech.md))*

### Pitfalls on pluralism and the good

- **"A neutral state's laws affect all ways of life equally."** Rawls requires only neutrality of aim.
  *([4.1](lessons/04-01-political-liberalism.md))*
- **"Public reason restricts religious speech."** It restricts which reasons settle fundamentals in the
  public political forum, as a moral duty; the line is public vs comprehensive. *([4.2](lessons/04-02-public-reason-and-religious-argument.md))*
- **"Perfectionism means coercing people into virtue."** Raz rejects that and George denies law can make
  anyone virtuous directly. *([4.3](lessons/04-03-perfectionism-and-the-common-good.md))*
- **"Common good means total welfare."** It names the conditions of flourishing, limited by subsidiarity
  and religious liberty. *([4.3](lessons/04-03-perfectionism-and-the-common-good.md))*
- **"The personal is political licenses state control of family life"; "Rawls's 1997 reply exempts the
  family from justice."** The first is a claim about where justice applies; Rawls calls the family part
  of the basic structure and disputes only direct application and "voluntary". *([4.4](lessons/04-04-feminist-political-philosophy.md))*
- **"Young's model blames everyone."** It blames no one for the structure; liability survives alongside.
  *([4.4](lessons/04-04-feminist-political-philosophy.md))*

### Pitfalls on democracy

- **"An intrinsic justification makes every majority decision legitimate."** Christiano limits
  democratic authority where public equality is violated. *([5.1](lessons/05-01-why-democracy.md))*
- **"Waldron opposes all judicial review."** His target is strong-form review of legislation in
  societies meeting four assumptions. *([5.2](lessons/05-02-majority-rule-vs-rights-judicial-review.md))*
- **"Deliberative democracy requires consensus"; "aggregation counts selfish preferences."** Cohen aims
  at consensus and votes when it fails; a preference can be a moral judgment, and Schumpeter is not
  anti-democratic. *([5.3](lessons/05-03-deliberative-vs-aggregative-democracy.md))*
- **"Domain restriction excludes some voters."** It is an empirical hypothesis about preference
  structure. *([5.4](lessons/05-04-does-social-choice-wound-democracy.md))*

### Pitfalls on the hard cases

- **"Statists say we owe foreigners nothing."** Nagel accepts a humanitarian minimum, Rawls a duty of
  assistance; the dispute is over egalitarian justice. *([6.1](lessons/06-01-global-justice-cosmopolitans-and-statists.md))*
- **"The Catholic position is open borders" or "states decide."** It affirms both rights and treats
  numbers as prudential. *([6.2](lessons/06-02-immigration-may-a-state-exclude.md))*
- **"Barry denies uniform laws burden minorities unequally."** He grants unequal impact and denies it
  is unequal treatment. *([6.3](lessons/06-03-conscience-and-exemptions.md))*
- **"Leiter and Laborde are on opposite sides."** Both deny religion as such deserves special treatment.
  *([6.3](lessons/06-03-conscience-and-exemptions.md))*
- **"Subsidiarity is a presumption for small government."** It binds both ways. *([6.4](lessons/06-04-welfare-property-and-subsidiarity.md))*

## Conventions

- **One card per course,** covering every lesson; quizzes and reviews are open book, so problems ask you
  to *use* these entries, never to recall them.
- **Verdict-neutral.** Every entry states a position or argument at the strength its defenders would
  recognize, names the premise critics attack, and records no verdict. Religious and secular positions
  get the same treatment.
- **Numbered premises** appear where the lessons give them; boss-problem arguments appear in outline only.
- **The card follows the lessons where the build's source checks corrected the syllabus.** Notable: TJ
  §46 lists the difference principle before fair equality of opportunity (the later works reverse the
  order); Dworkin's 1996 term is the "constitutional conception", later renamed "partnership"; the
  "asymmetry objection" names the justice-vs-good point (Sandel, Waldron), while Wolterstorff's and
  Eberle's points are the integrity and fairness objections; Simmons's tacit-consent test has five
  conditions; Devlin's figure is the man in the Clapham omnibus, unquoted; self-ownership is mainly
  Cohen's label for Nozick's root.
- **Headings are anchors.** Lessons link to `../reference.md#slug`; renaming a `###` heading breaks them.
  Headings are ASCII only, so possessives drop their apostrophes ("Humes ship objection").
- **No prose dollar signs:** "250,000 dollars", not the symbol.
