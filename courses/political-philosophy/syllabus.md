# Political Philosophy — Syllabus

> Politics & Society · Tier 1 · ~26 lessons · Prereqs: [`ethics`](../ethics/syllabus.md) (and through it [`philosophical-method`](../philosophical-method/syllabus.md)) · Roadmap id: `political-philosophy`

## Goal

This course asks what, if anything, justifies the state and its institutions. Why does anyone owe the state obedience? Who should get what? What may the state forbid? Why should the majority decide, and when may a court overrule it? What do we owe people outside our borders? You will learn to reconstruct the load-bearing arguments of the analytic tradition at the strength their best defenders would recognize: consent, fair play and philosophical anarchism; Rawls, Nozick and the equality-of-what debate; Mill, Berlin and the republicans; political liberalism and its perfectionist and communitarian critics; democratic theory and its epistocratic and social-choice challengers. You will finish able to run each position on a hard case, say where it stops giving a verdict, and name the premise two rivals actually disagree about. The course takes no side. It deliberately skips the thinkers as historical figures (Hobbes, Locke, Rousseau, Mill's *Representative Government*, owned by [`history-of-political-thought`](../history-of-political-thought/syllabus.md)), utilitarianism as a moral theory ([`ethics`](../ethics/syllabus.md)), the proofs of social choice ([`grad-game-theory`](../grad-game-theory/syllabus.md), `social-choice`), jurisprudence and punishment (`philosophy-of-law`), the moral limits of markets (`philosophy-of-economics`), just war theory, and continental and critical theory beyond a single lesson on feminist political philosophy.

## How practice works in this course

Problems follow [HUMANITIES-BUILD-BRIEF.md](../../HUMANITIES-BUILD-BRIEF.md): every part declares its kind, and every solution is a rubric (must-hit moves, wrong turns) plus a model answer. Here that plays out in four ways:

- **Exegetical parts are strict.** What Hume's ship objection actually attacks, which of Rawls's conditions make maximin rational, whether Mill's principle forbids a given law, what the liberal principle of legitimacy requires. These have right answers.
- **Evaluative parts are verdict-neutral.** No rubric rewards concluding that the state has authority or lacks it, that Rawls beats Nozick, that public reason is right, or that borders should be open or closed. Must-hit moves are ones either side has to make: state the view precisely, run it on the case, name the premise the objection attacks, say whether the objection generalizes. Religious and secular positions (natural-law perfectionism, Catholic social teaching, secular liberal egalitarianism, libertarianism) get the same treatment.
- **Apply-to-a-hard-case, counterexample and find-the-crux are the workhorses.** Political principles are tested on policies, and each module also uses reconstruct, diagnose (invented op-eds, speeches and memos, never attributed to real people) and steelman-and-reply. Every module uses at least three archetypes.
- **Close reading uses public-domain texts only:** Hume's "Of the Original Contract", Locke's *Second Treatise*, Mill's *On Liberty* and *Utilitarianism*, Kant, Sidgwick. Modern authors (Rawls, Nozick, Hart, Simmons, Dworkin, Sen, Anderson, Berlin, Pettit, Raz, Waldron, Estlund, Carens, Miller) are paraphrased and cited, never quoted at length. **Formal parts are light:** maximin vs expected utility on a payoff table (2.3), and a Condorcet cycle (5.4).

## Dangerous Checklist

When you finish, you can:

- [ ] Separate power, legitimacy, authority and political obligation, and say which one a given argument actually establishes
- [ ] Reconstruct the consent, hypothetical-consent, fair-play and natural-duty arguments for obligation, and state the objection each faces (Hume's ship, "a hypothetical contract binds no one", Nozick's unwanted benefits, the particularity problem)
- [ ] Say what utilitarianism implies as a principle for the basic structure of society, and how the separateness-of-persons objection changes when it targets institutions rather than acts
- [ ] Reconstruct Rawls's route from the original position to the two principles, and compute which scheme maximin, expected utility and the utilitarian sum each select from a payoff table
- [ ] Run Nozick's Wilt Chamberlain argument against a patterned principle, and name the premise a defender of patterns must deny
- [ ] Place a view on the map of welfare, resources and capabilities, and of luck, relational, prioritarian and sufficientarian equality, and build a case that separates two of them
- [ ] Tell negative, positive and republican liberty apart, and diagnose which one a political claim invokes
- [ ] Apply Mill's harm principle, the offense principle and the soft/hard paternalism distinction to a new law, and say where each stops determining a verdict
- [ ] State Rawls's liberal principle of legitimacy and the inclusive and exclusive views of public reason, and give the perfectionist's and the self-defeat objection's best case against them
- [ ] Separate instrumental, epistemic and intrinsic justifications of democracy, and assess the epistocrat's challenge and the counter-majoritarian difficulty using them
- [ ] Say exactly what Arrow's theorem does and does not show about "the will of the people", and whether democracy needs the thing it denies
- [ ] Run cosmopolitan, statist, national-self-determination and freedom-of-association arguments on a question of global aid or immigration, and name the crux between two of them

## Modules

### Module 1: Authority, legitimacy and obligation

Why should anyone obey the state, and does anything follow if no answer works? The thinkers who first framed the question belong to [`history-of-political-thought`](../history-of-political-thought/syllabus.md); this module takes their arguments apart analytically.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1.1 | The problem of political authority | See why the state's claims need justifying, and separate the four things a justification could establish | power vs authority, legitimacy (the right to rule) vs obligation (the duty to obey), justification vs legitimacy, the anarchist's challenge; Weber's descriptive legitimacy cited to [`social-theory`](../social-theory/syllabus.md) 4.3 |
| 1.2 | Consent: actual, tacit and hypothetical | Reconstruct consent-based arguments and test each against its standard objection | the state of nature as a device (Hobbes, Locke, Rousseau linked to HPT 3.3, 4.2, 4.4), express vs tacit consent (Locke's residence argument), Hume's ship objection, hypothetical consent and the "no contract at all" objection (Dworkin), Estlund's normative consent |
| 1.3 | Fair play, gratitude and associative obligation | Decide whether receiving the benefits of a cooperative scheme binds you to it | Hart's principle of fairness, Nozick's public-address-system objection, unavoidable public goods (Klosko), gratitude, associative obligations (Dworkin) and the family analogy |
| 1.4 | Natural duty and philosophical anarchism | Test whether a duty of justice binds you to *your* state, and what follows if nothing does | the natural duty of justice (Rawls, Waldron), the particularity problem (Simmons), a priori vs a posteriori anarchism (Wolff, Simmons), legitimacy without a general obligation to obey, civil disobedience as the test case |

**Boss problem 1:** (a) Close-read the passage in Hume's "Of the Original Contract" comparing a subject who stays in his country to a man carried on board a ship while asleep (public domain). What exactly does the analogy deny about tacit consent, and which words decide it? *Exegetical.* (b) Reconstruct Locke's tacit-consent argument from residence and use of the highway (*Second Treatise* ch. VIII) in numbered premises, and name the premise Hume's objection attacks. *Exegetical.* (c) An invented case: a village's volunteer-built flood wall protects every house, including that of a resident who never agreed to help. Apply Hart's principle of fairness and Nozick's unwanted-benefits objection, reach a verdict on whether the resident owes a share, and say where the principle stops determining one. *Evaluative.* (d) In 300-500 words: suppose every ground in 1.2-1.4 fails. Does it follow that the state is illegitimate, or only that there is no general duty to obey it? Any verdict, if the legitimacy/obligation distinction from 1.1 is used and the strongest opposing view is stated. *Evaluative.*

### Module 2: Justice and distribution

Who should get what, and by what standard? Utilitarianism as an institutional principle, the Rawls-Nozick debate, and the equality-of-what debate that followed it.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2.1 | Utilitarianism as a principle for institutions | State what maximizing welfare implies for the basic structure, and what changes when the objections target institutions | the basic structure as the subject of justice, utilitarianism as public philosophy (Goodin), diminishing marginal utility and the case for redistribution, separateness of persons at the institutional level; the moral theory and its objections cited to [`ethics`](../ethics/syllabus.md) 1.1, 1.3 |
| 2.2 | Rawls I: the original position | Understand the choice behind the veil and why it is built the way it is | justice as fairness, the veil of ignorance, primary goods, mutual disinterest, the original position as a device of representation; Scanlonian contractualism cited to [`ethics`](../ethics/syllabus.md) 5.2 |
| 2.3 | Rawls II: the two principles and maximin | Get from the veil to equal liberty and the difference principle, and compare maximin with expected utility | equal basic liberties and their priority, fair equality of opportunity, the difference principle, Rawls's three conditions for maximin, Harsanyi's equiprobability model (the decision-theoretic dispute cited to [`decision-theory`](../decision-theory/syllabus.md) 3.4) |
| 2.4 | Nozick: entitlement and the challenge to patterns | Mount the libertarian case against end-state and patterned justice | self-ownership, justice in acquisition, transfer and rectification, the Lockean proviso, "liberty upsets patterns" (Wilt Chamberlain), taxation and forced labor |
| 2.5 | Equality of what? | Compare welfare, resources and capabilities as the currency of justice | welfarism and expensive tastes, Dworkin's equality of resources (the envy test, hypothetical insurance), Sen's capabilities and Nussbaum's list, the indexing problem |
| 2.6 | Luck, relations, priority and sufficiency | Tell the four shapes an egalitarian view can take and build a case that separates them | luck egalitarianism (brute vs option luck), Anderson's democratic equality and the harshness objection, the leveling-down objection, prioritarianism (Parfit), sufficientarianism (Frankfurt) |

**Boss problem 2:** (a) A payoff table written for the problem gives three basic structures and the primary-goods index of three social positions under each. Compute which structure maximin selects, which expected utility selects with equiprobable positions, and which the utilitarian sum selects; the table is built so they diverge. *Formal.* (b) State Rawls's three conditions under which maximin is the rational rule, and say which of them the table's setup satisfies. *Exegetical.* (c) Reconstruct Nozick's Wilt Chamberlain argument in numbered premises and name the single premise a defender of the difference principle must deny. *Exegetical.* (d) In 300-500 words, assess whether Rawls's basic-structure reply answers Nozick or changes the subject. Any verdict, if the premise from (c) is addressed and a luck egalitarian's or Anderson's view of the dispute is stated at full strength. *Evaluative.*

### Module 3: Liberty and its limits

What may the state stop you from doing, and on what grounds? This course owns Mill's harm principle.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3.1 | Three concepts of liberty | Distinguish freedom as non-interference, as self-mastery and as non-domination | Berlin's negative and positive liberty, the totalitarian-risk argument, republican freedom as non-domination (Pettit), the benevolent master, freedom vs the conditions of its exercise |
| 3.2 | Mill's harm principle | State the one very simple principle, its scope and its motive | the harm principle (*On Liberty* ch. 1), self- vs other-regarding conduct, its exclusions (children, "backward" societies), harm vs offense, the argument from individuality (ch. 3), the utilitarian foundation and whether it can bear the principle's weight |
| 3.3 | Paternalism and legal moralism | Locate where the harm principle bites and where rival grounds take over | soft vs hard paternalism, Mill's bridge and slavery-contract cases (ch. 5), the Hart-Devlin debate, legal moralism, Feinberg's offense principle, nudges |
| 3.4 | Free speech | Apply the liberty framework to its hardest case | Mill's four grounds (ch. 2), the corn-dealer example (ch. 3), Scanlon's autonomy argument, incitement vs advocacy, the dignity argument for hate-speech restriction (Waldron); US doctrine cited to `constitutional-law` |

**Boss problem 3:** (a) Close-read Mill's unsafe-bridge and slavery-contract passages in *On Liberty* ch. 5 (public domain). Which restriction does each permit, and is the ground in each the harm principle, a form of soft paternalism, or a further principle? Point to the words that decide it. *Exegetical.* (b) Diagnose an invented op-ed defending a ban on selling one's own kidney "to protect the poor from exploitation": which concept of liberty and which ground (harm, paternalism, moralism, offense) is doing the work? *Exegetical.* (c) Build a case that the harm principle, as stated in ch. 1, misjudges: either it forbids a restriction nearly everyone accepts, or it permits one nearly everyone rejects. Then show whether the case survives Mill's likely reply. *Evaluative.* (d) In 300-500 words, decide whether republican freedom as non-domination gives a better account of the kidney case than Mill does. Any verdict, if both views are stated precisely and the strongest objection to your answer is met. *Evaluative.*

### Module 4: Legitimacy and public reason

If citizens reasonably disagree about religion, morality and the good life, on what terms may some of them coerce the others? Political liberalism's answer, and the critics who say it asks the wrong question.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4.1 | Political liberalism | State Rawls's answer to reasonable pluralism and what it requires of coercive law | the fact of reasonable pluralism, the burdens of judgment, comprehensive vs political doctrines, overlapping consensus, the liberal principle of legitimacy, neutrality of aim vs effect |
| 4.2 | Public reason and religious argument | Apply the inclusive and exclusive views to a case and test them on their standard objections | exclusive vs inclusive public reason, the wide view and its proviso, consensus vs convergence (Gaus), the asymmetry and integrity objections (Wolterstorff, Eberle), the self-defeat objection |
| 4.3 | Perfectionism and the common good | Present the case that the state may promote the good, in its secular and religious forms | liberal perfectionism and autonomy (Raz), the communitarian critique of the unencumbered self (Sandel, MacIntyre, Taylor), natural-law perfectionism (George; theory cited to [`ethics`](../ethics/syllabus.md) 4.4-4.5), Catholic social teaching's common good as one position (cited to `catholic-social-teaching`) |
| 4.4 | Feminist political philosophy | See what the public/private line hides and whether liberalism can absorb the critique | the family as part of the basic structure (Okin), the personal is political, care and dependency, structural injustice and the social connection model (Young) |

**Boss problem 4:** (a) An invented legislator's speech argues for a law in two ways: once from a religious premise, once from a premise about human flourishing she says any reasonable citizen could accept. Diagnose which argument exclusive public reason permits, which the inclusive view with the proviso permits, and which a perfectionist needs neither view to accept. *Exegetical.* (b) Reconstruct Rawls's liberal principle of legitimacy in numbered premises. *Exegetical.* (c) Name the crux between a political liberal and a perfectionist (Raz's or a natural-law version): the single premise about the state's role that one must deny. *Exegetical.* (d) Steelman the self-defeat objection (the principle of public reason is not itself acceptable to all reasonable citizens) in 150 words, then reply for a political liberal in 150 words. *Evaluative.* (e) Okin argues the family belongs to the basic structure. Say in 150 words whether that claim is easier to hold for a political liberal or a perfectionist, and why. *Evaluative.*

### Module 5: Democracy and its foundations

Why let the majority decide, when may a court overrule it, and does formal social choice undermine the whole idea?

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5.1 | Why democracy? | Separate instrumental, epistemic and intrinsic justifications, and face the epistocrat | procedural vs instrumental value, political equality, the jury theorem (cited to `social-choice`) and Aristotle's summation argument (HPT 1.4), epistocracy (Brennan), Mill's plural voting (HPT 6.4), Estlund's epistemic proceduralism and the demographic objection |
| 5.2 | Majority rule vs rights: judicial review | Face the counter-majoritarian difficulty as a question of legitimacy | tyranny of the majority (HPT 6.3), constitutional entrenchment and precommitment, Waldron's case against strong judicial review, Dworkin's moral reading and the partnership conception, weak-form review as a middle path (designs cited to [`political-institutions`](../political-institutions/syllabus.md) 5.3-5.4) |
| 5.3 | Deliberative vs aggregative democracy | Contrast counting preferences with transforming them | Schumpeterian minimalism, preference aggregation, deliberative legitimacy (Cohen, Habermas), public reason revisited (4.2), the empirical worry about deliberation |
| 5.4 | Does social choice wound democracy? | Say what Arrow's theorem shows about "the will of the people", and whether democracy needs that | Condorcet cycles, Arrow's conditions (proof cited to [`grad-game-theory`](../grad-game-theory/syllabus.md) 5.1), Riker's liberalism-vs-populism argument, Mackie's reply on how often cycles occur, domain restriction, what a proceduralist needs from aggregation |

**Boss problem 5:** (a) A three-voter, three-option preference profile written for the problem. Show it produces a majority cycle, and show that an agenda-setter choosing the order of pairwise votes can make any option win. *Formal.* (b) State precisely what Arrow's theorem forbids, and reconstruct Riker's inference from instability to the claim that populist democracy is incoherent. Name the premise Mackie denies. *Exegetical.* (c) Diagnose an invented op-ed claiming a court "defied the will of the people" by striking down a statute: which conception of democracy from 5.1 and 5.3 does the phrase assume, and does (a) undermine it? *Exegetical.* (d) In 300-500 words, assess whether the social-choice results strengthen or weaken Waldron's case against judicial review. Any verdict, if at least one instrumental and one intrinsic justification from 5.1 is used and Dworkin's reply is stated at full strength. *Evaluative.*

### Module 6: Hard cases: borders, conscience and provision

The positions from Modules 2-5 run on four disputed questions. Each lesson presents the live positions, religious and secular, and ends at the crux rather than a verdict.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 6.1 | Global justice: cosmopolitans and statists | Decide whether principles of distributive justice stop at the border | the duty to aid (Singer's argument cited to [`ethics`](../ethics/syllabus.md) 1.4), institutional cosmopolitanism (Beitz, Pogge), coercion-based statism (Nagel, Blake), duties of assistance vs distributive justice |
| 6.2 | Immigration: may a state exclude? | Run the main arguments for open and closed borders on a case | the feudal-privilege analogy (Carens), freedom of association (Wellman), national self-determination (Miller), refugees and the right to exit vs the right to enter; Catholic social teaching's pairing of a right to migrate with a state's right to regulate, as one position (cited to `catholic-social-teaching`) |
| 6.3 | Conscience and exemptions | Decide when religious or cultural objectors should be exempt from general laws | rule-and-exemption vs uniform rule (Barry), group-differentiated rights and internal minorities (Kymlicka), why religion specially? (Leiter), disaggregating religion (Laborde), the Catholic account of religious liberty as one position (cited to `catholic-social-teaching`) |
| 6.4 | Who should provide? Welfare, property and subsidiarity | Compare rival accounts of who owes provision against need, using basic income as the test case | welfare-state capitalism vs property-owning democracy (Rawls), taxation as forced labor revisited (2.4), basic income as non-domination (republicans), the reciprocity objection, subsidiarity and the universal destination of goods as one position (cited to `catholic-social-teaching`) |

**Boss problem 6:** (a) Diagnose an invented policy memo proposing to cut immigration and redirect the savings to foreign aid "so we help people where they are": which positions from 6.1 and 6.2 does it borrow, and which does it contradict? *Exegetical.* (b) State how a luck-egalitarian cosmopolitan, a Nagel-style statist, and a defender of freedom of association would each assess the memo, each graded strictly for fidelity to the view. *Exegetical.* (c) Name the crux between the cosmopolitan and the statist: the single premise about what triggers duties of distributive justice. *Exegetical.* (d) In 300-500 words, argue for or against the memo using at least two positions from Module 6 and one from Modules 2-5, any verdict, if the strongest rival position is stated at full strength and the crux from (c) is addressed. *Evaluative.*
