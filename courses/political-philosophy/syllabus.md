# Political Philosophy — Syllabus

> Tier 1 · ~20 lessons · Prereqs: none (a taste of [`proofs-primer`](../proofs-primer/syllabus.md) for argument structure is optional) · Roadmap id: `political-philosophy`

## Goal

Learn to reconstruct and evaluate the load-bearing arguments in the analytic tradition about the five enduring questions: political authority (why obey the state at all?), justice (who should get what?), liberty (what may the state stop you from doing?), equality (equal in what respect, and why?), and democracy (why let the majority decide?). You'll steelman each major position, name the strongest objection to it, and apply the machinery to concrete disputes rather than treat it as museum pieces. Deliberately skipped: a comprehensive history of political thought (Plato-to-present survey), the depth of continental and critical theory, and applied-ethics policy surveys — this is the analytic core with its historical roots, not a tour.

## Dangerous Checklist

When you finish, you can:

- [ ] Explain the state-of-nature device and why Hobbes, Locke, and Rousseau reach opposite conclusions from it
- [ ] Reconstruct a social-contract argument as premises-and-conclusion and locate its weakest premise
- [ ] Distinguish consent, fair play, and gratitude as grounds of political obligation — and say why each struggles
- [ ] Argue both sides of the utilitarianism-vs-rights dispute over how to distribute
- [ ] Reconstruct Rawls's route from the original position to the difference principle, then run Nozick's entitlement objection against it
- [ ] Distinguish negative from positive liberty and diagnose which one a given political claim is invoking
- [ ] Apply Mill's harm principle to a hard case and expose where it's indeterminate
- [ ] Distinguish equality of welfare, resources, and capabilities, and say what the communitarian and feminist critiques add
- [ ] Separate instrumental from intrinsic justifications of democracy
- [ ] Evaluate whether a formal result like Arrow's theorem is a real threat to democracy or a misapplied one
- [ ] Take a concrete political dispute and identify which philosophical question is actually doing the work

## Modules

### Module 1: Authority and the state

The fundamental question — why should anyone obey? — and the social-contract tradition's answers.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1.1 | The problem of political authority | See why obedience needs justifying at all | authority vs power, legitimacy, the anarchist's challenge, obligation vs coercion |
| 1.2 | The state of nature as a device | Understand what the thought experiment is for | state of nature, hypothetical baseline, what it isolates, its dangers |
| 1.3 | Hobbes: security and the sovereign | Reconstruct the argument from fear to absolute power | war of all against all, the covenant, the Leviathan, why the sovereign is unaccountable |
| 1.4 | Locke: consent, property, and limited government | Contrast a rights-first contract with Hobbes's | natural rights, tacit consent, property from labor, the right of revolution |
| 1.5 | Rousseau: the general will | Grasp freedom-through-collective-self-rule | general will vs will of all, forced to be free, popular sovereignty |
| 1.6 | Political obligation and its grounds | Test the candidate reasons we're bound to obey | consent theory, fair-play, gratitude, natural duty, philosophical anarchism |

**Boss problem 1:** Reconstruct one social-contract theorist's argument (Hobbes or Locke) as an explicit premise-conclusion chain from the state of nature to a duty of obedience. Then mount the philosophical anarchist's objection — that no actual person has consented — and assess whether the theory survives it or must retreat to a hypothetical-consent version that proves less.

### Module 2: Justice and distribution

Who should get what — and by what standard? The central debate of 20th-century political philosophy.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2.1 | Utilitarianism as a theory of justice | State the maximize-welfare view at its strongest | aggregate welfare, impartiality, the appeal of a single currency |
| 2.2 | What's wrong with utilitarianism | Name the objections that motivated everything after | separateness of persons, rights as trumps, distribution-blindness |
| 2.3 | Rawls I: the original position | Understand the choice-behind-the-veil setup | original position, veil of ignorance, primary goods, justice as fairness |
| 2.4 | Rawls II: the two principles | Get from the veil to equal liberty and the difference principle | equal basic liberties, fair equality of opportunity, difference principle, maximin |
| 2.5 | Nozick: the entitlement theory | Mount the libertarian challenge to patterned justice | self-ownership, acquisition/transfer/rectification, Wilt Chamberlain, the tax objection |
| 2.6 | The equality-of-what debate | See how luck egalitarians reframe the question | luck vs choice, luck egalitarianism, the leveling-down objection |

**Boss problem 2:** Reconstruct Rawls's argument from the original position to the difference principle — including why a rational chooser behind the veil would pick maximin over expected-utility maximization. Then present Nozick's strongest objection (that just distributions arise from just steps, so any end-state pattern requires "continuous interference with people's lives"), and assess who wins — being explicit about which premise each side must deny.

### Module 3: Liberty and its limits

What may the state stop you from doing — and what may it stop others from doing to you?

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3.1 | Two concepts of liberty | Distinguish freedom-from and freedom-to (Berlin) | negative vs positive liberty, non-interference, self-mastery, the totalitarian risk |
| 3.2 | Mill's harm principle | State the one very simple principle and its motive | harm principle, self-regarding acts, the case from fallibility |
| 3.3 | Paternalism and moralism | Locate where the harm principle bites | soft/hard paternalism, legal moralism, offense, autonomy |
| 3.4 | Free speech | Apply the liberty framework to its hardest case | marketplace of ideas, the offense principle, incitement, why bad speech is protected |
| 3.5 | What money shouldn't buy | Ask whether some things resist market distribution | commodification, corruption/coercion arguments, blocked exchanges, the moral limits of markets |

**Boss problem 3:** Take a concrete restriction currently defended as protecting people from themselves (e.g., mandatory seatbelt laws, bans on selling a kidney, or drug prohibition). Diagnose whether Mill's harm principle actually condemns it, show where the principle is indeterminate, and argue whether the best justification is really about harm-to-others, soft paternalism, or something the harm principle can't capture.

### Module 4: Equality, community, and difference

Equality of what — and the critiques that say liberalism asks the wrong question.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4.1 | Equality of welfare, resources, capabilities | Compare the three main answers to "equal in what?" | welfarism, Dworkin's resources, Sen's capabilities, expensive tastes |
| 4.2 | The communitarian critique | See the attack on the unencumbered self | the atomistic self, constitutive community, situated agency (Sandel, MacIntyre, Taylor) |
| 4.3 | Multiculturalism and recognition | Weigh group rights against liberal universalism | politics of recognition, group-differentiated rights, the internal-minorities problem |
| 4.4 | Feminist political philosophy | Expose what the public/private line hides | the personal is political, the public/private divide, care ethics, structural injustice |

**Boss problem 4:** The communitarian charges that liberalism rests on an implausible "unencumbered self" that chooses its ends from nowhere. Reconstruct that critique at full strength, then give the liberal's best reply (that political liberalism needs no such metaphysics). Assess whether the disagreement is really about the self or about which values a state may enforce.

### Module 5: Democracy and its foundations

Why let the majority decide — and does formal social-choice theory undermine the whole idea?

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5.1 | Why democracy? | Separate the two families of justification | instrumental vs intrinsic value, epistemic democracy, political equality, self-government |
| 5.2 | Majority rule vs rights | Face the counter-majoritarian problem | tyranny of the majority, judicial review, constitutional entrenchment, precommitment |
| 5.3 | Deliberative vs aggregative democracy | Contrast counting preferences with transforming them | preference aggregation, deliberation, public reason, legitimacy through discussion |
| 5.4 | Social choice meets democracy | Read Arrow's theorem as a claim about collective will | Arrow's impossibility theorem, the conditions, cycling, "the will of the people" |

**Boss problem 5:** Arrow's impossibility theorem shows no aggregation rule satisfies a handful of reasonable-sounding conditions at once. Assess whether this is a genuine problem for democracy or a misapplied result. Defend your answer by being precise about what the theorem does and doesn't show (e.g., whether it targets a coherent "popular will," whether domain restrictions or richer inputs than ordinal rankings escape it, and whether democracy even needs the thing the theorem denies). This is the formal-meets-normative capstone — cross to [`political-economy`](../political-economy/syllabus.md) for the machinery.

## Sources of truth

- Hobbes, *Leviathan*; Locke, *Second Treatise of Government*; Rousseau, *The Social Contract* (the contract tradition, primary texts)
- Mill, *On Liberty* (liberty and its limits); Berlin, *"Two Concepts of Liberty"*
- Rawls, *A Theory of Justice*; Nozick, *Anarchy, State, and Utopia* (the justice debate, in each other's margins)
- Will Kymlicka, *Contemporary Political Philosophy: An Introduction* (the map — argument-by-argument survey of the whole terrain)
- Sen, *"Equality of What?"* and *The Idea of Justice* (capabilities); a good anthology such as Goodin, Pettit & Pogge, *A Companion to Contemporary Political Philosophy*, for primary excerpts

## Notes

- This is the **normative companion** to [`political-economy`](../political-economy/syllabus.md): political-economy is the positive/formal theory of how institutions actually behave; this course asks which institutions are *justified*. They meet head-on in Module 5.
- The bearing of **social choice** on democratic theory is the explicit bridge. Arrow's impossibility theorem — developed formally in [`grad-game-theory`](../grad-game-theory/syllabus.md) (Module 5, "Social choice and impossibility") and [`political-economy`](../political-economy/syllabus.md) — is the technical result behind Lesson 5.4 and Boss problem 5. Take the formal statement from there; the normative question of whether it wounds democracy is settled here.
