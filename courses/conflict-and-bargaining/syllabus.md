# Conflict and Bargaining — Syllabus

> Politics & Society · Tier 2 · ~23 lessons · Prereqs: [`grad-game-theory`](../grad-game-theory/syllabus.md), [`international-relations`](../international-relations/syllabus.md) · Roadmap id: `conflict-and-bargaining`

## Goal

This is formal international relations: game theory aimed at the question [`international-relations`](../international-relations/syllabus.md) 1.4 leaves as a puzzle. War destroys value, so some deal should leave both sides better off than fighting. Why do states and armed groups fight anyway? The course starts from the bargaining model of war and its three rationalist answers: private information with incentives to misrepresent, commitment problems, and issue indivisibility. It then carries the same model through crises (costly signals, audience costs, brinkmanship, deterrence), war itself (fighting as information, the war of attrition, termination), civil war and terrorism, the security dilemma, arming, alliances and sanctions, and finally domestic politics and the international economy (the democratic peace, two-level games, trade agreements, interdependence). You will finish able to set up and solve each model, say which rationalist mechanism an account of a real war is using, and name the assumption a critic of that account must deny. The game-theory machinery (Bayesian games, perfect Bayesian equilibrium, signalling, repeated games, Rubinstein bargaining) is cited to [`grad-game-theory`](../grad-game-theory/syllabus.md) and [`game-theory-refresher`](../game-theory-refresher/syllabus.md), not re-derived; the history and the informal theory are cited to [`international-relations`](../international-relations/syllabus.md), not retold. Where the models meet interpretation (is the democratic peace informational? do audience costs exist? is indivisibility a real third mechanism?), problems are verdict-neutral. The course deliberately skips the ethics of war and of terrorism, lobbying and protection-for-sale models, formal models of democratization, coups and selectorate politics, sovereign-default models, trade theory proper, the econometrics of conflict data, behavioural and psychological theories of war beyond naming them as the rival to rationalist explanations, and the analysis of any current conflict.

## How practice works in this course

Problems follow [HUMANITIES-BUILD-BRIEF.md](../../HUMANITIES-BUILD-BRIEF.md). This is a mostly formal course: most parts are **formal** and graded as in any math course. Every equilibrium, threshold and probability in a lesson or boss problem is recomputed by script before it ships. Five archetypes carry the load:

- **Formal model (archetype 8), formal.** The workhorse. Find the bargaining range, the optimal demand under uncertainty, the power shift that makes war unavoidable, the semi-separating equilibrium of a crisis, the hazard rate in a war of attrition, the minimum guarantee that makes rebels disarm, the stability condition for an arms race, the punishment length that sustains a trade agreement.
- **Diagnose (archetype 4), exegetical.** An invented speech, memo or communiqué ("we drew a red line," "they will never honour a deal once we disarm," "one more offensive and they will come to the table"). Name the mechanism or model doing the work, and say which assumption must hold for the claim to follow. Invented quotations are never attributed to real people.
- **Find the crux (archetype 5), exegetical.** Two formal accounts of the same outcome (information or commitment? audience costs or selectorate incentives? opportunity cost or costly signalling?). Name the assumption one side must deny and the evidence that would separate them.
- **Close reading (archetype 7), exegetical.** Public-domain texts only, at most 150 words: Thucydides Book I on the cause of the Peloponnesian War (Crawley, 1874); Hobbes's *Leviathan* ch. 13 on anticipation; Clausewitz's *On War* Book I ch. 1 (Graham, 1873). Which mechanism does the text describe, and which words decide it? Modern authors (Schelling, Fearon, Powell, Kydd, Walter, Schultz) are paraphrased and cited, never quoted at length.
- **Steelman and reply (archetype 6), evaluative.** Reserved for interpretation: whether the rationalist framework explains a class of wars, whether audience costs are real, whether indivisibility is a separate mechanism. Verdict-neutral: any conclusion passes if the moves are made, and the must-hit moves always include stating precisely what the model assumes and predicts. Misstating a model's assumptions is an exegetical error, graded strictly, even inside an evaluative answer.

Cases are invented or historical and settled. The course never grades a verdict on a live conflict; where a current war illustrates a mechanism, lessons name the competing readings and take no side.

## Dangerous Checklist

When you finish, you can:

- [ ] Set up Fearon's bargaining model, compute the bargaining range, and prove that war is ex post inefficient whenever fighting is costly
- [ ] Solve a take-it-or-leave-it crisis with private information about costs or resolve, and explain the risk-return trade-off that makes war a chosen gamble
- [ ] Show why cheap talk cannot reveal resolve when each side has an incentive to bluff, and classify an account of a war as information, commitment or indivisibility
- [ ] Compute the largest power shift compatible with peace in a preventive-war model, and say why a rising state cannot buy off a declining one
- [ ] Solve a crisis-signalling game with audience costs for its semi-separating equilibrium, and tell tying hands from sinking costs from brinkmanship
- [ ] Explain why observed crises and imposed sanctions are selected samples, and predict what the selection does to their measured success rates
- [ ] Solve the war of attrition for its equilibrium quit rates and expected duration, and update beliefs from battlefield outcomes
- [ ] Model a civil-war settlement as a disarmament commitment problem, and compute the enforcement a third-party guarantee must supply
- [ ] Classify a terrorist campaign by strategy (attrition, intimidation, provocation, spoiling, outbidding) and model spoiling as Bayesian updating about a partner's sincerity
- [ ] Solve a Richardson arms-race model for its equilibrium and stability, and model reassurance in a security dilemma with uncertain types
- [ ] Compute the punishment length that sustains a trade agreement under limited retaliation, and find the negotiated outcome of a two-level game with a ratification constraint
- [ ] State the informational, institutional and selection accounts of the democratic peace and the opportunity-cost and signalling accounts of commercial peace, and name the crux in each pair

## Modules

### Module 1: The bargaining model of war

Why a costly war should never happen between rational states, and the three reasons it does.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1.1 | The puzzle of costly war and the bargaining range | Prove that a peaceful deal both sides prefer to war exists whenever war is costly | Fearon's setup: issue on $[0,1]$, win probability $p$, costs $c_A, c_B$; the bargaining range $[p - c_A,\, p + c_B]$; ex post inefficiency; rationalist vs non-rationalist explanations; point selection cited to [`grad-game-theory`](../grad-game-theory/syllabus.md) 3.5; the puzzle as posed in [`international-relations`](../international-relations/syllabus.md) 1.4 |
| 1.2 | Private information and the risk-return trade-off | Solve a crisis where one side does not know the other's cost of war | take-it-or-leave-it demand against an unknown type; screening; the optimal demand trades concessions against the probability of war; war as a chosen gamble; Bayes–Nash equilibrium cited to [`grad-game-theory`](../grad-game-theory/syllabus.md) 4.1 |
| 1.3 | Incentives to misrepresent | Show why states cannot simply announce their resolve, and where mutual optimism comes from | cheap talk and the babbling equilibrium (two types, owned here); bluffing; mutual optimism (Blainey's thesis named) and common priors; **diagnose** problems |
| 1.4 | Power shifts and preventive war | Compute when a declining state fights rather than accept a future it cannot be promised | commitment problem; a rising state's inability to promise future restraint; the threshold condition on the size and speed of the shift; Thucydides I.23 (Crawley), **close reading**; power-transition arguments cited to [`international-relations`](../international-relations/syllabus.md) 1.6 |
| 1.5 | First strikes, indivisibility, and war as a commitment problem | Place first-strike advantages and indivisible stakes inside the commitment framework, and weigh indivisibility as a separate cause | Hobbes ch. 13 on anticipation, **close reading**; offensive advantage and preemption; Schelling's reciprocal fear of surprise attack; indivisibility and its critique (side payments, lotteries, linkage); Powell's unification of commitment problems as large, rapid shifts in bargaining power |

**Boss problem 1:** Two states dispute a territory worth 1. A would win a war with probability 0.6; war costs A 0.1 and B 0.15. (a) Find the bargaining range and show that every division in it gives both sides more than war. *(Formal.)* (b) Now A knows its own cost, but B's cost is uniform on $[0, 0.3]$ and private. A makes a take-it-or-leave-it demand $x$. Find A's optimal demand and the probability of war, and explain in two sentences why A rationally accepts that risk. *(Formal.)* (c) A pie worth 1 is divided each period, with discount factor 0.9. War ends the game: the winner takes the pie in every period from then on, and each side pays a one-time cost of 2. A's win probability is 0.6 today and falls to $p_2$ from the next period on; B, the rising state, makes the offers. Find the smallest $p_2$ at which peace survives, and the share of today's pie B must concede when $p_2 = 0.55$. *(Formal.)* (d) Close-read Thucydides' statement in Book I of the truest cause of the Peloponnesian War (Crawley translation): which rationalist mechanism does it describe, and which words decide it? *(Exegetical.)* Then, in 150 words: does "fear of a rising power" name anything the commitment-problem model of (c) leaves out? *(Evaluative.)*

### Module 2: Signalling resolve and crisis bargaining

How states make threats believable when talk is cheap, and what the answer implies about the crises we observe.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2.1 | Costly signals: tying hands and sinking costs | Tell the two ways a signal can separate resolved from unresolved types, and solve for each | Fearon's tying-hands vs sinking-costs distinction; mobilization as sunk cost; public commitment as tied hands; separating and pooling equilibria cited to [`grad-game-theory`](../grad-game-theory/syllabus.md) 4.4–4.5 and [`game-theory-refresher`](../game-theory-refresher/syllabus.md) 3.3 |
| 2.2 | Audience costs | Solve an escalation game with domestic audience costs, and weigh the evidence over whether they exist | Fearon's audience-cost model; semi-separating equilibria; comparative statics in the audience cost; survey-experiment evidence (Tomz) vs historical skeptics (Snyder and Borghard; Trachtenberg); audience costs in autocracies (Weeks); **evaluative** problems |
| 2.3 | Brinkmanship and the threat that leaves something to chance | Model a crisis as a competition in risk-taking, and say why a threat nobody would carry out can still deter | Schelling's threat that leaves something to chance; Powell's brinkmanship model; the shared risk of losing control; resolve as willingness to bear risk; chicken as vocabulary; nuclear strategy's history cited to [`international-relations`](../international-relations/syllabus.md) 2.4 |
| 2.4 | Deterrence and selection effects | Explain why the crises we observe are unrepresentative, and what that does to tests of deterrence | general vs immediate deterrence; extended deterrence; balance of power vs balance of interests; selection effects in crisis data (Fearon); why the militarily weaker side often prevails in observed crises; empirical estimation cited to [`econometrics`](../econometrics/syllabus.md) |

**Boss problem 2:** A challenger C has made a demand. Defender D is resolved with probability 0.4 (war is worth 0.2 to it) or unresolved (war is worth $-0.6$). D can concede quietly (payoff 0) or issue a public threat. After a threat, C backs down (D gets 1, C gets 0) or stands firm; then D fights (D gets its war payoff, C gets $-1$) or backs down (D pays an audience cost $a$, C gets 1). (a) With $a = 0.25$, find the perfect Bayesian equilibrium: the probability the unresolved type threatens, the probability C backs down, and the probability of war. *(Formal.)* (b) Show how C's probability of backing down and the probability of war change as $a$ rises, and find the audience cost above which the equilibrium changes form. Say what the new equilibrium is. *(Formal.)* (c) An invented presidential address announces a "red line," a carrier deployment and a request for war funding in the same breath. Diagnose which element is tying hands, which is sinking costs, and which (if any) is brinkmanship, and state what each needs to be true to work. *(Exegetical.)* (d) In 300–500 words: the survey experiments find audience costs and the historical studies find few leaders punished for backing down. Is there a single audience-cost story consistent with both? Any verdict passes if both bodies of evidence are stated at full strength, the selection effect of 2.4 is used, and the answer says what finding would change its mind. *(Evaluative.)*

### Module 3: Fighting and ending wars

War as the continuation of bargaining: what fighting teaches each side, how long it lasts, and why it stops.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3.1 | Fighting as information | Model battles as signals that update each side's estimate of the war's outcome and move the terms of settlement | Clausewitz, *On War* Book I ch. 1 (Graham), **close reading**; the war-as-bargaining-process view (Wagner, Slantchev, Filson and Werner); Bayesian updating from battle outcomes; convergence of expectations; why wars end before one side is disarmed |
| 3.2 | The war of attrition | Solve the war of attrition, and say who wins and how much is wasted | continuous-time war of attrition; mixed equilibrium with quit hazard $c/V$; full dissipation of the prize; asymmetric values and the counterintuitive winner; asymmetric pure equilibria; the incomplete-information version (stronger types hold out longer); all-pay auctions cited to [`grad-game-theory`](../grad-game-theory/syllabus.md) 4.3 |
| 3.3 | War termination and the leader's gamble | Explain why wars continue after information converges, through commitment problems and leaders' private stakes | commitment problems in termination and demands for absolute victory (Reiter); gambling for resurrection (Downs and Rocke; Goemans on leaders' post-war fates); regime type and punishment of losing leaders; **find-the-crux** problems |

**Boss problem 3:** (a) Two sides fight a war of attrition over a prize worth 1, each paying 0.25 per unit time. Find the symmetric mixed equilibrium quit rate, the expected duration, and the expected total cost; show the prize is fully dissipated. *(Formal.)* (b) Now the prize is worth 1 to A and 2 to B, costs unchanged. Find each side's equilibrium quit rate in the mixed equilibrium, the probability A wins, and the expected duration. Explain in two sentences why the side that values the prize less wins more often, and name the other equilibria the game has. *(Formal.)* (c) B believes A is strong with probability 0.5. A strong A wins each battle with probability 0.7, a weak one with probability 0.4. A wins the first two battles. Find B's posterior, and say in one sentence what the 3.1 model predicts happens to B's settlement offer. *(Formal.)* (d) An invented war: information has converged (both sides' estimates of the outcome agree), yet one side still demands its adversary's unconditional surrender. Name the crux between an information account and a commitment account of the continued fighting, and say what evidence about the adversary's post-war capabilities would separate them. *(Exegetical.)* Then, in 150 words, say whether gambling for resurrection is a third rationalist explanation or a departure from the unitary-actor assumption of Module 1. *(Evaluative.)*

### Module 4: Civil war and terrorism

The same models where one side is a state and the other is not: why rebels cannot disarm, and what violence by the weak is for.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4.1 | Civil war as bargaining with the state | Model civil-war onset and duration as commitment problems between a government and a group | Fearon's commitment problem toward a minority after state change; "sons of the soil" wars and why some civil wars last longer; state weakness and insurgency feasibility described in [`comparative-politics`](../comparative-politics/syllabus.md) 5.3 and cited |
| 4.2 | Settling civil wars: disarmament and guarantees | Compute when a peace deal survives the rebels' disarmament, and what a guarantor or power-sharing must supply | Walter's credible-commitment theory; the disarmament commitment problem; third-party guarantees as enforcement probability; power-sharing as a self-enforcing balance; spoilers; use-of-force law for interveners cited to [`international-relations`](../international-relations/syllabus.md) 4.2 |
| 4.3 | Terrorism as costly signalling | Read a terrorist campaign as a strategy aimed at an audience, and model two of the strategies | Kydd and Walter's five strategies (attrition, intimidation, provocation, spoiling, outbidding); spoiling as Bayesian updating about a peace partner; provocation and the government's response; Bueno de Mesquita on the quality of terrorist operatives; Lake's rational extremism; the ethics of terrorism not taught, cited to [`ethics`](../ethics/syllabus.md) |

**Boss problem 4:** A government G and a rebel group R divide a pie worth 1 per period, with discount factor 0.8. War now gives R victory with probability 0.4, costs each side a one-time 0.5, and hands the winner the pie forever. A settlement gives R a share $s$ per period but requires R to disarm; once disarmed R cannot fight, and G reneges on any deal nobody enforces. A third party enforces the deal with probability $g$, known at signing. (a) Find each side's war value, and show that without a guarantor ($g = 0$) no settlement exists. *(Formal.)* (b) With $g = 0.6$, find the range of shares $s$ both sides accept; then find the smallest $g$ at which any settlement exists. *(Formal.)* (c) Suppose instead R keeps enough arms to win with probability $p'$ after the deal, and G offers only what keeps R from fighting again. Show that R accepts only if $p' \geq 0.4$, and say in two sentences what this implies about settlements without outside enforcement. *(Formal.)* (d) An invented communiqué from an armed group claims an attack timed to the eve of a peace vote. Classify the strategy using Kydd and Walter's typology and justify it from the text. Then model its effect: moderates believe the peace partner is sincere with probability 0.7; a sincere partner fails to prevent such an attack with probability 0.3, an insincere one with probability 0.9. Find the moderates' posterior after one attack. *(Exegetical, then formal.)*

**Boss problem 5:** (a) Two states arm according to $\dot x = 0.3y - 0.5x + 1$ and $\dot y = 0.2x - 0.4y + 2$. Find the equilibrium arms levels, show the equilibrium is stable, and state in one line the general stability condition and what it means in words. *(Formal.)* (b) A sender threatens sanctions unless a target makes a concession costing it 0.5. The sender is resolved with probability 0.8; a resolved sender imposes sanctions if the target refuses, inflicting an immediate cost of 0.3, after which the target either concedes (cost 0.5) or holds out (further cost $s$, private to the target and uniform on $[0,1]$). Find which targets concede at the threat stage, the fraction of threats that end in imposed sanctions, and the success rate of imposed sanctions. Explain what a study of imposed sanctions alone would conclude. *(Formal.)* (c) Two invented alliance treaties: one pledges automatic military assistance if the ally is attacked; the other pledges assistance only if the ally did not provoke the attack, with the patron deciding what counts as provocation. Diagnose the trade-off each clause strikes between deterring the adversary and restraining the ally, using 5.3's concepts. *(Exegetical.)* (d) In 300–500 words: can a status-quo power reassure a rising rival by costly unilateral restraint, or does restraint only invite exploitation? Any verdict passes if the answer states the Kydd reassurance mechanism and the deterrence-model objection precisely, says what prior beliefs each needs, and names what evidence would tell the two situations apart. *(Evaluative.)*

### Module 6: Domestic politics and the international economy

| 6.3 | Trade wars and trade agreements as repeated games | Compute what punishment sustains a trade agreement, and why agreements build in escape clauses | the terms-of-trade prisoner's dilemma in reduced form (trade theory gap noted); trigger strategies and limited retaliation; the WTO's authorized retaliation as a punishment phase; imperfect monitoring and escape clauses (Downs and Rocke; Rosendorff and Milner); tariff preferences from lobbying cited to [`political-economy`](../political-economy/syllabus.md) |
| 6.4 | Interdependence and conflict | Weigh the opportunity-cost and costly-signalling accounts of commercial peace, and model economic ties as leverage | trade as a cost of war widening the bargaining range, and why that can also raise demands; costly signalling through capital markets (Gartzke, Li and Boehmer); weaponized interdependence (Farrell and Newman) as coercive leverage; Angell and 1914 cited to [`international-relations`](../international-relations/syllabus.md) 1.4 and 3.2 |

**Boss problem 6:** (a) Two countries play a trade game each period: both free trade gives 3 each, both tariff gives 2 each, and a unilateral tariff gives the imposer 4 and the victim 1. Find the smallest discount factor at which grim trigger sustains free trade. Then suppose retaliation is capped at $T$ periods of mutual tariffs before a return to free trade: derive the condition on $\delta$ and $T$, and find the smallest $T$ for $\delta = 0.8$ and for $\delta = 0.6$. *(Formal.)* (b) B makes a take-it-or-leave-it offer $x$ to A over a pie of 1; B will concede at most 0.6. A's executive alone would accept any $x \geq 0.2$, but A's legislature ratifies only if $x \geq L$.
