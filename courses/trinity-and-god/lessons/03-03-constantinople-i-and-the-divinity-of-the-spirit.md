# The Triune God · Lesson 3.3: Constantinople I (381) and the divinity of the Spirit

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 3: The dogma in its defining texts · Builds on: [3.1](03-01-nicaea.md), [3.2](03-02-one-ousia-three-hypostaseis.md) · Unlocks: [3.4](03-04-the-latin-rules-of-speech.md), [5.2](05-02-the-greek-doctrine-and-the-addition.md)

## Why this matters

The creed said at Mass is not Nicaea's. Nicaea's third article was four words long — "and in the Holy Spirit" — and everything after it was supplied in 381. What was supplied is strange. It praises the Spirit at length, gives him titles no creature could carry, and never once says that he is God or that he is of one substance with the Father. That omission is not a gap in the doctrine. It is the clearest surviving example of how the Church defines: fixing what must be held using the least contested words that will do the job.

## The idea

The party this creed was written against had already conceded the hard half of the fight. The **[Pneumatomachi](../reference.md#pneumatomachi)** — "Spirit-fighters", also called Macedonians after Macedonius of Constantinople, and drawn out of the Semi-Arian middle — had come round to the Son's uncreated divinity, some of them while still disliking *homoousios* as a formula. They then refused to take the same step for the Spirit, holding him to be outside the one indivisible Godhead: the highest of the things made, a minister, not a third who is adored. Tillemont's verdict is the fair one — by the end, denying the Spirit's divinity was their only error.

State their case at its strength, because it was strong. Scripture calls the Son God outright (John 1:1; John 20:28) and nowhere, in so many words, calls the Spirit God. The verbs used of the Spirit are verbs of being handled: he is sent, given, poured out. And the Church's older doxology — glory to the Father *through* the Son *in* the Holy Spirit — looked on its face like a ranking, which is exactly what Basil's opponents at Caesarea said when he changed the preposition ([`patristics` 3.2](../../patristics/lessons/03-02-basil-the-great.md)). A man could hold all of that and think himself a loyal Nicene.

In 381 Theodosius summoned about 150 Eastern bishops to Constantinople. Its first canon reaffirms the faith of the 318 fathers of Nicaea and anathematizes a list of heresies by name, "the Semi-Arians or Pneumatomachi" among them. And [the creed the Church received](../reference.md#the-creed-of-381) from that council expands the third article into the clause below. On the events and the politics, see [`church-history` 2.1](../../church-history/lessons/02-01-nicaea-and-the-imperial-church.md).

## Source

The clause on the Spirit, Greek from Schaff's *Creeds of Christendom*, English from Percival's translation in the *Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers* (both public domain):

> Καὶ εἰς τὸ Πνεῦμα τὸ Ἅγιον, τὸ κύριον, τὸ ζωοποιόν, τὸ ἐκ τοῦ πατρὸς ἐκπορευόμενον, τὸ σὺν πατρὶ καὶ υἱῷ συμπροσκυνούμενον καὶ συνδοξαζόμενον, τὸ λαλῆσαν διὰ τῶν προφητῶν.
>
> And we believe in the Holy Ghost, the Lord and Giver-of-Life, who proceedeth from the Father, who with the Father and the Son together is worshipped and glorified, who spake by the prophets.

## The argument

Four predicates, and what each one does.

1. **"The Lord" (*to kyrion*).** The Greek is neuter, agreeing with *pneuma*, so this is not a second mention of the "one Lord Jesus Christ" earlier in the creed but a title predicated of the Spirit himself. *In words:* the Spirit stands on the ruling side of the line between God and what God made.
2. **"Giver of life" (*to zoopoion*).** Creatures receive life; they do not bestow it. *In words:* the Spirit does the thing only God does.
3. **"Proceeding from the Father" (*to ek tou patros ekporeuomenon*).** The participle is lifted from John 15:26, where the Spirit of truth *proceedeth from the Father* (Douay-Rheims). It gives him an origin, distinguishes that origin from the Son's begetting without explaining either, and rules out the one origin a creature has — being made from nothing. *In words:* he comes from the Father's own being, and coming from is not being made.
4. **"With the Father and the Son together worshipped and glorified" (*symproskynoumenon kai syndoxazomenon*).** The Greek verb for adoration is not by itself reserved to God; it can cover reverence shown to a creature. The weight therefore falls on the *syn-*, the "together with", which both compounds carry: the Spirit is inside the single act of adoration and glory offered to the Father and the Son, not a further object standing beside it. *In words:* whatever the Spirit is, he is what you may adore when you adore God, which no creature ever is.

A fifth, "who spoke by the prophets", ties the adored Spirit to the one who has been speaking through scripture all along. Notice what the list does not contain: no *ousia*, no *hypostasis*, no *homoousios*, no philosophical term of any kind.

### Why worship and not homoousios

Three things make this a deliberate [economy of doctrinal language](../reference.md#economy-of-doctrinal-language) rather than a hedge.

First, it defines in the idiom of the argument that had actually won. Basil's case for the Spirit ran from the baptismal command through the rule that faith conforms to baptism and glory to faith, to the conclusion that the Spirit is numbered *with* the Father and the Son and glorified with them — the [doxological argument](../reference.md#the-doxological-argument) ([`patristics` 3.2](../../patristics/lessons/03-02-basil-the-great.md)). A council that defines in the vocabulary of that argument is standing on ground its hearers already occupy — every one of them was baptized into the three names.

Second, this is a **baptismal** text, said by everybody, not a treatise. *Homoousios* is a technical term whose own history had to be explained before Nicaea could impose it ([3.1](03-01-nicaea.md)). Requiring it a second time would have made the Church's public profession turn on a word most of the baptized could not parse.

Third — and this is what shows it to be prudence and not evasion — the same bishops said the plain thing in the register where plainness belongs. Writing to Damasus and the Western bishops the following year, the synod of 382 summarized the faith they had defended:

> According to this faith there is one Godhead, Power and Substance of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Ghost; the dignity being equal, and the majesty being equal in three perfect hypostases, i.e. three perfect persons.

One substance, three hypostases, all three — the Cappadocian settlement of [3.2](03-02-one-ousia-three-hypostaseis.md), stated without flinching, in a letter. The creed withholds the term; the bishops do not withhold the doctrine. What varies is the register of the document, not the belief.

### Where the text comes from

The acts of the council of 381 do not survive, and the Tome it issued is lost; we know of it only because the letter of 382 refers to it. The creed's first secure attestation is at Chalcedon in 451, where it was read out in the acts under a heading calling it the holy creed the 150 fathers set forth. Ephesus, fifty years after 381, neither read nor mentioned it in its recorded acts. Historians therefore differ about what the 150 did. Hort argued the maximal case, that the council set forth no creed at all; the commoner view, from Tillemont and Hefele, is that the bishops adopted and adjusted a baptismal creed already in Eastern use — Epiphanius's *Ancoratus*, written in 374, carries nearly this text. Handle it as source criticism, not scandal. The creed's authority does not rest on a chain of minutes from 381; it rests on its reception by the whole Church and its solemn reception at an ecumenical council in 451.

Reception is also what the creed then became. Fifty years on, Ephesus [prohibited a rival faith](../reference.md#the-prohibition-of-another-faith) — in the seventh of the canons attached to it, taken from its sixth session — that it is unlawful for anyone "to bring forward, or to write, or to compose a different (*hetera*) Faith as a rival to that established by the holy Fathers assembled with the Holy Ghost in Nicaea". That sentence is what Leo III, answering Charlemagne's ambassadors, and the Greeks at Florence long after him would press against the Latin addition to this very clause. Fix now what the objection is and is not: it is about *adding to the creed*, not about the doctrine added. Whether *hetera* means "contradictory" or "any further word at all" is [5.2](05-02-the-greek-doctrine-and-the-addition.md)'s question.

## Map of positions

| Position | The Son | The Spirit |
|---|---|---|
| Arians, Eunomians | a creature, however exalted | a creature |
| **Pneumatomachi** | uncreated, from the Father; some still resisting *homoousios* as a word | outside the one Godhead: the first and highest of the things made through the Son |
| Nicenes, as the Cappadocians state it | of one substance with the Father | of the same Godhead, adored and glorified with the Father and the Son |

The council condemned the first two rows by name in canon 1 and wrote the third row into the creed — but wrote it in the vocabulary of the last column of the third row, not the first.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean: does the clause bite?).** Hand the Source clause to a Pneumatomachian and require his signature. Run the predicates. "Lord" he might swallow, pleading a courtesy title. "Giver of life" he might gloss as an instrument through which the Father gives life. "Proceeding from the Father" he might read loosely, as coming forth on a mission. But he cannot sign the fourth: he does not adore what is made, and the clause does not merely place the Spirit near the object of adoration, it places him inside it, *syn-* with the Father and the Son. The doxological predicate is the operative one, and it is operative precisely where a metaphysical term would have let him quibble about words rather than about practice. Against a party formed by rejecting a word, define by an act.

**Example 2 (hard: where it strains).** Harnack pressed the other way: the clause is in entire harmony with the doctrine of the Spirit as it stood in the 360s, a Pneumatomachian could have subscribed it at a pinch, and therefore the council of 381 cannot have accepted this creed. Take the first half seriously — it is the cost of defining by economy, and an honest reader should feel it. The replies are three, and they are of different weights. (i) Canon 1 anathematizes the Pneumatomachi by name, so the council's mind about them is not in doubt whatever a clever man might have signed. (ii) The letter of 382 shows the same bishops holding one substance in three hypostases. (iii) Whether a text could be signed in bad faith is a different question from what the text says; *homoousios* itself was subscribed in 325 by men who meant something else, which is the whole story of the next fifty years ([3.2](03-02-one-ousia-three-hypostaseis.md)). What the objection does establish is why the Church did not stop here: the Latin texts of [3.4](03-04-the-latin-rules-of-speech.md) say flatly what 381 said doxologically.

## Watch out

- **"The council would not call the Spirit God" is not "the council doubted it."** Reticence in a baptismal formula is a choice of register, and reading it as a doctrinal reservation is the level error this course grades strictly. That the Holy Spirit is God is defined dogma, at the top of the [ladder of doctrinal authority](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-04-the-ladder-of-doctrinal-authority.md) — the creed defines it in the predicates it uses, not in the terms it declines.
- **"Proceeding from the Father" in 381 does not settle the filioque.** The participle is quoted from John 15:26 against the claim that the Spirit is made; it says nothing for or against the Son's part in his origin, which is why both traditions read the clause as their own. That argument is [5.1](05-01-the-latin-doctrine.md) and [5.2](05-02-the-greek-doctrine-and-the-addition.md), not this lesson.
- **Do not turn the Spirit into a shared attribute of the other two.** "The Spirit is just God's power at work in us" is modalism if it means there is no third someone there. Every verb in the clause — proceeds, is adored, spoke — takes a subject, and a power is not adored together with the one whose power it is.

## One-liner

> Nicaea found a word for the Son; Constantinople defined the Spirit by what may be done to him — adored and glorified inside the one worship of the Father and the Son — because no creature may be.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢) *(Exegetical.)*** Reread the clause in the Source.

(a) It never says the Spirit is God and never calls him consubstantial. Name **two** predicates that nonetheless place him on the Creator's side of the line, and say in one sentence each what makes that predicate impossible for a creature.

(b) One predicate is quoted from scripture. Name it and the passage, state what it establishes, and state one thing it deliberately leaves open. Two sentences for (b).

**P2 (🟡) *(Exegetical.)*** An invented parish handout:

> "Why does the Creed never say the Holy Spirit is God? Because the Fathers were careful men. They knew the Spirit is the Father's own breath, the power by which he and the Son act within us, and a power is not a third God. That is why we adore the Spirit only together with the Father and the Son, and why the Church has always left the Spirit's divinity an open question for theologians to weigh."

(a) Name the **two** distinct errors, one doctrinal and one about authority, with the right label for each. (b) For each error, name the clause of the creed it runs into. 150 words or fewer in total.

**P3 (🔴, optional) *(Exegetical.)*** Take as given: the acts of the council of 381 do not survive and its Tome is lost; the creed's earliest secure attestation is its reading at Chalcedon in 451 under a heading ascribing it to the 150 fathers; the recorded acts of Ephesus in 431 neither read nor mention it; Epiphanius's *Ancoratus* of 374 carries nearly the same text.

(a) State the strongest conclusion about the council's work that this evidence supports, and one conclusion it does **not** support. (b) A critic concludes that the creed's authority is therefore doubtful. Name the move that answers him, and say what that answer has to concede. 150 words or fewer in total.

<details>
<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical.)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** two of the three below, with the reason attached; the second must be one of the two, since it is the predicate that actually decides the case.
- **"Giver of life"** — bestowing life is a work proper to God; a creature receives life and at most transmits what it was given.
- **"With the Father and the Son together worshipped and glorified"** — the *syn-* in both compounds puts the Spirit inside the single act of adoration offered to the Father and the Son, and that act is not offered to anything made.
- **"The Lord"** — a divine title, and the neuter form shows it is predicated of the Spirit rather than repeating the Son's title.

**Must hit, strict (b):** **"proceeding from the Father"**, quoted from **John 15:26**. It establishes that the Spirit has an origin from the Father and therefore is not made from nothing — coming forth from the Father's own being is not creation. It deliberately leaves open *how* that origin differs from the Son's begetting, and (credit) says nothing either way about the Son's part in it.

**Wrong turns:** claiming the clause implicitly says *homoousios*, which is exactly the word it declines; treating "who spoke by the prophets" as a divine predicate — prophets and angels also speak for God, and that phrase identifies the Spirit rather than ranking him; reading "proceeding" as the Spirit's being sent into the world, which is a *mission* and belongs to Module 6, not the eternal origin this participle names.

**Model answer:** (a) "Giver of life": to give life is God's own work, and a creature can only pass on life it has received. "Worshipped and glorified together with the Father and the Son": the "together with" places the Spirit inside the one act of adoration, and the Church adores nothing that is made. (b) "Proceeding from the Father", from John 15:26. It establishes that the Spirit's origin is from the Father himself and so is not a making out of nothing; it leaves open how this differs from the Son's begetting, and says nothing about whether the Son has a part in it.

---

**P2** *(Exegetical.)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** two errors, correctly labelled.
1. **Modalism** (or Sabellianism): reducing the Spirit to "the power by which the Father and the Son act" makes him an attribute or operation of the other two rather than a distinct person. An answer that says "it denies that the Spirit is a third someone" gets the credit even without the label; an answer that says "subordinationism" does not, and should notice that the handout is not ranking the Spirit lower but dissolving him.
2. **An error about authority — demoting a defined truth to a school opinion.** That the Holy Spirit is God is defined dogma, not an open question for theologians. This is the first of the three errors this course grades strictly, and it is an exegetical error, not a difference of opinion.

**Must hit, strict (b):**
- Against the modalism: **"proceeding from the Father"**, and equally **"who spoke by the prophets"** — both are predicates with a subject. A power does not proceed from anyone or speak to anyone; and nothing is adored *together with* the one whose power it is.
- Against the demotion: **"with the Father and the Son together is worshipped and glorified"** — the council defined the Spirit's divinity precisely there, in the language of worship. The absence of the word "God" is the creed's chosen idiom, not a reservation.

**Wrong turns:** accepting the handout's first sentence as a fair account of the council's reticence and quarrelling only with the last sentence — the reticence is real, the inference from it is the error; calling the handout tritheist, which is the accusation it is trying to avoid; answering that the Church later supplied the word "God", which is true but does not identify what is wrong *here*.

**Model answer:** Two errors. First, modalism: calling the Spirit "the power by which the Father and the Son act" makes him an operation of the other two, not a third person. The creed's own verbs refuse this — one who proceeds from the Father and spoke by the prophets is a subject, and a power is not adored alongside the one whose power it is. Second, an authority error: the Spirit's divinity is defined dogma, not an open question, and demoting a defined truth to a school opinion is graded as strictly as denying it. The creed defines it in the clause "with the Father and the Son together is worshipped and glorified"; withholding the word "God" was a choice of idiom, not a suspension of judgement.

---

**P3** *(Exegetical.)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** what it supports — **the text was not composed from nothing in 381**; a creed of nearly this wording was already circulating in the East by 374, so the council's work was very likely adoption and adjustment of an existing baptismal creed rather than fresh composition, and our secure link between that text and the 150 fathers comes only from 451. What it does **not** support: that the council held something other than what the creed says (canon 1 and the letter of 382 are independent evidence of its mind), nor Hort's maximal claim that the council issued no creed at all — silence in the acts of Ephesus is silence, not testimony.

**Must hit, strict (b):** the move is that the creed's authority rests on **the Church's reception of it**, sealed by an ecumenical council at Chalcedon, not on an unbroken documentary chain from 381. The concession: the question of authorship and of what exactly the 150 did is genuinely open, and a Catholic may hold that they adapted rather than composed the text without touching the creed's standing.

**Wrong turns:** treating the 451 attestation as showing the creed was forged or back-dated — the evidence is silence in the record, and the acts of 381 are lost wholesale, not selectively; supposing that a doubt about authorship is a doubt about doctrine; answering only "the Church says so" without naming reception and the conciliar act that sealed it.

**Model answer:** (a) It supports the conclusion that the council adopted and adjusted a creed already in Eastern use rather than composing one, since Epiphanius has nearly the same text seven years earlier, and that the text's link to the 150 fathers is attested only from 451. It does not support the conclusion that the council taught anything other than what the creed says: canon 1 names the Pneumatomachi and the letter of 382 states one substance in three hypostases. Nor does the silence of Ephesus establish that no creed was issued. (b) The answer is that the creed binds by the Church's reception of it, ratified at Chalcedon, not by the survival of the minutes of 381. It concedes that authorship is a genuinely open historical question.

## Flashback

**From Lesson [3.1](03-01-nicaea.md) (Nicaea (325)):** *(Exegetical.)* Arius argued from a premise his opponents granted in full: God is one, unoriginate and altogether simple. (a) Using that premise only, say why a Son who is *from* the Father looked to him impossible unless the Son were produced — name the two ways of coming from a source that simplicity rules out, and what each would do to God. (b) His argument then treats two classes as exhaustive: name them, and say whether that step rests on scripture or on metaphysics. (c) The creed answers by supplying a third term. Name it, say why the word by itself was not enough, and name the clause that had to fix its sense. **Two sentences for (a); one sentence each for (b) and (c).**

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):** **division** — a Son cut off as a piece of the Father would give God parts, and the altogether simple has no parts; and **emanation** — a Son flowing out of the Father would mean something happening to God, and the unoriginate does not change. With both gone, the only mechanism left is production by the Father's will, which is how everything else comes from God — so the Son falls among things produced, and Arius protected his dignity as far as that road allowed: before all ages, and the one through whom everything else was made.

**Must hit, strict (b):** **unoriginate** and **produced**, treated as an exhaustive pair — and the step is metaphysical, not scriptural: no text sets up the division, and it is the hinge of the whole argument.

**Must hit, strict (c):** ***begotten***. Arius used the word himself — his own creed of 328 has the Son begotten of the Father before all ages — so it needed **"of the essence of the Father"** (*ek tēs ousias tou patros*) to fix that the Son is from what the Father *is* rather than from what the Father decides, with "begotten, not made" keeping begetting and making apart.

**Wrong turns:** treating Arius as someone who thought little of Christ, when the pressure on him is simplicity and everyone in the argument held it; naming premise 1 as what the creed denies, when the Nicenes affirmed it too and the council attacked the exhaustive pair instead; answering (c) with *homoousios* — the creed's strongest word, but it says what the Son *is* and is not the term that refuses the pair.

**Model answer:** (a) A piece cut off would divide God, and simplicity forbids parts; an emanation would mean a change in him, and the unoriginate does not change. That leaves production by will, the way everything else comes from God, so the Son lands on the produced side however exalted he is made. (b) Unoriginate and produced, taken as the only two classes there are — a metaphysical claim, not a scriptural one. (c) *Begotten*, which Arius could say himself, and which needed "of the essence of the Father" to mean coming from what the Father is rather than from what he wills.

</details>
