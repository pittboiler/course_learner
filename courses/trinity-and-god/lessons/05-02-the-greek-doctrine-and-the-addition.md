# The Triune God · Lesson 5.2: The Greek doctrine, and the history of the addition

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 5: The filioque · Builds on: [5.1 The Latin doctrine](05-01-the-latin-doctrine.md), [3.3 Constantinople I and the divinity of the Spirit](03-03-constantinople-i-and-the-divinity-of-the-spirit.md) · Unlocks: [5.3 The clarifications](05-03-the-clarifications.md)

## Why this matters

[5.1](05-01-the-latin-doctrine.md) gave the Latin doctrine and its argument. If you now learn the Eastern position as the negation of that — "they leave the words out" — you have learned nothing, because a subtraction is not a doctrine. The Greek East holds a positive teaching about how God is one, worked out before the quarrel began. This lesson states it the way a Greek theologian states it, and only then asks what a Greek reader hears in the Latin creed. Whether the two accounts are one faith in two grammars is [5.3](05-03-the-clarifications.md)'s question; whether a Catholic can answer the Orthodox case belongs to [`apologetics-catholic-claims`](../../apologetics-catholic-claims/syllabus.md). Neither is argued here.

## The idea

Ask a Latin theologian why God is one and the answer is about a nature: one simple divine essence, wholly possessed by three. Ask a Greek theologian and the answer is about a person. God is one because there is one *who is from no one* — the Father, the unbegotten, from whom the Son is begotten and the Spirit proceeds. Divine unity is not a shared something; it is a single source. This is the [monarchy of the Father](../reference.md#monarchy-of-the-father): *monarchia*, from *monos* and *archē*, "single origin."

Two things follow.

First, the Father alone is called *aitia*, **cause**; the Son and Spirit are each *aitiaton*, caused. That word alarms a Latin ear, since in Latin a cause makes something less than itself. In Greek Trinitarian usage it does not: the Father gives the whole divine nature entire, so what is caused is not a lesser God but a distinct way of possessing the one Godhead. *Aitia* says where the three stand to one another ([3.1](03-01-nicaea.md)), not how much divinity each has.

Second, monarchy does the work that relation does in the Latin scheme ([4.4](04-04-four-relations-three-persons.md)). Ask a Greek why there are not three gods: because all three are referred back to one cause. Give the Spirit a second point of origin and the one thing holding the three together as one God has been split. So the *filioque* is not, inside this grammar, a small addition — it is a change to the reason God is one.

## Source

John of Damascus, *An Exposition of the Orthodox Faith* I.8 (Salmond, *Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers*, second series vol. 9, public domain), written around 743 — before the quarrel, and so not a polemic:

> And we speak likewise of the Holy Spirit as from the Father, and call Him the Spirit of the Father. And we do not speak of the Spirit as from the Son: but yet we call Him the Spirit of the Son. ... And we confess that He is manifested and imparted to us through the Son.

Three moves in fifty words. A denial of origin from the Son. An affirmation of a real relation to the Son — "the Spirit of the Son," grounded on Romans 8:9. And a third term for what that relation is: *through the Son*. Nothing is withheld; something different is said. In I.12 he gives the rule: "cause, source, begetter" are "to be ascribed to the Father alone."

## The argument

The Greek doctrine, in the order a Greek theologian builds it.

1. **What the three have in common is the** ***ousia***, **the one Godhead; what distinguishes them is** ***tropos hyparxeos***, **manner of existing.** *In words:* the persons never differ in what they are, only in how each one is.
2. **One of the three is from no one.** *In words:* the Father is unbegotten; that is his personal property.
3. **The Father alone is** ***aitia***. *In words:* everything in God that is not the Father comes from the Father, and from nowhere else.
4. **Generation and procession are two distinct modes of coming from that one cause, and we cannot say how they differ.** *In words:* the Son is begotten and the Spirit proceeds; that they differ is revealed, what the difference consists in is not.
5. **Therefore the Spirit's** ***hyparxis*** — **his very existing as a person — is from the Father.** *In words:* ask where the Spirit gets his being and the answer has exactly one term in it.
6. **The Son is not idle in this.** *In words:* the Spirit is the Spirit *of the Son*, rests on him, is sent by him, shines out through him — all of which the tradition says with the preposition *dia*, [through the Son](../reference.md#through-the-son), and none of which makes him a cause.

Step 6 is where the whole dispute lives.

### The two verbs

John 15:26, Greek: *to pneuma tēs alētheias ho para tou patros ekporeuetai*. Vulgate: *qui a Patre procedit*. Douay-Rheims: "the Spirit of truth, who proceedeth from the Father."

*Ekporeuomai* (my translation: **to issue out from**) is a verb of exit from a point of origin. The creed of 381 sharpens it further: *to ek tou patros ekporeuomenon*. Note the change — John writes *para tou patros*, "from beside the Father"; the creed writes *ek tou patros*, "out of the Father." The council chose the preposition of origin, and in Greek theological usage thereafter *ekporeusis* names one thing: the Spirit's derivation of existence from the cause.

*Procedere* (my translation: **to come forth, to go forward**) is much wider. The Vulgate uses it for *ekporeuetai* at John 15:26 — and also, at John 8:42, for a different Greek verb, *exēlthon*: *ego enim ex Deo processi*, "For from God I proceeded" (Douay-Rheims). One Latin verb, two Greek verbs; *procedere* names any coming-forth whatever and specifies no cause. The asymmetry is what the Greek argument turns on: [ekporeusis and processio](../reference.md#ekporeusis-and-processio).

Now the consequence. A Latin hears *ex Patre Filioque procedit* as: the Spirit comes forth from Father and Son. Put that creed into Greek and one verb is available — *ekporeuetai* — so a Greek reads: the Spirit takes his existence out of the Father and out of the Son. That is two causes in God; or, if the Son's causality is derived, a first cause and a second, with the Son promoted into an origin he does not have. The objection is not fastidiousness about vocabulary: the Latin creed, read in the language the creed was written in, says something the Greek tradition holds false.

### How the clause entered the creed

The events belong to [`church-history` 4.2](../../church-history/lessons/04-02-east-and-west-the-long-estrangement.md), which cedes the doctrine here. The Latin creed with *Filioque* appears in Visigothic Spain around the Third Council of Toledo (589); who inserted it is not known. It spread through Francia, was defended at Friuli (796) and Aachen (809), and reached Rome late — Leo III approved the doctrine while declining to alter the creed, and the clause is generally dated at Rome to 1014.

That history carries a second Greek objection, canonical rather than doctrinal. The acts of Ephesus (431) forbid composing *a different faith* as a rival to the one established at Nicaea (P3 quotes it). The complaint is that part of the Church altered a creed ratified by ecumenical councils, without a council. Keep it apart from the doctrinal question: it is about *adding*, not about what was added. See [the canonical objection to the addition](../reference.md#the-canonical-objection-to-the-addition).

## Map of positions

| Position | Does the Son have a role in the Spirit's **eternal being**? | The key word |
|---|---|---|
| **John of Damascus** (d. c. 749) | From the Father; the Spirit *of the Son*; manifested and given *through* the Son | *dia tou Huiou* |
| **Photius** (*Mystagogy*, c. 885) | No. "Through the Son" is read of the temporal sending, not of eternal origin | *ek monou tou Patros*, from the Father alone |
| **Gregory II of Cyprus** (Council of Blachernae, 1285) and **Gregory Palamas** (14th c.) | *Hypostasis* from the Father alone, but he eternally **shines forth** through the Son: a real eternal relation that is not origination | eternal manifestation, *ekphansis* |
| **The Latin doctrine** ([5.1](05-01-the-latin-doctrine.md)) | Yes: from Father and Son as from one principle, by a single spiration; Latins read *dia tou Huiou* as saying this | *tamquam ab uno principio* |

Rows two and three are both Greek and they disagree: "the Greek position" is a tradition with an internal argument, not a sentence.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean: the creed's own clause).** The Greek of 381 reads *to ek tou patros ekporeuomenon* — "the one who issues out from the Father." Insert the Latin clause and translate back: *to ek tou patros kai tou Huiou ekporeuomenon*. Now run steps 3 and 5. Step 3 says every term after "out from" names a cause, and the clause has put two names there. The sentence now asserts that the Spirit's existence derives from two causes — and since the Son's own existence is from the Father, the Godhead acquires a chain: Father, then Son, then Spirit. A Greek theologian sees two failures at once: the monarchy is gone, so the reason God is one is gone; and the Spirit has been pushed a step further out than the Son, which looks like rank. Neither is what a Latin means. Both are what the Greek sentence says. That gap, not a dispute about the Spirit's divinity, is the filioque question.

**Example 2 (hard: where the Greek position strains).** Press step 6. If "through the Son" is not about eternal origin, what *is* it about? Photius gives the tight answer: the Spirit's temporal mission, the sending in time. Clean — but it must explain away much Greek material that sounds eternal, including the Damascene's own I.12, which has the Spirit proceeding "from the Father through the Son" in the same breath as the eternal processions, and Cyril of Alexandria calling the Spirit "proper to" the Son.

The Byzantine answer is Gregory II of Cyprus at Blachernae in 1285, developed by Palamas: distinguish the Spirit's *existence*, which is from the Father alone, from his [eternal manifestation](../reference.md#eternal-manifestation) or shining-forth, which is through and from the Son and is not a second origin. This is a real theological move, not an evasion — it uses the existence-and-manifestation grammar the Greek tradition already uses elsewhere, and a council of the Eastern church adopted it. Latin critics press one question: is eternal manifestation a genuine distinction, or the filioque renamed, or nothing at all? Its defenders answer that it is the distinction their theology draws everywhere else, and that refusing it refuses a grammar rather than exposing an error.

## Watch out

- **"They just deny the filioque" is the error this lesson exists to prevent.** The Greek East affirms the monarchy of the Father, the Father as sole *aitia*, the Spirit as the Spirit of the Son, and a relation to the Son signified by *dia*. Someone who can only report a denial cannot state the position at all — and Boss 5 grades exactly that.
- **Monarchy is not subordinationism.** "The Father is cause, the Son and Spirit are caused" sounds like ranking, and if you let *aitia* mean what it means in creatures you land in [subordinationism](../reference.md#subordinationism). The Greek tradition blocks this: the cause communicates the whole and undiminished Godhead, so being caused is a manner of existing, not a grade of divinity — which is what Nicaea's "true God from true God" guards.
- **Photius is not "the Greek position,"** only its sharpest form: Gregory of Cyprus and Palamas hold more about the Son's eternal role, and a summary that cannot tell them apart has flattened a tradition into a slogan.
- **Do not fuse the two objections.** The canonical one — a creed altered unilaterally — could be granted without touching the doctrinal one; Leo III in 809 held the doctrine and refused the insertion.
- **This lesson does not qualify Catholic doctrine.** That the Spirit proceeds from the Father and the Son is defined — Lyons II, Florence — and so *de fide* on the ladder in [`fundamental-theology` 4.4](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-04-the-ladder-of-doctrinal-authority.md). Nothing above softens that; what is supplied is an accurate statement of what the other tradition holds.

## One-liner

> God is one, for a Greek theologian, because one person is the source of all that is God — and the quarrel begins the moment *ekporeusis*, derivation from that source, is rendered by *procedere*, which names any coming-forth at all.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢) *(Exegetical.)*** Three texts, in transliterated Greek, the Vulgate and the Douay-Rheims:

- John 15:26 — Greek: *to pneuma tēs alētheias ho para tou patros ekporeuetai* · Vulgate: *qui a Patre procedit* · Douay-Rheims: "who proceedeth from the Father."
- John 8:42 — Greek: *ego gar ek tou theou exēlthon, kai hēkō* · Vulgate: *ego enim ex Deo processi, et veni* · Douay-Rheims: "For from God I proceeded, and came."
- Creed of 381 — Greek: *to ek tou patros ekporeuomenon*.

(a) Using only these lines, state the fact about *procedere* that the Greek argument turns on, and say what follows for the question "does the Latin creed's *procedit* assert what *ekporeuomenon* asserts?" (b) Name the one word the creed of 381 changes from John 15:26, and say what the change does to the Greek reading. Two sentences per part.

**P2 (🟡) *(Exegetical.)*** An **invented** parish bulletin item, not the words of any real person:

> **Ecumenical corner.** Next Sunday we welcome our Orthodox neighbours. A word on the one thing that divides us: the Creed. We say the Spirit proceeds "from the Father and the Son"; they simply leave the last three words out. So the Orthodox position is a shorter creed, not a different belief — the same faith, stopping earlier out of respect for the older wording. Nothing doctrinal is at stake, only a preference for fourth-century text.

Name three distinct things this gets wrong about the Greek position, one sentence each, and say which two positions from the lesson's table it cannot tell apart. 150 words or fewer.

**P3 (🔴, optional) *(Exegetical.)*** From the acts of the Council of Ephesus (431), the decree carried as canon 7 (Percival, *Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers*, second series vol. 14, public domain):

> When these things had been read, the holy Synod decreed that it is unlawful for any man to bring forward, or to write, or to compose a different (*heteran*) Faith as a rival to that established by the holy Fathers assembled with the Holy Ghost in Nicæa.

(a) State in your own words exactly what this forbids, and name the single word on which a Latin reply turns. (b) Say what the canonical objection, granted in full, would and would not establish about the doctrine taught in [5.1](05-01-the-latin-doctrine.md). Two sentences per part.

<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical.)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** the Vulgate uses *procedere* to render two different Greek verbs — *ekporeuetai* at John 15:26 and *exēlthon* at John 8:42. So *procedere* is a wide word for any coming-forth and does not by itself name derivation from a cause, while *ekporeuomai* in Greek theological usage does. It follows that *procedit* and *ekporeuomenon* are not equivalent terms, so the Latin clause and its Greek translation do not make the same claim. Credit for seeing that this cuts both ways: the same asymmetry is what allows a Latin to say the clause was never meant in the narrow Greek sense.

**Must hit, strict (b):** *para* ("from beside") becomes *ek* ("out of"). The creed's preposition is the one of origin, so the creed states derivation from the Father more sharply than John's own wording does — which strengthens the reading on which *ekporeuomenon* names hypostatic origin, and so makes an inserted "and the Son" a claim about origin rather than about mission.

**Wrong turns:** saying the Vulgate mistranslates John 15:26 — it does not, *procedit* is a fair rendering, merely a wider word. Concluding from (a) that the Latin doctrine is false; what (a) shows is that the two creeds do not say the same thing in the same words. Asserting that John 15:26 itself reads *ek*.

**Model answer:** (a) One Latin verb covers two Greek ones: *procedere* translates *ekporeuetai* in 15:26 and *exēlthon* in 8:42, so it names any coming-forth and specifies no cause, whereas *ekporeuomai* names derivation from the source. So *procedit* does not assert what *ekporeuomenon* asserts — the Latin word is broader, which is why the clause reads differently in the two languages. (b) *Para* becomes *ek*. The council picked the preposition of origin, so the creed's own wording pushes *ekporeuomenon* toward hypostatic origin, and an added "and the Son" then reads as naming a second origin.

---

**P2** *(Exegetical.)*

**Must hit, strict (any three of the following four):** (1) the Greek position is a positive doctrine — the monarchy of the Father, the Father alone as *aitia*, the reason God is one — not the deletion of three words; (2) something doctrinal is claimed, because on the Greek reading of *ekporeuomai* the clause asserts a second origin in the Godhead, which the Greek tradition denies, so this is not a preference about wording; (3) the Greek tradition affirms a real relation of the Spirit to the Son — "Spirit of the Son," *dia tou Huiou* — so "the same faith, stopping earlier" misdescribes both what is denied and what is affirmed; (4) two objections are being collapsed into one, since the complaint about altering a conciliar creed is canonical and separate from the doctrinal complaint.

**Cannot tell apart:** Photius's *ek monou tou Patros*, on which the Son has no role in the Spirit's eternal being, and the Blachernae–Palamite position, on which the Spirit's *hypostasis* is from the Father alone while he eternally shines forth through the Son. Both look like "leaving the words out" from outside, and they are different doctrines.

**Wrong turns:** answering by arguing that the Latin doctrine is correct — not asked, and the Orthodox case is not argued in this course. Attributing to the Orthodox a denial of the Spirit's divinity or of his relation to the Son. Giving one error in three rewordings.

**Model answer:** First, it treats a positive doctrine as a subtraction: the East teaches the monarchy of the Father, the one *aitia* from whom Son and Spirit come, which is its account of why God is one. Second, something doctrinal is at stake: read in Greek the clause makes the Son a second origin of the Spirit's existence, which the East denies, so this is not a preference about wording. Third, the East affirms a real eternal relation of the Spirit to the Son — the Spirit *of* the Son, manifested *through* the Son — so "same faith, stopping earlier" misstates the affirmation as well as the denial. It cannot distinguish Photius, for whom "through the Son" is temporal only, from Gregory of Cyprus and Palamas, for whom there is an eternal manifestation through the Son.

---

**P3** *(Exegetical.)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** it forbids bringing forward, writing or composing *a different faith* set up as a rival to the one established at Nicaea — a prohibition on rival creeds, aimed in 431 at the Nestorian controversy. The word a Latin reply turns on is *heteran*, "different": the reply is that an explanatory clause is not a *different* faith, and that the creed of 381 had itself already expanded Nicaea's text. Credit for naming the Greek counter — that whatever was intended, a part of the Church altered a conciliar creed without a council.

**Must hit, strict (b):** granted in full, it establishes a canonical conclusion only — that the clause ought not to have been inserted into the conciliar creed by the West acting alone. It establishes nothing about whether the doctrine the clause expresses is true, because the two questions are independent; a theologian can hold the doctrine and regret the insertion, which is roughly Leo III's position in 809, and the modern Catholic practice of reciting the Greek creed without the clause shows the same separation.

**Wrong turns:** reading the canon as forbidding all doctrinal development, which would condemn 381 as well. Assuming the canon was aimed at the filioque. Treating the canonical objection as settling the doctrinal one, or the doctrinal one as dissolving the canonical one.

**Model answer:** (a) It forbids composing or putting forward a *different* faith as a rival to Nicaea's — a rule against competing creeds, made in 431 against the rival formulas of that controversy. The reply turns on *heteran*: a Latin argues that a clarifying addition is not a different faith, and that 381 had already enlarged Nicaea. (b) Granted entirely, it shows only that the West should not have altered the common creed on its own authority. It says nothing about the truth of the doctrine, which is why Leo III could approve the teaching and still refuse to change the creed.

## Flashback

**From Lesson [4.5](04-05-common-proper-appropriated.md) (What is common, what is proper, what is appropriated):** *(Exegetical.)* Sort each of these into **common**, **proper** or **appropriated**, with the reason in one clause; then state in one sentence the rule that decides all three. **Two sentences per item at most.**

(i) *Gift*, said of the Holy Spirit.
(ii) *just*, said of God.
(iii) The scheme Aquinas quotes from Augustine at ST I q.39 a.8: "Unity is in the Father, equality in the Son, and in the Holy Ghost is the concord of equality and unity."

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (i):** **proper.** ST I q.38 a.2 holds that Gift, taken personally in God, is the proper name of the Holy Spirit: love is the first gift, through which every free gift is given, and the Spirit proceeds as Love. The reply to that article's second objection gives the reason in the terms this sort needs — the name Gift implies belonging to the giver through origin, and so imports the property of the Spirit's origin, his procession.

**Must hit, strict (ii):** **common** — the tradition's *essential*. Justice is a perfection of the one nature and names no relation of origin, so each person is wholly just and the three are one just God, said in the singular.

**Must hit, strict (iii):** **appropriated.** Unity, equality and union name no relation of origin, so by the rule they are common; they are fastened to single persons only by likeness to what is proper to each — unity to the Father, who is principle without principle; equality to the Son, principle from a principle; union to the Spirit, who proceeds from two (q.39 a.8) — and never exclusively (q.39 a.7 ad 1).

**The rule:** whatever names a relation of origin, or follows immediately on one, is proper to one person; everything else belongs to the one nature and so is common; and a common attribute may still be assigned to one person to make him manifest, which is appropriation, and it stays wholly true of the other two.

**Wrong turns:** calling (iii) proper because Aquinas endorses the scheme — he endorses it as *fitting*, which is precisely what an appropriation is; calling (i) merely appropriated because the Son too is given, when q.38 a.2 ad 1 answers that the Son is given out of the Father's love while the Spirit is called Gift because he himself proceeds as Love; assigning (ii) to a person because justice is often preached of one, when an appropriation has to be made by the text or the tradition being read and "God is just" on its own is simply common.

**Model answer:** (i) Proper: Gift is a personal name of the Holy Spirit, because a gift belongs to the giver by origin, so the name carries his procession. (ii) Common: justice is a perfection of the one nature, true wholly of each person and of the three as one just God. (iii) Appropriated: unity, equality and union are common attributes assigned to single persons by their likeness to what is proper to each — the principle without principle, the principle from a principle, the one who proceeds from both — and they remain true of all three. The rule: only relations of origin are proper; everything else is common; a common thing fastened to one person to manifest him is appropriated, never exclusively.

</details>
