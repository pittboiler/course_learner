# The Triune God · Lesson 2.3: The Holy Spirit in the New Testament

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 2: The Trinity revealed · Builds on: [2.1 The one God of Israel](02-01-the-one-god-of-israel.md), [2.2 The Father and the Son in the New Testament](02-02-the-father-and-the-son-in-the-new-testament.md) · Unlocks: [2.4 From confession to creed](02-04-from-confession-to-creed.md), [3.3 Constantinople I and the divinity of the Spirit](03-03-constantinople-i-and-the-divinity-of-the-spirit.md)

## Why this matters

In [2.2](02-02-the-father-and-the-son-in-the-new-testament.md) you could point. The Word was God; Thomas falls down and says *my Lord and my God*. Whatever the arguments about those verses, there are sentences to argue *about*.

For the Spirit there is no such sentence. Nowhere in the New Testament is the Holy Spirit called God in so many words. That is not a modern discovery; it is a fact the fourth century worked with openly, and a Catholic who says "Scripture plainly calls the Holy Spirit God" will be handed a concordance and will lose. The honest statement is harder and far more useful: the divinity of the Holy Spirit is **defined dogma**, at the top of the [ladder of doctrinal authority](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-04-the-ladder-of-doctrinal-authority.md), and the road to it runs not through a proof-text but through what the Spirit *does* and how the Church *prays*.

## The idea

Keep two questions apart, because the texts answer them unevenly.

1. **Is the Spirit someone?** Distinct from the Father and from the Son — or is "the Spirit of God" just a way of naming God's own power at work, as it mostly is in the prophets ([`old-testament`](../../old-testament/syllabus.md) 4.5)?
2. **Is that someone God?**

On the first question the New Testament is loud. On the second it is indirect throughout. And the evidence for the second comes in three streams, none of which is a predication of deity:

- **What the Spirit does.** He searches God's own depths, gives life, sanctifies, makes us sons, raises the dead.
- **Where the Spirit is ranked.** The triadic formulas set him beside the Father and the Son in baptism, in blessing, in the ordering of the Church.
- **How the Spirit is spoken of.** He is sent, he teaches, he bears witness, he asks, he is grieved, he is lied to.

A fourth-century argument has to move from those to "therefore God," and the move needs a premise scripture does not supply. Notice what that already rules out: the dogma is not read off a verse. What is defined is the doctrine, not a reading of any one text, and Catholic authors have weighted these texts differently ever since.

## Source

Paul, 1 Corinthians 2:10-11 (Douay-Rheims):

> But to us God hath revealed them, by his Spirit. For the Spirit searcheth all things, yea, the deep things of God. For what man knoweth the things of a man, but the spirit of a man that is in him? So the things also that are of God no man knoweth, but the Spirit of God.

Two things happen here, and only one of them helps.

**What helps.** The Spirit knows God's own interiority — "the deep things of God" — and Paul's contrast is not that the Spirit knows *more* than we do but that he knows from *inside*. God's self-knowledge is not something a creature can be let into. Every fourth-century writer on the Spirit reaches for this verse.

**What cuts the other way.** The analogy is a man's own spirit knowing the man. Read flatly, that makes the Spirit God's self-knowledge rather than a distinct someone — which is a modalist reading, and the text does not by itself block it. What blocks it is the Paraclete material, where the Spirit is sent by another and speaks of another. The verse is strong on divinity, weak on distinction; John is the reverse. Neither is enough alone. Together they are the shape of the whole case.

## The argument

The fourth century's route — the [argument from worship](../reference.md#the-argument-from-worship) — in the form Basil gave it ([`patristics` 3.2](../../patristics/lessons/03-02-basil-the-great.md) has his text; here we take the doctrinal outcome). The bracket after each premise says what it rests on.

1. **The Spirit is named where God is named, in the rite that makes a Christian.** Christ commands baptism "in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Ghost" (Matthew 28:19) — one *name*, singular, with three in it. *In words: the Church's founding rite puts the Spirit on God's side of the line.* [scripture]
2. **Faith must conform to baptism, and worship to faith.** You cannot be baptized into one thing, believe another, and praise a third. *In words: the Church's prayer is evidence of the Church's faith, or Christian practice is incoherent.* [liturgical practice, plus a principle about the coherence of practice]
3. **The Church in fact ascribes glory to the Spirit together with the Father and the Son.** *In words: she worships him.* [practice — and this was the disputed premise]
4. **No creature may be worshipped.** *In words: the first commandment.* [common ground; nobody in the fight denied it]
5. **The Spirit performs works that are not delegated creaturely works:** searching God's depths (the Source), giving life, sanctifying, adopting, raising the dead (Romans 8:11). *In words: he does what only God does.* [scripture, plus the assumption that these works cannot be handed to a creature]
6. **Therefore the Spirit is not a creature, but is ranked with the Father and the Son as God.** *In words: the conclusion comes from what he does and how he is worshipped, never from a title he is given.*

Mark the levels before moving on. The **conclusion** — that the Holy Spirit is God, one in substance with the Father and the Son — is defined dogma. **This route to it** is theology: a good argument, the one the Church actually used, and not itself defined. And the **premise that God is one** ([1.2](01-02-the-one-true-god-and-what-reason-can-reach.md)) is what makes the conclusion cost something, because it forbids the easy answer that there are simply three divine beings.

The tell that the argument really ran this way is what the council of 381 then said. It did not extend *homoousios* to the Spirit. It confessed him as Lord and giver of life, adored and glorified together with the Father and the Son — premise 3 canonized, not premise 6 restated. Why a council would define in the language of worship is [3.3](03-03-constantinople-i-and-the-divinity-of-the-spirit.md).

## Map of the evidence

| Text | Distinct someone? | Divine? | What it leaves open |
|---|---|---|---|
| **The Jordan** (Mark 1:10-11) and **Pentecost** (Acts 2:1-4, quoting Joel at 2:17) | Suggestive: three at once at the Jordan — voice, Son, Spirit. Weak at Pentecost | Nothing directly | Whether "spirit poured out" is God's own action given, as the prophets use the phrase, or a giver |
| **The [Paraclete](../reference.md#paraclete) discourses** (John 14:16-17, 14:26, 15:26, 16:7-15) | **Strongest.** *Another* Paraclete; he teaches, reminds, bears witness, convicts the world, guides, hears, speaks, glorifies the Son | Not asserted | Whether a personal agent who is sent and does not speak of himself is thereby a divine person |
| **Romans 8:9-27** | Yes: God searches hearts and knows what the Spirit desires, so they are not simply one | Strong indirectly: indwelling, life, adoption, raising the dead | Whether an intercessor who "asketh for us" is thereby equal to the one asked |
| **1 Corinthians 2:10-11** | Weak, and readable the wrong way | **Strongest indirectly:** only God knows God from inside | Whether the Spirit is God's self-knowledge rather than another |
| **Triadic formulas** (Matthew 28:19; 2 Corinthians 13:13; 1 Corinthians 12:4-6; Ephesians 4:4-6) | Yes: three are named and not merged | By rank, not by predication | Whether being listed with God makes one God — Paul lists angels with God and Christ at 1 Timothy 5:21 |
| **Acts 5:3-4** | Assumed | Closest to an identification in the whole New Testament | Whether the parallel means *the Spirit is God* or *lying to God's Spirit is lying to God* |

Two notes on this table. **John 15:26** has the Spirit proceeding from the Father and sent by the Son; its two verbs are the hinge of the entire filioque question and are left where they belong, in [5.1](05-01-the-latin-doctrine.md) and [5.2](05-02-the-greek-doctrine-and-the-addition.md). And the famous grammatical datum — that John uses the masculine demonstrative *ekeinos* of the Spirit — is real but must be handled carefully: its nearest antecedent is the masculine noun *parakletos*, not the neuter *pneuma*, so the masculine is at least partly what any Greek sentence would do. It is a straw in the wind, not a proof. The weight sits on the verbs, not the pronoun.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean: Acts 5:3-4).** Peter confronts Ananias for keeping back part of the price of the land: he asks why Satan tempted his heart that he should lie to the Holy Ghost, and then says, "Thou hast not lied to men, but to God."

Run the ledger. *Distinction*: not in view; Peter is not distinguishing anyone from anyone. *Divinity*: this is as close as the New Testament comes. The two sentences are parallel, the second restating the first with the term changed, so the natural reading identifies the one lied to. *What it leaves open*: parallelism is not predication. The sentence works just as well if Peter means that deceiving the Spirit whom God has given is deceiving God — the way insulting a king's envoy insults the king. Excellent evidence, poor proof: push it as proof and an opponent has an easy reply.

**Example 2 (where it strains: Romans 8:26-27).** "The Spirit himself asketh for us with unspeakable groanings," and God, who searches hearts, knows what the Spirit desires.

This is the most personal sentence Paul writes about the Spirit: someone who wants something, and someone else who knows what he wants. As evidence for distinction it is superb — a faculty does not intercede with the mind that has it.

Now feel the strain. Intercession is what a *mediator* does, and mediators are normally lesser than the one they petition. A fourth-century opponent of the Spirit's divinity could take this verse as his best text: the Spirit is the highest of creatures, closer to God than we are, pleading on our behalf. The Church's reading refuses the inference, and on the same ground it used for the Son at John 14:28 ([2.2](02-02-the-father-and-the-son-in-the-new-testament.md)): language of being sent, given and speaking for another states *origin and mission*, not a lesser nature. But notice that this is a *reading*, arrived at by fitting Romans 8 to everything else — including the verse two lines earlier where the Spirit raises the dead. No single verse settles it, and pretending otherwise is how apologetics gets embarrassed.

## Watch out

- **You might think the New Testament calls the Spirit God somewhere and you have just forgotten where.** It does not. Saying it does is an exegetical error, and it is worse than useless: it trades a certain doctrine for an easily refuted claim about a text. The right sentence is: the doctrine is defined, and the New Testament supplies the evidence from which the Church argued it.
- **The opposite error is just as bad.** "Not stated in so many words" is not "not taught." The same reasoning would unmake half the creed; nothing in the New Testament says *consubstantial* either. [2.4](02-04-from-confession-to-creed.md) and the account of development in [`fundamental-theology`](../../fundamental-theology/syllabus.md) 5.1-5.2 are where that gets answered properly.
- **Calling the Spirit God's power, presence or influence is the back door into [modalism](../reference.md#modalism)** — or below it, into treating a divine person as an attribute of God. It is the single most common way ordinary Catholic speech goes wrong about the Spirit, usually with no bad intent, because "spirit" in English sounds like a force. The Paraclete verbs are the corrective: forces are not sent, do not bear witness, and are not grieved.
- **Reading the Spirit's being sent, given, or "not speaking of himself" as a lower rank is [subordinationism](../reference.md#subordinationism)** — the identical mistake the fourth century made about the Son, transposed. Origin is not inferiority.
- **Co-listing proves less than it looks.** Basil had to answer this objection, since Paul charges Timothy before God, Christ and the elect angels. [Triadic formulas](../reference.md#triadic-formulas) are strong because of what *else* the Spirit is said to do, not on their own.

## One-liner

> The New Testament never calls the Holy Spirit God; it shows him doing what only God does and receiving what only God may receive, and the Church argued from there.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢) *(Exegetical.)*** From the Paraclete discourse, John 16:13-15 (Douay-Rheims):

> But when he, the Spirit of truth, is come, he will teach you all truth. For he shall not speak of himself; but what things soever he shall hear, he shall speak; and the things that are to come, he shall shew you. He shall glorify me; because he shall receive of mine, and shall shew it to you. All things whatsoever the Father hath, are mine. Therefore I said, that he shall receive of mine, and shew it to you.

(a) Name the words that carry the strongest evidence that the Spirit is distinct from both the Father and the Son. (b) Which clause here would a fourth-century opponent of the Spirit's divinity quote, and what does the Church's reading say it means? (c) State one thing this passage does **not** establish. Two sentences per part.

**P2 (🟡) *(Exegetical.)*** An invented parish handout on Confirmation, from no real author:

> "The Holy Spirit is the power of God's love poured into our hearts — not a separate God, but God's own energy reaching us, the way warmth reaches you from a fire. Scripture calls the Spirit God outright in Acts 5, where Peter tells Ananias he has lied to God. So the doctrine is simply there in the Bible for anyone who reads it, and we can move straight on to the seven gifts."

Name the **two** distinct errors, one in each of the first two sentences, and say for each what true thing the writer was reaching for. Then correct the handout's claim about Acts 5 in one sentence that a careful reader could not refute. 150 words or fewer.

**P3 (🔴, optional) *(Exegetical.)*** Reconstruct the lesson's argument from worship in your own numbered premises — you may compress it to four — and mark each premise as resting on **scripture**, on **the Church's practice**, or on **an assumption neither side disputed**. Then name the premise a fourth-century opponent of the Spirit's divinity had to attack, and say what he would have to show. Premises may be one line each; 80 words or fewer for the last part.

<details>
<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical.)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** the verbs of receiving and reporting between three parties — the Spirit "shall hear" and then "speak", he "shall receive of mine", and all that the Father has is the Son's. A hearer is not the one heard, and a receiver is not the one received from, so the passage has three parties in real relation, not one subject under three names. Credit also for "he shall glorify me," since glorifying is directed at another.

**Must hit, strict (b):** "he shall not speak of himself" (and, less centrally, "what things soever he shall hear, he shall speak"). Taken alone it sounds like a subordinate relaying a superior's message. The Church's reading takes it as a statement of **origin**, not of rank: what the Spirit has, he has as given rather than as independently his own, and to have one's being from another is not to be less than the other. The same move answers John 14:28 about the Son ([2.2](02-02-the-father-and-the-son-in-the-new-testament.md)).

**Must hit, strict (c):** any one of — that the Spirit is God (no divine title or predicate appears); that the Spirit is uncreated; what these verses settle about the Spirit's eternal origin, which is not a question this lesson opens ([5.1](05-01-the-latin-doctrine.md) and [5.2](05-02-the-greek-doctrine-and-the-addition.md) take it up).

**Wrong turns:** citing the masculine pronoun as the strongest evidence in (a) — its antecedent is the masculine noun *parakletos*, so it is a straw in the wind, not the load-bearing datum; answering (b) by denying that the clause has any subordinationist surface at all, which is not honest exegesis; answering (c) with "it doesn't say the Spirit is a person," which the passage arguably does support.

**Model answer:** (a) "What things soever he shall hear, he shall speak" and "he shall receive of mine": hearing and receiving require someone else to hear and to receive from, so the Spirit is distinct from both. "He shall glorify me" adds the same, since one does not glorify oneself in this sense. (b) "He shall not speak of himself" — it reads like a messenger under orders. The Church takes it as a statement of where the Spirit is *from*, not of what he is worth, exactly as with the Son at John 14:28. (c) It does not establish that the Spirit is God; no divine title or predicate is applied to him anywhere in the passage.

---

**P2** *(Exegetical.)*

**Must hit, strict — error 1 (first sentence):** reducing the Spirit to God's power, energy or love-as-force. That is [modalism](../reference.md#modalism) at best, and at worst makes a divine person an attribute; "not a separate God" is the right instinct expressed in a way that dissolves the distinction of persons. The true thing being reached for: the Spirit is not a second or third *God*, and the Church's monotheism is intact — the three are one God ([1.2](01-02-the-one-true-god-and-what-reason-can-reach.md)).

**Must hit, strict — error 2 (second sentence):** claiming scripture calls the Spirit God outright. It does not, anywhere; Acts 5:3-4 sets two sentences in parallel and does not predicate deity of the Spirit. This overstates what the text does and, by implication, mislocates the doctrine's authority: the dogma is defined by the Church, not read off a verse. The true thing being reached for: Acts 5:3-4 really is the closest the New Testament comes, and it really is good evidence.

**Wrong turns:** treating the fire-and-warmth image as simply false — it is a traditional image for what is *communicated* and the error is using it to explain who the Spirit *is*; saying Acts 5 proves nothing at all, which overcorrects; adding a third error about the seven gifts, which is not asked for and is not an error.

**Model answer:** First, "God's own energy reaching us" makes the Spirit a power rather than someone, which is the modalist error, or worse reduces a person to an attribute. The writer was reaching for a true point: the Spirit is not a separate God, since the three are one God. Second, scripture nowhere calls the Spirit God outright; Acts 5 is a parallel, not a title. He was reaching for the fact that Acts 5 is the strongest such text there is. Corrected: in Acts 5:3-4 Peter says that lying to the Holy Ghost is lying to God, which is the closest the New Testament comes to identifying them and is strong evidence — but it is an inference from the parallel, not a place where scripture calls the Spirit God.

---

**P3** *(Exegetical.)*

**Must hit, strict (the reconstruction):** four premises in any wording that preserves the chain, correctly marked. A compressed version: (P1) Christ commands baptism in the one name of Father, Son and Holy Ghost — **scripture**; (P2) what the Church is baptized into she must believe, and what she believes she must glorify, so her worship reveals her faith — **the Church's practice**, plus a coherence principle; (P3) the Church ascribes glory to the Spirit together with the Father and the Son — **the Church's practice**; (P4) no creature may be worshipped — **an assumption neither side disputed**. Conclusion: the Spirit is no creature. A five-premise version adding the Spirit's divine works from scripture is equally good.

**Must hit, strict (the attack):** P3, the claim that the Church's worship is in fact addressed *to* the Spirit and not merely offered *through* or *in* him. (Accept P2 as an alternative — that practice is reliable evidence of faith — provided the answer says what the opponent must then show.) What he must show: that the doxologies giving glory *with* the Spirit are an innovation rather than the Church's received practice, or that the older form "in the Holy Spirit" ranks the Spirit as the medium of worship rather than its recipient.

**Wrong turns:** attacking P4 — nobody in the fourth century held that a creature may be worshipped, and putting the opponent there is a caricature; marking P1 as practice because baptism is a rite, when the premise is a quoted command of Christ; leaving the conclusion at "therefore the Spirit is God" without noticing that the argument as it stands delivers "not a creature, and ranked with the Father and the Son."

**Model answer (last part):** He must attack P3. His move is to grant that the Church prays in the Spirit while denying that she worships him, and to argue that the doxology giving glory *with* the Spirit is a novelty rather than received practice. To win he would have to show that the older prepositions rank the Spirit as the medium through which worship passes rather than as one to whom it is given — which is precisely the ground Basil chose to fight on.

</details>

## Flashback

**From Lesson [2.1](02-01-the-one-god-of-israel.md) (The one God of Israel, and the Church's backward reading):** *(Exegetical.)* Isaias 6:3, Douay-Rheims — the words the Church sings at Mass in the Sanctus:

> And they cried one to another, and said: Holy, holy, holy, the Lord God of hosts, all the earth is full of his glory.

A colleague who does not share the faith asks whether the three *holies* show that God is three. (a) Say what the verse establishes on its own about a plurality in God, and name the course that owns the question of why the cry runs three times. (b) Of the two jobs Aquinas allows reason in ST I q.32 a.1, name the one a Trinitarian reading of this cry can do, and say what you may therefore offer your colleague. (c) In one sentence, name the cost Aquinas attaches to offering such a reading as a proof, and say what he claims the hearer concludes. **Two sentences per part at most.**

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):** nothing. The verse is an acclamation, and every noun and pronoun in it is singular — one Lord God of hosts, *his* glory; a repeated word is a feature of the cry, not a count of persons. Why it runs three times is an exegetical question, and it belongs to [`old-testament`](../../old-testament/syllabus.md), not here.

**Must hit, strict (b):** the second job — confirming a principle already held by showing how its consequences fit — and not furnishing sufficient proof (q.32 a.1, reply to objection 2). So the reading may be offered to someone who already receives the authority; to this colleague you may show only that what faith teaches is not impossible, and you may not put the verse forward as evidence for the doctrine.

**Must hit, strict (c):** he says the man who brings non-cogent reasons for the faith falls under the unbelievers' ridicule — and that what they conclude is not that the argument is weak but that the faith stands on such grounds. A bad proof of a true doctrine misrepresents why the doctrine is held.

**Wrong turns:** calling the threefold cry "at least a hint" and then letting the hint function as a premise — an intimation is not evidence, and the direction of reading is the whole point; saying the Trinitarian reading is illegitimate, when it is legitimate as confirmation and the liturgy uses these words; answering (b) with "it is an argument from authority", which is true of scripture generally and is not the distinction the article draws.

**Model answer:** (a) On its own, nothing about a plurality in God: the cry addresses one Lord God of hosts and speaks of his glory in the singular, and the repetition is part of the acclamation. Why it is threefold is `old-testament`'s question. (b) The second job, confirmation of a doctrine already held, not proof — so to a colleague outside the faith the most I may claim is that the doctrine is not impossible, and I may not offer the verse as grounds for it. (c) Aquinas says such reasons expose the believer to ridicule, because the unbeliever takes them for the grounds on which the faith is actually held.

</details>
