# The Triune God · Lesson 1.5: Impassibility, and the God who suffers

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 1: De Deo Uno - the one God the Church confesses · Builds on: [1.3 Simplicity as doctrine](01-03-simplicity-as-doctrine.md), [1.4 Immutability, eternity, immensity](01-04-immutability-eternity-immensity.md) · Unlocks: [2.1 The one God of Israel](02-01-the-one-god-of-israel.md), [6.3 Rahner's rule and the immanent Trinity](06-03-rahners-rule-and-the-immanent-trinity.md)

## Why this matters

Of everything in Module 1 this is the claim a modern reader is least willing to grant. After 1945 a generation of theologians decided that a God untouched by anything has nothing to say at Auschwitz, and said so in print; "the crucified God" has been in circulation ever since. Against them stands a tradition that thought it was defending something precious, not something cold. This lesson hands you no verdict. It makes sure that when you take one you know which sentence you are affirming, and how much weight the Church has put behind it — because most of the argument you will hear is conducted with that question unasked.

## The idea

*Passio* in Latin means being acted on. To suffer something, in the old sense, is to *undergo* it: to receive a change from another. So [impassibility](../reference.md#impassibility) is first of all the denial of a passive capacity — nothing in God can be acted on from outside. It is not, first of all, a claim about God's emotional temperature.

Three things it therefore does not say. It does not say God is unaware of us; his knowledge of creatures is not received from them. It does not say God does nothing about our misery. And — the contested one — it does not deny that God loves, rejoices or has mercy. The tradition affirms all three, *as acts of will* rather than as states we produce in him.

The modern objection fastens on exactly that move. If God's love is in no way drawn out of him by the beloved, in what sense is it love? A love guaranteed in advance to be unaffected by anything the beloved does looks less like the strongest love than like no relationship. The tradition answers that the comparison runs backwards: our being altered by those we love is a mark of a love not yet full. Both halves of that exchange are serious, and neither is a misunderstanding of the other.

## Source

Two sentences eighteen verses apart in one chapter (1 Kings, called 1 Samuel in other numbering, 15:11 and 15:29, Douay-Rheims):

> It repenteth me that I have made Saul king: for he hath forsaken me, and hath not executed my commandments.

> But the triumpher in Israel will not spare, and will not be moved to repentance: for he is not a man that he should repent.

Scripture is not embarrassed here, and drops neither sentence. Verse 29 gives the rule and the reason: God does not repent *because he is not a man*. The tradition takes its reading rule from inside the text, and Challoner's note on the parallel at Genesis 6:6 states it flatly:

> God, who is unchangeable, is not capable of repentance, grief, or any other passion. But these expressions are used to declare the enormity of the sins of men.

The machinery underneath is [analogy](../reference.md#analogy), argued at [`thomistic-synthesis` 4.4](../../thomistic-synthesis/lessons/04-04-analogy.md): the words are neither univocal nor empty. Take "it repenteth me" univocally and you get a God who was surprised. That is the exegetical trap, and the one place in this debate where the tradition thinks its opponents simply misread a text.

## The argument

The manuals derive impassibility rather than assume it, in four steps:

1. **God is pure act: there is no passive potency in him** ([`thomistic-synthesis` 2.1](../../thomistic-synthesis/lessons/02-01-act-and-potency-extended.md), and simplicity in [1.3](01-03-simplicity-as-doctrine.md)). *In words:* nothing in God is waiting to be brought about, so there is nothing for anything to bring about.
2. **To undergo a passion is to be moved from potency to act by another.** *In words:* a passion is something that happens *to* you.
3. **Therefore God undergoes no passion.** This is impassibility, and it is a conclusion, not a premise.
4. **But love, joy and mercy are nevertheless affirmed of God.** Aquinas raises the objection himself at ST I q.20 a.1 — in God there are no passions, love is a passion, so love is not in God — and answers that love and joy are passions only as acts of the sense appetite; as acts of the will they are not, and "it is in this latter sense that they are in God." At q.21 a.3 mercy is attributed to God supremely as seen in its effect, not as an affection of passion. *In words:* the derivation subtracts the passivity and keeps the perfection.

**Now the level, where most of the heat gets lost.** What the councils define is that God is altogether simple and unchangeable: Lateran IV's *Firmiter* and *Dei Filius* chapter 1, read in [1.2](01-02-the-one-true-god-and-what-reason-can-reach.md) and [1.3](01-03-simplicity-as-doctrine.md). Neither defines a proposition under the name *impassibility*. What the councils say about suffering they say of the incarnate Son, and locate *in the flesh* — Cyril's twelfth anathematism against Nestorius anathematizes anyone refusing to recognize that the Word of God suffered in the flesh, and Constantinople II does the same (Example 1).

So impassibility as the manuals state it carries a **[theological note](../reference.md#theological-note)** rather than a conciliar definition. A note is a theologian's label for how securely a proposition follows from the sources — a different instrument from the rung a magisterial act fixes, and the seam between the two is [`fundamental-theology` 4.4](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-04-the-ladder-of-doctrinal-authority.md)'s. Manualists who read impassibility as immutability restated give it the note of the defined truth it restates; those who read it as a conclusion drawn from immutability give it the lower note of a theologically certain conclusion. Ott's *Fundamentals of Catholic Dogma* shows how the manual tradition labelled the attributes — a report of the labels, not a magisterial act. Look up which note your manual prints before citing one; the distinction between the readings is the stable part.

**Two errors, graded strictly, pointing opposite ways.** Calling impassibility a conciliar definition promotes an explanation to dogma. Calling it a school opinion demotes what the Fathers held without dissent and what the Church acted on against patripassianism. The defensible statement is narrow: *that God is unchangeable is defined; that he therefore cannot be acted upon is the tradition's reading of what being unchangeable entails.*

## Map of positions

| Position | Claim about God | Impassibility | Constraint |
|---|---|---|---|
| **The manual tradition** | No passive potency; love, joy and mercy as acts of will, not states induced in him | The fullness that receives nothing | Reading scripture's grief without emptying it |
| **Kitamori, *Theology of the Pain of God* (1946)** | The pain of God, his love embracing the object of his wrath, is the gospel's centre | A category the gospel does not need | Whether that pain is in God as God |
| **Moltmann, *The Crucified God* (1972)** | The cross is an event *between* Father and Son, determining God's own being; a God who cannot suffer cannot love | A Greek axiom the gospel did not bring | Keeping the suffering free, not necessary |
| **Jüngel, *God as the Mystery of the World* (1977)** | God's being is his self-giving; Jesus's death states something about God, not only about a man | Too thin to carry that | Keeping God distinct from the world's process |
| **Process theology** | God is affected by the world *by his nature*, being in time and dipolar | Straightforwardly false | Simplicity and immutability as defined ([1.3](01-03-simplicity-as-doctrine.md), [1.4](01-04-immutability-eternity-immensity.md)) |
| **Balthasar, *Mysterium Paschale*** | The cross read back into the Trinity: the Father's self-giving and the Son's obedience as its eternal ground | Preserved, but re-described | Not turning distinction of persons into conflict ([6.3](06-03-rahners-rule-and-the-immanent-trinity.md)) |
| ***Dominum et Vivificantem* 39 (1986)** | A fatherly "pain" glimpsed in the depths of God, in scare quotes and fenced | Retained, and used as the fence | Saying what the fence does (Example 2) |
| **Weinandy, *Does God Suffer?* (2000)** | A love that cannot be increased or diminished; "God suffers" is true of the Son in his flesh | The fullness of love, not indifference | Showing that love so understood keeps content |

Moltmann and process theology agree on one question and part on another, and collapsing them is the commonest error in the literature: Moltmann's God suffers *freely*, choosing the cross out of love, where process theology's God is affected because that is the kind of being he is. Stating Moltmann without the word "freely" is not yet stating him.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (the machinery on a clean case: Constantinople II).** The Second Council of Constantinople (553), in the tenth of its capitula, anathematizes anyone who does not confess that the one crucified in the flesh is true God, the Lord of glory, and one of the Holy Trinity. Read it for what it says about God, and nothing else. The subject who hung on the cross is a divine person — not a man conjoined to God, not a fourth thing — and the suffering was *in the flesh*. It does not say the divine nature suffered, nor that it was untouched. The [theopaschite formula](../reference.md#one-of-the-trinity-suffered-in-the-flesh) is therefore true, and this canon is the Church's warrant for it. *Why* it can be true — the rule by which what belongs to one nature is predicated of the person named from the other — is the communication of idioms, which belongs to [`christology`](../../christology/syllabus.md) 3.2. Module 1 takes one thing from the canon: whatever impassibility means, it cannot mean that God is at a safe distance from the cross.

**Example 2 (the hard case, where it strains).** John Paul II's *Dominum et Vivificantem* (1986), n. 39, asks whether convincing the world of sin must not also mean revealing suffering — a pain, unimaginable and inexpressible, which Genesis in its anthropomorphic vision seems to glimpse in the depths of God and in a certain sense in the heart of the Trinity. Then the fence, same paragraph: the concept of God as the necessarily most perfect being excludes any pain deriving from deficiencies or wounds. The word "pain" carries quotation marks whenever the document returns to it.

Notice what that paragraph concedes and what it will not. It refuses to treat the Genesis language as noise: something in God answers to it, and the answer is the Father's love reacting to sin. It equally refuses what Moltmann needs, a God whose being is *lessened* by what we do. Read it as the tradition absorbing the complaint about coldness while holding its ground, or as a formula not yet paid for; the strain is real. What you may not say is that a 1986 encyclical defines either side. It is authentic ordinary magisterium — real authority, question open — the third rung of [`fundamental-theology` 4.4](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-04-the-ladder-of-doctrinal-authority.md).

## Watch out

- **You might think impassibility means God is indifferent.** It denies that anything acts on God; it does not deny love, joy or mercy, which the same tradition affirms as acts of will (ST I q.20 a.1; q.21 a.3). An argument against a cold God is not yet an argument against this doctrine, and much popular writing never gets past that first target.
- **"The Father suffered on the cross" is [patripassianism](../reference.md#patripassianism)**, refused in the third century, and a corollary of modalism rather than a separate mistake: only if Father and Son are one person under two names can the Father be the one hanging there. Hence Tertullian's double charge against Praxeas, that he drove out the Paraclete *and* crucified the Father (*Against Praxeas* 1; [`patristics` 2.3](../../patristics/lessons/02-03-tertullian.md)). What Constantinople II licenses is "one *of* the Trinity suffered in the flesh," which distinguishes the persons instead of collapsing them.
- **"The Godhead died" predicates of a nature what belongs to a person.** Grant the modern case everything it asks and natures still do not die; persons do. Watch hymn texts, where the metre does the theology.
- **Both level errors turn up in the same paragraph.** "The Church has always defined that God is impassible" overstates; "impassibility is a Greek opinion the manuals liked" understates. Neither is a verdict — they are failures to state the question.

## One-liner

> The Church defines that God does not change; whether "and therefore nothing can touch him" is that same sentence restated or the next sentence after it is the entire argument.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢) *(Exegetical.)*** The Second Council of Constantinople (553), the tenth of its capitula, in Schaff's public-domain translation:

> If anyone does not confess that our Lord Jesus Christ who was crucified in the flesh is true God and the Lord of Glory and one of the Holy Trinity: let him be anathema.

(a) Which words carry the claim that the one crucified is a divine person, and which words locate the suffering? (b) A reader says this canon defines that the divine nature suffers. Name the phrase that refutes him, and say what the canon leaves for Christology to explain. Two sentences per part.

**P2 (🟡) *(Exegetical.)*** An invented stanza from an invented parish hymnal, printed with an invented editorial footnote:

> Eternal Father, torn and bleeding, / on the tree thy body broken; / the Godhead died that Friday morning, / love's last word at last was spoken.

> *Footnote: As the Church has always defined, the divine nature is impassible. The hymn is poetry.*

(a) Name the heresy the stanza lands in, the earlier error it presupposes, and one text that corrects it. (b) The footnote contains a level error of its own. Name it and give the accurate statement in one sentence. 150 words or fewer in total.

**P3 (🔴, optional) *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)*** (a) State in one sentence each what Moltmann's *The Crucified God* and Weinandy's *Does God Suffer?* each claim love requires. (b) Name the single premise the dispute turns on, and say what would have to be shown to move you off whatever view you currently hold. Any verdict. 150 words or fewer in total.

<details>
<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical.)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** "true God and the Lord of Glory and one of the Holy Trinity" identifies the crucified as a divine person — the subject on the cross is one of the three, not a man conjoined to God and not some fourth thing. "Crucified **in the flesh**" locates the suffering: in the humanity he took.

**Must hit, strict (b):** the refuting phrase is **"in the flesh."** The canon predicates the crucifixion of the *person* and specifies the flesh as that in which he underwent it; it asserts nothing about a passible divine nature. What it leaves to Christology is the rule that makes such predication legitimate — the communication of idioms, by which what belongs to one nature is said of the person named from the other ([`christology`](../../christology/syllabus.md) 3.2).

**Wrong turns:** reading "one of the Holy Trinity" as "one third of the Trinity" (it names a person, not a part); treating the canon as neutral about Christ's divinity, when asserting it is the canon's whole purpose; settling the impassibility question from this canon in either direction — it neither defines that the divine nature suffered nor that it was unaffected.

**Model answer:** (a) "True God and the Lord of Glory and one of the Holy Trinity" carries the divinity of the subject: the one crucified is a divine person. "Crucified in the flesh" says where the suffering was undergone. (b) "In the flesh" refutes him — the canon predicates the suffering of the person and names the flesh as that in which he suffered, not the divinity. It leaves Christology to explain the rule by which a property of the humanity may be said of the person who is God, which is the communication of idioms.

---

**P2** *(Exegetical.)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** **patripassianism** — the Father himself as the one who suffers and dies on the cross. It presupposes **modalism** (Noetus, Praxeas, Sabellius): only if Father and Son are one person under two names can the Father be the crucified one. Corrective text: Constantinople II's tenth capitulum ("one *of* the Holy Trinity," the Son, crucified in the flesh), or Cyril's twelfth anathematism, or Tertullian's *Against Praxeas*. Full credit also notes the second fault in line three: "the Godhead died" predicates death of a *nature* rather than of a person.

**Must hit, strict (b):** "as the Church has always defined" **overstates the level**. No council defines a proposition under the name *impassibility*; what is defined is that God is altogether simple and unchangeable (Lateran IV's *Firmiter*, *Dei Filius* ch. 1), and impassibility as the manuals state it is a derived conclusion carrying a theological note, not a conciliar definition. Accurate statement: *the Church defines that God is unchangeable, and the tradition holds, with the Fathers behind it, that he therefore undergoes no passion.*

**Wrong turns:** calling the stanza tritheism — it errs by collapsing the persons, not multiplying them; "correcting" the footnote by denying impassibility, when the error is the overstated level and not the doctrine; defending the stanza as the communication of idioms at work, which licenses "God suffered" said of the incarnate Son and never "the Father was crucified" or "the Godhead died."

**Model answer:** The stanza is patripassianism: the Father is made the one broken on the tree. It presupposes modalism, since only one person under two names could be both Father and crucified; Constantinople II's tenth capitulum corrects it by confessing *one of* the Trinity crucified in the flesh. "The Godhead died" adds a second fault, predicating death of a nature rather than a person. The footnote's error is the level: nothing has been "always defined" under the name impassibility. What is defined is that God is altogether simple and unchangeable; impassibility is the tradition's conclusion from that, carrying a theological note rather than a conciliar definition.

---

**P3** *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** *Moltmann* — to love is to be open to being affected by the beloved, so a God incapable of suffering is incapable of love; the cross is an event within God's own being, and God suffers **freely, out of love**, not from any lack. (The "freely" clause is part of stating him accurately and is what separates him from process theology; an answer that omits it is not yet fair to him.) *Weinandy* — love is a perfection, and being *altered* by the beloved is the mark of a love that was not yet complete; God's love cannot be increased or diminished because it is already full, so impassibility names the fullness of that love rather than its absence.

**Must hit, any verdict (b):** the crux is **whether being affected by the beloved belongs to the perfection of love or to its imperfection** — equivalently, whether "affected" is being said of God univocally with what it means of us. The answer must also say what evidence or argument would move it: for instance, whether scripture's ascriptions of divine grief can be read analogically without being emptied, or whether "love" predicated of a being that cannot be acted on retains determinate content. Any verdict passes if the crux is named and a movable test is given.

**Wrong turns:** reporting Moltmann as a process theologian — he does not make divine suffering metaphysically necessary; treating Constantinople II as settling it, when that canon speaks of the incarnate Son and not of the divine nature; making the crux "does God love?", which neither side denies; giving a verdict with no statement of what would change it.

**Model answer (one of several):** (a) Moltmann: love requires being open to the beloved, so a God who cannot suffer cannot love — and at the cross God freely suffers, in his own being, out of love. Weinandy: love is a perfection, and being changed by the beloved shows a love that was incomplete, so God's unalterable love is the fullest love, not a withheld one. (b) The dispute turns on whether receptivity belongs to love's perfection or to its deficiency. I would be moved if it were shown that "love" said of a being nothing can act on has no content beyond benevolent causation — or, the other way, that every biblical ascription of grief to God can be read analogically without loss, on the rule 1 Kings 15:29 supplies from inside the text.

## Flashback

**From Lesson [1.3](01-03-simplicity-as-doctrine.md) (Simplicity as doctrine):** *(Exegetical.)* The Fourth Lateran Council (1215), in the constitution rejecting Joachim of Fiore's account of how the divine persons are one, closes with the rule that has governed Catholic speech about God ever since: between creator and creature no likeness can be noted so great that a greater unlikeness is not to be noted between them (the course's translation of the Latin). Two preachers invoke it:

> (i) "God is my Father in just the way my own father was, only without the faults."
>
> (ii) "The unlikeness always wins, so nothing we say of God is really true of him; the safest homily makes no positive claim at all."

Say what the rule does to each — **one or two sentences each** — and then, in **one further sentence**, say what the rule is a ranking of.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (i):** the rule does not cancel the likeness; it denies that the creaturely case is the measure. "In just the way, only without the faults" says *father* of God and of a man in one and the same sense, differing by degree, and the council's ranking forbids exactly that — the unlikeness between them is the greater.

**Must hit, strict (ii):** this over-reads the rule into scepticism. It ranks two true things and cancels neither, and the council states it at the end of an argument about the divine substance, so it plainly holds that true things can be said; what keeps the ranking from silencing speech is analogy ([`thomistic-synthesis` 4.4](../../thomistic-synthesis/lessons/04-04-analogy.md)).

**Must hit, strict (the ranking):** a real likeness against a greater unlikeness — never a likeness against nothing, and never a bare denial.

**Wrong turns:** correcting (i) by dropping the name rather than the measure — scripture calls God Father, and it is the "in just the way" that fails; conceding (ii) that theology is guesswork, when the rule appears as the conclusion of an argument rather than in place of one; answering with simplicity or incomprehensibility and never saying what *this* rule adds, which is the ordering of the two claims.

**Model answer:** (i) The rule leaves the likeness standing and refuses it the role of measure: said in one sense of God and of a man, "father" makes the creature the standard and God an improved instance, and the council says the unlikeness between the two is greater than any likeness noted. (ii) It is the rule turned into scepticism; the council ranks two true things without denying the first, and it states the rule at the close of a page of argument about the divine substance — analogy is what keeps the ranking from ending speech. The rule ranks a real likeness against a greater unlikeness.
