# The Triune God · Lesson 6.3: Rahner's rule and the return of the immanent Trinity

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 6: The missions, and modern Trinitarian theology · Builds on: [6.1 The missions](06-01-the-missions-of-the-son-and-the-spirit.md), [1.1 The treatise and its order](01-01-the-treatise-and-its-order.md), [1.5 Impassibility](01-05-impassibility-and-the-god-who-suffers.md) · Unlocks: [6.4 Social trinitarianism](06-04-social-trinitarianism-and-its-critics.md)

## Why this matters

In [1.1](01-01-the-treatise-and-its-order.md) you met Karl Rahner's complaint: the manuals' two tracts had produced Christians who recite the creed correctly and pray as though God were one person. He did not leave it as a grievance. He proposed a repair — one sentence, which became the most quoted line in twentieth-century Trinitarian theology, meant by different people in very different ways, some of them ways the Church has defined against. This lesson makes you able to say, of any argument leaning on the rule, which half carries the weight, what that half wins, and what its Catholic critics say it costs.

## The idea

Two terms first. The [**immanent Trinity**](../reference.md#immanent-and-economic-trinity), older name *theologia*, is God's own eternal life: the Father begetting the Son, the two together breathing the Spirit, with or without a world. The [**economic Trinity**](../reference.md#immanent-and-economic-trinity), *oikonomia*, is the Trinity as it turns up in history: the Father who sends, the Son who is sent and becomes man, the Spirit poured out at Pentecost and given in grace.

Nobody doubts the two are the same God. The dispute is over what kind of sameness, and whether the sentence may be read in both directions.

One direction is uncontroversial and always was. The Son who is sent is the Son eternally begotten, not a created stand-in; the Spirit given in grace is God himself, not a divine influence. [6.1](06-01-the-missions-of-the-son-and-the-spirit.md) says it in Thomist form: a mission is the eternal procession itself, plus a new way of being present in the creature. No unknown God stands behind the missions wearing them as a mask.

The other direction is where the trouble lives. If God's eternal life simply *is* what we meet in the economy, then a world, an Incarnation and a cross start to look like part of what God is. And the Church has defined that God made the world freely, under no necessity ([1.2](01-02-the-one-true-god-and-what-reason-can-reach.md)).

That asymmetry is the whole lesson.

## The argument

Rahner puts [the rule](../reference.md#rahners-rule) as a single axiom in *The Trinity* (the essay of 1967 written for the *Mysterium Salutis* series, later published in English under that title): "The 'economic' Trinity is the 'immanent' Trinity, and the 'immanent' Trinity is the 'economic' Trinity." Every other modern author here is paraphrased; this sentence is the exception, because the argument is about its words.

**The first half.** The Trinity meeting us in salvation history is the Trinity God eternally is. The threeness we encounter is not a way God appears to creatures, nor three created effects under three appropriated names ([4.5](04-05-common-proper-appropriated.md)). *In words:* what you get in the economy is the real thing, all the way down.

**The second half.** Read backwards: God's eternal life is not a further, differently shaped mystery behind the economy. The processions and the missions are not two structures; the missions *are* those processions, reaching us. *In words:* there is no second, hidden Trinity to be known apart from the one given.

**What the first half wins.** Christian prayer, addressed to the Father through the Son in the Spirit ([6.2](06-02-the-trinity-in-the-economy-liturgy-and-prayer.md)), is not a pious form laid over a God indifferent to its shape. Grace is God's self-gift, not a created something handed across a gap. And the three are not an appendix to a God already fully described without them — the idea the manual arrangement never taught but could leave standing.

**What critics fear the second half risks.** Two distinct worries.

*God's freedom.* The economy contains free acts: God need not have created or become man. If the immanent Trinity simply is the economic, and the economic includes those acts, then either they belong to what God is — so creation was not free, against what *Dei Filius* defines ([1.2](01-02-the-one-true-god-and-what-reason-can-reach.md)) — or the "is" is weaker than identity and needs saying. Paul Molnar has pressed this line in Catholic and Protestant debate under the heading of divine freedom; Thomas Weinandy and Gilles Emery press the same point from the side of simplicity ([1.3](01-03-simplicity-as-doctrine.md), [`thomistic-synthesis` 3.4](../../thomistic-synthesis/lessons/03-04-divine-simplicity-and-the-attributes.md)).

*God in himself and God for us.* God remains incomprehensible even to the blessed ([1.2](01-02-the-one-true-god-and-what-reason-can-reach.md)), and a strict reading of the second half threatens that: if the immanent Trinity has no content the economy does not display, theology can read God's inner life off salvation history. Then the cross is evidence about God's being, and the questions of [1.5](01-05-impassibility-and-the-god-who-suffers.md) reopen with the persons in hand.

**Rahner's own qualifications**, which belong to any fair statement of him. God's self-communication is free and gratuitous: the rule identifies *who* is given, not that the giving had to happen. God remains absolute mystery; the identity is no licence to comprehend. And he denies modalism outright, proposing three distinct manners of subsisting in place of "three persons" because the modern ear hears "person" as a separate centre of consciousness — a proposal that earned him the very charge he was avoiding.

**The crux.** Critics say the qualifications and the axiom pull apart: read strongly enough to do new work, the rule costs freedom; read weakly enough to keep freedom, it says what [6.1](06-01-the-missions-of-the-son-and-the-spirit.md) already said. Defenders call that a false choice and read the rule as a *methodological* identity — it forbids building an immanent Trinity out of anything but the economy, without asserting that God's being is constituted by history. Which description fits Rahner's text is the argument, and it is genuinely open among Catholic theologians. This lesson takes no side.

## Map of positions

| Position | The two Trinities | What it wins | What its critics press |
|---|---|---|---|
| **The received Latin account** (ST I q.43; [6.1](06-01-the-missions-of-the-son-and-the-spirit.md)) | Mission = eternal procession plus a created effect; nothing new in God | Freedom and immutability untouched; the person sent is the eternal person | That it let the treatise drift out of Christian life ([1.1](01-01-the-treatise-and-its-order.md)) |
| **Barth**, *Church Dogmatics* I/1 (1932) | God is toward us antecedently what he is in himself; one subject in three modes of being | Revelation is trustworthy; the doctrine is read off revelation | Whether "mode of being" keeps the three really distinct ([3.4](03-04-the-latin-rules-of-speech.md)) |
| **Rahner**, *The Trinity* (1967) | Identity, stated in both directions | The missions are how God really is; grace is self-gift | The second half: freedom, and God in himself |
| **Balthasar**, the theodramatic works | The processions are the ground and condition of possibility of the economy, cross included | The cross is Trinitarian all the way down | Whether "self-giving" and "distance" in God stay analogical ([1.3](01-03-simplicity-as-doctrine.md), [1.5](01-05-impassibility-and-the-god-who-suffers.md)) |
| **The radicalized rule** (Moltmann; LaCugna — [6.4](06-04-social-trinitarianism-and-its-critics.md)) | The immanent Trinity has no standing apart from the economy | Nothing left idle; the doctrine is wholly about salvation | That this is the reading the freedom objection was written for |

**Barth's distance from Rahner** is worth stating exactly, because the two get merged. Barth, a generation earlier and a Protestant, asserts the first half emphatically and refuses the symmetry: God's being toward us corresponds to and is grounded in his eternal being, and God would be the same triune God without a world. The grounding runs one way only. The echo between his "modes of being" and Rahner's "manners of subsisting" is no accident — both react against the modern concept of person — but it is shared vocabulary, not a shared axiom.

**Balthasar** accepts the first half and goes where Rahner does not. The Father's begetting of the Son is a total self-giving, an [*Ur-kenosis*](../reference.md#ur-kenosis) in which he keeps nothing back, and the self-emptying of Philippians 2 is its created expression. Because Father and Son are already held apart by an infinite distance that the Spirit holds open in love, God can go into the far country of the cross without ceasing to be himself: the distance of sin is contained within a greater Trinitarian distance. The kenosis question of [1.5](01-05-impassibility-and-the-god-who-suffers.md) returns sharpened, now with the persons in hand. His guard is that the language is analogical and that what grounds the cross is fullness, not lack; Karen Kilby objects that the account claims a view of God's inner life no theologian is positioned to have. His Holy Saturday thesis and his Christology belong to [`christology`](../../christology/syllabus.md) (6.1 and 6.4 there); the Trinitarian frame is owned here.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (the machinery on a clean case: the Spirit given in grace).** A person in prayer is moved to an act of charity genuinely beyond him. [6.1](06-01-the-missions-of-the-son-and-the-spirit.md) analyses this as an invisible mission: the Spirit's eternal procession plus a new presence in the creature, the created effect in the soul ceded to [`creation-grace-last-things`](../../creation-grace-last-things/syllabus.md). Run the rule's first half over it. The Spirit present here is the Spirit who eternally proceeds: God gives himself, not a created substitute and not one undifferentiated Giver under a third name. That is not a new doctrine — it is the tradition's teaching that the gift of grace includes the uncreated Gift, restated as a rule for reading. Notice what it forbids: treating "Father, Son and Spirit" in the economy as three appropriations of one divine action fails the rule, and had already failed [4.5](04-05-common-proper-appropriated.md). Here the rule wins and costs nothing.

**Example 2 (the hard case, where it strains: could the Father have become man?).** Aquinas answers yes. At ST III q.3 a.5 the principle of the assumption is the divine power, common to the three, and the term is a person; a power regarding several terms indifferently can terminate in any of them. So "the Father or the Holy Ghost could have assumed flesh even as the Son."

Rahner treats that article as a symptom. If any person could have become incarnate, the Incarnation tells us nothing proper about the Son, and the economy has stopped revealing who the persons are — exactly what the rule forbids. His counter-thesis: the Son is the Father's self-utterance, so the person who can be uttered into the world is the Word, and the Incarnation does show a property.

Watch the cost each way. Rahner's denial must explain how assuming, an outward work, can be governed by a personal property without breaching the rule that the outward works are undivided ([4.5](04-05-common-proper-appropriated.md)); his answer is that the union terminates in one person, so what is proper fixes the term without dividing the power. Aquinas's yes must live with the conclusion that the economy's central event is, as to which person it befell, not revelatory of the persons. Both argue inside the same constraints, and the level is the same for both: a freely disputed school question fixed by no magisterial act, so calling either answer dogma is the strict error of [1.1](01-01-the-treatise-and-its-order.md). The Christology is [`christology`](../../christology/syllabus.md)'s; here the article is a test rig.

## Watch out

- **You might think the rule says God is nothing but what he does for us.** That is the strong reading, and Rahner refuses it when he insists the self-communication is free and God remains mystery. The error has a name: if God's eternal life just is the three ways he acts toward us, Father, Son and Spirit become three roles of one actor — **[modalism](../reference.md#modalism)**, refused by the Athanasian Creed's rule against confounding the persons ([3.4](03-04-the-latin-rules-of-speech.md)).
- **["Modes of being" and "manners of subsisting"](../reference.md#modes-of-being-and-manners-of-subsisting) are not by themselves modalism, but they are one sentence away.** The test is not the noun but whether the three are really distinct *in God* and not only in our account of him. Barth and Rahner both say they are, and both were accused anyway.
- **Two level errors, pointing opposite ways.** The rule is freely disputed: no magisterial act has adopted it and none has condemned it. Calling it "the Church's modern teaching on the Trinity" promotes an explanation to dogma; calling it heterodox ignores how widely Catholic theologians of every school use it ([`fundamental-theology` 4.4](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-04-the-ladder-of-doctrinal-authority.md)).
- **Do not read Balthasar's "distance" in God as suffering in the divine nature.** He describes the relations of origin under an image of self-gift, and is not conceding what the modern passibility case wants ([1.5](01-05-impassibility-and-the-god-who-suffers.md)). Whether the image stays analogical is the live question.

## One-liner

> Everybody grants that the God who meets us is the God who is; the argument is over whether that sentence can be read backwards without making the world necessary.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢) *(Exegetical.)*** An invented paragraph from a parish adult-formation handout:

> "Modern theology has recovered a great truth: the economic Trinity is the immanent Trinity. What God is toward us is all that God is. Toward us he is Creator, Redeemer and Sanctifier — and there is nothing further, no hidden inner life behind those three. The old speculation about an eternal Trinity apart from what God does for us is exactly what the recovery leaves behind."

(a) Name the error the paragraph lands in and say which half of the rule it is misusing. (b) Name one thing Rahner himself holds that the paragraph contradicts, and one thing the paragraph gets right. Two sentences per part.

**P2 (🟡) *(Exegetical.)*** Assign a level on the ladder to each of the following, and where a level is fixed by an act, name the act. One line each.

(i) That the one sent in the Incarnation is the eternally begotten Son himself.
(ii) That God created the world freely, under no necessity.
(iii) That a mission is the eternal procession plus a new way of being present in a creature.
(iv) Rahner's rule.
(v) Aquinas's thesis that the Father could have assumed flesh.

**P3 (🔴, optional) *(Evaluative.)*** Balthasar proposes that the Father's begetting of the Son is a self-giving in which he keeps nothing back, and that this eternal self-gift is the ground and condition of possibility of the cross. Any verdict, 150 words or fewer: does the proposal require weakening what [1.3](01-03-simplicity-as-doctrine.md) and [1.5](01-05-impassibility-and-the-god-who-suffers.md) hold, or is it analogical language the tradition can absorb? State the proposal first as its best readers would state it, name the one constraint you are testing it against, and say what would have to be shown to settle the question.

<details>
<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical.)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** **modalism**. "Creator, Redeemer and Sanctifier" with "nothing further" turns the three into three roles of one actor toward the world — and the trio is itself an appropriation scheme, not the proper names ([4.5](04-05-common-proper-appropriated.md)). The misuse is of the **second half**: from "there is no hidden Trinity behind the economy" it slides to "God has no eternal inner life," which is a different claim. The first half is not what the paragraph gets wrong.

**Must hit, strict (b):** something from Rahner's own qualifications — that the self-communication is free and gratuitous, that God remains absolute mystery, or that he denies modalism and affirms the real distinction of the three. What the paragraph gets right: that the Trinity given in the economy is God himself and not a mask over an unknown God.

**Wrong turns:** calling the error tritheism (nothing here multiplies the divine subject); saying Rahner would endorse the paragraph, which reverses his express denial; treating "Creator, Redeemer, Sanctifier" as the problem on its own — the fatal word is "nothing further."

**Model answer:** (a) Modalism: the three become three roles one God plays toward us, which the Athanasian Creed's refusal to confound the persons excludes. It misuses the second half of the rule, reading "no hidden Trinity behind the economy" as "no eternal life at all." (b) Rahner holds the self-communication to be free and gratuitous and God to remain absolute mystery, both of which the paragraph's "all that God is" denies. It is right that what is given in the economy is God himself, not a created stand-in.

---

**P2** *(Exegetical.)*

**Must hit, strict:**

(i) **Rung 1, defined dogma** — Nicaea (325) and the creed of 381; the Son begotten of the Father, consubstantial, who came down and was made man. One and the same subject.
(ii) **Rung 1, defined dogma** — *Dei Filius* chapter 1 with its canons on God the creator ([1.2](01-02-the-one-true-god-and-what-reason-can-reach.md)), which attach an anathema to denying that the world was created freely.
(iii) **Rung 5, freely disputed** — no act fixes it. *That* the Son and the Spirit are sent is in scripture and the creeds; the Thomist analysis of what a mission *adds* to a procession is the Latin tradition's explanation, carried by the common doctor. Credit for naming that seam, and for noting that some manuals give the analysis the higher note of common teaching.
(iv) **Rung 5, freely disputed**, fixed by no magisterial act: a twentieth-century theologian's proposal, neither adopted nor condemned.
(v) **Rung 5, freely disputed**, fixed by no act; the common opinion of the schools, and Aquinas's, but not doctrine.

**Wrong turns:** promoting (iii) to dogma because it is Aquinas's and appears in every manual — the standing error of this course; demoting (ii) to "the common teaching" because the word "freely" sounds philosophical; giving (iv) a level on the strength of how many theologians use it, which is not what fixes a rung; answering (v) with "condemned," which no act does.

---

**P3** *(Evaluative.)*

**Must hit, any verdict:** (1) the proposal stated at strength before any critique — the begetting as total self-gift, of which the Incarnation's self-emptying is the created expression, and the Trinitarian distance as what makes it possible for God to enter God-forsakenness without ceasing to be God; the point is fullness, not lack. (2) One named constraint, used precisely: simplicity as doctrine ([1.3](01-03-simplicity-as-doctrine.md)) if the worry is that "giving away" implies parts or loss in God; the unchangeableness of *Dei Filius* and *Firmiter* if the worry is that something is received; the level question of [1.5](01-05-impassibility-and-the-god-who-suffers.md) if the worry is passibility. (3) What would settle it — some statement of whether the language can be cashed analogically, e.g. whether "self-giving" can be predicated of the eternal generation with the greater-dissimilarity rule of Lateran IV honoured, or whether it only works if something in God is genuinely diminished.

**Wrong turns:** arguing against a Balthasar who says the divine nature suffers, which is not his thesis and fails the Fairness discipline before the argument starts; treating the question as closed by impassibility, when the level of impassibility is itself the issue ([1.5](01-05-impassibility-and-the-god-who-suffers.md)); answering with a verdict and no constraint.

**Model answer, one of several:** Balthasar's claim is that the Father's begetting is an unreserved handing over of all he is, and that this eternal self-gift is why the Son can be sent into God-forsakenness without the Trinity breaking: the distance sin opens is smaller than the distance love already holds. Tested against simplicity, the language strains: "keeping nothing back" suggests something that could have been kept, and in a God with no parts and no potency there is nothing to withhold. But the tradition already predicates generation, procession and love of God analogically, and Lateran IV's rule that every likeness carries a greater unlikeness is exactly the instrument for this. What would settle it is whether the proposal needs the loss to be real to do its work on the cross. If it does, simplicity bites; if the fullness reading holds, it is an image the tradition can absorb.

## Flashback

**From Lesson [6.1](06-01-the-missions-of-the-son-and-the-spirit.md) (The missions of the Son and the Spirit):** *(Exegetical.)* Isaias 48:16 (Douay-Rheims) has the speaker say "and now the Lord God hath sent me, and his spirit" — read of the Son, a text in which he is sent by the Holy Ghost, though the Son does not proceed from the Holy Ghost. Aquinas makes it the *sed contra* of ST I q.43 a.8, "whether a divine person is sent only by the person whence he proceeds eternally"; Augustine had already written that the Son is sent by himself and by the Holy Ghost, and the Holy Ghost by himself and by the Son (*De Trinitate* II.5), against the older opinion that a divine person is sent only by the one whence he proceeds. Aquinas answers that there is truth in both opinions.

Reconstruct that answer in **at most four numbered premises and a conclusion**, and mark the premise that rests on the rule that the outward works of the three are undivided. Then, in **one sentence**: the third objection had argued that on this account a man could send the Holy Ghost — say how the answer blocks it.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict:** the reconstruction must turn on a double signification of *sender*, never on a double procession — nothing in the answer gives the Son any origin from the Spirit.

- **P1.** To be sent is to be from another, together with a new effect, visible or invisible, in a creature — the mission of q.43 a.2, the eternal procession plus a temporal term.
- **P2.** So "sender" may designate either the principle of the *person* sent, or the principle of the *effect* implied in the mission.
- **P3.** Taken as principle of the person, only the one whence that person proceeds sends: the Son by the Father alone, the Holy Ghost by the Father and the Son.
- **P4.** *(The premise resting on the undivided outward works.)* Taken as principle of the effect, that effect is a work toward creatures, and the outward works of the three are undivided; so the whole Trinity sends.
- **∴** Both opinions hold in part: *to be sent* belongs only to a person who is from another, while *to send* belongs to each person. Augustine's sentence is true in the second sense, the older opinion in the first, and Isaias 48:16 needs no explaining away.

**The last sentence:** sending in the second sense means being the principle of the effect, and the effect implied in this mission is grace; a man cannot cause the effect of grace, so nothing in P4 lets a man send the Holy Ghost.

**Wrong turns:** resolving the puzzle by referring Isaias only to Christ's human nature — that is the older opinion, which Aquinas says holds part of the truth and not the whole, so a reconstruction that stops there has dropped half the answer; marking P3 rather than P4 as the undivided-works premise, when relation to the principle of the *person* is exactly what is not common to the three; inferring from P4 that the Father may be sent, which a.4 excludes because to be sent still requires being from another; introducing any procession of the Son from the Spirit.

**Model answer:** the four premises and the conclusion as set out above, with P4 marked. For the objection: because sending in the effect sense requires causing the effect, and the effect here is grace, which no man can cause.

</details>
