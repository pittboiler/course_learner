# The Triune God — Syllabus

> Catholic Theology · Tier 2 · ~25 lessons · Prereqs: [`thomistic-synthesis`](../thomistic-synthesis/syllabus.md), [`patristics`](../patristics/syllabus.md) · Roadmap id: `trinity-and-god`

## Goal

This is the treatise the manuals call *De Deo Uno et Trino*: what the Catholic Church holds about God — first about the one God and his attributes, then about the three persons — taught from within the tradition, with the weight of every claim marked. You will finish able to read the defining texts (the creed of Nicaea and its anathemas, the creed of 381, the Athanasian Creed, Lateran IV's *Firmiter*, Lyons II, Florence, Vatican I's *Dei Filius* chapter 1) and say exactly what each defines and what it leaves to the schools; to gather the scriptural evidence and say what each text does and does not establish on its own; to run the Latin analysis of the mystery — two processions, four real relations, three subsistent persons, the missions in which those processions reach us — as Augustine's *De Trinitate* and *Summa Theologiae* I qq.27-43 give it; to state the filioque and the Greek monarchy of the Father each as its own tradition states it; and to place modern Trinitarian theology (Rahner's rule, Balthasar, social trinitarianism) against the constraints the dogma actually imposes. It deliberately skips a great deal. The Incarnation and the Son's two natures from Ephesus onward belong to [`christology`](../christology/syllabus.md); creation, the image of God, grace, the Spirit's indwelling as grace and the last things to `creation-grace-last-things`; the events and politics of the councils and of the 1054 estrangement to [`church-history`](../church-history/syllabus.md); the Fathers' own arguments in their own texts to [`patristics`](../patristics/syllabus.md); the Thomist metaphysics of simplicity, analogy and the names of God to [`thomistic-synthesis`](../thomistic-synthesis/syllabus.md); whether the divine attributes are philosophically coherent and whether any argument proves God exists to [`philosophy-of-religion`](../philosophy-of-religion/syllabus.md); and the filioque argued out against Orthodox objections to `apologetics-catholic-claims`. It also skips Trinitarian liturgy and iconography as such, the non-Chalcedonian and Reformation Trinitarian debates, and any comparative treatment of Jewish and Islamic objections to the doctrine.

## How practice works in this course

Problems follow [HUMANITIES-BUILD-BRIEF.md](../../HUMANITIES-BUILD-BRIEF.md), with the Catholic Theology stance: taught from within the tradition, with each claim's level marked on the ladder in the Notes of [`fundamental-theology`](../fundamental-theology/syllabus.md) 4.4. This course's characteristic discipline is that **the dogma and its explanation are different things, and confusing them in either direction is an exegetical error.** That there are three persons in one divine nature is defined. That a divine person *is* a subsistent relation, that the Son proceeds by way of intellect and the Spirit by way of will, that "person" in God is best approached through the mind's memory, understanding and love — these are the Latin theological tradition's way of holding the dogma without contradiction, carried by the common doctor and never defined as such. Demoting the first to a school opinion and promoting the second to dogma are both graded strictly.

- **Close reading is the workhorse**, graded as exegesis: a passage of 150 words or fewer from a creed or conciliar constitution, from Augustine's *De Trinitate* (Haddan's translation in the *Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers*), or from the *Summa Theologiae* (English Dominican Province). The question is always which reading the words support and which words decide it.
- **Reconstruct** puts an article's *respondeo* or a conciliar argument into numbered premises, marking which premises rest on scripture, which on an earlier definition, and which on the metaphysics assumed from [`thomistic-synthesis`](../thomistic-synthesis/syllabus.md).
- **Apply to a hard case** runs the analysis on something the texts do not discuss: which mission is at work in a given event; whether a given predicate is common, proper or appropriated; what the relational rule forbids saying.
- **Diagnose** uses invented homilies, hymn texts, catechetical handouts and book reviews that slide into modalism, subordinationism, tritheism, or the treatment of an appropriation as proper. No invented words are ever attributed to a real person, living or dead.
- **Evaluative parts are verdict-neutral and reserved for questions genuinely open among Catholic theologians**: whether the treatise should begin with the one God or with the persons; whether divine impassibility survives the modern objections in the form the manuals gave it; whether *homoousios* is development or importation; whether the psychological analogy illuminates or distorts; whether the Latin and Greek formulations of the Spirit's procession are complementary; whether Rahner's rule wins more than it risks; whether social trinitarianism stays inside the dogma. Any verdict passes if the required moves are made.
- **Apologetic parts** are rare and never aimed at the Orthodox, whose case belongs to `apologetics-catholic-claims`. There are two: the charge that Trinitarian dogma is the Hellenization of a simpler gospel (Harnack, whose *What is Christianity?* is public domain), and the logical problem of the Trinity as contemporary analytic unitarians press it. Each states the other side first, and that statement is graded before the reply.
- There are **no formal parts.**

Scripture is quoted in the Douay-Rheims (Challoner), with the lesson's own translation of a Greek or Latin word where the word is the point (*homoousios*, *hypostasis*, *ekporeuomai*, *processio*, *persona*). Modern authors (Rahner, Balthasar, Moltmann, Zizioulas, Kilby, Emery, Weinandy) are paraphrased and cited by work, never used for close reading.

## Dangerous Checklist

When you finish, you can:

- [ ] State what *Dei Filius* chapter 1 and Lateran IV's *Firmiter* define about the one God, word by word, and separate the defined attributes from the scholastic explanations of them
- [ ] Say what divine simplicity as *doctrine* commits a Catholic to, what it leaves to the schools, and why it is the hinge on which the Trinitarian analysis later turns
- [ ] State the case for divine impassibility as the tradition holds it and the modern case against it at its best, and weigh them without overstating the authority of either
- [ ] Gather the scriptural evidence for the Trinity and say exactly what each text establishes about distinction and about divinity, and what no single text settles
- [ ] Trace the road from the baptismal confession to *homoousios* to the creed of 381, and answer the charge that the dogma is a Greek philosophical accretion
- [ ] Read the Athanasian Creed, *Firmiter*, and Florence's "everything is one where there is no opposition of relation", and state the rule each one supplies for speaking about God
- [ ] Explain the two processions, say why there are exactly two, and show why procession in God is neither creation nor change nor a succession in time
- [ ] Derive the four real relations and the three persons from the two processions, and say what "person" means when it is said of God
- [ ] Tell what is common to the three, what is proper to each, and what is merely appropriated, and catch an appropriation being preached as a property
- [ ] Say what a divine mission adds to a procession, tell the visible from the invisible missions, and stop cleanly where the doctrine of grace begins
- [ ] State the Latin doctrine of the Spirit's procession and the Greek doctrine of the monarchy of the Father, each as its own tradition states it, and say what the 1995 clarification claims is terminological and what is not
- [ ] Place Rahner's rule, Balthasar's Trinitarian theology and social trinitarianism on one map, name the doctrinal constraint each must satisfy, and state where its critics say it fails

## Modules

### Module 1: De Deo Uno - the one God the Church confesses

Before the three, the one: what kind of treatise this is, and what the Church actually defines about God's existence, his simplicity, his unchangeableness, and whether he can be said to suffer.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1.1 | The treatise and its order | Say what *De Deo Uno et Trino* is for, why it is built in that order, and how to cite inside it | the manual structure (one God, then triune) and its Greek alternative (begin from the Father and the persons); Rahner's complaint that separating the two treatises leaves Christians practically monotheist; the counter-case that the order follows the road of knowledge, not of being; Théodore de Régnon's "Greeks start from the persons, Latins from the essence" paradigm and the modern critique of it (Barnes, Ayres); citation conventions (creed and canon, council and constitution, CCC paragraph, ST part-question-article); the ladder cited from [`fundamental-theology`](../fundamental-theology/syllabus.md) 4.4 |
| 1.2 | The one true God, and what reason can reach | State what Vatican I and Lateran IV define about God, and what they say about knowing him | *Dei Filius* ch. 1: one true living God, creator and lord, almighty, eternal, immense, incomprehensible, infinite in intellect and will and in every perfection, really and essentially distinct from the world; Lateran IV's *Firmiter*: one principle of all things, three persons but one altogether simple essence; the dogma that God *can* be known by reason cited from [`fundamental-theology`](../fundamental-theology/syllabus.md) 1.2 and not re-taught; the Trinity as a mystery strictly so called, not demonstrable by reason (ST I q.32 a.1) and the limits that places on what arguments may claim; incomprehensibility and the beatific vision named and ceded |
| 1.3 | Simplicity as doctrine | Say what simplicity commits a Catholic to, and where the schools begin | Lateran IV and *Dei Filius* on God as altogether simple; the metaphysics assumed from [`thomistic-synthesis`](../thomistic-synthesis/syllabus.md) 3.4 and not redone; what follows for God-talk: the attributes are not parts, not really distinct from the essence, not accidents; Scotus's formal distinction as a Catholic alternative; Lateran IV's rule that no likeness between creator and creature can be stated without a greater unlikeness, and analogy cited from [`thomistic-synthesis`](../thomistic-synthesis/syllabus.md) 4.4; the property and modal-collapse objections cited to [`philosophy-of-religion`](../philosophy-of-religion/syllabus.md) 1.4, not answered again; why simplicity is what makes the Trinitarian problem hard |
| 1.4 | Immutability, eternity, immensity | State the three attributes as the Church teaches them and say what each rules out | unchangeable (*Dei Filius* ch. 1, *Firmiter*) and what "change" means here; eternity as "the simultaneously-whole and perfect possession of interminable life" (Boethius's definition in the English Dominican wording Aquinas defends at ST I q.10 a.1 — not the familiar "whole, simultaneous and perfect" paraphrase; whose definition is read in its own terms in [`ancient-medieval-philosophy`](../ancient-medieval-philosophy/syllabus.md) 5.3 and assessed in [`philosophy-of-religion`](../philosophy-of-religion/syllabus.md) 1.4); timeless vs everlasting as a theological dispute, not a defined point; immensity and omnipresence (ST I q.8: by essence, presence and power), and the difference between God's presence to all things and pantheism; what these attributes do to petitionary prayer, and the answer that God's unchanging will includes the prayer |
| 1.5 | Impassibility, and the God who suffers | State the tradition's case for impassibility, the modern case against it, and what is actually defined | Patripassianism condemned in the third century; Constantinople II's confession that one of the Holy Trinity was crucified in the flesh, and the communication of idioms that licenses it (the Christology ceded); the manuals' derivation of impassibility from immutability and simplicity, and its theological note; the twentieth-century turn: Moltmann's *The Crucified God* (1972), Jüngel, Kitamori, process theology, Balthasar's Trinitarian reading of the cross; John Paul II's *Dominum et Vivificantem* (1986) on a divine "pain" in a carefully qualified sense; Weinandy's defence; the exegetical trap of taking scripture's language of divine grief or repentance univocally |

**Boss problem 1:** Supplied: *Dei Filius* chapter 1 (Schaff's public-domain translation) and Lateran IV's *Firmiter* (the lesson's own translation of the Latin). (a) Close-read both: list every attribute predicated of the one God, and mark which are predicated of the three persons together and which the texts attach to a single person. Which words in *Firmiter* carry the claim that the persons do not divide the essence? *(Exegetical.)* (b) Assign a level, and name the act that fixes it, for each of: that God is altogether simple; that God is immutable; that God is impassible; that God's attributes are not really distinct from his essence; that God's eternity is timeless rather than everlasting. Overstating any of these is an error; so is calling a defined attribute a school opinion. *(Exegetical.)* (c) Diagnose two invented passages: a homily in which God "changes his mind when we pray hard enough", and a review claiming that the God of classical theism "cannot love, because love means being affected". Name what each gets wrong, cite the text that corrects it, and say what true thing each is reaching for. *(Exegetical.)* (d) In 300-500 words, any verdict: does the modern case that God genuinely suffers require denying what *Dei Filius* chapter 1 defines, or only the manuals' explanation of it? Use (a) and (b), state the strongest form of the case you reject, and say what would have to be shown for the question to be settled. *(Evaluative.)*

### Module 2: The Trinity revealed

The dogma is not a deduction. This module gathers what the Church reads in scripture, says carefully what each text does and does not establish, and follows the road from the baptismal confession to a defined faith.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2.1 | The one God of Israel, and the Church's backward reading | Say how the Church reads the Old Testament triadically without claiming the Trinity is taught there | the Shema and the divine name; the word, the wisdom and the spirit of God as God's own action, not as persons in the text's own sense (exegesis cited to [`old-testament`](../old-testament/syllabus.md) 4.5, 5.2, 5.4); the patristic and liturgical reading of Genesis 1:26 and Genesis 18, and why Aquinas warns that non-cogent arguments for the faith expose it to ridicule (ST I q.32 a.1); *sensus plenior* and typology cited to [`old-testament`](../old-testament/syllabus.md) 1.2; the rule this course keeps: the Trinity is known from the New Testament and confessed by the Church, and the Old Testament is read in that light, not mined for proofs |
| 2.2 | The Father and the Son in the New Testament | State what the New Testament establishes about the Son's distinction from and unity with the Father | Jesus's address to the Father and the *Abba* usage; the Johannine prologue and "the Word was God"; John 5, 10:30, 14:9-11, 17:5; Philippians 2:6-11 and Hebrews 1:1-4 with the Wisdom 7:26 background; 1 Corinthians 8:6 as a split Shema; the early high Christology debate cited to [`new-testament`](../new-testament/syllabus.md) 2.5, 4.3, 6.2 and left to the exegetes; what each text settles on its own, and the texts subordinationists leaned on (John 14:28, Proverbs 8:22, Mark 13:32) with the Church's reading of each |

**Boss problem 2:** Supplied (Douay-Rheims): Matthew 28:19; 2 Corinthians 13:13; 1 Corinthians 8:6; John 15:26; John 14:28. ### Module 3: The dogma in its defining texts

Four moments at which the Church fixed words to the mystery: Nicaea, the Cappadocian settlement, Constantinople, and the Latin texts that gave the doctrine its rules of speech.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3.1 | Nicaea (325) | Say exactly what Nicaea defined, and what it did not | Arius's position as his opponents report it and as the surviving fragments give it; the creed's clauses: "from the substance of the Father", "begotten not made", *homoousios*; the anathemas ("there was when he was not"; "he came into being from nothing"; "of another *hypostasis* or *ousia*"); the council's use of *hypostasis* and *ousia* as equivalents, and what that later cost; what the creed says about the Spirit (almost nothing) and why; events ceded to [`church-history`](../church-history/syllabus.md) 2.1, Athanasius's soteriological argument to [`patristics`](../patristics/syllabus.md) 3.1 |

| 3.3 | Constantinople I (381) and the divinity of the Spirit | Say what the creed of 381 teaches about the Spirit and how the Church got there | the Pneumatomachi and what they conceded and denied; the creed's clause: Lord and giver of life, proceeding from the Father, adored and glorified with the Father and the Son, who spoke by the prophets; why the council praised the Spirit in the language of worship rather than applying *homoousios* to him; Basil's argument from the doxology cited to [`patristics`](../patristics/syllabus.md) 3.2; the text's first secure attestation in the acts of Chalcedon and what historians make of that; the creed as the Church's own rule of faith, and Ephesus's prohibition of a rival creed, later invoked against the Latin addition |

Use Vincent's canon and at least three of Newman's notes ([`fundamental-theology`](../fundamental-theology/syllabus.md) 5.1-5.2), the texts of (a), and 2.4, and state the strongest case for the view you reject. *(Evaluative.)*

### Module 4: Processions, relations, persons

The Latin analysis: how the tradition holds three real distinctions inside a God with no composition at all. Augustine finds the logic of relation and then looks for the mystery's image in the mind; Aquinas turns both into a system.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4.1 | Augustine: relation, substance and the rules of predication | Explain Augustine's solution to the logical problem, and what "person" is doing in his Latin | *De Trinitate* Books 5-7 (NPNF): the Arian dilemma that whatever is said of God is said substantially, so "unbegotten" and "begotten" would name different substances; Augustine's answer that some things are said *ad se* and some *ad aliquid*, and relation is neither substance nor accident in God; why the three are not three gods, three goods or three almighties; the sentence on why the Latins say "three persons" at all (Haddan, *De Trinitate* V.9: "not that it might be [completely] spoken, but that it might not be left [wholly] unspoken"; Latin *non ut illud diceretur sed ne taceretur*) — quote the translation, not the familiar paraphrase; *persona* against Greek *hypostasis*, and the mutual suspicion the translation caused; the equality of the persons and the unity of their outward works |
| 4.2 | Augustine: the image in the mind | State the psychological analogy, what it claims, and the limits Augustine himself puts on it | *De Trinitate* Books 8-15: the movement inward from love (lover, beloved, love) to mind, knowledge and love (Book 9) to memory, understanding and will (Book 10); the analogy as an *image* in a rational creature, not a proof; Book 15's own dismantling of it — the distance between the image and what it images; what the analogy is for (an inward road to a mystery already believed); the modern charge that it makes the persons into faculties of one subject, and the reply |
| 4.3 | The two processions | Explain what procession in God is, why there are exactly two, and what each is called | ST I q.27: procession as remaining within, not going outside, against both emanationism and creation; generation by way of intellect, because what proceeds is a likeness of its source in the same nature; the procession of love by way of will, and why it is not called generation; why exactly two, given that only intellect and will are immanent operations in an intellectual nature; what "eternal generation" excludes (priority in time, change, a passive potency in the Father); the whole analysis marked as theological explanation, not defined doctrine |
| 4.4 | Four relations, three persons | Derive the relations and the persons, and say what "person" means when said of God | ST I q.28: the four real relations (paternity, filiation, spiration, procession) and why spiration is not a distinct person, since it stands in no relative opposition to paternity or filiation; Florence's "no opposition of relation" applied (3.4); ST I q.29: Boethius's definition of person (cited to [`metaphysics`](../metaphysics/syllabus.md) 6.5) and why it must be stretched for God; person in God as a subsistent relation — relation that *is* the essence, distinguished only by its opposite; ST I qq.30-31: the language rules (*alius* not *aliud*; how "Trinity", "only" and "alone" may be used); why three, and not four or two |
| 4.5 | What is common, what is proper, what is appropriated | Sort any statement about God into common, proper or appropriated, and get the rules right | ST I qq.33-38: the Father as principle without principle, innascibility; the Son as Word and as Image; the Spirit as Love and as Gift; qq.39-41: essence and person, the notional acts; the rule that the persons' outward works are undivided (*opera Trinitatis ad extra indivisa sunt*), which ST states and applies at **q.45 a.6**, whose objection 2 raises the creed's own three clauses; appropriation defined (q.39 a.7), with the power/wisdom/goodness triad at **a.8** as one of three traditional schemes: attributing a common perfection to one person because it resembles what is proper to him — power to the Father, wisdom to the Son, goodness to the Spirit; the standing abuse of preaching an appropriation as a property; q.42 a.5 on the mutual indwelling of the persons, and *perichoresis* / *circumincessio* as the Greek and Latin names for it |

**Boss problem 4:** Supplied (English Dominican Province): ST I q.27 a.1; q.28 a.3; q.29 a.4; and Florence's sentence "everything is one where there is no opposition of relation" (the lesson's own translation). (a) Reconstruct q.27 a.1's *respondeo* in numbered premises: what must "procession" mean for it to apply to God at all, and which premise rules out the emanationist and the creationist readings? Mark which premises come from scripture and which from the metaphysics assumed from [`thomistic-synthesis`](../thomistic-synthesis/syllabus.md) 2.1. *(Exegetical.)* (b) Close-read q.29 a.4: which words carry the claim that "person" in God signifies relation, and which words keep that from making the persons mere relations of reason ([`thomistic-synthesis`](../thomistic-synthesis/syllabus.md) 4.3)? *(Exegetical.)* (c) Sort each of the following into common, proper, or appropriated, and give the rule and the text that decides: the Father is wise; the Father begets; the Father creates; the Son is sent; the Spirit is Gift; the Spirit sanctifies; the Son is eternal. Then diagnose two invented sentences from a parish catechetical handout — "the Father made the world, the Son redeemed it, and the Spirit makes us holy, each doing his own job" and "God shows us three different sides of himself" — naming the error in each and the text that corrects it. *(Exegetical.)* (d) A contemporary analytic unitarian argues that the doctrine is inconsistent: the Father is God, the Son is God, the Father is not the Son, and there is one God, so identity is being used in two ways at once. State that objection at its strongest in 150 words. Then reply in 150 more, using the relational analysis of (a)-(c), and say plainly what the reply does and does not remove. *(Apologetic.)*

### Module 5: The filioque

Why the West says the Spirit proceeds from the Father *and the Son*, what the Greek tradition holds instead, and what the modern clarifications claim is a difference of language rather than of faith.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5.1 | The Latin doctrine | State the Western teaching and the argument for it | the scriptural texts (John 15:26, 16:14-15, Romans 8:9, Galatians 4:6) as the West reads them; Augustine's *principaliter* — the Spirit proceeds principally from the Father, who gave the Son that the Spirit should proceed from him too; ST I q.36 a.2's argument that if the Spirit did not proceed from the Son there would be no relative opposition between them, and so no distinction; Lyons II and Florence: from both as from one principle and by a single spiration; the level this carries, and what it does *not* claim (two principles, two spirations, a diminished Father) |
| 5.2 | The Greek doctrine, and the history of the addition | State the Greek teaching as Greek theologians state it, and how the clause entered the Latin creed | the monarchy of the Father as sole source and cause within the Godhead; *ekporeuomai* in John 15:26 as origination from the cause, against the wider Latin *procedere*; "through the Son" (*dia tou Huiou*) and the eternal shining-forth reading; Photius and the "from the Father alone" formula; later Byzantine positions (Gregory of Cyprus, Palamas) on an eternal manifestation distinct from origination; the clause's spread through Spain and Francia and its late acceptance at Rome, with the events ceded to [`church-history`](../church-history/syllabus.md) 4.2; the canonical objection from Ephesus (3.3), which is about adding to the creed rather than about the doctrine |
| 5.3 | The clarifications | Say what the modern Catholic clarifications claim, and what remains open | CCC 246-248 on the two traditions; the 1995 clarification of the Pontifical Council for Promoting Christian Unity, *The Greek and Latin Traditions regarding the Procession of the Holy Spirit*: the monarchy of the Father affirmed, the asymmetry between *ekporeusis* and *processio*, and the statement that the filioque must not be understood as contradicting the Father's monarchy; the Catholic use of the Greek creed without the addition; the 2003 agreed statement of the North American Orthodox-Catholic Consultation and its recommendations; what is doctrinally at stake (the Son's role in the Spirit's eternal origin) and what is terminological; the argued dispute ceded to `apologetics-catholic-claims` |

**Boss problem 5:** (a) State the Latin doctrine exactly as Lyons II and Florence state it, then reconstruct ST I q.36 a.2's argument in numbered premises and mark the premise that carries the whole weight. *(Exegetical.)* (b) State the Greek doctrine as a Greek theologian would state it: what the monarchy of the Father claims, what *ekporeusis* names, and what "through the Son" affirms and denies. This part is graded on fidelity to the Greek position, strictly, and an answer that states it only as the Latin tradition summarizes it fails. *(Exegetical.)* (c) Close-read the 1995 clarification's treatment of the two verbs: which distinction does it draw, what does it say follows for the creed in Greek, and what does it explicitly refuse to concede? *(Exegetical.)* (d) In 300-500 words, any verdict: are the two formulations complementary expressions of one faith, or genuinely different doctrines whose reconciliation is still owed? Catholic doctrine on the procession is not in question here; what is disputed among Catholic theologians is the relation between the two formulations. Use (a)-(c), name the single premise the two sides part on, and say what would settle it. *(Evaluative.)*

### Module 6: The missions, and modern Trinitarian theology

The Trinity reaches us: the persons are sent, and the whole economy is Trinitarian. Then the twentieth century's attempt to put the doctrine back at the centre of Christian life, and the argument about whether it stayed inside the dogma.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 6.1 | The missions of the Son and the Spirit | Say what a mission adds to a procession, and tell the visible from the invisible | ST I q.43: a mission is the eternal procession plus a new way of being present in a creature; the sender is the one from whom the sent person proceeds, so the Father is never sent; nothing new in God, everything new in the creature; visible missions (the Incarnation, the Jordan, Pentecost) and invisible missions (the persons given in grace); the Incarnation as the term of the Son's visible mission ceded to [`christology`](../christology/syllabus.md), the created grace of the invisible mission ceded to `creation-grace-last-things`; why the whole Trinity effects a mission that terminates in one person |
| 6.2 | The Trinity in the economy, the liturgy and prayer | Read the shape of Christian worship and life as evidence of, and answer to, the doctrine | the Trinity as the central mystery of Christian faith and life (CCC 234); the Trinitarian shape of prayer (*to the Father, through the Son, in the Holy Spirit*) and of the doxologies, with the baptismal formula's sacramental law ceded to `sacraments-and-liturgy`; appropriations at work in the liturgy and in the creed's own tripartite structure; Augustine's *vestigia trinitatis* in creation, and why the tradition treats them as traces rather than proofs (2.1); what a Christian is actually claiming in the sign of the cross |
| 6.3 | Rahner's rule and the return of the immanent Trinity | State Rahner's rule precisely, say what it was reacting against, and what its critics say it risks | Rahner's diagnosis that the treatise's isolation left believers practically monotheist; the rule that the economic Trinity is the immanent Trinity and the immanent the economic; what it wins (the missions are how God really is, not a mask) and what critics fear it risks (collapsing God's freedom and the distinction between God in himself and God for us); Rahner's own qualifications; Balthasar's Trinitarian reading of the cross, the self-giving of the Father as the ground of everything, and the kenosis question that ties back to 1.5; Barth's "modes of being" named as the Protestant parallel and its distance from Rahner |
| 6.4 | Social trinitarianism and its critics | Place social trinitarianism on the map and name the doctrinal constraints it has to satisfy | Moltmann's Trinity as a communion open to history; Zizioulas on person, communion and the priority of the Father as cause; Boff's political use; LaCugna's programme of a Trinity "for us"; *perichoresis* turned into a social and political model; Kilby's objection that the doctrine is filled with a projected social ideal and then presented as a revealed one; Holmes and Ayres on whether the reading is patristic at all; Marshall and Ormerod on what Lateran IV, Florence and simplicity (1.3, 3.4, 4.4) actually permit; the honest scorecard: what the movement recovered and where the constraints bite |

**Boss problem 6:** (a) Close-read ST I q.43 a.1 (English Dominican Province): what does mission add to procession, which words locate the newness in the creature rather than in God, and why does the article's logic entail that the Father cannot be sent? *(Exegetical.)* (b) For each of three cases — the Annunciation, Pentecost, and a person moved to a genuine act of charity in prayer — say which person is sent, whether the mission is visible or invisible, what is created and what is not, and where the answer stops being this course's business and becomes the doctrine of grace. *(Exegetical.)* (c) State Rahner's rule word for word in your own careful formulation, name the two things it was reacting against, and state the strongest worry his Catholic critics raise about the second half of it. *(Exegetical.)* (d) In 400-500 words, any verdict: does social trinitarianism, as Moltmann and Zizioulas develop it, stay inside what Lateran IV and Florence require and what simplicity (1.3) permits — and does Kilby's projection objection land against it? Use at least three constraints from Modules 1, 3 and 4, state the strongest form of the position you reject, and say what the movement gets right regardless of your verdict. *(Evaluative.)*

- **Accuracy discipline.** **No DH numbers appear in this syllabus and none should be added to a lesson without checking it against Denzinger-Hünermann directly.** Councils and constitutions are cited by name (Lateran IV's *Firmiter*; Florence's *Laetentur Caeli* and its decree for the Jacobites; *Dei Filius* ch. 1) and never by section number unless verified. The following are used above on strong but unverified recall and must each be checked against a printed text before the lesson that carries it is published: the exact wording and clause order of *Dei Filius* chapter 1; the wording of *Firmiter* and of Lateran IV's second constitution against Joachim, including the greater-dissimilarity sentence; Lyons II's "as from one principle, by a single spiration"; the Florentine sentence on the opposition of relation and its attribution to Anselm; Ephesus's prohibition of a rival creed (the canon number is deliberately omitted); the anathema of Constantinople II that carries "one of the Holy Trinity crucified in the flesh" (the tenth capitulum); the section of *Dominum et Vivificantem* that speaks of a divine "pain" (named without a number for that reason); the article divisions of ST I qq.27-43 cited in Module 4, and in particular that q.28 a.3 is the article on the real distinction of the relations and q.29 a.4 the article on person as relation; the 1995 clarification's precise treatment of *ekporeusis* and *processio*; and the CCC paragraph numbers 234, 246-248 and 253-255. The Athanasian Creed's date and provenance are given as a range with the attribution to Athanasius denied outright, which is uncontested. Where a lesson cannot verify a number, it names the document without one.
- **Authority discipline.** Three errors are graded strictly in every module. The first is demoting a defined truth — that God is altogether simple and unchangeable, that there are three persons in one divine nature, that the Spirit proceeds from the Father and the Son — to a school opinion. The second is promoting the Latin explanation of that truth — subsistent relations, procession by intellect and by will, the psychological analogy, the whole apparatus of notions and appropriations — to the status of dogma. The third is speaking loosely enough to land in modalism, subordinationism or tritheism without noticing, which is why the diagnose archetype appears in every module. Levels come from [`fundamental-theology`](../fundamental-theology/syllabus.md) 4.4, and where that syllabus and this one differ, that syllabus wins.
- **Fairness discipline.** The Greek doctrine of the Spirit's procession is stated as Greek theologians state it, and a problem that asks for it grades fidelity before anything else (Boss 5b). Moltmann, Zizioulas, Rahner, Balthasar and LaCugna are presented at the strength their best readers would recognize before any critique is offered. This course argues no case against the Orthodox: that is `apologetics-catholic-claims`, and Module 5's job is to make Jacob able to state both positions accurately enough that the later argument is worth having.
- **Where this goes next.** [`christology`](../christology/syllabus.md) takes 4.3-4.5 (the eternal generation, the properties of the Son) and 6.1 (the Son's visible mission) as its starting point, and 1.5's treatment of impassibility as the background to the communication of idioms. `creation-grace-last-things` takes 6.1's invisible missions as the doorway to the indwelling and to grace, and 1.2-1.4 for the God who creates. `apologetics-catholic-claims` takes Module 5 whole: the Latin case, the Greek case and the clarifications, already stated, so that its lessons can argue rather than explain. `sacraments-and-liturgy` takes 6.2. `ecclesiology-and-mariology` takes 4.5's rule on the undivided outward works when it treats the Spirit and the Church.
