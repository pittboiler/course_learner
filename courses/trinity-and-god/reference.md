# The Triune God · Reference Card

> Open book. This card is meant to be open while you work — including during
> quizzes. Nothing here is something you're expected to recall cold.

This course has one recurring question and one recurring trap. The question is
*what does the text actually say*; the trap is confusing a defined truth with the
Latin tradition's explanation of it, in either direction. So this card is built as
a lookup surface for both: the texts in the wording the lessons verified, and the
level of every claim that carries weight. Levels come from
[`fundamental-theology` 4.4](../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-04-the-ladder-of-doctrinal-authority.md),
which owns the ladder; where that course and this card differ, that course wins.

## What is defined, and where

The spine of the course. Each row is a claim, the act that fixes it, and its rung
on the ladder of [`fundamental-theology` 4.4](../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-04-the-ladder-of-doctrinal-authority.md).
Rung 1 is defined dogma, believed with theological faith; rung 3 is authentic
ordinary magisterium; rung 4 theologically certain or common teaching; rung 5
freely disputed among Catholics. **Councils and constitutions are named, never
numbered** — see [Conventions](#citing-the-tradition).

### Dogma and its explanation

The course's governing distinction, applied at every level judgement. That there
are three really distinct persons in one altogether simple divine nature is
defined. The Latin apparatus that holds the two together without contradiction —
**subsistent relations**, **procession by way of intellect and by way of will**,
**the psychological analogy**, **the notions and the appropriations**, **mission
as procession plus a new mode of presence** — is the tradition's *explanation*,
carried by the common doctor and proposed as such by no magisterial act.
Promoting the explanation and demoting the dogma are one error facing opposite
ways, and both are graded strictly.

*Used at:* [4.2](lessons/04-02-augustine-the-image-in-the-mind.md), [6.4](lessons/06-04-social-trinitarianism-and-its-critics.md)

### Dogma and its Latin explanation

The same distinction, drawn on the processions in particular. **Defined:** the Son
is begotten of the Father, the Spirit proceeds. **Below the seam:** that the
begetting is an act of intellect and the procession an act of will; that there are
exactly two because only understanding and willing remain wholly within an
intellectual nature. That is school theology of high standing and no conciliar
weight.

*Used at:* [4.3](lessons/04-03-the-two-processions.md)

### The Latin explanations, listed

Everything below is explanation, not definition. Keep the list; it is half the
course's exam surface.

- **Subsistent relation** as what a divine person *is* (ST I q.29 a.4).
- **Procession by way of intellect and by way of will**, and the argument that there are exactly two (ST I q.27).
- **The psychological analogy** — memory, understanding, will (Augustine, *De Trinitate* 8-15).
- **The notions, the notional acts and the appropriations** (ST I qq.32, 39).
- **Mission** as eternal procession plus a new way of being present in a creature (ST I q.43).
- **Timeless eternity** as the analysis of *aeternus* (Boethius, defended at ST I q.10 a.1) — rung 4, no higher.
- **Impassibility** as derived from immutability and simplicity — a [theological note](#theological-note), not a conciliar definition.
- **The Thomist and Scotist analyses of simplicity** — both affirm *Firmiter*; rung 5.
- **Rahner's rule**, **social trinitarianism**, **Balthasar's Ur-kenosis**, **Barth's modes of being** — rung 5; no act adopts or condemns any of them.
- **The de Regnon paradigm** — historiography, not doctrine, and contested history at that.

## The defining texts

One entry per text, in the wording the lessons verified. Where a Latin word
decides a reading it is printed; English marked *(lesson's translation)* is the
lesson's own work on a text with no public-domain English.

### The creed of 325 and its anathemas

The Church's first ecumenical definition. Four load-bearing elements: **"of the
essence of the Father"** (*ek tes ousias tou patros*) — from what the Father *is*,
not from what he decides; **"begotten, not made"**; **[*homoousios*](#homoousios)**;
and **the anathemas**, which are part of the definition and not an appendix. On
the Spirit it says only "And in the Holy Ghost".

Schaff's English is loose twice, and the lessons print their own translation
alongside: *en pote hote ouk en* has no word for time in it — **"there was when he
was not"**, not "there was a time when he was not"; and *prin gennethenai ouk en*
is **"before being begotten he was not"**, not "before he was made". The final
anathema condemns saying the Son is "of another *hypostasis* or *ousia*", using
the two words as a single pair — the fusion that costs the next two generations.

*Defined at:* [3.1](lessons/03-01-nicaea.md)

### The creed of 381

The creed said at Mass. Its third article, in Schaff's Greek and Percival's
English: the Holy Ghost, **the Lord** (*to kyrion*, neuter, so predicated of the
Spirit himself) and **Giver-of-Life**, **who proceedeth from the Father**
(*ek tou patros ekporeuomenon*, lifted from John 15:26), **who with the Father and
the Son together is worshipped and glorified** (*symproskynoumenon kai
syndoxazomenon* — the weight is on the *syn-*), **who spake by the prophets**. No
*ousia*, no *hypostasis*, no *homoousios*: see [economy of doctrinal language](#economy-of-doctrinal-language).

Source criticism, not scandal: the acts of 381 do not survive and its Tome is
lost; the creed's first secure attestation is in the acts of Chalcedon (451),
where it is read out as the creed of the 150 fathers. Its authority rests on
reception by the whole Church and on that solemn conciliar reception.

*Defined at:* [3.3](lessons/03-03-constantinople-i-and-the-divinity-of-the-spirit.md)

### The Athanasian Creed

The Latin *Quicumque*: fifth or sixth century, Western, most likely southern Gaul;
**not by Athanasius**, who wrote in Greek and died in 373; never received in the
East, with no authorized Greek text. Sung in the Western Office for a thousand
years, which is how a document with no council behind it came to work as a test of
orthodoxy. Its method is a drumbeat: *the Father is X, the Son is X, the Spirit is
X — and yet not three Xs but one X*, run through uncreated, unlimited
(*immensus*), eternal, almighty, God, Lord. Verses 19-20 state the rule as
obligations: we are **compelled** to confess each person *singulatim* God and
Lord, and **forbidden** to say three Gods or three Lords. Verses 21-23 give the
only permitted ground of difference — the Father from none, the Son from the
Father alone, the Spirit from Father and Son.

Level: not a conciliar definition but a received symbol of the Western Church,
quoted as such in the Catechism. What it professes is rung 1, by the councils and
the ordinary and universal magisterium, not by the creed's own act.

*Read at:* [3.4](lessons/03-04-the-latin-rules-of-speech.md)

### Neither confounding nor dividing

*Quicumque* v.4: *Neque confundentes personas: neque substantiam separantes* —
"Neither confounding the Persons: nor dividing the Substance [Essence]" (Schaff).
Two failures named in one line. **Confounding** runs the persons together and
lands in [modalism](#modalism); v.5's *alia... alia... alia* is the guard.
**Dividing** — the verb is *separare*, to pull apart — treats the divinity as a
stock the three draw on, and lands in [tritheism](#tritheism).

*Read at:* [3.4](lessons/03-04-the-latin-rules-of-speech.md)

### Firmiter

Lateran IV (1215), first constitution, named from its first word. Latin from the
conciliar text; English is the lesson's translation.

> *Firmiter credimus et simpliciter confitemur quod unus solus est verus deus aeternus et immensus omnipotens incommutabilis incomprehensibilis et ineffabilis, pater et filius et spiritus sanctus: tres quidem personae, sed una essentia, substantia seu natura simplex omnino.*
>
> We firmly believe and straightforwardly confess that there is one only true God, eternal and immense, almighty, unchangeable, incomprehensible and ineffable, Father and Son and Holy Spirit: three persons indeed, but one essence, substance or nature altogether simple. *(lesson's translation)*

Three words decide readings. *Quidem ... sed* — "indeed ... but" — concedes the
three and denies in the same breath that they multiply anything. *Seu*, "or",
flattens essence, substance and nature into one thing, so the persons cannot be
shelved under one word and the unity under another. *Omnino* is the definition of
simplicity: altogether, not "in a manner of speaking". The same paragraph calls
the three together *unum universorum principium*, one principle of all things.
Note also that *pater et filius et spiritus sanctus* stands in apposition to *unus
solus verus deus*: the one true God **is** Father and Son and Holy Spirit.

*Read at:* [1.2](lessons/01-02-the-one-true-god-and-what-reason-can-reach.md), [1.3](lessons/01-03-simplicity-as-doctrine.md), [1.4](lessons/01-04-immutability-eternity-immensity.md), [3.4](lessons/03-04-the-latin-rules-of-speech.md)

### The essence is not a fourth thing

Lateran IV's second constitution, against Joachim of Fiore's charge that Peter
Lombard had posited a quaternity. The council sides with Lombard: there is one
supreme reality (*summa res*) which truly **is** Father and Son and Spirit, the
three together and each singly, and therefore *in Deo solummodo Trinitas est, non
quaternitas* — in God there is only trinity, not quaternity. And that reality
neither begets nor is begotten nor proceeds: *illa res non est generans, neque
genita, nec procedens*.

Two rules follow. The essence is **not a fourth item** — not a shared stuff, not a
container, not a committee's charter; it is each person entire. And the essence is
**not a subject of the notional acts**: "the divine nature begets the Son" is a
forbidden sentence, because the *Father* begets. The same constitution argues that
the Father could not have given the Son part of his substance and kept part,
*utpote simplex omnino* — since it is altogether simple.

*Read at:* [3.4](lessons/03-04-the-latin-rules-of-speech.md), with the squeeze it creates at [1.3](lessons/01-03-simplicity-as-doctrine.md)

### The greater dissimilarity rule

Lateran IV, second constitution, closing sentence:

> *quia inter creatorem et creaturam non potest tanta similitudo notari, quin inter eos maior sit dissimilitudo notanda*
>
> because between creator and creature no likeness can be noted so great that a greater unlikeness is not to be noted between them *(lesson's translation)*

In words: every true thing you say about God on the strength of a creature is
outrun by what the comparison gets wrong. It is a **ranking of two true things**,
not scepticism — the council has just spent a page arguing about the divine
substance, and *utraque suo modo* shows the likeness is capped, not withdrawn. A
rule forbidding all likeness would forbid itself. What keeps it from paralysing
speech is [analogy](#analogy). Note where the council states it: inside a
Trinitarian argument.

*Read at:* [1.3](lessons/01-03-simplicity-as-doctrine.md), [3.4](lessons/03-04-the-latin-rules-of-speech.md)

### No opposition of relation

Florence, decree for the Jacobites (1442), in a subordinate clause that governs
the whole of Module 4:

> *...quia trium est una substantia, una essentia, una natura, una divinitas, una immensitas, una aeternitas, omniaque sunt unum ubi non obviat relationis oppositio.*
>
> ...because the three have one substance, one essence, one nature, one divinity, one immensity, one eternity, and everything is one where there is no opposition of relation. *(lesson's translation)*

Read it as an instruction. Take any predicate and ask whether it puts two terms in
**relative opposition**, requiring an opposite number as *father* requires *son*.
If not, it is one in God, said of each and never counted. If it does, you have a
distinction, exactly there and nowhere else. Licence and limit at once: it allows
three and forbids four.

**Attribution hedge.** The formula is regularly traced to Anselm's *De processione
Spiritus Sancti*, and the lesson could not reach that text; it verified only that
the phrase was a scholastic commonplace well before Florence (Ockham quotes it
verbatim in *Summa logicae* III-4). Treat the Anselmian attribution as the
tradition's, not as established.

*Read at:* [3.4](lessons/03-04-the-latin-rules-of-speech.md); applied at [4.4](lessons/04-04-four-relations-three-persons.md), [4.5](lessons/04-05-common-proper-appropriated.md), [5.1](lessons/05-01-the-latin-doctrine.md)

### Dei Filius chapter 1

Vatican I (1870), dogmatic constitution on the Catholic faith, ch. 1, "Of God, the
Creator of all Things". **One sentence, every clause load-bearing** (Schaff,
*Creeds of Christendom* vol. II — the same volume's plain-text copy at ccel.org
carries the Latin and English together):

> The holy Catholic Apostolic Roman Church believes and confesses that there is one true and living God, Creator and Lord of heaven and earth, almighty, eternal, immense, incomprehensible, infinite in intelligence, in will, and in all perfection, who, as being one, sole, absolutely simple and immutable spiritual substance, is to be declared as really and essentially distinct from the world, of supreme beatitude in and from himself, and ineffably exalted above all things which exist, or are conceivable, except himself.

Latin of the clause that carries the attributes: *omnipotentem, aeternum,
immensum, incomprehensibilem, intellectu ac voluntate omnique perfectione
infinitum; qui cum sit una singularis, simplex omnino et incommutabilis substantia
spiritualis, praedicandus est re et essentia a mundo distinctus.*

Three things the wording decides. It reads **"infinite in intelligence, in will,
and in all perfection"** — not "intellect and will". The simplicity and
immutability clause is grammatically the **ground** of "really and essentially
distinct from the world", so ch. 1 already defines both attributes before Lateran
IV is needed for them. And the sentence closes on **"ineffably exalted above all
things which exist, or are conceivable, except himself"** — no concept of ours
contains him.

Five claims of the chapter carry canons with anathemas: one true God, creator and
lord of things visible and invisible; that matter is not all there is; that God's
substance is not the world's; that finite things did not emanate from the divine
substance; and that the world was created from nothing, freely, for God's glory.

*Read at:* [1.2](lessons/01-02-the-one-true-god-and-what-reason-can-reach.md), [1.3](lessons/01-03-simplicity-as-doctrine.md), [1.4](lessons/01-04-immutability-eternity-immensity.md)

### One of the Trinity suffered in the flesh

The theopaschite formula. Constantinople II (553), **tenth capitulum**:
anathema on anyone who does not confess that the one crucified in the flesh is
true God, the Lord of glory, and one of the Holy Trinity. Read it for what it
says about God and nothing else. It asserts that **the subject on the cross is a
divine person**, and locates the suffering **in the flesh**. It asserts nothing
about a passible divine nature, and nothing about a divine nature untouched. The
rules that make such a sentence true — the communication of idioms — belong to
[`christology`](../christology/syllabus.md) 3.2 and are not taught here. Module 1
takes one thing from it: whatever impassibility means, it cannot mean that God is
at a safe distance from the cross.

*Read at:* [1.5](lessons/01-05-impassibility-and-the-god-who-suffers.md)

### The prohibition of another faith

Ephesus (431), **canon 7, from the council's sixth session** (Percival's
numbering): it is unlawful for anyone to bring forward, write or compose a
different (*hetera*) faith as a rival to that established by the fathers at
Nicaea. Pressed by Leo III against Charlemagne's ambassadors and by the Greeks at
Florence against the Latin addition. Fix what the objection is: **about adding to
the creed, not about the doctrine added**. Whether *hetera* means "contradictory"
or "any further word at all" is the live question.

*Read at:* [3.3](lessons/03-03-constantinople-i-and-the-divinity-of-the-spirit.md); taken up at [5.2](lessons/05-02-the-greek-doctrine-and-the-addition.md)

## The one God as the Church confesses him

### De Deo Uno et Trino

The manual treatise on God in two tracts — the one God and his attributes, then
the three persons. It is a post-Tridentine *packaging* of one continuous sequence:
*Summa* I runs essence (qq.2-26), persons (qq.27-43), creatures (qq.44-119)
without a break, and q.27's prologue calls what follows an order *of the
doctrine*, not a claim that the persons are later than the essence.

*Introduced:* [1.1](lessons/01-01-the-treatise-and-its-order.md)

### The order of knowledge and the order of being

Two available teaching orders that do not disagree about facts. The **order of
being** starts where the reality starts, with the Father who begets and breathes
forth; the persons come first and the attributes describe the life they share. The
**order of knowledge** starts where we start, with God as cause of a world we can
see — so the one God first, then what only revelation adds. Neither is dogma;
preferring one is not a doctrinal position. What the order of knowledge buys is a
fence against the field's worst error, arguing your way to the Trinity and calling
it a proof; what it risks is Rahner's complaint, that the God described in the
first tract looks like the God of the philosophers.

Both John of Damascus (*On the Orthodox Faith* I) and the *Summa* establish that
God is and that God is one before saying anything about three. The real difference
is where the **attributes** sit: after the Trinity for the Damascene, before it in
the Latin order.

*Introduced:* [1.1](lessons/01-01-the-treatise-and-its-order.md); Rahner's side of it at [6.3](lessons/06-03-rahners-rule-and-the-immanent-trinity.md)

### Theologia and oikonomia

The Fathers' distinction, restated at CCC 236. *Theologia* is God's own inner
life; *oikonomia* the works by which he reveals himself and communicates his life.
Through the economy the theology is revealed; the theology lights up the economy.

*Introduced:* [1.1](lessons/01-01-the-treatise-and-its-order.md); at work in [6.1](lessons/06-01-the-missions-of-the-son-and-the-spirit.md), [6.2](lessons/06-02-the-trinity-in-the-economy-liturgy-and-prayer.md), [6.3](lessons/06-03-rahners-rule-and-the-immanent-trinity.md)

### The de Regnon paradigm

The slogan drawn from Théodore de Régnon (*Études de théologie positive sur la
Sainte Trinité*, 1892-98), or from what textbooks made of him: Greek theology
begins from the persons and moves to the nature, Latin theology from the nature to
the persons. Michel René Barnes (1995) and Lewis Ayres (*Nicaea and Its Legacy*,
2004) took it apart as a nineteenth-century schema read back into the fourth
century; Augustine and the Cappadocians share far more method than it allows, and
Greek writers argue from the divine nature freely. **Historiography, not
doctrine**, and contested historiography — using it to settle a doctrinal question
smuggles in a premise nobody has argued for.

*Introduced:* [1.1](lessons/01-01-the-treatise-and-its-order.md)

### Mystery strictly so called

A revealed truth natural reason can neither reach on its own nor refute once it is
given. The Trinity is one. The argument (ST I q.32 a.1): by natural reason we know
God only from creatures, as effects show a cause; but creative power belongs to
the unity of the essence and not to the distinction of persons, so the evidence
reason has is the wrong kind. Consequence for apologetics: you may show the
doctrine is **not impossible**; you may not claim a demonstration.

*Introduced:* [1.2](lessons/01-02-the-one-true-god-and-what-reason-can-reach.md); used at [2.1](lessons/02-01-the-one-god-of-israel.md), [3.4](lessons/03-04-the-latin-rules-of-speech.md)

### Incomprehensibility

*Incomprehensible* does not mean *unknowable*. God is truly known; no created mind
ever takes his measure — grasps him in the way he is graspable — not even the
blessed seeing him. The beatific vision itself is ceded to
`creation-grace-last-things`.

*Introduced:* [1.2](lessons/01-02-the-one-true-god-and-what-reason-can-reach.md)

### Really and essentially distinct from the world

*Dei Filius* ch. 1's anti-pantheist clause, backed by canons against one shared
substance, against emanation from the divine substance, and against the divine
essence becoming all things by its own unfolding. With "of supreme beatitude in
and from himself" (*in se et ex se*) it also rules out a God completed, enriched or
fulfilled by creating. **Distinction, not distance**: the same tradition holds God
more intimately present to each thing than it is to itself. Read the clause as
spatial withdrawal and you have traded pantheism for deism.

*Introduced:* [1.2](lessons/01-02-the-one-true-god-and-what-reason-can-reach.md); the guardrail on omnipresence at [1.4](lessons/01-04-immutability-eternity-immensity.md)

### Altogether simple

A **negation**: there is nothing in God that is not God. No parts, no ingredients,
no subject underneath the attributes holding them the way a wall holds its paint.
Defined by *Firmiter* (*una essentia, substantia seu natura simplex omnino*) and
by *Dei Filius* ch. 1 (*simplex omnino et incommutabilis substantia spiritualis*)
— rung 1, twice over.

*Introduced:* [1.3](lessons/01-03-simplicity-as-doctrine.md)

### The attributes are not parts

The three denials *omnino* commits a Catholic to. (1) The attributes are **not
parts** — God is no assembly, however grand the pieces. (2) They are **not really
distinct** from the essence or from each other: "God is just" and "God is merciful"
name one reality twice, under two concepts we cannot merge. (3) They are **not
accidents**: God does not possess goodness, God is goodness. These are not further
doctrines stacked on the definition; they are what *omnino* excludes.

"Not really distinct" is not "merely verbal". On the Thomist account the
distinction has a foundation in the thing: God really is what each concept
imperfectly grasps.

*Introduced:* [1.3](lessons/01-03-simplicity-as-doctrine.md)

### The formal distinction

Scotus's Catholic alternative to the Thomist analysis. Where the Thomist treats
the difference between wisdom and goodness in God as a distinction of reason with
a foundation in the thing, Scotus holds them distinct **formalities on the side of
the thing**, prior to any act of our minds, in a reality nonetheless wholly
indivisible. Thomists answer that no such middle distinction exists; Scotists that
this assumes the two-way division it needs to prove. Both lie below the seam, both
**affirm *Firmiter***, and this is the cleanest level case in the course: calling
the formal distinction a denial of simplicity promotes a school's analysis to a
council's level, and calling simplicity "the Thomist view of God" demotes a
council's definition to a school's analysis. The metaphysics is
[`thomistic-synthesis` 3.4](../thomistic-synthesis/lessons/03-04-divine-simplicity-and-the-attributes.md);
Scotus's neighbouring thesis of univocity is
[`ancient-medieval-philosophy` 8.2](../ancient-medieval-philosophy/lessons/08-02-scotus-the-univocity-of-being.md).

*Introduced:* [1.3](lessons/01-03-simplicity-as-doctrine.md)

### The trinitarian squeeze

Why simplicity is the course's expensive doctrine. Two defined claims: one
essence, altogether simple, so no composition and therefore no parts to be three
of; and three persons, really distinct, the distinction not made by our minds. Two
exits, both condemned — drop the distinction and you have [modalism](#modalism),
divide the essence and you have [tritheism](#tritheism). What the doctrine supplies
is only the *form* of an answer: whatever distinguishes the persons must add no
composition to what it is in. The Latin candidate is **relation**, and it is
explanation, not dogma.

*Introduced:* [1.3](lessons/01-03-simplicity-as-doctrine.md); answered across [4.3](lessons/04-03-the-two-processions.md) and [4.4](lessons/04-04-four-relations-three-persons.md)

### Immutability

*Incommutabilis*, in *Firmiter* and *Dei Filius* ch. 1 — rung 1. "Change" here
means the passage from potency to act; a being with no unrealized potency has
nothing to acquire and nothing to lose. A claim about **fullness**, not coldness.
ST I q.9 a.1 gives three arguments (change requires potency and God is pure act;
change leaves something composite and God is simple; what is moved acquires what
it lacked, and infinite perfection has nothing to acquire) — the Thomist
explanation, not the definition.

What it does not rule out: that God knows, wills and loves creatures. Creating
changes the creature, not God; the relation is real on the creature's side and a
relation of reason on God's (ST I q.13 a.7,
[`thomistic-synthesis` 4.3](../thomistic-synthesis/lessons/04-03-the-names-of-god.md)).

*Introduced:* [1.4](lessons/01-04-immutability-eternity-immensity.md)

### Eternity

*Aeternus*, in both texts. The Latin tradition's account is Boethius's, quoted by
Aquinas at ST I q.10 a.1 and defended there in the English Dominican wording:
**"the simultaneously-whole and perfect possession of interminable life"**. Two
denials and a possession: *interminable* — no beginning, no end; *simultaneously
whole* — no succession; *possession of life* — not empty duration but a life had
entire. Boethius's own contrast is with **perpetuity**, endless duration lived
piecemeal.

**Level, stated plainly.** That God is eternal is rung 1. That *aeternus* means
timelessness in Boethius's sense rather than duration without beginning or end is
the Latin tradition's analysis, fixed by no magisterial act — **rung 4**, and no
higher. Which does not make the dispute cheap: anyone arguing for an everlasting
God still owes *incommutabilis*, which is defined.

*Introduced:* [1.4](lessons/01-04-immutability-eternity-immensity.md); Boethius in his own terms at [`ancient-medieval-philosophy` 5.3](../ancient-medieval-philosophy/lessons/05-03-time-eternity-and-foreknowledge.md)

### Immensity and omnipresence

*Immensus* is *in-mensus*, **un-measured** — not enormous. Nothing bounds God,
space included, so nothing that exists can hold him at a distance. Omnipresence
follows, not as a second attribute but as the same one seen from the creature's
side: God is in all things not as part of their essence nor as an accident but as
an agent present to what it acts on, and since being is innermost in each thing,
God is in all things innermostly (ST I q.8 a.1).

ST I q.8 a.3 sorts the presence into three modes, each answering a particular
denial: **by power**, inasmuch as all things are subject to him; **by presence**,
inasmuch as all things are bare and open to his eyes; **by essence**, inasmuch as
he is present to all as the cause of their being. "By essence" misfires in
English — the reply to the first objection settles it: God is in things by **his
own** essence, not by theirs. A further mode is named in the same article: God in
the rational creature that knows and loves him, and in the saints by grace, which
is not the common mode at all.

*Introduced:* [1.4](lessons/01-04-immutability-eternity-immensity.md)

### Impassibility

*Passio* is being acted on. Impassibility is first of all the **denial of a
passive capacity** — nothing in God can be acted on from outside — and not a claim
about God's emotional temperature. Three things it does not say: that God is
unaware of us; that God does nothing about our misery; and that God does not love,
rejoice or have mercy. The tradition affirms all three, **as acts of will** rather
than as states we produce in him (ST I q.20 a.1 on love and joy; q.21 a.3 on mercy
as attributed to God supremely, in its effect, not as an affection of passion).

The manuals **derive** it: God is pure act, so there is no passive potency in him;
to undergo a passion is to be moved from potency to act by another; therefore God
undergoes no passion. It is a conclusion, not a premise.

**Level.** No council defines a proposition under the name *impassibility*. What
the councils define is that God is altogether simple and unchangeable; what they
say about suffering they say of the incarnate Son and locate *in the flesh*
(Cyril's twelfth anathematism; [Constantinople II](#one-of-the-trinity-suffered-in-the-flesh)).
So impassibility carries a [theological note](#theological-note) rather than a
conciliar definition, and the manuals split: those who read it as immutability
restated give it the note of the defined truth it restates; those who read it as a
conclusion drawn from immutability give it the lower note of a theologically
certain conclusion. **The lesson could not reach Ott to fix which note his manual
prints — look it up in your own manual before citing one.** The stable part is the
distinction between the two readings, and the defensible statement is narrow:
*that God is unchangeable is defined; that he therefore cannot be acted upon is
the tradition's reading of what being unchangeable entails.*

*Introduced:* [1.5](lessons/01-05-impassibility-and-the-god-who-suffers.md)

### Theological note

A theologian's label for how securely a proposition follows from the sources
(*de fide*, theologically certain, common teaching, freely disputed) — a
**different instrument** from the rung a magisterial act fixes. Ott's
*Fundamentals of Catholic Dogma* is a report of how the manual tradition labelled
the attributes, not a magisterial source. The seam between notes and rungs is
[`fundamental-theology` 4.4](../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-04-the-ladder-of-doctrinal-authority.md)'s.

*Introduced:* [1.5](lessons/01-05-impassibility-and-the-god-who-suffers.md)

### The map of positions on divine suffering

Outline only; the worked verdict is Boss 1's.

| Position | Claim about God | What impassibility becomes | Constraint it must clear |
|---|---|---|---|
| The manual tradition | no passive potency; love, joy, mercy as acts of will | the fullness that receives nothing | reading scripture's grief without emptying it |
| Kitamori, *Theology of the Pain of God* (1946) | the pain of God, his love embracing the object of his wrath, is the gospel's centre | a category the gospel does not need | whether that pain is in God as God |
| Moltmann, *The Crucified God* (1972) | the cross is an event *between* Father and Son, determining God's own being | a Greek axiom the gospel did not bring | keeping the suffering **free**, not necessary |
| Jüngel, *God as the Mystery of the World* (1977) | God's being is his self-giving | too thin to carry that | keeping God distinct from the world's process |
| Process theology | God is affected by the world **by his nature**, being in time and dipolar | straightforwardly false | simplicity and immutability as defined |
| Balthasar, *Mysterium Paschale* | the cross read back into the Trinity | preserved, but re-described | not turning distinction of persons into conflict |
| *Dominum et Vivificantem* n. 39 (1986) | a fatherly "pain" glimpsed in the depths of God, in scare quotes | retained, and used as the fence | saying what the fence does |
| Weinandy, *Does God Suffer?* (2000) | a love that cannot be increased or diminished | the fullness of love, not indifference | showing that love so understood keeps content |

Moltmann and process theology agree on one question and part on another, and
collapsing them is the commonest error in the literature: **Moltmann's God suffers
freely**, choosing the cross out of love, where process theology's God is affected
because that is the kind of being he is. Stating Moltmann without "freely" is not
yet stating him.

*From* [1.5](lessons/01-05-impassibility-and-the-god-who-suffers.md)

## Scripture the doctrine rests on

The reading discipline first, then the ledger. Every row answers three questions,
and the third column is where honesty lives.

### The backward reading rule

> The Trinity is known from the New Testament and confessed by the Church. The Old Testament is read in that light — not mined for proofs.

The Church did not assemble the doctrine from clues in Genesis and check it
against the Gospels; she met the Father, the Son and the Spirit in the events the
New Testament reports, confessed what she had met, and then recognized the same God
in Israel's scriptures. The recognition is real. It is not the road anyone
travelled. The rule forbids **one move only**: presenting a backward reading as
evidence to a reader who does not yet grant the light in which it is read.

*Introduced:* [2.1](lessons/02-01-the-one-god-of-israel.md)

### Proof and confirmation

Aquinas's two uses of reason (ST I q.32 a.1 ad 2): reason that furnishes
**sufficient proof** of a principle, and reason that **confirms** a principle
already held by showing how its consequences fit. Trinitarian arguments from
scripture and from analogy do the second only. Swapping them has a cost outside
logic — in the body of the same article: one who brings forward reasons which are
not cogent "falls under the ridicule of the unbelievers: since they suppose that we
stand upon such reasons, and that we believe on such grounds." The unbeliever
concludes not that your argument is weak but that your faith is.

*Introduced:* [2.1](lessons/02-01-the-one-god-of-israel.md); governs [4.2](lessons/04-02-augustine-the-image-in-the-mind.md) and [6.2](lessons/06-02-the-trinity-in-the-economy-liturgy-and-prayer.md)

### Sensus plenior

The fuller sense a passage carries in the light of later revelation. Legitimate,
used constantly by the Church, and belonging to the order of **confirmation**, not
of proof. How far it and typology may go is owned by
[`old-testament`](../old-testament/syllabus.md); the Fathers' own rules of reading
are [`patristics` 3.5](../patristics/lessons/03-05-allegory-and-history-alexandria-and-antioch.md).

*Introduced:* [2.1](lessons/02-01-the-one-god-of-israel.md)

### What a text settles on its own

Module 2's discipline: for each passage ask what it establishes about the
**distinction** of the persons, what about their **divinity**, and what it leaves
**open** — and never confuse any of the three with what the Church confesses on
the whole testimony. No verse defines the faith; it is the council's act that fixes
the definition, not any verse's self-evidence. Claiming for your reading of a verse
the authority of a council's definition is the overstatement error.

*Introduced:* [2.2](lessons/02-02-the-father-and-the-son-in-the-new-testament.md)

### The split Shema

1 Corinthians 8:6. Paul takes the Shema's own two words — *God* and *Lord* — and
distributes them, one to the Father and one to Jesus Christ, keeping "one" in front
of each and giving each a creation formula (all things *of* the Father, all things
*by* the Lord Jesus Christ), one sentence after denying there is any other God.
Distinction and divinity in one breath, and the split runs **inside** the Shema,
not alongside it. What it leaves open: how the two are one God, whether "God" may
be said of Jesus, and the Spirit entirely.

*Introduced:* [2.2](lessons/02-02-the-father-and-the-son-in-the-new-testament.md)

### The Father is greater than I

John 14:28. The subordinationist reading stated fairly: the comparison is made by
the Son, about himself, unprompted, and "greater" compares *within* an order. The
Church's reading denies one premise — that "greater" must name a difference of
**nature** — on either of two genuinely different grounds:

1. **Of the incarnate Son.** He who speaks is the Son made man, and in the assumed human nature the Father is greater. The standard Latin reading.
2. **Of the order of origin.** Even as God the Son is *from* the Father; greater in nothing except in being the one from whom. That is the [monarchy of the Father](#monarchy-of-the-father), not a concession to subordinationism.

Neither reading claims the verse by itself teaches equality. It is compatible with
equality, and that is all a single verse was ever going to give.

*Introduced:* [2.2](lessons/02-02-the-father-and-the-son-in-the-new-testament.md)

### The created Wisdom of Proverbs 8

Proverbs 8:22. The Douay, following the Vulgate's *possedit*, reads "The Lord
possessed me in the beginning of his ways"; the Greek Fathers argued from the
Septuagint's *ektisen*, the ordinary verb for making a creature — the lesson's
translation, "The Lord **created** me a beginning of his ways." So the Arian
argument was not a misquotation. Three replies in ascending order of importance:
the Hebrew verb behind it is disputed (acquire, possess, beget, create) and its
literal sense is [`old-testament`](../old-testament/syllabus.md) 4.5's question;
where the Fathers read it of the Son they refer the creating to the **economy**;
and the method point that decides it — **the fourth century did not settle the
doctrine by settling Proverbs 8, it settled Proverbs 8 by settling the doctrine.**

*Introduced:* [2.2](lessons/02-02-the-father-and-the-son-in-the-new-testament.md)

### Paraclete

John's name for the Spirit in the farewell discourses (John 14-16): advocate,
comforter, **another** Paraclete, with the personal verbs that are the New
Testament's strongest evidence for his distinctness — he teaches, reminds, bears
witness, convicts the world, guides, hears, speaks, glorifies the Son. Handle the
famous grammatical datum carefully: John's masculine demonstrative *ekeinos* has
the masculine noun *parakletos* as its nearest antecedent, not the neuter *pneuma*,
so the masculine is at least partly what any Greek sentence would do. **The weight
sits on the verbs, not the pronoun.**

*Introduced:* [2.3](lessons/02-03-the-holy-spirit-in-the-new-testament.md)

### Triadic formulas

The passages naming Father, Son and Spirit together: Matthew 28:19 (one singular
*name*, with three in it), 2 Corinthians 13:13, 1 Corinthians 12:4-6, Ephesians
4:4-6. Evidence of **rank**, not a predication of deity — co-listing alone proves
little, as Basil had to concede, since Paul charges Timothy before God, Christ and
the elect angels (1 Timothy 5:21). They are strong because of what else the Spirit
is said to do.

*Introduced:* [2.3](lessons/02-03-the-holy-spirit-in-the-new-testament.md)

### The argument from worship

The fourth century's route to the Spirit's divinity, in Basil's form. The
**conclusion** is dogma; **this route to it** is theology.

1. The Spirit is named where God is named, in the rite that makes a Christian (Matthew 28:19).
2. Faith must conform to baptism, and worship to faith.
3. The Church in fact ascribes glory to the Spirit together with the Father and the Son. *(the disputed premise)*
4. No creature may be worshipped.
5. The Spirit performs works not delegated to creatures: searching God's depths, giving life, sanctifying, adopting, raising the dead.
6. Therefore the Spirit is not a creature, but is ranked with the Father and the Son as God.

The tell that the argument really ran this way: 381 did not extend *homoousios* to
the Spirit but confessed him adored and glorified with the Father and the Son —
**premise 3 canonized**, not premise 6 restated.

*Introduced:* [2.3](lessons/02-03-the-holy-spirit-in-the-new-testament.md); the conciliar outcome at [3.3](lessons/03-03-constantinople-i-and-the-divinity-of-the-spirit.md); Basil's own text at [`patristics` 3.2](../patristics/lessons/03-02-basil-the-great.md)

### The ledger

| Text | Distinction | Divinity | What it leaves open |
|---|---|---|---|
| John 1:1-3 | **strong** — "with God" requires two | **strong** — "was God"; what made everything made is not among the things made | how the Word relates to the man Jesus |
| 1 Corinthians 8:6 | **strong** — two named, each with "one" | **strong** — inside Israel's confession, not beside it | how the two are one God; whether "God" may be said of Jesus |
| John 10:30, 14:9, 17:5 | by the plural verb ("in that He said *are*, He delivers you from Sabellius" — Augustine, *Tractates* 36) | strong — glory held before creation | — |
| John 5:18-26 | **strong** — "the Son cannot do any thing of himself" | cuts both ways: has everything the Father has, and has it *from* him | how to read *cannot* and *hath given*; the raw material of eternal generation |
| Philippians 2:6-11; Hebrews 1:1-4 | strong | strong — something given up was held first; the homage Isaiah 45 reserves to the Lord | the Wisdom vocabulary it borrows brings Proverbs 8 with it |
| John 14:28; Mark 13:32 | strong | only with help: of the incarnate Son, or of the order of origin | whether "greater", and ignorance, bear on nature |
| the Jordan; Pentecost | suggestive at the Jordan, weak at Pentecost | nothing directly | whether "spirit poured out" is God's action given, or a giver |
| the Paraclete discourses | **strongest for the Spirit** — *another* Paraclete, with personal verbs | not asserted | whether a personal agent who is sent is thereby a divine person |
| Romans 8:9-27 | yes — God knows what the Spirit desires, so not simply one | strong indirectly: indwelling, life, adoption, raising the dead | whether an intercessor is thereby equal to the one asked |
| 1 Corinthians 2:10-11 | weak, and readable the wrong way | **strongest indirectly** — only God knows God from inside | whether the Spirit is God's self-knowledge rather than another |
| the triadic formulas | yes — three named and not merged | by rank, not by predication | whether being listed with God makes one God |
| Acts 5:3-4 | assumed | closest to an identification in the New Testament | whether the parallel identifies, or says that deceiving God's Spirit is deceiving God |

**The honest summary.** Nowhere in the New Testament is the Holy Spirit called God
in so many words. That is not a modern discovery; it is a fact the fourth century
worked with openly. The doctrine is defined; the New Testament supplies the
evidence from which the Church argued it. John 15:26's two verbs are deliberately
left to [Module 5](#ekporeusis-and-processio).

### Rule of faith

The short, public, tripartite summary of the faith a catechumen holds, and which
any reading of scripture must fit. Irenaeus's and Tertullian's are both tripartite
and both presented as **received, not composed**. The three are not a topic in the
faith; they are its skeleton.

*Introduced:* [2.4](lessons/02-04-from-confession-to-creed.md)

### Lex orandi, lex credendi

The law of prayer is the law of belief: what the Church does at the font and in
her doxologies is admissible evidence of what she holds, usable against a
theologian who says otherwise. The tag is condensed from a fifth-century formula
**usually attributed** to Prosper of Aquitaine's *Indiculus*; the lessons carry the
hedge and do not print the Latin.

*Introduced:* [2.4](lessons/02-04-from-confession-to-creed.md); extended from font and doxology to the collect, the creed and the sign of the cross at [6.2](lessons/06-02-the-trinity-in-the-economy-liturgy-and-prayer.md); the same instinct at [3.3](lessons/03-03-constantinople-i-and-the-divinity-of-the-spirit.md)

### Logos theology and its ambiguity

The second century's tool for keeping the Father's uniqueness as source, and the
hinge of the period. Theophilus of Antioch (*To Autolycus* II.10, 22, about 180)
says the Word always exists, residing within the heart of God as his own reason —
and then that when God willed to make what he had determined on, he **begot** this
Word, *uttered*, the firstborn of all creation. So the Son's distinct existence
hangs on the world's, and God without the world is not yet a Father. Theophilus
also supplies the first surviving *trias*, and his triad is "God, and His Word, and
His wisdom" (II.15) — **not** Father, Son and Holy Spirit. The vocabulary arrives
before its referents are fixed. Origen closes the ambiguity from the other side
with eternal generation.

Two corrections to carry: *una substantia, tres personae* is a **later
condensation** and is not a sentence of Tertullian's; and pre-Nicene
subordinationist idiom is not Arianism — Arius denies the Son is from the Father's
substance at all, where Theophilus and Tertullian assert he is and then rank him.

*Introduced:* [2.4](lessons/02-04-from-confession-to-creed.md)

## The vocabulary settlement

### Homoousios

Greek, "of one substance"; the non-scriptural word Nicaea used of the Son's
relation to the Father. **Whatever it is to be God, the Son has it, not a likeness
of it.** Latin *consubstantialis* / *unius substantiae*. Underdetermined in 325
between "same kind" — two men are *homoousioi* in that sense, which would license
two gods — and strict identity of one concrete being, which seems to leave nothing
for Father and Son to differ in. The council left it that way; the sense is
settled later.

*Introduced:* [3.1](lessons/03-01-nicaea.md)

### Ousia

Greek, being or substance. At Nicaea used interchangeably with *hypostasis*; from
the Cappadocians on, reserved for **what the three share** — the answer to *what
is it*.

*Introduced:* [3.1](lessons/03-01-nicaea.md); settled at [3.2](lessons/03-02-one-ousia-three-hypostaseis.md)

### Hypostasis

Greek, subsistence. At Nicaea paired with *ousia* as an equivalent, so the final
anathema condemns "another *hypostasis*" — the very word the next generation
needed for **the concrete distinct one**, the answer to *which one*. A bishop who
wanted to say *three hypostaseis* found his word under a Nicene anathema; one who
said *one hypostasis* could be heard denying the distinction. Fifty-six years of
contested formulas follow from a single fused word pair.

*Introduced:* [3.1](lessons/03-01-nicaea.md); settled at [3.2](lessons/03-02-one-ousia-three-hypostaseis.md)

### One ousia, three hypostaseis

The Cappadocian settlement: two words for two questions. *Ousia* answers **what is
it** and is common to the three; *hypostasis* answers **which one** and is the
concrete subsistent in which that nature stands. A divine person is the whole
Godhead together with what is proper to him. "Three" and "one" now answer
different questions, so they stop colliding. Basil's own argument is
[`patristics` 3.2](../patristics/lessons/03-02-basil-the-great.md); this course
takes the outcome.

**A change of vocabulary is not a change of doctrine — provided the new words say
what the old words meant.** That proviso is the whole burden of proof, and what a
critic of the development has to attack.

*Introduced:* [3.2](lessons/03-02-one-ousia-three-hypostaseis.md)

### Substantia, the translation trap

Latin *substantia* is the natural rendering of *ousia* **and** of *hypostasis* —
both say "what stands under". So the Greek formula translated word for word comes
out "one essence, three substances", which to a Latin ear says three beings:
tritheism. And *una substantia, tres personae* translated back gives a Greek "one
*hypostasis*", which is what Sabellius was condemned for — while *prosopon*, the
Greek match for *persona*, could mean a face or a part in a play, and so did not by
itself exclude him. Each side heard the other confess a heresy it had never held.
Augustine reports the impasse from inside it (*De Trinitate* V.8-9, Haddan): the
Greeks "intend to put a difference, I know not what, between *ousia* and
*hypostasis*", and "we do not dare to say one essence, three substances, but one
essence or substance and three persons."

*Introduced:* [3.2](lessons/03-02-one-ousia-three-hypostaseis.md)

### Tome to the Antiochenes

Athanasius's synodal letter from the council he held at Alexandria in 362 (NPNF
second series vol. 4, Ellershaw, who renders *hypostasis* as "Subsistence"). The
**procedure** is the point, and it is reusable: nobody is asked to drop a word.
Each party is asked what its word **denies** — do you mean subsistences alien in
essence from one another, or three Beginnings and three Gods? do you use "one
Subsistence" in the sense of Sabellius? — and once both have answered, each accepts
the other's confession. A local council, not a definition: an agreement about
language, reached by interrogating language. It never explains how three are one;
it clears the charges.

*Introduced:* [3.2](lessons/03-02-one-ousia-three-hypostaseis.md)

### Distinction only by origin

The persons are distinguished by **relations of origin alone** — unbegotten,
begotten, proceeding — and by nothing else: not by nature, not by power, not by
degree of divinity, not by any perfection, and not by the works they are associated
with. Gregory of Nazianzus insists the Spirit lacks nothing, since God has no
deficiency, and that it is the bare fact of being unbegotten, begotten or
proceeding that gives the three their names (*Oration* 31.9). CCC 254-255 states
the same rule. **If you can say what makes one person not another without
mentioning where he is from, you have said something the Church's texts do not
allow.** What a relation of origin *is*, metaphysically, is the Latin explanation
of [4.4](lessons/04-04-four-relations-three-persons.md) and is not itself defined.

*Introduced:* [3.2](lessons/03-02-one-ousia-three-hypostaseis.md); in Latin dress at *Quicumque* vv.21-23, [3.4](lessons/03-04-the-latin-rules-of-speech.md)

### Monarchy of the Father

**The Greek grammar of unity, stated as Greek theologians state it.** Why is there
one God and not three? The answer does not begin from the shared essence. It begins
from the Father, who alone is without principle and who is the single *arche* — in
the later vocabulary the sole *aitia*, cause — from whom the Son is begotten and
the Spirit proceeds. Unity is secured at its **origin**: the three are one not
because they are alike, but because two of them are wholly from one, having
everything except being him.

Is that subordination? The Greek answer: "from another" is an origin claim and
nothing more, and what the Son receives is the whole, identical divinity, so the
receiving cannot make it less. Gregory of Nazianzus denies that the monarchy is
limited to one person in the sense that would make the others lesser (*Oration*
29.2) and grants that the Father is greater **in respect of being the Cause** while
refusing the inference that he is greater by nature (*Oration* 29.15).

Where the traditions differ in emphasis: a Latin theologian starting from the one
essence hears any priority of the Father as a risk, and a Greek hears the Latin
start as a risk of making the essence a fourth thing behind the three. Neither is
a heresy.

*Introduced:* [3.2](lessons/03-02-one-ousia-three-hypostaseis.md); taken up as doctrine at [5.2](lessons/05-02-the-greek-doctrine-and-the-addition.md); behind Zizioulas at [6.4](lessons/06-04-social-trinitarianism-and-its-critics.md)

### Pneumatomachi

The "Spirit-fighters", also called Macedonians after Macedonius of Constantinople,
drawn out of the Semi-Arian middle. **State their case at its strength:** they
granted the Son's uncreated divinity (some while still disliking *homoousios* as a
formula) and then refused the same step for the Spirit, holding him outside the one
indivisible Godhead — the highest of the things made, a minister, not a third who
is adored. Their evidence: scripture calls the Son God outright and nowhere calls
the Spirit God; the verbs used of the Spirit are verbs of being handled; and the
older doxology, glory to the Father *through* the Son *in* the Holy Spirit, looked
on its face like a ranking. Condemned by name in canon 1 of Constantinople I.

*Introduced:* [3.3](lessons/03-03-constantinople-i-and-the-divinity-of-the-spirit.md)

### The doxological argument

Basil's route, and the idiom the creed of 381 defines in: the baptismal command,
faith conforming to baptism and glory to faith, so the Spirit is numbered **with**
and glorified **with** the Father and the Son. Against a party formed by rejecting
a word, define by an act. See [the argument from worship](#the-argument-from-worship)
for the full skeleton.

*Introduced:* [3.3](lessons/03-03-constantinople-i-and-the-divinity-of-the-spirit.md)

### Economy of doctrinal language

Defining what must be held in the **least contested words that will do the job**.
381 praises the Spirit in the language of worship rather than applying *homoousios*
to him — and three things show this is prudence, not reservation. It defines in the
idiom of the argument that had actually won. It is a **baptismal** text said by
everybody, not a treatise, and requiring a technical term would make the Church's
public profession turn on a word most of the baptized could not parse. And the same
bishops said the plain thing in the register where plainness belongs: writing to
Damasus in 382, the synod summarized "one Godhead, Power and Substance of the
Father and of the Son and of the Holy Ghost; the dignity being equal, and the
majesty being equal in three perfect hypostases, i.e. three perfect persons."

Harnack pressed the cost honestly: a Pneumatomachian could have subscribed the
clause at a pinch. The replies, of different weights: canon 1 anathematizes them by
name; the letter of 382 shows the same bishops holding one substance in three
hypostases; and whether a text could be signed in bad faith is a different question
from what the text says — *homoousios* itself was subscribed in 325 by men who
meant something else.

*Introduced:* [3.3](lessons/03-03-constantinople-i-and-the-divinity-of-the-spirit.md)

## The Latin analysis

Two processions, four real relations, three persons, the notions, the
appropriations, the missions — as numbered skeletons. **All of it is
[explanation](#dogma-and-its-explanation), not definition.** Augustine's logic
first, then Aquinas's system.

### The Arian dilemma

The objection Augustine calls the opponents' "most crafty device", set out
(*De Trinitate* V):

1. Whatever is said of anything is said either substantially or accidentally.
2. Nothing is said of God accidentally, since nothing in God can change.
3. So whatever is said of God is said substantially. *(1, 2)*
4. "Unbegotten" is said of the Father and "begotten" of the Son.
5. Unbegotten and begotten are different.
6. So the Father's substance and the Son's are different — the Son is not *homoousios*. *(3, 4, 5)*

The argument is valid and premise 2 is the Church's own teaching, so everything
depends on **premise 1**. Augustine's first reply is a trap rather than a
refutation: is "I and the Father are one" said substantially? If yes, the
conclusion fails; if no, premise 1 is gone.

*From* [4.1](lessons/04-01-augustine-relation-substance-and-predication.md)

### Relation as a third way of predicating

Augustine's answer: the two boxes were never exhaustive. Some things are said
**in respect of oneself** (*ad se*) and some **toward another** (*ad aliquid*).
In creatures relative terms are accidents because a man can stop being a father —
a fact about creatures, not about relation. Take a case where the relation cannot
be lost and you have something that is neither accident, since it cannot change,
nor substance, since it does not say what the thing is. *De Trinitate* V.5,
Haddan:

> But in God nothing is said to be according to accident, because in Him nothing is changeable; and yet everything that is said, is not said, according to substance. ... Wherefore, although to be the Father and to be the Son is different, yet their substance is not different; because they are so called, not according to substance, but according to relation, which relation, however, is not accident, because it is not changeable.

"Because it is not changeable" is the argument, not decoration: simplicity and
immutability, which created the problem, are made to solve it.

*From* [4.1](lessons/04-01-augustine-relation-substance-and-predication.md)

### The rule of singular and plural

What denying premise 1 buys (*De Trinitate* V.8). Whatever is said of God **in
respect of himself** is said of each person separately *and* of the Trinity in the
singular: the Father is God, the Son is God, the Spirit is God — and not three
Gods but one God; great, good, almighty, eternal run the same way, three bearers
and never three greatnesses. Whatever is said **toward another** is said of one
person toward another, is plural, and never applies to the Trinity as a whole:
the Trinity is not its own Father. The doctrine therefore needs no arithmetic —
the three are not three of anything counted in the first column.

*From* [4.1](lessons/04-01-augustine-relation-substance-and-predication.md)

### Negation keeps the category

The Arian comeback: "unbegotten" is not relative, since you could call a being
unbegotten who had begotten nobody — so it is said of the Father *ad se*, hence
substantially. Augustine's general rule: **prefixing a negative denies in the same
category in which the positive affirmed.** "He is not a man" denies about
substance; "he is not near" denies about relation. "Begotten" just means *son*,
and *son* is relative, so "unbegotten" means *not from a begetter* — a relative
denial, reporting not what the Father is but what he is not from. A denial in the
relative column cannot yield a substantial difference.

*From* [4.1](lessons/04-01-augustine-relation-substance-and-predication.md)

### Person as a word of last resort

In Book 7 Augustine runs his own rule on his own vocabulary and it comes out
wrong: we never call the Father the person *of* the Son, so "person" is said *ad
se*, and the rule then demands it be said of the Trinity in the singular. By his
own logic the answer is *one* person. He does not patch the rule; he reports the
breakdown (Haddan, *De Trinitate* V.9):

> The answer, however, is given, three persons, not that it might be [completely] spoken, but that it might not be left [wholly] unspoken.

The bracketed adverbs are the translator's; the Latin is *Dictum est tamen tres
personae non ut illud diceretur sed ne taceretur*. **Quote the translation, not
the familiar paraphrase** — the widely circulated "not in order to say that, but
in order not to be silent" conflates this sentence with a separate one in Book 7
ch. 4. The sentence is usually quoted to license vagueness; it licenses the
opposite, since Augustine names exactly which job the word fails at and why he
keeps it. **The distinction is carried by the relations, not by the noun.**

*From* [4.1](lessons/04-01-augustine-relation-substance-and-predication.md)

### Inseparable operation

One substance, therefore one work: Father, Son and Holy Spirit, "as they are
indivisible, so work indivisibly" (*De Trinitate* I.4.7). One nature, one power,
one operation — no outward work belongs to one person to the exclusion of the
others. Augustine's very next chapter asks how, working indivisibly, they yet do
some things severally. Taken up as [the undivided outward works](#the-undivided-outward-works).

*From* [4.1](lessons/04-01-augustine-relation-substance-and-predication.md)

### The psychological analogy

Augustine's image of the Trinity in the mind — memory, understanding and will: one
substance, three distinguished only by mutual reference, each whole in each. What
it asserts, stated so that the limits are part of the assertion:

1. **There is an image of the Trinity in the rational creature's mind** — something created is genuinely like the Triune God, because God made it so.
2. **The likeness is of structure, not of content:** one substance; three really distinct by mutual reference alone; each whole in each.
3. **The image is read in the light of a faith already held, never used to establish it** ([proof and confirmation](#proof-and-confirmation)).
4. **The image is obscure, and the obscurity is scriptural** — we see through a glass, in an enigma; Book 15 insists on the preposition.
5. **The image is realized, not merely possessed:** it images God when turned to God (Book 14).

*De Trinitate* X.11.18 fixes the claim: the three "are not three lives, but one
life; nor three minds, but one mind ... But they are three, in that wherein they
are mutually referred to each other." It does **not** say the mind's powers are
persons, or that God's persons are powers.

**Level.** Proposed by nobody. No council defines it and no papal act proposes it
as revealed or as definitively held: below the seam, freely disputed in its
details, carried for sixteen centuries because it works.

*From* [4.2](lessons/04-02-augustine-the-image-in-the-mind.md)

### The road inward in De Trinitate

The search runs inward in stages, each fixing a defect in the last.

| Book | The triad | The defect it fixes or leaves |
|---|---|---|
| 8 | lover, beloved, love (VIII.10.14) | the two can be two people — a trace, not an image |
| 9 | the mind, its knowledge of itself, its love of itself (IX.4.4-5) | one substance, named relatively — but it depends on the mind turning to look at itself |
| 10 | memory, understanding, will | there all at once, each containing the other two whole; nothing prior, nothing waits |
| 14 | the same three, turned toward God | the sharpest turn: the image is not a structure you possess but a **capacity** that can be turned toward its original or away (XIV.12.15) |

*From* [4.2](lessons/04-02-augustine-the-image-in-the-mind.md)

### Book 15's three breaks

Book 15 is not a footnote of modesty. Augustine turns the analogy around and
measures it against what it images.

1. **XV.22.42** — "it is I that remember, I that understand, I that love, who am neither memory, nor understanding, nor love, but who have them." A man **has** three powers and **is** one person; God does not *have* three persons. The analogy fails at the point it was built to illuminate.
2. **XV.7.12** — in us the powers are the only instruments of their own acts. Push that across and the Father would understand only by the Son, each person being the others' faculty — which Book 7 had already destroyed.
3. **XV.23.43** — in us the three are mutually unequal and changeable. In God they are equal and unchangeable, and Augustine's conclusion is the reverse of deflating: **God's Trinity of three persons is more indivisible than this image of one.**

*From* [4.2](lessons/04-02-augustine-the-image-in-the-mind.md)

### Verbum cordis

The inner word of the heart: when you know something and say it silently, a word
is born of your knowledge, belonging to no language. Three marks — it is not a
piece of the mind broken off but **the whole knowledge formed**; it is **of the
same nature** as the mind that begets it; and it is **not later** than the knowing.
The payoff: scripture calls the Son *Word*, and this gives the name a content —
generation conceived as an intelligible utterance that stays inside the utterer,
which is why it is neither creation nor change. Augustine marks off the second
procession by the same means: love proceeds from the mind's knowledge and is
common to parent and offspring, so it is neither parent nor offspring — which is
why the Spirit proceeds and is not begotten.

*From* [4.2](lessons/04-02-augustine-the-image-in-the-mind.md); redeployed at [4.3](lessons/04-03-the-two-processions.md)

### Procession in God

**An action that remains in the agent instead of passing outward to an effect**
(ST I q.27 a.1). Two readings were tried and both fail the same way. **Arius**
read procession as an effect coming from a cause, so the Son is the Father's first
creature; **Sabellius** read it as a cause reaching into its effect, so the one God
is called *Son* in taking flesh. One diagnosis covers both: each takes procession
as an act going **outward**, so neither puts procession in God at all — one puts it
in the creature produced, the other in the creature acted on. What both overlooked
is the act that stays in the agent, of which understanding is the clearest case:
whenever you understand, a conception of the thing understood comes to be in you.
It proceeded, and it stayed. And the more perfectly something proceeds within, the
more closely it is one with its source — which removes the picture of procession
as distance from the source.

*From* [4.3](lessons/04-03-the-two-processions.md); reused at [6.1](lessons/06-01-the-missions-of-the-son-and-the-spirit.md)

### Generation by way of intellect

ST I q.27 a.2 splits the word. "Generation" in its commonest sense — change from
not-being to being — is exactly what cannot happen in God. The other sense belongs
to living things: **the origin of a living being from a conjoined living principle,
by way of likeness, in the same specific nature.** A hair grows from a living thing
and is not begotten; a worm bred from an animal has only a generic likeness.

Run the marks on the procession of the Word: a vital operation; from a conjoined
principle; by way of likeness, since a concept is a likeness of what is conceived;
and in the same nature, because in God understanding and existing are the same.
Four out of four. Hence the proceeding Word is called Son. **The fourth mark does
the work, and our own case fails it** — your inner word is not of the same nature
as you, since your intellect is not your substance. The analogy has to be
corrected, not applied flat.

*From* [4.3](lessons/04-03-the-two-processions.md)

### The procession of love

ST I q.27 a.4, the article that decides why the two processions genuinely differ:
the intellect is made actual **by a likeness** of the object residing in it,
whereas the will is made actual **by an inclination** toward the thing willed. So
the procession of the intellect is by way of similitude and is called generation,
"because every generator begets its own like"; the procession of the will is by
way of impulse and movement toward an object, and what proceeds by way of love
"does not proceed as begotten, or as son, but proceeds rather as spirit."

The article nowhere makes the Spirit later or lower; it argues only that *begotten*
is the wrong word, and the *sed contra* has already fixed the right one by quoting
Athanasius — not made, nor begotten, but proceeding. Since in creatures generation
is the only way a nature is communicated, our language had **no name** for this
procession at all; Aquinas proposes **spiration**.

*From* [4.3](lessons/04-03-the-two-processions.md)

### Why there are exactly two processions

ST I q.27 aa.3 and 5 together:

1. A procession in God must follow on an action that **remains in the agent**, since an action passing outward produces a creature, not a divine person.
2. In an intellectual nature the actions that remain in the agent are **two**: understanding and willing.
3. Sensation looks like a third and is not — it belongs to no purely intellectual nature, and it is completed by the sensible object acting on the sense.
4. Power and goodness supply no third: power is the principle by which one thing acts on *another*, so it points outward to creatures; goodness belongs to the essence, not to an operation.

∴ The only processions possible in God are the procession of the Word and the
procession of Love. *In words:* count the ways a mind can act without reaching
outside itself. There are two. So there are two.

Nor does the count run away upward: in God there is one act of understanding and
one of willing, so one perfect Word and one perfect Love, and love terminates the
series rather than starting a new one.

**The hardest objection** (a.3 obj. 3) is that in God the will *is* the intellect,
so if the processions are distinguished by them the processions are not two either.
Aquinas concedes the premise and denies the inference: the distinction is taken not
from two faculties but from the **order** of one procession to the other, since
love proceeds only from what the intellect has conceived. Feel the strain honestly
— the reply shows only that the analysis is not self-defeating, not that the
processions ground a real distinction of persons. That is [relative
opposition](#relative-opposition)'s job.

*From* [4.3](lessons/04-03-the-two-processions.md)

### Real relation in God

ST I q.28 a.1. A regard to another is **of reason only** when it is found merely in
our comparing minds; it is **real** when what proceeds and its source agree in the
same order. "But when something proceeds from a principle of the same nature, then
both the one proceeding and the source of procession, agree in the same order; and
then they have real relations to each other."

The hinge is **identity of the same nature**. God's relation to creatures is of
reason in God, because the creature proceeds in a *different* nature; the divine
processions stay inside one nature, and that is what makes their relations real.
The *sed contra* names the stake: if there were no real paternity and filiation in
God, God would be Father and Son only in our manner of understanding, and that is
the Sabellian heresy. Contrast the relations of reason of ST I q.13 a.7
([`thomistic-synthesis` 4.3](../thomistic-synthesis/lessons/04-03-the-names-of-god.md))
— the single likeliest error in this course is carrying those into the Trinity.

Corollary (q.28 a.2): **each relation is the divine essence.** In a creature a
relation is an accident inhering in a subject; in God there are no accidents, so a
relation really in God has the existence of the divine essence and differs from it
only in our mode of understanding. Paternity is not something the Father *has*. It
is God.

*From* [4.4](lessons/04-04-four-relations-three-persons.md)

### Relative opposition

ST I q.28 a.3. The idea of relation means a regard of one to another **as
relatively opposed**; a real relation therefore carries a real opposition, and
opposition of itself includes distinction. So there is real distinction in God —
**not** on the side of the absolute, the essence, "wherein there is supreme unity
and simplicity", but on the side of the relative. Facing-from and facing-toward
cannot be the same term, and that is the only distinction God will admit. This is
the metaphysical form of Florence's [no opposition of relation](#no-opposition-of-relation),
and it is the constraint that bites hardest on modern proposals: intellect, will,
consciousness and operation carry no relative opposition, so they are one in God.

*From* [4.4](lessons/04-04-four-relations-three-persons.md); used at [3.4](lessons/03-04-the-latin-rules-of-speech.md), [5.1](lessons/05-01-the-latin-doctrine.md), [6.4](lessons/06-04-social-trinitarianism-and-its-critics.md)

### The four real relations

ST I q.28 a.4. Relations rest either on quantity or on action; God has no quantity,
and his relations to creatures are not real in him, so only **immanent action** is
left. Two immanent operations, therefore two processions, and each procession
yields two opposed relations — one on the side of the principle, one on the side of
what proceeds.

| Procession | On the side of the principle | On the side of what proceeds |
|---|---|---|
| generation of the Word | **paternity** | **filiation** |
| the procession of love | **spiration** | **procession** |

Four, because two processions have two ends each. Aquinas notes the second pair has
no better names, because the procession of love has none.

*From* [4.4](lessons/04-04-four-relations-three-persons.md)

### Subsistent relation

ST I q.29 a.4, and the step no magisterial act has ever taken. Boethius's
definition of person — an individual substance of a rational nature — belongs to
philosophy and is owned by
[`metaphysics` 6.5](../metaphysics/lessons/06-05-what-a-person-is.md). Aquinas
keeps it and then asks what "person" signifies **in God**. "Person" in any nature
signifies what is distinct in that nature: in a man, this flesh and these bones. In
God distinction is only by relation of origin, and relation in God is not an
accident but the essence itself, so it **subsists**. Therefore a divine person
signifies **relation as subsisting** — relation signified by way of substance, a
hypostasis subsisting in the divine nature. Person names relation directly, essence
indirectly. The definition is stretched, not dropped: "individual" has to mean
incommunicable subsistence rather than a thing marked off by matter (a.3 ad 4).

**The relation does not replace the someone**; it is what the someone is
distinguished by. "The Father is a relationship God is in" is modalism wearing
scholastic clothes.

*From* [4.4](lessons/04-04-four-relations-three-persons.md); pressed at [6.4](lessons/06-04-social-trinitarianism-and-its-critics.md)

### Common spiration

ST I q.30 a.2. The persons are the subsisting relations that are really distinct,
and real distinction comes only from relative opposition. Paternity and filiation
are opposed: two persons. Spiration and procession are opposed to each other, but
spiration is opposed to **neither** paternity nor filiation — so it cannot
constitute a third person and belongs to the Father and the Son together. What
remains, procession, is the person of the Holy Spirit. Spiration is a real relation
but **not a personal property**, since a property belongs to one person alone.
*The count of persons is a count of oppositions, not a count of relations.*

*From* [4.4](lessons/04-04-four-relations-three-persons.md)

### Other but not another thing

The rules of speech of ST I q.31. Aquinas steers between two ditches he names:
Arius, who put a trinity of substance alongside the trinity of persons, and
Sabellius, who put unity of person alongside unity of essence.

- **Against Arius**, banned: "diversity", "difference", "separation", "division", "alien", "discrepant". Say **distinction**.
- **Against Sabellius**, banned: "singularity", "solitary", and "only" used in a way that cancels the number of persons.
- **The licit one:** masculine *alius*, "other", marks a distinct *suppositum*, so "the Son is other than the Father" is true; neuter *aliud*, "another thing", would mark a distinct essence, so "the Son is something else than the Father" is false. Aquinas's summary: the Father is other than the Son, but not something else; they are one thing, but not one person.
- Q.31 a.1: "Trinity" signifies the determinate number of persons — which is why "the Trinity is trine" is forbidden, since it would multiply the number by itself.

*From* [4.4](lessons/04-04-four-relations-three-persons.md)

### Common, proper and appropriated

Three boxes, and every predicate of God goes in exactly one.

| Box | Tradition's word | Test | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Common** | essential | belongs to the one nature, so true of each person and of the three together, each person being the whole of it | wise, almighty, eternal, merciful, creator |
| **Proper** | personal / notional | a relation of origin, or something following immediately on one; true of one person and impossible of the others | the Father begets; the Son is begotten; the Spirit proceeds |
| **Appropriated** | — | a **common** attribute assigned to one person because it resembles what is his alone | power to the Father, wisdom to the Son, goodness to the Spirit |

The sorting rule is one line, and it is Florence's: in God nothing distinguishes
but relative opposition, so **whatever is not a relation of origin is common,
without exception.** A predicate fastened to one person that is not a relation of
origin is being fastened for some other reason, and appropriation is the name of
that reason.

*From* [4.5](lessons/04-05-common-proper-appropriated.md)

### The proper names of the persons

Each defended by Aquinas as proper and not merely appropriated.

| Person | Proper names | Article |
|---|---|---|
| Father | principle without principle; unbegotten (*innascibilitas*) | q.33 a.1; q.33 a.4 |
| Son | Word; Image | q.34 a.2; q.35 a.2 |
| Spirit | Love; Gift | q.37 a.1; q.38 a.2 |

**The trap in the table:** "Love" and "Word" are also said of God *essentially* —
God is love, God knows himself — and said that way they are common to the three.
Each article asks whether the name can *also* be said personally, and answers yes.
**The same word lands in two different boxes depending on how it is said.**

*From* [4.5](lessons/04-05-common-proper-appropriated.md)

### Appropriation

ST I q.39 a.7: appropriation is a device of **manifestation**, not of attribution —
its job is to make persons we cannot demonstrate visible through attributes we can
reason about. It works in **two directions**: by likeness (intellect to the Son,
who proceeds as Word) and by **unlikeness** (power to the Father, precisely because
human fathers grow feeble and the image needs correcting). Q.39 a.8 sorts three
traditional schemes, Hilary's and Augustine's among them; the power / wisdom /
goodness triad is one of them, and it is a.8's, not a.7's.

**The safety catch, q.39 a.7 ad 1: never exclusive.** "The essential attributes are
not appropriated to the persons as if they exclusively belonged to them; but in
order to make the persons manifest by way of similitude, or dissimilitude."
Whatever is appropriated to one remains wholly true of the other two.

A sentence worth memorizing (q.39 a.7 ad 2, citing Augustine): **the Father is not
wise by the wisdom he begets.** If he were, one person would stand to another as
form to subject, and the Father would be wise by something received. Each is of
himself wisdom, and both together are one wisdom.

*From* [4.5](lessons/04-05-common-proper-appropriated.md); at work in the liturgy at [6.2](lessons/06-02-the-trinity-in-the-economy-liturgy-and-prayer.md)

### The undivided outward works

*Opera Trinitatis ad extra indivisa sunt.* The argument, in seven steps:

1. Real distinction in God comes only from relative opposition (Florence).
2. Therefore **every perfection of the nature is common** — if being wise or almighty distinguished a person, it would be a fourth kind of distinction, and there is none.
3. Therefore **every work toward creatures is common too**: one nature, one power, one operation, so the three are not three principles of the creature but one principle (Florence, decree for the Jacobites; CCC 258; Augustine, *De Trinitate* I.4.7).
4. **But** scripture and the creed do parcel out the works — creator of all things; through whom all things were made; Lord and giver of life.
5. So the parcelling is of another kind: [appropriation](#appropriation). The creed teaches you to recognize the persons; it does not divide the labour.
6. And it is not arbitrary: the appropriated attribute is chosen by its likeness to what is proper.
7. Nor is the undivided work undifferentiated. **Each person performs the one common work according to his own personal property** (CCC 258; ST I q.45 a.6): the Father makes the creature through his Word and through his Love, so the order of the processions is really present in the work without splitting it — and even the power of creating is one but possessed in order, the Son having it from the Father and the Spirit from both (q.45 a.6 ad 2).

**Where ST states and applies it:** q.45 a.6, whose second objection raises the
creed's own three clauses. Its patristic ancestor is Gregory of Nyssa's argument
from the single divine operation in *To Ablabius*
([`patristics` 3.3](../patristics/lessons/03-03-the-two-gregories.md)) — the
argument is fourth-century, the axiom's formula is not.

**The one genuine qualification:** only the Son became man. The whole Trinity
effects the Incarnation and the union is the Son's alone, so a mission is not just
an appropriation.

*From* [4.5](lessons/04-05-common-proper-appropriated.md); the qualification at [6.1](lessons/06-01-the-missions-of-the-son-and-the-spirit.md)

### Mutual indwelling

ST I q.42 a.5: each person is in the others, argued three ways — by essence, by
relation, and by origin, the last being cleanest (the word conceived does not leave
the mind that conceives it). Greek *perichoresis*, Latin *circumincessio*. One
operation is not a coincidence of three wills; it follows on three who are never
outside one another. Augustine's creaturely likeness of it is *De Trinitate*
X.11.18's "not only is each contained by each, but also all by each".

*From* [4.5](lessons/04-05-common-proper-appropriated.md); what social trinitarianism does with it at [6.4](lessons/06-04-social-trinitarianism-and-its-critics.md)

## The missions and the economy

### Mission

**A mission is the eternal procession, plus a new way of a divine person's being
present in a creature** (ST I q.43 a.2). The sum is lopsided: the first addend is
eternal and contributes no newness at all; the second sits entirely on the
creature's side. **Nothing new in God, everything new in the creature.** Aquinas's
own words: the procession "may be called a twin procession, eternal and temporal,
not that there is a double relation to the principle, but a double term, temporal
and eternal." *Term* is what is doubled, not the relation of origin — the Son
acquires no second dependence on the Father in time.

The skeleton:

1. Mission names two relations at once: of the one sent to the sender, and of the one sent to the term he is sent to (a.1).
2. In God the "from whom" can only be **procession of origin** — a master sends a servant by command, a counsellor sends a king by advice, and both make the sender greater; what is left is origin, "as a tree sends forth its flowers", and origin in God is according to equality (a.1 ad 1).
3. **Therefore the Father is never sent** (a.4): he is from no one, so the first addend is simply missing for him. Augustine had noticed scripture never says it.
4. The "to where" is not a place: the person sent does not begin to exist where he was not, nor cease to be where he was (a.1 ad 2). What is new is the **manner** of his presence.
5. So mission is a temporal word though the procession inside it is eternal (a.2); the novelty comes "not from change in the divine person but from change in the creature", exactly as God is called Lord in time.

Level: revealed and confessed that the Son and the Spirit were sent. The
**analysis** — procession plus temporal term, visible and invisible, the two senses
of sender — is the Latin tradition's explanation, common teaching, never defined.

*From* [6.1](lessons/06-01-the-missions-of-the-son-and-the-spirit.md)

### Visible and invisible missions

**Invisible** (ST I q.43 a.3): the person himself is given and indwells,
"according to the gift of sanctifying grace". What the created effect in the soul
is, and the schools' disputes about it, belong to `creation-grace-last-things`.

**Visible** (a.7): the person is manifested by a creature made to be a sign — the
Incarnation, the dove at the Jordan, the bright cloud, the breathing on the
apostles, the tongues of fire. Two clauses do the real work:

- Only the Son **assumed** the visible creature into the unity of his person, so that what is said of it is said of him (a.7 ad 1). Nothing said of the dove is said of the Spirit.
- **A visible mission signifies an invisible one** (a.7 ad 6). This is why the Old Testament apparitions — the burning bush, the three at Mamre — are theophanies and not missions: they were not given to designate the indwelling of a person by grace. Augustine's sharper version: if appearing in a creature were enough to be sent, the Father would have been sent in the fire of the bush, and scripture never says he was.

And the sign appears when it is useful to the Church, not when the grace begins:
the visible mission to Christ at the Jordan manifests an invisible mission made to
him at the first moment of his conception.

*From* [6.1](lessons/06-01-the-missions-of-the-son-and-the-spirit.md)

### The two senses of sender

ST I q.43 a.8, and the move that lets a mission terminating in one person survive
[the undivided outward works](#the-undivided-outward-works).

- **Sender as principle of the PERSON sent:** then only the Father sends the Son, and the Father and the Son send the Spirit.
- **Sender as principle of the EFFECT in the creature:** then the whole Trinity sends, because that new presence is an outward work and outward works are undivided.

Both are true, of different things. *The three do the sending, and one of them is
what is sent.* Augustine reaches the same place from scripture: the Father sends
the Son by his Word, and his Word is the Son, so the Son is sent by the Father and
by himself — while the form of a servant is the Son's person and not the Father's
(*De Trinitate* II.5).

*From* [6.1](lessons/06-01-the-missions-of-the-son-and-the-spirit.md)

### The two shapes of Christian prayer

Both ancient, both orthodox, and the doctrine needs both.

| Shape | Form | What it asserts |
|---|---|---|
| **Prepositional** | *to the Father, through the Son, in the Holy Spirit* — the Roman collect, the Canon's *per ipsum et cum ipso et in ipso* | maps the **economy**, the road God took to us walked in reverse; asserts no rank |
| **Coordinate** | *Glory to the Father, and to the Son, and to the Holy Spirit* | asserts **equal glory**, therefore equal nature |

The prepositional is the older; up to the Arian crisis it was the commoner form,
and it is "perfectly consistent with Trinitarian belief: it, however, expresses not
the coequality of the Three Persons, but their operation in regard to man" (Joyce,
*Catholic Encyclopedia*, 1912). The fourth century **added** the coordinate form,
it did not replace the prepositional; the West appended *Sicut erat in principio*,
which the Second Synod of Vaison (529) says was used at Rome and in Africa against
Arianism. **Drop the coordinate form and the economy reads as a hierarchy —
subordinationism. Drop the prepositional and the missions stop signifying — three
interchangeable names for one addressee, which is modalism in practice.**

The level trap: that the Spirit is adored and glorified with the Father and the Son
is rung 1. The **wording** of any particular doxology is liturgical law, which the
Church makes and can change — the Mozarabic rite's *Gloria et honor Patri et Filio
et Spiritui sancto* was ordered by the Fourth Synod of Toledo in 633. Reading a
rubric as a dogma is promoting the explanation, in liturgical dress.

*From* [6.2](lessons/06-02-the-trinity-in-the-economy-liturgy-and-prayer.md)

### Vestigia trinitatis

Augustine's traces of the Trinity in creation (*De Trinitate* VI.10.12, Haddan):
everything made is *one* something, has *a* form, and keeps *an* order; he borrows
and generalizes Hilary's "eternity in the Father, form in the Image, use in the
Gift". **Traces, not proofs** — a trace is what a foot leaves, and you can name the
walker from it only if you already know him. Augustine's own instruction proves it:
let the man who sees it give thanks, and the man who does not "strive to see it
through piety, not to cavil at it through blindness" — advice to a believer, not an
argument to an unbeliever, which is [proof and confirmation](#proof-and-confirmation)
sixteen centuries early. The same paragraph rules on Romans 11:36: do not flatten
*of whom, through whom, to whom* into one preposition (that loses the persons) and
do not read them as many gods (that loses the unity). CCC 237 affirms that creation
bears traces of God's Trinitarian being without endorsing any particular triad;
Augustine's own triad is a theologian's construction, below the seam.

*From* [6.2](lessons/06-02-the-trinity-in-the-economy-liturgy-and-prayer.md)

### The sign of the cross

*In nomine Patris et Filii et Spiritus Sancti.* Three claims in one gesture.
**One name:** *nomine* is singular and governs three genitives — the grammar of
Matthew 28:19, where the Catechism draws the inference: not "in their names", for
there is only one God (CCC 233). Unity of essence and distinction of persons, by a
case ending. **Both halves of the treatise at once:** the words name the Trinity as
it is in itself, and the figure traced is the cross, the term of the Son's visible
mission — *theologia* and *oikonomia* in one act (CCC 236). **What it is not:** an
appropriation of body parts. The head is not the Father's and the shoulders are not
the Spirit's; nothing is distributed, because the persons are not parts and the
name is one.

Where it strains: the formula developed. The earliest evidence is a small cross
traced on the forehead with the thumb (Tertullian describes the habit around 200),
and the words varied for centuries; the large cross and the settled Trinitarian
formula arrive much later. **A developed form carrying a defined faith.**

*From* [6.2](lessons/06-02-the-trinity-in-the-economy-liturgy-and-prayer.md)

## The filioque

Each tradition stated as it states itself. **This card adjudicates nothing** — the
argued case belongs to `apologetics-catholic-claims`.

### Procession from the Father and the Son

**Defined, rung 1**, by two ecumenical councils in definitional acts: the Holy
Spirit proceeds eternally from the Father and the Son, as from one principle and by
a single spiration — Lyons II (1274) and Florence's *Laetentur Caeli* (1439).

Lyons II's constitution on the procession of the Holy Spirit, Latin verified, with
the lesson's own translation (no public-domain English exists):

> *Fideli ac devota professione fatemur quod spiritus sanctus aeternaliter ex patre et filio, non tanquam ex duobus principiis sed tanquam ex uno principio, non duabus spirationibus sed unica spiratione procedit.*
>
> With faithful and devoted profession we confess that the Holy Spirit proceeds eternally from the Father and the Son, not as from two principles but as from one principle, not by two spirations but by a single spiration. *(lesson's translation)*

The same constitution condemns **two** errors by name: denying that the Spirit
proceeds eternally from the Father and the Son, **and** asserting that he proceeds
from them *tanquam ex duobus principiis*. *Laetentur Caeli* has *tamquam ab uno
principio et unica spiratione* — note the preposition differs from Lyons's *ex uno
principio* — and adds the hinge: everything that is the Father's, he gave his
only-begotten Son in begetting him, **except being Father**, so the Son has from the
Father, eternally, that the Spirit proceeds from him. It glosses the Fathers'
"from the Father **through** the Son" as meaning that the Son too is what the Greeks
call cause and the Latins call principle of the Spirit's subsistence.

*From* [5.1](lessons/05-01-the-latin-doctrine.md); read again at [5.2](lessons/05-02-the-greek-doctrine-and-the-addition.md), [5.3](lessons/05-03-the-clarifications.md)

### One principle and one spiration

What the Latin doctrine **denies**, which is where most of the heat comes from.

- **Not two principles.** Lyons II condemns the formula explicitly.
- **Not two spirations.** One spirative power in one act; two acts would make the Spirit doubly caused and God composite.
- **Not two spirators.** Aquinas will say Father and Son are "two spirating" — an adjective takes its number from the persons — but not "two spirators", since the noun takes its number from the one form it names (q.36 a.4).
- **Not a secondary cause.** If the Son had a numerically distinct spirative power he would be an instrumental cause and the Spirit would proceed more from the Father than from the Son (q.36 a.3). The power is one and the same.
- **Not a diminished Father.** He remains [principle without principle](#principle-without-principle); the Son's share in the spiration is itself the Father's gift.
- **Not the essence spirating.** Locate the one principle in the essence rather than in the Father and the Son as possessing one spirative power, and the Spirit proceeds from himself, since he is that essence.

*From* [5.1](lessons/05-01-the-latin-doctrine.md); used at [5.3](lessons/05-03-the-clarifications.md)

### Principally from the Father

Augustine's *principaliter* (*De Trinitate* XV.17.29, Haddan):

> God the Father alone is He from whom the Word is born, and from whom the Holy Spirit principally proceeds. And therefore I have added the word principally, because we find that the Holy Spirit proceeds from the Son also. But the Father gave Him this too... whatever He gave to the only-begotten Word, He gave by begetting Him.

Repeated at XV.26.47: the Father "has given to the Son that the same Holy Spirit
should proceed from Him." Florence makes the point doctrine; the 1995 clarification
names it as the Latin side of the agreement that the Father is the sole Trinitarian
cause.

*From* [5.1](lessons/05-01-the-latin-doctrine.md)

### Principle without principle

The Father alone is from no one — unbegotten and unspirated. Innascibility (ST I
q.33 a.4) is **proper** to him and marks an order of origin, not a degree of
divinity; reading it as superiority is subordinationism in one step. He remains the
source from whom the Son has even the spirative power.

*From* [4.5](lessons/04-05-common-proper-appropriated.md), [5.1](lessons/05-01-the-latin-doctrine.md), [5.2](lessons/05-02-the-greek-doctrine-and-the-addition.md)

### The Latin argument from relative opposition

ST I q.36 a.2 — the argument, **not** the definition, and a Catholic may find it
unpersuasive while holding everything Lyons II and Florence define.

1. The divine persons cannot be distinguished by anything said of God absolutely, since whatever is said absolutely belongs to the one essence.
2. Therefore they are distinguished only by relations — and relations distinguish only when they are **opposed**. Evidence from inside the doctrine: the Father bears two relations, to the Son and to the Spirit, and because these are not opposed to each other they do not make two Fathers.
3. Suppose the Spirit did not proceed from the Son. Then the Son and the Spirit each bear only a relation to the Father, and those two relations are not opposed to each other.
4. Unopposed relations do not distinguish, so the Son and the Spirit would be one person — which destroys the faith in the Trinity.
5. The only relations that can be opposed in God are relations of origin, so either the Son is from the Spirit, which no one holds, or the Spirit is from the Son.

Two **confirmations**, not proofs: the order of the processions (the Son proceeds
as Word, the Spirit as Love, and love proceeds from a word), and the order of
nature (two things never come from one without some order between them, except
where they differ merely by matter).

The scriptural base the West reads: John 15:26 (the Son **sends**, the Spirit
**proceeds from the Father** — the verse does not say from the Son), John 16:14-15
("he shall receive of mine", with "All things whatsoever the Father hath, are
mine"), Romans 8:9 ("the Spirit of Christ"), Galatians 4:6 ("the Spirit of his
Son"). **Be honest:** no New Testament text states that the Spirit's eternal origin
is from the Son. The West reads these texts as disclosing the eternal relation in
the temporal one; a Greek theologian reads the same texts and hears only the
mission.

On "from the Father alone" in the Fathers and liturgies, Aquinas's rule (q.36 a.2
ad 1): what is said of the Father applies to the Son even with an exclusive term
added, **except** where the opposed relations that distinguish them are concerned —
and being the principle of the Spirit is not something Father and Son are opposed
in. That tells you how a *Latin* sentence behaves; it does not tell you what a
*Greek* sentence means.

*From* [5.1](lessons/05-01-the-latin-doctrine.md)

### Ekporeusis and processio

The asymmetry the whole dispute rides on.

- ***Ekporeuomai*** — "to issue out from", a verb of exit **from a point of origin**. John 15:26 has *para tou patros ekporeuetai*, "from beside the Father"; the creed of 381 sharpens the preposition to *ek tou patros ekporeuomenon*, "out of the Father". In Greek theological usage thereafter *ekporeusis* names one thing: **the Spirit's derivation of existence from the cause**.
- ***Proienai*** — the wide Greek verb for any coming-forth, including the communication of the one divinity from the Father to the Son, and from the Father through and with the Son to the Spirit.
- ***Procedere*** — one Latin verb covering both. The Vulgate uses it for *ekporeuetai* at John 15:26 **and** for a different Greek verb, *exelthon*, at John 8:42 (*ego enim ex Deo processi*). It names any coming-forth whatever and specifies no cause.

Consequence: a Latin hears *ex Patre Filioque procedit* as "the Spirit comes forth
from Father and Son". Put that creed into Greek and only *ekporeuetai* is
available, so a Greek reads "the Spirit takes his existence out of the Father and
out of the Son" — two causes in God, or a first and a second cause. The 1995
clarification's own phrase for the mismatch: **"a false equivalence was
involuntarily created"**. *Filioque* is true in the wide verb and false in the
narrow one.

*From* [5.2](lessons/05-02-the-greek-doctrine-and-the-addition.md), [5.3](lessons/05-03-the-clarifications.md)

### The Greek doctrine, in the order a Greek theologian builds it

1. **What the three have in common is the *ousia*, the one Godhead; what distinguishes them is *tropos hyparxeos*, manner of existing.** The persons never differ in what they are, only in how each one is.
2. **One of the three is from no one.** The Father is unbegotten; that is his personal property.
3. **The Father alone is *aitia*.** Everything in God that is not the Father comes from the Father, and from nowhere else. The Son and the Spirit are each *aitiaton*, caused — and in Greek Trinitarian usage that is not a grade of divinity, because the cause gives the whole divine nature entire.
4. **Generation and procession are two distinct modes of coming from that one cause, and we cannot say how they differ.** That they differ is revealed; what the difference consists in is not.
5. **Therefore the Spirit's *hyparxis*, his very existing as a person, is from the Father.** Ask where the Spirit gets his being and the answer has exactly one term in it.
6. **The Son is not idle in this.** The Spirit is the Spirit *of the Son*, rests on him, is sent by him, shines out through him — all said with the preposition *dia*, and none of it making the Son a cause.

John of Damascus, *An Exposition of the Orthodox Faith* I.8 (Salmond, NPNF second
series vol. 9), written around 743 and so not a polemic: "And we speak likewise of
the Holy Spirit as from the Father, and call Him the Spirit of the Father. And we
do not speak of the Spirit as from the Son: but yet we call Him the Spirit of the
Son. ... And we confess that He is manifested and imparted to us through the Son."
At I.12 he gives the rule: "cause, source, begetter" are "to be ascribed to the
Father alone."

**Step 6 is where the whole dispute lives.** And note: inside this grammar the
*filioque* is not a small addition, because [monarchy](#monarchy-of-the-father) does
the work that relation does in the Latin scheme — give the Spirit a second point of
origin and the reason God is one has been split.

*From* [5.2](lessons/05-02-the-greek-doctrine-and-the-addition.md)

### Through the Son

*Dia tou Huiou.* The Greek formula affirming a **real relation** of the Spirit to
the Son — Spirit of the Son, resting on him, shining through him — **without**
making the Son a cause. Latins read it as equivalent to the filioque, and Florence
glosses it that way; Photius reads it of the temporal sending only. The 1995
clarification collects the Eastern witnesses for it itself: Basil, Maximus the
Confessor, John Damascene, and the patriarch Tarasius at Nicaea II in 787.

*From* [5.2](lessons/05-02-the-greek-doctrine-and-the-addition.md)

### Eternal manifestation

Gregory II of Cyprus (Council of Blachernae, 1285), developed by Palamas: distinguish
the Spirit's **existence**, which is from the Father alone, from his **eternal
manifestation** or shining-forth (*ekphansis*), which is through and from the Son
and is not a second origin. **A real theological move, not an evasion** — it uses
the existence-and-manifestation grammar the Greek tradition already uses elsewhere,
and a council of the Eastern church adopted it. It exists because Photius's tight
answer (that *dia tou Huiou* is only about the temporal mission) must explain away
much Greek material that sounds eternal, including the Damascene's own I.12 and
Cyril of Alexandria calling the Spirit "proper to" the Son. Latin critics ask
whether eternal manifestation is a genuine distinction, the filioque renamed, or
nothing at all; its defenders answer that refusing it refuses a grammar rather than
exposing an error.

*From* [5.2](lessons/05-02-the-greek-doctrine-and-the-addition.md)

### The map of Greek positions

Rows two and three are both Greek and they disagree: **"the Greek position" is a
tradition with an internal argument, not a sentence.** Photius is not "the Greek
position", only its sharpest form.

| Position | Does the Son have a role in the Spirit's **eternal being**? | Key word |
|---|---|---|
| **John of Damascus** (d. c. 749) | from the Father; the Spirit *of the Son*; manifested and given *through* the Son | *dia tou Huiou* |
| **Photius** (*Mystagogy*, c. 885) | no — "through the Son" is read of the temporal sending, not of eternal origin | *ek monou tou Patros*, from the Father alone |
| **Gregory II of Cyprus** (Blachernae, 1285), **Palamas** | *hypostasis* from the Father alone, but he eternally shines forth through the Son: a real eternal relation that is not origination | *ekphansis* |
| **The Latin doctrine** | yes: from Father and Son as from one principle, by a single spiration; *dia tou Huiou* read as saying this | *tamquam ab uno principio* |

*From* [5.2](lessons/05-02-the-greek-doctrine-and-the-addition.md)

### The canonical objection to the addition

Distinct from the doctrinal one, and independent of whether the doctrine is true.
Ephesus forbade composing a different faith as a rival to Nicaea's
([the prohibition of another faith](#the-prohibition-of-another-faith)); the Greek
complaint is that part of the Church altered a creed ratified by ecumenical
councils **without a council**. Keep the two objections apart: the canonical one
could be granted without touching the doctrinal one — Leo III in 809 held the
doctrine and refused the insertion. The Latin creed with *Filioque* appears in
Visigothic Spain around the Third Council of Toledo (589), by an unknown hand;
spread through Francia; was defended at Friuli (796) and Aachen (809); and is
generally dated at Rome to 1014. The events belong to
[`church-history` 4.2](../church-history/lessons/04-02-east-and-west-the-long-estrangement.md).

*From* [5.2](lessons/05-02-the-greek-doctrine-and-the-addition.md)

### The 1995 clarification

Pontifical Council for Promoting Christian Unity, *The Greek and Latin Traditions
regarding the Procession of the Holy Spirit*, published in *L'Osservatore Romano*,
September 1995, at John Paul II's request. **A curial text interpreting doctrine
already taught: it defines nothing.** The document is under copyright, so the
lessons quote only short exact phrases and paraphrase the rest.

What it claims:

1. The creed professed in Greek at Constantinople in 381 has conciliar, ecumenical, normative and irrevocable value, and no profession proper to one liturgical tradition may contradict it.
2. In that creed and in John 15:26 the verb is *ekporeuesthai*, marking origin from the Father alone as the principle without principle — the Spirit takes his origin from the Father "in a principal, proper and immediate manner". So **both traditions agree that the monarchy of the Father means the Father is the sole Trinitarian cause (*aitia*) or principle (*principium*)** of the Son and of the Spirit; the Latin side of that agreement is Augustine's *principaliter*.
3. The Greek tradition does not thereby deny every eternal relation of the Spirit to the Son — the text collects the Eastern witnesses for *dia tou Huiou* itself.
4. Latin *processio* is the wider term. Because the Vulgate and the Latin creed used it for the narrow Greek verb, **"a false equivalence was involuntarily created"**.
5. Maximus the Confessor, writing from Rome in the seventh century (*Letter to Marin*), had already reported that the Latins do not make the Son the Cause of the Spirit.
6. Therefore the *Filioque* must be understood and presented so that it cannot appear to contradict the monarchy of the Father or his being sole origin of the Spirit's *ekporeusis* — and therefore the Catholic Church refuses the addition of *kai tou Huiou* to the Greek formula.

**What it does not concede.** It cites Lateran IV's dogma that the substance neither
generates nor is begotten nor proceeds, and Lyons II on one principle and one
spiration. It warns that the *Filioque* must not lead to a **subordination of the
Holy Spirit**. It affirms an eternal communication of the consubstantial divinity
from the Father and the Son to the Spirit. **The Son's role in the Spirit's eternal
origin is not surrendered: what is claimed to be terminological is which verb, not
whether the Son is involved.**

*From* [5.3](lessons/05-03-the-clarifications.md)

### Legitimate complementarity

CCC 246-248's judgement on the two traditions: the East expresses first that the
Father is the first origin of the Spirit, the West first the consubstantial
communion of Father and Son, and the complementarity is legitimate **"provided it
does not become rigid"** and does not affect the identity of the faith professed.
**The qualifier is part of the claim** — quoting "complementarity" without the
condition turns a careful judgement into a slogan.

*From* [5.3](lessons/05-03-the-clarifications.md)

### The Greek creed in Catholic use

The Catholic Church refuses the addition of *kai tou Huiou* to the Greek formula of
the creed of 381 in the churches that recite it in Greek, **including those of Latin
rite**; that original text remains always legitimate in the Catholic Church. A real
and instructive fact about how the Church reads the two texts, not an ecumenical
courtesy.

*From* [5.3](lessons/05-03-the-clarifications.md)

### The 2003 agreed statement

North American Orthodox-Catholic Theological Consultation, *The Filioque: A
Church-Dividing Issue?*, Washington DC, 25 October 2003. **A bilateral body of
theologians: no magisterial authority whatever.** Its recommendations are addressed
*to* the bishops of both churches; they are not teaching *by* bishops. Among them:
that both sides stop labelling the other's tradition heretical on this point; that
theologians distinguish the Spirit's divinity and hypostatic identity, which is
received dogma, from the manner of his origin; that the Catholic Church use the
**original Greek text alone** when making translations of the creed; and that the
Catholic Church declare Lyons II's condemnation "no longer applicable".

Two things to notice when weighing it. The last recommendation proposes that a
rung-1 conciliar condemnation be reinterpreted, and only the authority that
condemned can do that. And the third goes **further than the 1995 clarification**,
which refuses the addition in the *Greek* creed while keeping the Latin version in
the Latin liturgy.

*From* [5.3](lessons/05-03-the-clarifications.md)

## Heresies and the phrases that land in them

Each entry: what the error claims, the giveaway phrasing, and the text that
corrects it. The diagnose archetype runs on this section.

### Modalism

**One God appearing in three forms, modes or roles rather than three really
distinct persons** — one actor, three parts. Also Sabellianism; what we know of
Sabellius comes from later and hostile reporters, so name the doctrine and hedge
the biography.

Giveaway phrasing: "God shows us three different sides of himself"; "three faces of
one God"; "God was the Father in Israel, the Son in the gospels, and is the Spirit
in the Church"; "God is Father in creation, Son in redemption, Spirit in the
Church" (Sabellius with a calendar); "God is one substance who shows himself as
three"; "the Spirit is just God's power at work in us"; "the Father is a
relationship God is in".

Corrected by: *Firmiter*'s *tres quidem personae*; the *Quicumque*'s
[neither confounding nor dividing](#neither-confounding-nor-dividing) and v.5's
*alia... alia... alia*; *aeternus* and *incommutabilis*, which rule out stages in a
life possessed whole and at once, before the Trinity is even introduced;
[real relation in God](#real-relation-in-god)'s *sed contra*, which names the
Sabellian heresy as the stake.

**Note the gap:** the creed of 325 does **not** reach modalism. Every positive
clause is signable by someone who thinks Father and Son are one subsisting God
under two descriptions, and the anathema against "another *hypostasis*" sounds, to
later ears, positively friendly to him — Marcellus of Ancyra, one of the creed's own
defenders, was anathematized in eastern creeds of the 340s alongside Sabellius. The
rule against confounding comes from the Latin texts.

*Met at* [2.1](lessons/02-01-the-one-god-of-israel.md), [2.2](lessons/02-02-the-father-and-the-son-in-the-new-testament.md), [2.3](lessons/02-03-the-holy-spirit-in-the-new-testament.md), [2.4](lessons/02-04-from-confession-to-creed.md), [3.1](lessons/03-01-nicaea.md), [3.4](lessons/03-04-the-latin-rules-of-speech.md), [6.3](lessons/06-03-rahners-rule-and-the-immanent-trinity.md)

### Monarchianism and modalism

**What the error was protecting**, which is the whole reason it was attractive.
Noetus of Smyrna, summoned twice by his presbyters, defended himself: "What evil,
then, am I doing in glorifying Christ?" That is not a man reducing Jesus — he is
protecting **the full divinity of Christ** in the only way his vocabulary allowed:
if Christ is God without remainder and there is one God, then Christ is the one
God, the Father himself. At Rome a generation later, Praxeas's party carried a
slogan: they maintained the *monarchy*, the sole rule of the one God, and charged
their opponents with preaching two. The second protected thing is **monotheism**.
Both motives are correct, and the Church defines both. The cost is
[patripassianism](#patripassianism), and a gospel in which the one praying in
Gethsemane and the one prayed to are the same actor in two costumes.

*From* [2.4](lessons/02-04-from-confession-to-creed.md)

### Subordinationism

**Making the Son or the Spirit less than the Father in nature, or dependent for
being on the Father's will, rather than only in order of origin.**

Giveaway phrasing: reading "the Father is greater than I" as a difference of
nature; treating being sent, given, received-from or "not speaking of himself" as a
lower rank; "the Father is the source, therefore the Father is more fully God";
"unbegotten" as something the Father has and the Son lacks; "the Son is a secondary
cause, an instrument the Father spirates through"; reading *through the Son* as
*beneath the Son*; innascibility read as superiority; reading "the Father sent me"
as a chain of command.

Corrected by: Nicaea's "of the essence of the Father" and "begotten, not made";
[distinction only by origin](#distinction-only-by-origin) (Gregory of Nazianzus,
*Oration* 31.9 — the Spirit lacks nothing, since God has no deficiency); the
*Quicumque* vv.25-26, nothing prior or posterior, nothing greater or less;
[negation keeps the category](#negation-keeps-the-category); ST I q.36 a.3 on the
one spirative power; ST I q.43 a.1 ad 1, that mission in God is procession of origin
alone, which is according to equality. **Origin is not inferiority**, and the Greek
tradition blocks the same inference by insisting the cause communicates the whole
undiminished Godhead.

Also: pre-Nicene subordinationist idiom is **not** Arianism. Arius denies the Son is
from the Father's substance at all; Theophilus and Tertullian assert he is and then
rank him.

*Met at* [2.2](lessons/02-02-the-father-and-the-son-in-the-new-testament.md), [2.3](lessons/02-03-the-holy-spirit-in-the-new-testament.md), [2.4](lessons/02-04-from-confession-to-creed.md), [3.1](lessons/03-01-nicaea.md), [5.2](lessons/05-02-the-greek-doctrine-and-the-addition.md), [5.3](lessons/05-03-the-clarifications.md)

### Tritheism

**Three divine agents with three minds and wills, the unity grounded in their
agreement.**

Giveaway phrasing: "three persons share the divine nature as three men share
humanity"; reading Genesis 1:26 as a committee conferring; "the Father made the
world, the Son redeemed it, and the Spirit makes us holy, each doing his own job";
"each almighty in his own way"; three centres of consciousness; "the Father and the
Son are two sources who pool their effort"; treating the divinity as a stock the
three draw on.

Corrected by: *Firmiter*'s *una essentia, substantia seu natura simplex omnino* — a
kind is not an altogether simple nature; the *Quicumque*'s *neque substantiam
separantes* and its refusal of the plural; [the undivided outward
works](#the-undivided-outward-works) in its conciliar form, not three principles of
the creature but one principle; and the decisive constraint — **intellect, will,
consciousness and operation carry no [relative opposition](#relative-opposition),
so they are one in God.** Will follows nature, so three wills means three natures;
that premise is the one Constantinople III used to confess two wills in the
incarnate Son.

*Met at* [2.1](lessons/02-01-the-one-god-of-israel.md), [2.4](lessons/02-04-from-confession-to-creed.md), [3.4](lessons/03-04-the-latin-rules-of-speech.md), [4.5](lessons/04-05-common-proper-appropriated.md), [6.4](lessons/06-04-social-trinitarianism-and-its-critics.md)

### Patripassianism

**The claim that the Father himself suffered and died on the cross.** A corollary of
modalism rather than a separate error: only if Father and Son are one person under
two names can the Father be the one hanging there. Hence Tertullian's double charge
against Praxeas, that he drove out the Paraclete **and** crucified the Father
(*Against Praxeas* 1). Refused in the third century — the Church acting on the
doctrine before anyone had a formula for it.

The licit sentence is
[one of the Trinity suffered in the flesh](#one-of-the-trinity-suffered-in-the-flesh),
which **distinguishes** the persons instead of collapsing them. And "the Godhead
died" is a third error again: it predicates of a nature what belongs to a person.
Grant the modern case everything it asks and natures still do not die; persons do.
Watch hymn texts, where the metre does the theology.

*Met at* [1.5](lessons/01-05-impassibility-and-the-god-who-suffers.md), [2.4](lessons/02-04-from-confession-to-creed.md)

### Quaternity

**Three persons plus a common essence as a fourth item.** Joachim of Fiore's charge
against Peter Lombard, and the council sided with Lombard:
[the essence is not a fourth thing](#the-essence-is-not-a-fourth-thing) —
*in Deo solummodo Trinitas est, non quaternitas*.

Giveaway phrasing: "*De Deo Uno* is about the essence, *De Deo Trino* about the
persons", heard as a container that then *has* three persons in it; "the divine
essence begets the Son"; "the divine nature" as the subject of any notional act;
"almighty is really the name of the one God behind the three". The repair for the
begetting sentence is not a softer verb but **a change of subject: the Father
begets.**

*Met at* [1.1](lessons/01-01-the-treatise-and-its-order.md), [3.4](lessons/03-04-the-latin-rules-of-speech.md)

## Modern Trinitarian theology

Each position at the strength its best readers would recognize, then the constraint
that bites. **All of it is rung 5** — no magisterial act adopts or condemns any of
it.

### Immanent and economic Trinity

The **immanent** Trinity (older name *theologia*) is God's own eternal life: the
Father begetting the Son, the two together breathing the Spirit, with or without a
world. The **economic** Trinity (*oikonomia*) is the Trinity as it turns up in
history: the Father who sends, the Son sent and made man, the Spirit poured out and
given in grace. Nobody doubts the two are the same God; the dispute is over what
kind of sameness, and whether the sentence may be read in both directions.

*From* [6.3](lessons/06-03-rahners-rule-and-the-immanent-trinity.md)

### Rahner's rule

Karl Rahner's axiom (*The Trinity*, the 1967 essay for *Mysterium Salutis*) — the
one modern sentence this course quotes rather than paraphrases, because the argument
is about its words: **"The 'economic' Trinity is the 'immanent' Trinity, and the
'immanent' Trinity is the 'economic' Trinity."**

- **First half:** what meets us in the economy is God himself — no mask, no created stand-in, not three created effects under three appropriated names. **Uncontroversial, and always was**; [6.1](lessons/06-01-the-missions-of-the-son-and-the-spirit.md) says it in Thomist form.
- **Second half:** there is no further, differently shaped Trinity behind the economy; the missions *are* the processions reaching us.
- **What the first half wins:** Christian prayer is not a pious form laid over a God indifferent to its shape; grace is God's self-gift, not a created something handed across a gap; the three are not an appendix to a God already fully described without them.
- **What critics press on the second half.** *God's freedom:* the economy contains free acts, so if the immanent Trinity simply is the economic, either those acts belong to what God is — against *Dei Filius* on free creation — or the "is" is weaker than identity and needs saying (Molnar; Weinandy and Emery from the side of simplicity). *God in himself and God for us:* if the immanent Trinity has no content the economy does not display, theology can read God's inner life off salvation history, and [1.5](lessons/01-05-impassibility-and-the-god-who-suffers.md)'s questions reopen with the persons in hand.
- **Rahner's own qualifications**, which belong to any fair statement of him: the self-communication is **free and gratuitous** (the rule identifies *who* is given, not that the giving had to happen); God remains absolute mystery; and he denies modalism outright.
- **The crux.** Critics say the qualifications and the axiom pull apart — read strongly enough to do new work the rule costs freedom, read weakly enough to keep freedom it says what q.43 already said. Defenders call that a false choice and read the rule as a **methodological** identity: it forbids building an immanent Trinity out of anything but the economy, without asserting that God's being is constituted by history. Which description fits Rahner's text is genuinely open among Catholic theologians.

*From* [6.3](lessons/06-03-rahners-rule-and-the-immanent-trinity.md)

### Modes of being and manners of subsisting

Barth's *Seinsweise* (*Church Dogmatics* I/1, 1932) and Rahner's "distinct manners
of subsisting", both offered in place of "three persons" and both reacting against
the modern ear's hearing of *person* as a separate centre of consciousness. **Not
modalism by themselves, but one sentence away**: the test is not the noun but
whether the three are really distinct *in God* and not only in our account of him.
Both say they are; both were accused anyway.

**Barth's distance from Rahner**, worth stating exactly because the two get merged:
Barth, a generation earlier and a Protestant, asserts the first half emphatically
and **refuses the symmetry**. God's being toward us corresponds to and is grounded
in his eternal being, and God would be the same triune God without a world. The
grounding runs one way only. The echo of vocabulary is no accident; the axiom is not
shared.

*From* [6.3](lessons/06-03-rahners-rule-and-the-immanent-trinity.md)

### Ur-kenosis

Balthasar. The Father's begetting of the Son is a **total self-giving in which he
keeps nothing back**, and the self-emptying of Philippians 2 is its created
expression. Because Father and Son are already held apart by an infinite distance
that the Spirit holds open in love, God can go into the far country of the cross
without ceasing to be himself: the distance of sin is contained within a greater
Trinitarian distance. His own guard is that the language is **analogical** and that
what grounds the cross is fullness, not lack.

Constraints its defenders must clear: keeping "self-giving" and "distance"
analogical (simplicity, [1.3](lessons/01-03-simplicity-as-doctrine.md);
impassibility, [1.5](lessons/01-05-impassibility-and-the-god-who-suffers.md)); not
turning the distinction of persons into conflict. Kilby objects that the account
claims a view of God's inner life no theologian is positioned to have. **Do not
read Balthasar's "distance" in God as suffering in the divine nature** — he
describes the relations of origin under an image of self-gift, and is not conceding
what the modern passibility case wants.

*From* [6.3](lessons/06-03-rahners-rule-and-the-immanent-trinity.md)

### Social trinitarianism

The movement that **starts from the three** rather than the one essence and locates
divine unity in the **communion** (*perichoresis*) of the persons, then reads that
communion as the pattern for human society. Its case at full strength:

1. **The revealed data is the economy** — three in relation, not an undifferentiated essence.
2. **The economic Trinity is the immanent Trinity**: the communion we see is not a mask.
3. **To be a person is to be in relation.** Zizioulas, its most serious metaphysician, argues that the Greek Fathers made *hypostasis* prior to *ousia*, so that being itself is communion rather than closed substance (*Being as Communion*, 1981).
4. **God is one because one person is the source** — Zizioulas grounds this on the [monarchy of the Father](#monarchy-of-the-father), which is the Eastern tradition's own answer, not a modern innovation.
5. **Perichoresis is what unity means.** Moltmann (*The Trinity and the Kingdom*, 1980) makes the mutual indwelling, not a shared substance, the form of divine unity, and calls the doctrine open to history.
6. **Therefore the Trinity has social consequences.** Moltmann argues that a strictly monarchical monotheism has been used to sanction monarchs and patriarchs and offers the Trinity as the antidote; Boff turns it to liberation, under the slogan that the Trinity is our social programme (traced to Nikolai Fedorov — **an attribution the lesson did not verify**); LaCugna's *God for Us* (1991) makes the programmatic version, that the doctrine decayed when speculation about God in himself was cut loose from the economy.

**Two things critics are regularly unfair about.** None of these writers is a
tritheist by intention — Moltmann's unity is perichoretic and eternal, Boff denies
tritheism explicitly, Zizioulas's whole account of unity is the one cause. And
LaCugna is not abolishing the immanent Trinity; her claim is that treating immanent
and economic as two *objects* is what went wrong.

The critics come in three kinds. **Method:** [the projection
objection](#the-projection-objection). **History:** Holmes (*The Quest for the
Trinity*, 2012) and Ayres (*Nicaea and its Legacy*, 2004) argue that the pro-Nicene
settlement, Greek and Latin alike, holds simplicity, inseparable operation and
distinction by relations of origin alone — so the story of Cappadocian personalism
smothered by Augustinian essentialism is the [de Regnon
paradigm](#the-de-regnon-paradigm) again, and the retrieval retrieves nothing.
**Dogma:** Marshall (*Trinity and Truth*, 2000; "The Unity of the Triune God", *The
Thomist* 74, 2010) and Ormerod (*The Trinity: Retrieving the Western Tradition*) ask
what the defining texts actually permit.

*From* [6.4](lessons/06-04-social-trinitarianism-and-its-critics.md)

### The projection objection

Karen Kilby, "Perichoresis and Projection: Problems with Social Doctrines of the
Trinity" (*New Blackfriars* 81, 2000), in three steps. **First**, *perichoresis* in
the Fathers is a nearly formal term — it names that the three are in one another,
without explaining how. **Second**, modern writers fill it with content drawn from
somewhere else: an ideal of human relationality, mutuality, non-competitive love.
**Third**, the filled-in concept is presented back as a revealed truth about God's
inner life and as a norm binding on us.

**Not a censure**: it is an objection to a *method*, and Kilby proposes instead that
the doctrine be held more apophatically — the grammar of what may be said, not a
picture of what God is like inside. Its targets reply that the objection cuts at
any doctrine whose content arrives from our own ideals, [the psychological
analogy](#the-psychological-analogy) included, since that too is an image drawn from
a creature.

*From* [6.4](lessons/06-04-social-trinitarianism-and-its-critics.md)

### Analogy

Assumed here, taught in
[`thomistic-synthesis` 4.4](../thomistic-synthesis/lessons/04-04-analogy.md). Words
said of God are neither univocal with their creaturely use nor unrelated to it.
This course uses it at three points and derives it at none: it is what licenses
reading scripture's language of divine grief and repentance non-univocally
([1.5](lessons/01-05-impassibility-and-the-god-who-suffers.md)); it is what keeps
[the greater dissimilarity rule](#the-greater-dissimilarity-rule) from silencing
theology ([1.3](lessons/01-03-simplicity-as-doctrine.md)); and it is the guard
Balthasar's defenders must keep on "self-giving" and "distance" in God
([6.3](lessons/06-03-rahners-rule-and-the-immanent-trinity.md)).

## Pitfalls

### Level errors

- **Overstating.** "The Church has always defined that God is impassible"; "the Church teaches that the three at Mambre are the three persons"; calling timeless eternity dogma; calling Rahner's rule "the Church's modern teaching on the Trinity"; citing Nicaea for the Spirit's divinity. The procedure is [`fundamental-theology` 4.4](../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-04-the-ladder-of-doctrinal-authority.md)'s first step: **find the act.** Age and liturgical use are not acts. *([1.4](lessons/01-04-immutability-eternity-immensity.md), [1.5](lessons/01-05-impassibility-and-the-god-who-suffers.md), [2.1](lessons/02-01-the-one-god-of-israel.md), [3.1](lessons/03-01-nicaea.md), [6.3](lessons/06-03-rahners-rule-and-the-immanent-trinity.md))*
- **Understating.** "Impassibility is a Greek opinion the manuals liked"; "simplicity is the Thomist view of God"; calling timeless eternity "just Boethius's theory"; "Augustine only meant a poetic comparison, so the three are just ways of speaking". Book 15 shrinks the *analogy*, never the Trinity. *([1.3](lessons/01-03-simplicity-as-doctrine.md), [1.4](lessons/01-04-immutability-eternity-immensity.md), [4.2](lessons/04-02-augustine-the-image-in-the-mind.md))*
- **Promoting the explanation.** Subsistent relations, procession by intellect and will, the psychological analogy, the five notions, the appropriation schemes, mission as procession plus temporal term, the relative-opposition argument for the filioque — none is defined. *(every module)*
- **Promoting a rubric or a Father.** A liturgical wording is law the Church can change; a Father's sentence is not a definition — Challoner's note on Genesis 1:26 says *insinuate*, and a bishop's annotation is no rung at all. *([2.1](lessons/02-01-the-one-god-of-israel.md), [2.4](lessons/02-04-from-confession-to-creed.md), [6.2](lessons/06-02-the-trinity-in-the-economy-liturgy-and-prayer.md))*

### Reading scripture as proof

- **Running the traffic backwards.** Mining the Old Testament for proofs inverts the order of knowing, and ST I q.32 a.1 says the cost is that unbelievers conclude the faith rests on such reasons. *([2.1](lessons/02-01-the-one-god-of-israel.md))*
- **Claiming a verse defines.** No text in Module 2 is a definition; claiming for your reading of a verse the authority of a council's act is the overstatement error in exegetical dress. *([2.2](lessons/02-02-the-father-and-the-son-in-the-new-testament.md))*
- **"Scripture plainly calls the Holy Spirit God."** It does not, anywhere. Saying so trades a certain doctrine for an easily refuted claim about a text. The opposite error — "not stated in so many words" means "not taught" — would unmake half the creed, since nothing says *consubstantial* either. *([2.3](lessons/02-03-the-holy-spirit-in-the-new-testament.md))*
- **Treating a hard verse as a problem to explain away.** "The Father is greater than I" is a datum the Church has always kept; only the extra premise is denied. *([2.2](lessons/02-02-the-father-and-the-son-in-the-new-testament.md))*
- **Over-reading co-listing, or a pronoun.** Paul lists angels with God and Christ; John's *ekeinos* follows the masculine *parakletos*. The weight sits elsewhere. *([2.3](lessons/02-03-the-holy-spirit-in-the-new-testament.md))*

### Speaking about the essence

- **Making the essence a fourth thing, or a subject.** "The divine nature begets the Son" is forbidden; the Father begets. "Almighty is really the name of the one God behind the three" posits the same fourth item. *([1.1](lessons/01-01-the-treatise-and-its-order.md), [3.4](lessons/03-04-the-latin-rules-of-speech.md))*
- **Reading simplicity as a positive description.** "God is simple" does not mean thin or minimal; it is a denial. And "not really distinct" is not "merely verbal" — the distinction has a foundation in the thing. *([1.3](lessons/01-03-simplicity-as-doctrine.md))*
- **Hearing immensity as size.** *Immensus* is un-measured. Read it as infinite extension and God has parts where he is spread; push it and you reach pantheism, anathematized in the third canon of *Dei Filius* on God the Creator. *([1.2](lessons/01-02-the-one-true-god-and-what-reason-can-reach.md), [1.4](lessons/01-04-immutability-eternity-immensity.md))*
- **Hearing "really and essentially distinct from the world" as distance.** It denies that God is the world's substance, not that he is present to it. Trade pantheism for deism and you have lost both. *([1.2](lessons/01-02-the-one-true-god-and-what-reason-can-reach.md))*
- **Taking the greater-dissimilarity rule as licence for silence.** It ranks two true things and caps the second; a rule forbidding all likeness would forbid itself. *([1.3](lessons/01-03-simplicity-as-doctrine.md), [3.4](lessons/03-04-the-latin-rules-of-speech.md))*
- **Treating relation as a link between three things.** Then there are three things. The relations are not extra items added to the substance; they are the only thing distinguishing the three. *([4.1](lessons/04-01-augustine-relation-substance-and-predication.md))*

### Appropriations preached as properties

- **Handing each person a department.** "The Father made the world, the Son redeemed it, the Spirit makes us holy, each doing his own job." An appropriation is never exclusive; what is appropriated to one stays wholly true of the other two. The single most common error in catechesis about the Trinity. *([4.5](lessons/04-05-common-proper-appropriated.md), [6.2](lessons/06-02-the-trinity-in-the-economy-liturgy-and-prayer.md))*
- **The over-correction.** If the persons make no difference to what God does toward us, the economy is uninformative and the three names are three masks. Each person performs the one common work **according to his personal property**. *([4.5](lessons/04-05-common-proper-appropriated.md), [6.3](lessons/06-03-rahners-rule-and-the-immanent-trinity.md))*
- **Treating a mission as just an appropriation.** Only the Son became man; a mission terminates in one person and not the others. Collapse that and you lose the Incarnation. *([6.1](lessons/06-01-the-missions-of-the-son-and-the-spirit.md))*
- **Distributing one subject's faculties among the three.** "The Father is God's memory, the Son his understanding, the Spirit his will" is not a slight overstatement of Augustine — it is the thing he calls absurd (XV.7.12). *([4.2](lessons/04-02-augustine-the-image-in-the-mind.md))*

### Procession, mission and the persons

- **Taking the analysis for the dogma.** The clicking sound when q.27 lands is not the sound of learning the doctrine of the Trinity. *([4.3](lessons/04-03-the-two-processions.md))*
- **"The Son is the Father's idea of himself and the Spirit their mutual affection."** Then the three are acts of one subject, and the subject is the Father — modalism with a psychological accent. *([4.3](lessons/04-03-the-two-processions.md))*
- **Reading procession as distance from the source, or spiration as a demotion.** The more perfectly something proceeds within, the more closely it is one with its source; and that love is not called *generation* is a claim about the word, not the rank. *([4.3](lessons/04-03-the-two-processions.md))*
- **Counting relations instead of oppositions.** Four relations, three persons: spiration opposes neither paternity nor filiation. *([4.4](lessons/04-04-four-relations-three-persons.md))*
- **Taking the missions for three episodes.** The Father in Israel, the Son for thirty-three years, the Spirit from Pentecost — one God working a shift rota. All three are wholly present throughout; the Father, who is never sent, is not thereby absent. *([6.1](lessons/06-01-the-missions-of-the-son-and-the-spirit.md))*
- **Reading "person" as a centre of consciousness.** Three of those is tritheism; one with three names is modalism. Augustine's own admission guards against reading his word in the modern sense. *([4.1](lessons/04-01-augustine-relation-substance-and-predication.md), [6.4](lessons/06-04-social-trinitarianism-and-its-critics.md))*

### Stating the other tradition

- **"They just deny the filioque."** A subtraction is not a doctrine. The Greek East affirms the monarchy of the Father, the Father as sole *aitia*, the Spirit as the Spirit of the Son, and a relation to the Son signified by *dia*. Someone who can only report a denial cannot state the position at all. *([5.2](lessons/05-02-the-greek-doctrine-and-the-addition.md))*
- **Flattening the Greek tradition into Photius.** Gregory of Cyprus and Palamas hold more about the Son's eternal role. *([5.2](lessons/05-02-the-greek-doctrine-and-the-addition.md))*
- **Fusing the two objections.** The canonical complaint about altering a creed could be granted without touching the doctrinal one — Leo III held the doctrine and refused the insertion. *([5.2](lessons/05-02-the-greek-doctrine-and-the-addition.md), [3.3](lessons/03-03-constantinople-i-and-the-divinity-of-the-spirit.md))*
- **Deflating the 1995 text.** It says the *equivalence* of the two verbs was a mistranslation, not that the doctrine was a clerical error, and it defends the Latin doctrine as true in its own verb. Nor does it concede that the Son's role is only economic. *([5.3](lessons/05-03-the-clarifications.md))*
- **Quoting "complementarity" without CCC 248's condition** — *provided it does not become rigid*. *([5.3](lessons/05-03-the-clarifications.md))*
- **Collapsing Moltmann into process theology.** Moltmann's God suffers **freely**; process theology's God is affected because that is the kind of being he is. *([1.5](lessons/01-05-impassibility-and-the-god-who-suffers.md))*
- **Merging Barth and Rahner.** Barth refuses the symmetry the axiom asserts. *([6.3](lessons/06-03-rahners-rule-and-the-immanent-trinity.md))*
- **Using the de Regnon paradigm as a premise.** It is contested historiography; leaning on it to settle a doctrinal question smuggles in a thesis nobody has argued for. *([1.1](lessons/01-01-the-treatise-and-its-order.md), [4.2](lessons/04-02-augustine-the-image-in-the-mind.md), [6.4](lessons/06-04-social-trinitarianism-and-its-critics.md))*
- **Treating a "social" doctrine as automatically unorthodox.** It is not; what crosses the line is three centres of consciousness each with its own will. And Kilby's objection is an argument about method, not a censure. *([6.4](lessons/06-04-social-trinitarianism-and-its-critics.md))*

### Apologetic pitfalls

- **Offering a strong argument for the Trinity as a proof.** Richard of St Victor's argument from charity is licit as a display of coherence and illicit as a demonstration; the difference lies entirely in what the speaker claims for it. *([1.2](lessons/01-02-the-one-true-god-and-what-reason-can-reach.md))*
- **The opposite error, fideism.** "Not demonstrable" is not "not rationally defensible", and it is not licence for silence when the doctrine is called incoherent. *([1.2](lessons/01-02-the-one-true-god-and-what-reason-can-reach.md))*
- **Quoting *una substantia, tres personae* as Tertullian's.** It is a later condensation of his vocabulary, not a sentence of his. *([2.4](lessons/02-04-from-confession-to-creed.md))*
- **Arguing from vocabulary instead of practice.** Theophilus uses *trias* before anyone else, and his three are God, his Word and his Wisdom. The word is there and the doctrine is not. *([2.4](lessons/02-04-from-confession-to-creed.md))*
- **Quoting the wrong creed.** The creed recited on Sunday is the creed of 381, which differs in its clauses and drops the anathemas. *([3.1](lessons/03-01-nicaea.md))*
- **Treating a change of vocabulary as a change of mind, or as no change at all.** The test for a word is what it denies; the burden is to show the old denials are kept, not to assume it either way. *([3.2](lessons/03-02-one-ousia-three-hypostaseis.md))*

## Conventions

- **One card per course**, covering every lesson. The linter checks that every lesson file is cited somewhere on this card.
- **Intuition first**, same as the lessons: a plain-English line before any formula or Latin.
- **Headings are anchors.** Renaming a `###` breaks inbound lesson links; the linter catches it, but prefer not to rename. Where a definition and a pitfall theme share a noun, the pitfall theme is renamed, not the definition.
- **No prose dollar signs** — write "10 dollars", not the symbol (see CLAUDE.md).
- **Boss problems are reserved.** Entries touching boss material stay at outline level: the texts and the positions are here, the worked reading is not.
- Length is not capped the way a lesson's is: this is a lookup surface, not a read-through.

### Citing the tradition

| Kind of text | How this course cites it | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Creed | council and year, then the clause | the creed of 381, on the Spirit |
| Conciliar definition | council and document name; a section number **only when verified** | Lateran IV's *Firmiter* |
| Catechism | paragraph, paraphrased rather than quoted at length | CCC 236 |
| *Summa* | part, question, article, reply | ST I q.27 a.1 ad 2 |
| Fathers | work, book, chapter, in a public-domain translation | *De Trinitate* V, ch. 5 |
| Scripture | book, chapter, verse, Douay-Rheims (Challoner) | John 15:26 |
| Modern theologians | paraphrase, cited by work and year | Rahner, *The Trinity* (1967) |

**And one refusal: this course uses no Denzinger numbers.** A DH number points into
one edition of one handbook, and editions renumber; a wrong one looks authoritative
and cannot be caught by anyone who trusts you. Names do not rot — *Firmiter* is
*Firmiter* in every edition. The same rule governs section numbers generally: where
a lesson could not verify a number, it names the document without one.

**Hedges this card carries, and will not quietly drop.**

- The attribution of "no opposition of relation" to **Anselm** is traditional, not established; the phrase was a scholastic commonplace before Florence (Ockham quotes it in *Summa logicae* III-4).
- *Lex orandi, lex credendi* is **usually attributed** to Prosper of Aquitaine's *Indiculus*; no Latin is printed.
- Which **theological note** a manual gives impassibility depends on whether it reads the claim as immutability restated or as a conclusion drawn from it; check your own manual.
- Florence's **decree for the Jacobites** is given in the lessons' own translation of the Latin, with no section number, because only a copyrighted English was reachable.
- Boff's "the Trinity is our social programme" is traced to **Nikolai Fedorov** by an attribution the lessons did not verify.
- **Sabellius's** doctrine is named; his biography is hedged, since what we know comes from later and hostile reporters.
- The **de Regnon paradigm** is stated as it circulates; de Régnon's own French text was not checked.
