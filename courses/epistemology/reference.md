### JTB analysis

**The tripartite analysis: knowledge is justified true belief.** Assumed from
[philosophical-method 3.1](../philosophical-method/lessons/03-01-conceptual-analysis.md); its card
entry is [there](../philosophical-method/reference.md#gettier-case).

$$S \text{ knows } p \iff p \text{ is true},\ S \text{ believes } p,\ S \text{ is justified in believing } p$$

- **Gettier (1963) showed the three conditions are not sufficient.** The course does not re-run his
  two cases; it starts from the diagnosis ([Gettier case](#gettier-case)).
- **Every Module 1 repair keeps truth and belief and changes or adds a condition.**

*Lessons:* [1.1](lessons/01-01-repairing-jtb.md)

### Gettier case

**A justified true belief that is true by luck: what makes it true is disconnected from what justifies
it.** The course uses the name for the lucky-truth structure, not for Gettier's own inferential
construction: [fake barns](#fake-barn-case) have no inference at all. Gettier's originals and the
analyse-break-repair loop: [philosophical-method 3.1](../philosophical-method/reference.md#gettier-case).

- **Two sources of luck:** in the route (a false step, Gettier's own cases) or in the environment
  (fake barns). A condition that inspects reasoning cannot see environmental luck.
- **Pritchard's secondary value problem** asks why knowledge beats a Gettiered JTB ([value problem](#value-problem)).

*Lessons:* [1.1](lessons/01-01-repairing-jtb.md), [1.2](lessons/01-02-sensitivity-and-safety.md)

### No false lemmas

**Knowledge is JTB reached without passing through a false step.** Michael Clark, "Knowledge and
Grounds: A Comment on Mr. Gettier's Paper" (*Analysis*, 1963): a known belief must be *fully grounded*.

> **NFL.** S knows $p$ iff (1) $p$ is true, (2) S believes $p$, (3) S is justified in believing $p$,
> and (4) S's belief that $p$ is not inferred from, or based on, any falsehood.

- **Handles Gettier's own cases.**
- **Not sufficient, luck with no lemma:** [fake barns](#fake-barn-case) satisfy 1-4 (perceptual, no
  inference) and are judged not knowledge.
- **Not sufficient, lemma skipped:** Feldman, "An Alleged Defect in Gettier Counterexamples" (1974):
  the subject reasons from what his evidence *says* (true) straight to the lucky conclusion. NFL must
  then ban a falsehood the belief "essentially depends on".
- **Not necessary, harmless falsehoods:** widen "based on" and ordinary knowledge inferred from a
  slightly false premise with a wide margin is ruled out. The NFL defender's reply (the belief "really"
  rests on a nearby truth) shifts the condition from the actual route to truths that *could* have served.
- **Against the recipe:** to catch a perceptual misidentification, NFL must count implicit
  identifications as lemmas; that does nothing for fake barns, where Henry's identification is true.

*Lessons:* [1.1](lessons/01-01-repairing-jtb.md)

### Defeasibility analysis

**Knowledge is justification the whole truth would not overturn.** Keith Lehrer and Thomas Paxson,
"Knowledge: Undefeated Justified True Belief" (*Journal of Philosophy*, 1969), developed further by
Peter Klein.

> **DEF.** S knows $p$ iff (1)-(3) of JTB hold and (4) there is no true proposition $d$ such that, if
> $d$ were added to S's evidence, S would no longer be justified in believing $p$.

Such a $d$ is a **defeater**.

- **Handles fake barns** ($d$ = most barn-looking things here are facades) **and Gettier's cases.**
- **Breaks on** [misleading defeaters](#misleading-defeater) (Tom Grabit); the repair counts only
  genuine defeaters.
- **DEF quantifies over every truth,** discoverable by S or not: it is not about what S could have
  found out.

*Lessons:* [1.1](lessons/01-01-repairing-jtb.md)

### Misleading defeater

**A true proposition that defeats only by supporting a falsehood, and whose force is undone by a
further truth.**

- **Tom Grabit** (Lehrer and Paxson 1969): you watch Tom, whom you know well, take a book from the
  library. His mother, unknown to you, says Tom was miles away and his twin John was in the library.
  She is deluded; there is no John. Intuitively you know. *Mrs Grabit said Tom's twin was in the
  library* is true and would undercut your justification, so simple DEF says you don't.
- **Revised DEF** counts only **genuine** defeaters: a misleading one defeats only by supporting a
  falsehood (John exists), and adding a further truth (she is deluded) restores the justification.
- **Pressure point:** can "defeats only by supporting a falsehood" be stated without already using
  knowledge, or the right connection to truth? Each formulation has met new cases.

*Lessons:* [1.1](lessons/01-01-repairing-jtb.md)

### Inescapability argument

**As long as there is a gap between an analysis's conditions and the truth, luck fits in the gap; close
the gap and you demand infallibility.** Linda Zagzebski, "The Inescapability of Gettier Problems"
(*Philosophical Quarterly*, 1994). Let X be whatever an analysis adds to true belief.

*The recipe:* (1) take a case where S's belief satisfies X but is false through ordinary bad luck;
(2) add a stroke of good luck that makes it true without changing anything X looks at.

1. **P1.** For any analysis "knowledge = true belief + X", either X entails truth or it doesn't.
2. **P2.** If X does not entail truth, a belief can satisfy X and be false (step 1 is available).
3. **P3.** If step 1 is available, so is step 2: some lucky fact makes the belief true without altering what X inspects.
4. **P4.** The result satisfies X and truth and is not knowledge.
5. **P5.** If X entails truth, the truth condition is redundant and X is an infallibility condition.
6. **C.** Every such analysis is refuted by a recipe case or is infallibilist.

- **Weakest premise: P3.** Conditions about the *connection* between belief and truth ([safety](#safety);
  success from ability, [2.5](lessons/02-05-virtue-epistemology.md)) are built so a merely lucky truth
  cannot satisfy them; then step 2 changes something X looks at. Zagzebski: such a condition either
  still leaves room for luck or quietly entails truth.
- **Three exits:** accept infallibilism (conclusive evidence: skeptical cost; or a factive state such as
  *seeing that p*: no reductive analysis), deny P3 (1.2, 2.5), stop analysing ([knowledge first](#knowledge-first)).
- **The conclusion is a dilemma,** not "knowledge is unanalysable". Infallibilism is about the relation
  between X and truth, not about feeling certain.

*Lessons:* [1.1](lessons/01-01-repairing-jtb.md), [1.2](lessons/01-02-sensitivity-and-safety.md)

### Sensitivity

**Your belief would notice if it were false.** Robert Nozick, *Philosophical Explanations* (1981).
With $B_M p$ for "S believes $p$ by method $M$" and $\Box\!\!\rightarrow$ the counterfactual:

$$\neg p \;\Box\!\!\rightarrow\; \neg B_M p$$

In the nearest worlds where $p$ is false (and S uses $M$), S does not believe $p$, however far away
those worlds are. One of the four conditions of the [tracking account](#tracking-account).

- **Fails, though we seem to know:** "I am not a BIV" (a BIV would believe it too) and the
  [garbage chute](#garbage-chute-case). Both are insensitive and safe.
- **Over-generates:** [Kripke's red barn](#red-barn-objection).
- **Needs** [method-relativity](#method-relativity) (Nozick's grandmother).
- **Lottery:** insensitive (in the nearest winning world you still believe you lost), matching the
  intuition ([lottery belief](#lottery-belief)).
- **Fake barns:** insensitive (had it been a facade, still "barn"): no knowledge.
- **Its failure on the BIV belief drives closure denial** ([3.2](lessons/03-02-denying-closure.md)).
- **Externalist:** it assesses the world around the belief, not the believer's evidence.

*Lessons:* [1.2](lessons/01-02-sensitivity-and-safety.md), [3.2](lessons/03-02-denying-closure.md)

### Safety

**You couldn't easily have believed $p$ falsely.** Ernest Sosa, "How to Defeat Opposition to Moore"
(1999); Timothy Williamson, *Knowledge and Its Limits* (2000); Duncan Pritchard, *Epistemic Luck*
(2005). With $C$ the set of worlds close to the actual world $@$:

$$\forall w \in C:\ B_M p \text{ at } w \to p \text{ at } w$$

- **Strong (Sosa, Williamson):** *all* close worlds. **Weak (Pritchard):** *nearly all*, and all of the very closest.
- **Not sensitivity rewritten.** Safety is roughly $B_M p \;\Box\!\!\rightarrow\; p$, the contrapositive of
  sensitivity, and counterfactuals do not contrapose: one is settled by the nearest $B_M p$-worlds, the
  other by the nearest $\neg p$-worlds.
- **Anti-luck argument:** (1) knowledge excludes luck in the truth of the belief; (2) a belief is luckily
  true iff, formed the same way, it is false in close worlds; (3) so a known belief is safe.
  **Weakest: premise 2, the word *close*.** Closeness is similarity, not probability; unless it can be
  fixed without first deciding which beliefs are knowledge, the condition is circular.
- **Against Zagzebski:** the actual world is among the close worlds, so safety plus belief entails
  truth and the recipe has no foothold.
- **Verdicts:** BIV belief and garbage chute safe (knowledge); fake barns unsafe; the lottery belief
  unsafe on Pritchard's reading (the winning world is close) while a newspaper report is safe (misprints
  need more to go differently).
- **Margin for error** is safety applied to discrimination ([KK principle](#kk-principle)).
- **In 2.5:** Pritchard pairs safety with an ability condition, because Temp shows safety without
  ability ([anti-luck virtue epistemology](#anti-luck-virtue-epistemology)).
- **In 3.2-3.3:** safety lets the Moorean claim to know the skeptical hypothesis false; it does not
  conflict with closure the way sensitivity does.

*Lessons:* [1.2](lessons/01-02-sensitivity-and-safety.md), [1.3](lessons/01-03-why-knowledge.md), [2.5](lessons/02-05-virtue-epistemology.md), [3.2](lessons/03-02-denying-closure.md)

### Method-relativity

**Hold fixed the method actually used when evaluating sensitivity or safety.** Nozick's grandmother
sees her grandson is well; had he been ill, the family would have told her he was well. Plain
sensitivity: insensitive, no knowledge (wrong verdict). Relativized: in the nearest world where he is
ill *and she sees him*, she sees he is ill. Sensitive.

- **The price:** a method can be described at many grains ("looking", "looking at a relative in good
  light", "visual perception") and verdicts flip with the description: the
  [generality problem](#generality-problem) of 2.4.

*Lessons:* [1.2](lessons/01-02-sensitivity-and-safety.md), [2.4](lessons/02-04-reliabilism.md)

### Lottery belief

**"My ticket lost", believed on the odds alone, before the result: highly probable and, intuitively,
not known.**

$$\Pr(\text{loses}) = 1 - \tfrac{1}{N}$$

- **Sensitivity:** insensitive (in the nearest winning world you still believe you lost).
- **Safety (Pritchard):** unsafe: the winning world is close, since only a few balls need fall
  differently.
- **Newspaper contrast:** a report with a misprint rate worse than $1/N$ intuitively gives knowledge,
  and safety agrees, since a misprint needs more to go differently.
- **The strain:** the verdict depends on the closeness ordering, chosen to fit the verdict. Is the
  world where your parked car was stolen close? If so, you don't know where your car is.
- **Probability is not safety:** odds of a million to one are overwhelming evidence in the odds-form
  sense and still fail safety (the "naked statistics" puzzle in the law of evidence). Returns with
  credences as the [lottery paradox](#lottery-paradox).

*Lessons:* [1.2](lessons/01-02-sensitivity-and-safety.md), [5.1](lessons/05-01-credences-and-probabilism.md)

### Garbage chute case

**Sosa (1999):** you drop a trash bag down your building's chute and believe it is now in the
basement. Had it snagged, you would still believe it reached the basement: **insensitive**. Snagging
does not happen in close worlds: **safe**. Counted as knowledge, so it is the case where the two
conditions come apart, with the same profile as "I am not a BIV".

*Lessons:* [1.2](lessons/01-02-sensitivity-and-safety.md)

### Red barn objection

**Sensitivity can say you know a conjunction-like claim but not its obvious consequence.** Saul
Kripke, "Nozick on Knowledge" (*Philosophical Troubles*, 2011). In fake barn county the one real
barn is red and no facade is red (1.2 makes them green; 3.2 says only "no facade is ever red").

- "There is a red barn": **sensitive** (had it not been a red barn, he'd face a non-red facade and not believe it).
- "There is a barn": **insensitive** (a facade would still produce "barn").
- So sensitivity says Henry knows there is a red barn but not that there is a barn: a failure of
  closure under conjunction elimination ([3.2](lessons/03-02-denying-closure.md)).

*Lessons:* [1.2](lessons/01-02-sensitivity-and-safety.md), [3.2](lessons/03-02-denying-closure.md)

### Fake barn case

**Henry drives through a county dotted with barn facades, convincing from the road, looks at the one
real barn and believes "that's a barn".** Published by Alvin Goldman, "Discrimination and Perceptual
Knowledge" (*Journal of Philosophy*, 1976), who credits Carl Ginet. True, perceptually justified,
uninferred; most judge it not knowledge. The luck is environmental.

- **NFL:** satisfied (no inference), so NFL wrongly counts it knowledge.
- **DEF:** defeated by *most barn-looking things here are facades*: no knowledge.
- **Sensitivity:** insensitive. **Safety:** unsafe. Both modal conditions were built to handle it.
- **Sosa (2.5):** bites the bullet: Henry's belief is apt, so animal knowledge, but no reflective
  knowledge. **Pritchard:** unsafe, so no knowledge. **Credit theory:** trouble; a credit theorist can
  relativize abilities to environments, which reopens the generality problem.
- **Red barn variant:** [red barn objection](#red-barn-objection).

*Lessons:* [1.1](lessons/01-01-repairing-jtb.md), [1.2](lessons/01-02-sensitivity-and-safety.md), [1.4](lessons/01-04-internalism-and-externalism.md), [2.5](lessons/02-05-virtue-epistemology.md)

### Value problem

**Why is knowledge worth more than mere true belief?** Plato's *Meno* (97a-98a): a man with true
opinion about the road to Larisa guides as well as one who knows; true opinions "run away" like the
statues of Daedalus unless tied down by working out the reason (read historically in
[ancient-medieval-philosophy 2.2](../ancient-medieval-philosophy/lessons/02-02-recollection-the-line-and-the-cave.md)).
Pritchard's three versions:

1. **Primary:** why is knowledge more valuable than mere true belief?
2. **Secondary:** why more valuable than *any* proper part of it, such as a Gettiered JTB?
3. **Tertiary:** why valuable in *kind*, not just degree, so the line between knowing and nearly
   knowing marks a jump in value?

- **Answers on the map:** instrumental (more true beliefs to come: Goldman and Olsson); stability
  (Plato's tether; Williamson's burglar who ransacks all night because he *knew* the diamond was
  there); final value of achievement (Greco, Sosa, Riggs; objection: testimony, Lackey); value lies in
  understanding (Kvanvig); question misposed ([knowledge first](#knowledge-first)).
- **The sharpest form** is the [swamping problem](#swamping-problem).

*Lessons:* [1.3](lessons/01-03-why-knowledge.md), [2.5](lessons/02-05-virtue-epistemology.md)

### Swamping problem

**If reliability matters only because it yields truth, then once the belief is true, reliability adds
nothing.** Zagzebski (2003): a good cup from a reliable coffee machine is no better than an equally
good cup from an unreliable one. Similar worries in Jones (1997) and Swinburne (1999); Kvanvig (2003)
made it central under this name.

1. **P1 (truth monism).** The only thing of fundamental epistemic value is true belief.
2. **P2.** The reliability (or justification) of a belief-forming process is epistemically valuable only as a means to true belief.
3. **P3 (swamping principle).** If X is valuable only as a means to Y, a thing that already has Y gains no value from also having X.
4. **C.** A reliably formed true belief is no more valuable than an equally true belief formed unreliably; if knowledge is reliably formed true belief, it is no more valuable than true belief.

| Reply | Premise | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| Credit and achievement (Greco, Sosa, Riggs; [2.5](lessons/02-05-virtue-epistemology.md)) | denies P1: success from ability has final value | Lackey: testimonial knowledge owes little to the hearer |
| Conditional probability (Goldman and Olsson 2009) | limits P2's "only": a reliable process you still have raises the probability your *future* beliefs are true | instrumental and contingent: vanishes if the process will never be used again |
| Understanding (Kvanvig) | accepts C | the distinctive value belongs to understanding, not knowledge |
| Knowledge first | rejects the decomposition the question presupposes | no reductive analysis |

- **Target:** any account on which the extra ingredient is valuable only as a means to truth, so
  process reliabilism ([2.4](lessons/02-04-reliabilism.md)) above all. It does not show knowledge has
  no extra value.
- **Weakest: P3.** A reliable machine is worth more *now* because of tomorrow's cups; critics reply the
  surplus then attaches to the process, not the belief.

*Lessons:* [1.3](lessons/01-03-why-knowledge.md), [2.4](lessons/02-04-reliabilism.md), [2.5](lessons/02-05-virtue-epistemology.md)

### Knowledge first

**Stop analysing knowledge and use it to explain everything else.** Williamson, *Knowledge and Its
Limits* (2000). The repair cycle of 1.1 is read as a symptom: knowledge is not belief plus conditions.

1. **Knowledge is a mental state,** the most general **factive** one (being in it entails its content
   is true: seeing that, remembering that, knowing that; believing is not factive).
2. **Necessary conditions, no analysis.** Knowledge entails truth and belief, and safety may
   characterize it, but no list of conditions builds it (red entails coloured; "coloured and X" does
   not define red).
3. **Explains other notions:** [E = K](#e-equals-k), the [knowledge norm of assertion](#knowledge-norm-of-assertion), justification.

- **Its answer to the value problem:** the question "what does the add-on add?" presupposes the
  decomposition the view denies. Also explanatory robustness (the burglar).
- **Against KK** via margins for error ([KK principle](#kk-principle)).
- **Takes Zagzebski's third exit** (and her infallibilist horn in the factive form).

*Lessons:* [1.3](lessons/01-03-why-knowledge.md), [5.3](lessons/05-03-conditionalization.md)

### E equals K

**Your evidence is exactly what you know.** Williamson (2000).

$$E = K$$

- **Evidence is factive, not infallible:** you can be wrong about what your evidence is. Someone
  fooled by an illusion lacks evidence she thinks she has.
- **Not always accessible:** an objector says you cannot always tell what evidence you have; the
  knowledge-first theorist accepts it (contrast [access internalism](#access-internalism)).
- **In Module 5:** if E = K, [conditionalization](#strict-conditionalization) updates on what you come
  to know.

*Lessons:* [1.3](lessons/01-03-why-knowledge.md), [1.4](lessons/01-04-internalism-and-externalism.md), [5.3](lessons/05-03-conditionalization.md)

### Knowledge norm of assertion

**Assert $p$ only if you know $p$.** Williamson (2000). Explains why "It's raining, but I don't know
that it is" sounds absurd though it could be true. Returns as the
[knowledge-action principle](#knowledge-action-principle) behind subject-sensitive invariantism.

*Lessons:* [1.3](lessons/01-03-why-knowledge.md), [3.4](lessons/03-04-contextualism-and-its-rivals.md)

### KK principle

**If you know $p$, you know that you know $p$:** $Kp \to KKp$, with $K$ for "the subject knows that".
Defended by Jaakko Hintikka, *Knowledge and Belief* (1962). Williamson's argument against it from the
[margin-for-error principle](#margin-for-error-principle):

1. **M.** If you know $p$ in case $\alpha$, then $p$ is true in every case close to $\alpha$.
2. If you know that you know $p$ in $\alpha$, then by M (applied to "I know $p$") you know $p$ in every case close to $\alpha$.
3. So $KKp$ needs $p$ across a margin of *two* steps from $\alpha$; $Kp$ needs one.
4. **C.** Where $p$ holds one step out but fails two steps out, you know $p$ without knowing that you know it.

- **Weakest: premise 2.** A KK defender says the margin for "I know $p$" need not equal the margin for
  $p$: the second-order belief need not come from the same discriminating capacity.
- **Anti-luminosity sibling:** a woman feels freezing at dawn and warms so slowly she feels hot by
  noon; a margin principle applied moment by moment shows even "I feel cold" is not always knowable
  when it holds.
- **Rejecting KK** does not mean you never know that you know: near the margin they come apart; far
  from it both hold. Arithmetic: [margin-for-error arithmetic](#margin-for-error-arithmetic).

*Lessons:* [1.3](lessons/01-03-why-knowledge.md)

### Margin for error principle

**Knowledge cannot be a near miss: if you know $p$ in a case, $p$ is true in every close case within
your discrimination margin.** Safety applied to discrimination. Toy model (true quantity $x$, margin
$m$, claim $L > y$):

$$K(L>y) \iff x - m > y$$

$$KK(L>y) \iff x - 2m > y$$

The model assumes the same margin governs both levels and that the subject cannot read her own
estimate more finely than the world; a KK defender attacks exactly those assumptions.

*Lessons:* [1.3](lessons/01-03-why-knowledge.md)

### Propositional and doxastic justification

**Having good reasons for $p$ versus believing $p$ because of them.**

- **Propositional:** S has justification for $p$ iff S has adequate reasons for $p$, whether or not S believes $p$.
- **Doxastic:** S's *belief* that $p$ is justified iff S believes $p$ *on the basis of* adequate reasons (the basing relation).

The detective whose clues point to the butler but who believes it because she dislikes him has the
first and lacks the second.

- **The internalism dispute is mainly about propositional justification** (what makes a reason good for you).
- **Klein's infinitism runs on it** ([finite minds objection](#finite-minds-objection)): propositional
  justification needs only the endless chain's existence; doxastic justification comes in degrees.

*Lessons:* [1.4](lessons/01-04-internalism-and-externalism.md), [2.1](lessons/02-01-the-regress-problem.md)

### Access internalism

**Every factor contributing to the justification of S's belief is accessible to S by reflection
alone.** You could, by thinking, tell what your justifiers are.

- **Not equivalent to [mentalism](#mentalism):** an unidentifiable cue in experience, or a memory not
  now recalled, counts for mentalism and not for access internalism.
- **Truetemp, Norman:** not justified, unless "it just seems so to me" is itself an accessible reason
  (if it is, as on [phenomenal conservatism](#phenomenal-conservatism), Truetemp stops being a case for internalism).
- **Problem of stored beliefs:** you are not now thinking about most of what you justifiably believe,
  so what does reflection access?
- **Sosa's reflective knowledge** (2.5) is a concession to access intuitions.
- **Externalism** denies it: some justifier lies outside the mind or outside reflective reach
  ([process reliabilism](#process-reliabilism) is the standard-bearer). Nearly everyone is an
  externalist about *knowledge* (truth is external); the dispute is about *justification*.

*Lessons:* [1.4](lessons/01-04-internalism-and-externalism.md), [2.5](lessons/02-05-virtue-epistemology.md)

### Mentalism

**Fix the mind and you fix the justification.** Earl Conee and Richard Feldman, "Internalism
Defended" (2001): justification supervenes on the subject's mental states; mental twins are
justificational twins.

- **Counts inaccessible mental states** (unlike [access internalism](#access-internalism)).
- **Strains on** [forgotten evidence](#problem-of-forgotten-evidence): replies bite the bullet (a
  confident memory is itself evidence) or deny the stipulation (some trace of the bad source survives).

*Lessons:* [1.4](lessons/01-04-internalism-and-externalism.md)

### New evil demon problem

**Your demon-world counterpart has your experiences, memories and reasoning and is as justified as
you, though her perception is wholly unreliable; so reliability is not necessary for justification.**
Keith Lehrer and Stewart Cohen (1983); Cohen (1984).

1. V, the demon's victim, forms perceptual beliefs by unreliable processes.
2. V's experiences, memories and reasoning are the same as yours, and she forms beliefs from them the same way.
3. **Parity:** subjects alike in experiences, memories and reasoning, forming beliefs the same way, are equally justified.
4. Your perceptual beliefs are justified.
5. So V's perceptual beliefs are justified (2-4).
6. **C.** Reliability is not necessary for justification (1, 5).

- **Weakest: premise 3.** Read strictly it *is* mentalism for these states, so the reliabilist says it
  begs the question; the internalist says it rests on the widely shared verdict, not a theory.
- **Normal-worlds reliabilism** (Goldman 1986): reliability assessed in normal worlds, where perception
  is reliable: V justified ([normal-worlds reliabilism](#normal-worlds-reliabilism)).
- **Strong and weak justification** (Goldman, "Strong and Weak Justification", 1988): V is weakly
  (blamelessly) but not strongly (reliably) justified. Concedes an internalist notion exists; possibly a
  verbal dispute.
- **Reappears** as a skeptical hypothesis (3.1) and as the counterinductivist in a counterinductive
  world ([externalist answer to induction](#externalist-answer-to-induction), 4.3).

*Lessons:* [1.4](lessons/01-04-internalism-and-externalism.md), [2.4](lessons/02-04-reliabilism.md), [4.3](lessons/04-03-answering-hume.md)

### Norman the clairvoyant

**A completely reliable clairvoyant with no evidence for or against such a power, or his having it,
who finds himself believing the President is in New York City.** Laurence BonJour, "Externalist
Theories of Empirical Knowledge" (1980). Against the *sufficiency* of reliability: internalists say he
believes irresponsibly.

- **No evidence either way is the stipulation.** With counterevidence, many reliabilists would say a
  defeater is present.
- **Goldman's answer:** the [non-undermining clause](#non-undermining-clause).
- **Case grid (1.4):** reliable process, no internal reasons: reliabilist justified, internalist not.

*Lessons:* [1.4](lessons/01-04-internalism-and-externalism.md), [2.4](lessons/02-04-reliabilism.md)

### Truetemp case

**A surgeon secretly implants a device (a "tempucomp") that makes Mr Truetemp think accurate thoughts
about the temperature, which he accepts without reflecting on their source.** Keith Lehrer, *Theory
of Knowledge* (1990). The technological Norman: reliable, so justified for the process reliabilist;
nothing accessible favours the belief, so not justified for the access internalist or mentalist
(unless a bare seeming counts as a reason).

*Lessons:* [1.4](lessons/01-04-internalism-and-externalism.md), [2.4](lessons/02-04-reliabilism.md)

### Problem of forgotten evidence

**Two subjects hold the same belief with the same confidence; one learned it from an excellent source,
one from a notoriously bad one; both forgot the source.** Alvin Goldman, "Internalism Exposed" (1999),
with the problem of stored beliefs. Mentalism must rate them alike; intuitively the first is justified
and the second not. Reliabilism separates them by the causal chain.

*Lessons:* [1.4](lessons/01-04-internalism-and-externalism.md)

## Module 2 The structure of justification

Where justification stops: the regress and its three exits, then the externalist theories that change
the question from "what are your reasons?" to "how was the belief produced?".

| Position | Regress premise denied | Agrippan horn | Its burden | Standard objection |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| [Foundationalism](#classical-foundationalism) | 2 (no basic beliefs) | hypothesis: the chain stops | show the stopping points are not arbitrary | Sellarsian dilemma; too thin (classical) or too generous (modest) |
| [Coherentism](#coherentism) | 3, or the linear picture itself | reciprocity: mutual support | show fit connects the system to the world | isolation; alternative systems |
| [Infinitism](#infinitism) | 4 (no infinite chains) | infinite regress | show finite minds are served by infinite chains | finite minds; no starting point |
| Skepticism | none | all three, as failures | live with the conclusion | (the other three) |
| [Reliabilism](#process-reliabilism) | changes the question: basic beliefs justified by causal origin, not reasons | (a foundationalist structure) | fix the process type | generality; Norman; bootstrapping |

### Regress argument

**Follow any belief back through its reasons and the chain stops, loops or never ends; if each is
fatal, nothing is justified.** With S a subject and $p$ any proposition S believes:

1. **Inferential justification.** If S is justified in believing $p$ on the basis of a reason $r$, then S is justified in believing $r$.
2. **No basic beliefs.** Every justified belief is justified on the basis of some other belief.
3. **No circles.** No belief is among its own reasons, however far back you trace.
4. **No infinite chains.** No belief is justified by an infinite chain of reasons.
5. **C.** No belief is justified.

*Valid:* from a justified $p_0$, premise 2 gives a reason $p_1$, premise 1 makes it justified, and so
on; premise 3 makes the chain non-repeating, so it is infinite, which 4 forbids.

- **Each non-skeptical view is defined by the premise it denies** (table above): foundationalism 2,
  infinitism 4, coherentism 3 or the linear picture behind 1 and 3.
- **Positions mix:** a foundationalist may let coherence add support above the foundations.
- **Reliabilism** stops the regress at beliefs justified by causal origin, with no further belief.
- **Historical forms:** [Agrippa's trilemma](#agrippas-trilemma); Aristotle, *Posterior Analytics* I.3:
  demonstration bottoms out in first principles grasped without proof.

*Lessons:* [2.1](lessons/02-01-the-regress-problem.md), [2.2](lessons/02-02-foundationalism.md), [2.3](lessons/02-03-coherentism.md)

### Agrippas trilemma

**Any chain of reasons stops (hypothesis), loops (reciprocity) or never ends (infinite regress).** A
modern distillation of three of Sextus Empiricus's five Agrippan modes; the name "trilemma" is a modern
coinage. History in [ancient-medieval-philosophy 4.3](../ancient-medieval-philosophy/lessons/04-03-the-skeptics.md).
Foundationalism embraces hypothesis, coherentism reciprocity, infinitism the regress.

*Lessons:* [2.1](lessons/02-01-the-regress-problem.md), [2.2](lessons/02-02-foundationalism.md), [2.3](lessons/02-03-coherentism.md)

### Inferential justification

**A reason passes on only the justification it has.** Premise 1 of the regress argument, weak form:
if S is justified in believing $p$ on the basis of $r$, S is justified in believing $r$.

- **Stronger version** adds: S must also be justified in believing that $r$ makes $p$ probable. That
  starts a second regress at every link; many foundationalists and externalists reject it.
- **Reliabilism's recursive clause** is the externalist counterpart: conditionally reliable processes
  on justified inputs.

*Lessons:* [2.1](lessons/02-01-the-regress-problem.md)

### Infinitism

**Justification is an infinite, non-repeating chain of available reasons, and that is no defect.**
Peter Klein, "Human Knowledge and the Infinite Regress of Reasons" (*Philosophical Perspectives*,
1999). Two principles (paraphrased):

1. **Avoid circularity.** No belief may appear in the evidential ancestry of its own reasons (premise 3).
2. **Avoid arbitrariness.** If S has a justification for $x$, there is a reason $r_1$ *available* to S for $x$, a reason $r_2$ available for $r_1$, and so on without end.

- **Available** = objectively a good reason that S could call on if asked; S need not have thought it.
- **Objections:** [finite minds](#finite-minds-objection); the **no-starting-point objection** (Klein
  traces it to the Pyrrhonists): if every reason borrows its justification from one further back, where
  does justification come from? Klein: offering a reason *generates* a new kind of warrant. The
  foundationalist: then something about the reasons themselves makes them good, and that is not
  another reason.
- **Bergmann** (*Philosophical Studies*, 2007): Klein's infinitism about doxastic justification is a
  disguised arbitrary foundationalism (the first reason actually given had none behind it). Klein:
  warrant from origin plus a *distinct* reason-giving warrant that knowledge needs.

*Lessons:* [2.1](lessons/02-01-the-regress-problem.md)

### Finite minds objection

**Finite minds cannot hold infinitely many beliefs or traverse infinitely many inferences.** Aristotle
observed that a finite mind cannot traverse an infinite series; John Williams pressed it against modern
infinitism. Klein's reply runs on the [propositional/doxastic split](#propositional-and-doxastic-justification):

- **Propositional justification** is fully present if an endless path of available reasons *exists*; no traversal needed.
- **Doxastic justification** comes in degrees, grows as S supplies reasons, and is never complete.
  Knowledge needs "enough" reasons, set by context (which questions have actually been raised).

Pressure: whether a reason is "available" when S could find it only after research has no sharp answer.

*Lessons:* [2.1](lessons/02-01-the-regress-problem.md)

### Basic belief

**A belief justified non-inferentially: its justification does not come from inference from other
beliefs.** Not an unjustified belief, and not an arbitrary assumption (that is Agrippa's hypothesis
horn, which the foundationalist must show the base is not).

- **Negative versus positive dependence:** on modest views, other beliefs may *defeat* a basic belief
  (learning the lighting is red defeats "the tomato is red") but do not supply its support.
- **A reliabilist with basic beliefs from reliable non-inferential processes is a foundationalist too:**
  foundationalism is a claim about structure, not an internalist theory.

*Lessons:* [2.2](lessons/02-02-foundationalism.md)

### Classical foundationalism

**Basic beliefs must be error-proof; everything else is derived from them by deduction or strong
induction.** In practice the base is beliefs about your own present mental states ("I seem to see
something red") and self-evident truths of reason. Descartes is the paradigm (Meditation II: "I seem
to see light, hear a noise, and feel heat; this cannot be false").

Three grades of privilege, not to be run together:

- **Infallible:** believing it entails its truth.
- **Incorrigible:** no one else could be in a position to correct you.
- **Indubitable:** you cannot, on reflection, doubt it.

- **Too thin a base:** "there is a tomato" is not basic, not deducible from seemings, and induction from
  seemings needs premises about the world: slide toward external-world skepticism (Module 3).
- **Self-referential problem:** its criterion is neither self-evident nor derivable from what is, so by
  its own standard it should not be believed (Plantinga; its use in the evidentialist objection to
  theism belongs to [`philosophy-of-religion`](../philosophy-of-religion/syllabus.md) 5.1).
- **Answer to the Sellarsian dilemma:** deny P2 for direct **acquaintance** with one's own experience,
  an awareness that cannot misfire.

*Lessons:* [2.2](lessons/02-02-foundationalism.md)

### Modest foundationalism

**Keep the structure, lower the bar: basic beliefs are fallible and prima facie justified, justified
unless defeated.** Ordinary perceptual, memory and simple a priori beliefs can be basic. Negative
dependence on other beliefs (defeat) allowed; positive dependence not.

- **Not coherentism:** a coherentist says other beliefs supply positive support; here they can only take it away.
- **Its internalist filling:** [phenomenal conservatism](#phenomenal-conservatism); its externalist
  filling: reliable processes.
- **Critics of coherentism's escape from the witness results** say individual credibility then does the
  justifying: modest foundationalism in coherentist vocabulary.

*Lessons:* [2.2](lessons/02-02-foundationalism.md), [2.3](lessons/02-03-coherentism.md)

### Myth of the given

**Nothing given can both justify a belief and end the regress.** Wilfrid Sellars, "Empiricism and the
Philosophy of Mind" (1956), attacked the idea of a state with epistemic standing independent of other
cognitive states that also supports further knowledge. Laurence BonJour sharpened it ("Can Empirical
Knowledge Have a Foundation?", 1978; *The Structure of Empirical Knowledge*, 1985, ch. 4) into the
**Sellarsian dilemma**:

1. **P1.** If the given has no propositional content, it stands in no logical or evidential relation to a belief, so cannot justify one.
2. **P2.** If it has propositional content, it can be accurate or inaccurate, so it needs justification itself.
3. **P3.** If a state needs justification, a belief justified by it inherits a further demand, so is not basic.
4. **P4.** The given has propositional content or it does not.
5. **C.** Nothing given can both justify a belief and end the regress; no belief is basic.

- **Weakest: P2.** It conflates being assessable for *accuracy* with being assessable for
  *justification*. The Sellarsian: then why should an unjustified seeming make a belief justified?
- **Exits:** acquaintance (classical); seemings that need no justification ([phenomenal
  conservatism](#phenomenal-conservatism) denies P2); coherentism takes the Sellarsian side.

*Lessons:* [2.2](lessons/02-02-foundationalism.md)

### Phenomenal conservatism

**Things seeming a certain way to you is a defeasible reason to believe they are that way.** Michael
Huemer (*Skepticism and the Veil of Perception*, 2001; "Compassionate Phenomenal Conservatism", 2007).

> **PC.** If it seems to S that P, then, in the absence of defeaters, S thereby has at least some
> justification for believing that P.

- **A seeming** has propositional content but is not a belief (the stick in water seems bent while you
  believe it straight). Perceptual, memory and intellectual seemings, so PC also explains why
  case-intuitions are evidence ([philosophical-method 3.4](../philosophical-method/lessons/03-04-intuitions-and-reflective-equilibrium.md)).
- **Denies P2 of the Sellarsian dilemma:** seemings have content but do not need justification.
- **Self-defeat argument:** (S1) all your beliefs, including epistemological ones, are formed on how
  things seem; (S2) a belief formed on a basis conferring no justification is unjustified; (S3) so if
  seemings never justified, the belief that they never justify would be unjustified. **Weakest: S1** (an
  externalist says many beliefs are based on reliable processes, not seemings as reasons); and S3
  shows only that *some* seemings justify, not universal PC.
- **Strains on** [cognitive penetration](#cognitive-penetration).
- **Perceptual special case:** Pryor's [dogmatism](#dogmatism) (3.3). If bare seemings count as
  reasons, Truetemp may be justified after all.

*Lessons:* [2.2](lessons/02-02-foundationalism.md), [3.3](lessons/03-03-moore-and-the-dogmatist.md)

### Cognitive penetration

**An unjustified attitude shapes how things look, and PC then counts the look as justification.**
Susanna Siegel's case: Jill fears, without good reason, that Jack is angry; her fear makes his face
*look* angry to her, and she has no idea. PC: seeming yes, no possessed defeater, so some
justification. Many find that wrong: the fear has laundered itself through experience. Peter Markie
(2005) pressed the parallel worry about seemings from wishful thinking.

| Reply | Cost |
|---|---|
| Bite the bullet: from Jill's perspective the seeming is all she has (roughly Huemer's line) | justification cut loose from whether the belief's origin was any good |
| Restrict PC to seemings with a proper causal history | proper history is not accessible to Jill: an externalist condition, drift toward reliabilism |

*Lessons:* [2.2](lessons/02-02-foundationalism.md)

### Coherentism

**No belief is basic; a belief is justified by membership in a sufficiently coherent system.**
Justification is **holistic**, not linear ([linear and holistic justification](#linear-and-holistic-justification)):
Neurath's sailors rebuilding the ship at sea, the stones of an arch. BonJour's version (*The Structure
of Empirical Knowledge*, 1985). S's empirical belief that $p$ is justified iff:

1. **(C1)** $p$ belongs to S's system of beliefs;
2. **(C2)** that system is coherent to a high degree, on the five dimensions of [coherence](#coherence);
3. **(C3)** the system meets the [observation requirement](#observation-requirement).

- **BonJour was an access internalist:** S must be able, in principle, to grasp that the system coheres.
  He abandoned coherentism for foundationalism in "The Dialectic of Foundationalism and Coherentism" (1999).
- **Positive versus negative** (John Pollock's labels, *Contemporary Theories of Knowledge*, 1986):
  positive needs support from fit; negative holds a belief innocent until a conflict convicts it
  (Harman's conservatism: keep what you believe unless you have reason to change it).
- **Lehrer's local version** (*Theory of Knowledge*, 1990): justified in accepting $p$ when, relative to
  your acceptance system, $p$ beats or neutralizes every competitor.
- **Objections:** [isolation](#isolation-objection), [alternative systems](#alternative-systems-objection),
  the [witness results](#coherence-impossibility-results). Hard case: a closed-loop conspiracy system
  that absorbs every anomaly seems to meet C1-C3; the coherentist says ad hoc absorption lowers
  coherence, the critic that the unstated weighting is then doing the work.
- **Not circular reasoning:** it denies the linear picture on which "circle" makes sense.

*Lessons:* [2.3](lessons/02-03-coherentism.md)

### Linear and holistic justification

**Linear:** a belief is justified by a chain of reasons, each by the next (the regress argument's
assumption). **Holistic:** a system is justified in the first instance, and a belief derivatively, by
membership in it. How coherentism takes the circle horn without arguing in a circle: mutual support,
not a loop of transmission.

*Lessons:* [2.1](lessons/02-01-the-regress-problem.md), [2.3](lessons/02-03-coherentism.md)

### Coherence

**More than consistency: positive support among the beliefs.** BonJour's five dimensions (1985):

1. **Logical consistency** (a necessary condition).
2. **Probabilistic consistency:** not believing both $p$ and that $p$ is highly improbable.
3. **Inferential connections:** rises with the number and strength of support relations, explanatory above all.
4. **Integration:** falls as the system splits into unconnected subsystems.
5. **Anomalies:** falls with every unexplained anomaly.

No agreed weighting of the five, then or since. A consistent heap of unrelated beliefs (the moon is
cheese; Tuesday follows Monday) is not coherent.

*Lessons:* [2.3](lessons/02-03-coherentism.md)

### Observation requirement

**The system must include the belief that a reasonable variety of S's cognitively spontaneous beliefs
(ones that strike S unbidden, as perceptual beliefs do) are likely to be true.** BonJour's answer to
isolation: the system is made to *take* worldly input as evidence, so ignoring what you keep seeing
creates anomalies. Critics: it is only a *belief* about input, and a belief can be false; a closed
conspiracy system can satisfy it as stated.

*Lessons:* [2.3](lessons/02-03-coherentism.md)

### Isolation objection

**Coherence is a relation among beliefs only, so it cannot by itself connect a system to the world**
(a well-crafted novel coheres).

1. **P1.** Coherence is a relation among beliefs only.
2. **P2.** A relation among beliefs only is indifferent to what the world is like.
3. **P3.** Justification must make a belief likely to be true of the world.
4. **C.** Coherence is not sufficient for justification.

The coherentist's target is P2, via the [observation requirement](#observation-requirement). It is
"garbage in, garbage out" applied to reflective equilibrium in philosophical-method 3.4.

*Lessons:* [2.3](lessons/02-03-coherentism.md)

### Alternative systems objection

**For any coherent system there are equally coherent rivals incompatible with it, so coherence cannot
pick out the one to believe.** The isolation objection's twin; the observation requirement is again the
reply (rival systems would have to absorb your actual spontaneous inputs).

*Lessons:* [2.3](lessons/02-03-coherentism.md)

### Coherence impossibility results

**Coherence amplifies credibility already there; it cannot create it, and no coherence measure tracks
truth in all cases.**

- **The agreeing witnesses.** C. I. Lewis (*An Analysis of Knowledge and Valuation*, 1946): agreement
  among independent witnesses convinces only if each has *some* credibility; BonJour (1985) denied
  antecedent credibility was needed. With conditionally independent reports, agreement multiplies the
  witnesses' likelihood ratios ([witness odds formula](#witness-odds-formula)); at $L_1 = L_2 = 1$ the
  posterior equals the prior. Erik Olsson (2002; *Against Coherence*, 2005): Lewis was right.
- **Impossibility.** Luc Bovens and Stephan Hartmann (*Bayesian Epistemology*, 2003) and Olsson (2005):
  even granting independence and some individual credibility, no measure of coherence is
  truth-conducive even ceteris paribus.
- **Weakest assumption:** that coherentism needs coherence to raise probability *by itself*, measured
  by one number. A coherentist can say C3 supplies each input's $L_i > 1$ from inside the system; the
  critic replies that individual credibility is then doing the justifying.

*Lessons:* [2.3](lessons/02-03-coherentism.md)

### Process reliabilism

**A belief is justified by the reliability of the process that produced it.** Alvin Goldman, "What Is
Justified Belief?" (1979). A process is typed by its inputs and how it handles them; its reliability is
the proportion of true beliefs it yields; $\theta$ is a high threshold Goldman does not fix.

1. **Base clause (belief-independent processes).** A belief from a process whose inputs are not beliefs, with reliability at least $\theta$, is justified.
2. **Recursive clause (belief-dependent processes).** A belief from a **conditionally reliable** process (mostly true outputs when its inputs are true) operating on justified input beliefs is justified.
3. **[Non-undermining clause](#non-undermining-clause).** No reliable or conditionally reliable process available to S would, had S used it as well, have led S not to believe $p$.

- **The paradigm externalism:** the justifier is a track record outside reflective reach; S needs no
  belief about her own eyesight, and the regress stops at causal origin. A theory of *doxastic*
  justification (beliefs evaluated by how they were formed). Target is justification, not knowledge.
- **Three objections:** [generality](#generality-problem); Norman (answered by non-undermining);
  [bootstrapping](#bootstrapping) and easy knowledge. Plus the [new evil demon](#new-evil-demon-problem)
  (answered by [normal-worlds reliabilism](#normal-worlds-reliabilism)) and the
  [swamping problem](#swamping-problem). Truetemp: justified.
- **Weakest: the base clause** (sufficiency). Defenders: each objection assumes internalism, or (the
  generality problem) afflicts every theory using types, since "basing on evidence" must be typed too;
  Conee and Feldman deny this parity.
- **Applied to induction:** [externalist answer to induction](#externalist-answer-to-induction) (4.3).

*Lessons:* [1.4](lessons/01-04-internalism-and-externalism.md), [2.4](lessons/02-04-reliabilism.md), [4.3](lessons/04-03-answering-hume.md)

### Generality problem

**A token process falls under many types with different reliabilities, and nothing in the theory
chooses the type.** Goldman flagged it (1979); Richard Feldman pressed it (1985); Earl Conee and
Feldman, "The Generality Problem for Reliabilism" (1998): no principled typing yet proposed yields a
determinate verdict.

- **Narrowest type** (this token only): reliability 1 if true, 0 if false, so justification collapses into truth.
- **Broad type** ("vision"): ignores the conditions that matter.
- **Not a quibble:** without the rule the theory issues no verdict, or several.
- **Recurs** as Nozick's [method-relativity](#method-relativity) (1.2), as which environment an
  ability is relative to (2.5), and as the reference-class problem of frequentist probability.

*Lessons:* [1.2](lessons/01-02-sensitivity-and-safety.md), [2.4](lessons/02-04-reliabilism.md), [2.5](lessons/02-05-virtue-epistemology.md)

### Non-undermining clause

**A reliable source does not justify you if you are ignoring evidence you could have used against it.**
Goldman (1979): no reliable or conditionally reliable process available to S which, had S used it,
would have led S not to believe $p$. His answer to [Norman](#norman-the-clairvoyant): Norman could have
reasoned from background knowledge that claims to clairvoyance rarely pan out. The critic: internalist
work in externalist clothing. Does not help against bootstrapping (nothing available tells against the
gauge).

*Lessons:* [2.4](lessons/02-04-reliabilism.md)

### Normal-worlds reliabilism

**A process counts as reliable if it is reliable in normal worlds: those fitting our general beliefs
about how the actual world works.** Goldman, *Epistemology and Cognition* (1986). The demon victim's
perception is reliable in normal worlds, so she is justified. Accepts that reliability in the
believer's own world is unnecessary while keeping a reliability condition of a different kind. Its
verdicts are hostage to our general beliefs about the world, and shift when those beliefs do.

*Lessons:* [1.4](lessons/01-04-internalism-and-externalism.md), [2.4](lessons/02-04-reliabilism.md)

### Bootstrapping

**Using a source's own outputs to confirm its reliability.** Two forms in the course.

- **Roxanne's gas gauge** (Jonathan Vogel, "Reliabilism Leveled", 2000). Roxanne trusts her gauge
  with no prior reason to; it is in fact reliable. Each look: the gauge reads F (vision), the tank is
  full (trusting the gauge), the conjunction (conditionally reliable), and after many episodes, by
  induction, *the gauge is reliable*. Every step is licensed, so reliabilism says she is justified;
  almost everyone finds that wrong. Options: deny basic justification from an unverified source
  (abandons reliabilism's core); bar self-confirming processes (ad hoc, or smuggled internalism); bite
  the bullet (justified but dialectically useless). Same shape: Field's reliability challenge to
  mathematics, evolutionary debunking of moral intuitions.
- **Easy knowledge against dogmatism** (3.3): the same structure ([easy knowledge](#easy-knowledge)).
- **Kelly's objection to equal weight** (Kelly 2010, crediting Aaron Bronfman): two peers who both
  reasoned badly average into a "justified" credence, while the one who reasoned well is moved off it
  ([equal weight view](#equal-weight-view)).

*Lessons:* [2.4](lessons/02-04-reliabilism.md), [3.3](lessons/03-03-moore-and-the-dogmatist.md), [4.5](lessons/04-05-peer-disagreement.md)

### Easy knowledge

**If a source can give knowledge before you know it is reliable, you can learn it is not misleading
you just by consulting it.** Stewart Cohen, "Basic Knowledge and the Problem of Easy Knowledge"
(2002). Against any view allowing basic knowledge from a source: reliabilism (bootstrapping, 2.4) and
[dogmatism](#dogmatism) (3.3: "the wall is red, so it is not a white wall lit by red lights"). The
dogmatist's best reply: the inference is justified but dialectically useless against a doubter.

*Lessons:* [2.4](lessons/02-04-reliabilism.md), [3.3](lessons/03-03-moore-and-the-dogmatist.md)

### Proper functionalism

**Reliability plus the right teleology.** Alvin Plantinga (*Warrant and Proper Function*, 1993)
targets **warrant**, whatever turns true belief into knowledge. A belief is warranted only if:

1. it is produced by faculties functioning properly,
2. according to a design plan aimed at truth,
3. that is a good plan (in its designed environment it gives true beliefs with high objective probability),
4. and the belief is formed in that environment.

- **Against plain reliabilism** (*Warrant: The Current Debate*, 1993): a brain lesion causing mostly
  false beliefs also reliably causes the true belief that one has a brain lesion. Reliable, not knowledge.
- **Against proper functionalism:** Sosa's use of Davidson's Swampman, a lightning-made duplicate with
  no design history whose perceptual beliefs seem warranted. Plantinga: maybe impossible, and we would
  ascribe functions anyway.
- **Not just reliabilism plus God:** the conditions are naturalistically stateable (evolution as
  designer). The theistic use is [`philosophy-of-religion`](../philosophy-of-religion/syllabus.md) 5.2.

*Lessons:* [2.4](lessons/02-04-reliabilism.md)

### Apt belief

**A belief is apt when it is true because it manifests competence.** Ernest Sosa, *A Virtue
Epistemology: Apt Belief and Reflective Knowledge*, vol. 1 (2007). A performance aiming at a goal is:

1. **accurate** iff it reaches its aim (the belief is true);
2. **adroit** iff it manifests a competence of the performer (a reliable ability exercised in suitable conditions);
3. **apt** iff it is accurate *because* adroit.

- **Animal knowledge** = apt belief. **Reflective knowledge** = apt belief aptly endorsed by an apt
  second-order belief that it is apt (Sosa's concession to access internalism, not necessary for all knowledge).
- **Aptness is the explanatory link,** not accuracy plus adroitness: the archer's two gusts, and the
  Gettier victim, have both and are not apt.
- **Barn county:** Sosa canvasses absent competence, unmanifested competence, and aptness; he takes
  aptness: animal knowledge, no reflective knowledge.

*Lessons:* [2.5](lessons/02-05-virtue-epistemology.md)

### Credit theory of knowledge

**S knows $p$ iff S believes $p$ truly because of S's intellectual ability, so the true belief is
creditable to S.** John Greco, *Achieving Knowledge* (2010); "because of" read as explanatory salience.

The shared virtue argument:

1. Knowledge is a kind of cognitive success.
2. Success in the right way is success from ability: an achievement creditable to the agent.
3. In a Gettier case the success is not from ability: luck explains why the belief is true.
4. So knowledge is true belief from ability (apt or creditable belief), and Gettier cases fail it.
5. Achievements are finally valuable.
6. So knowledge is more valuable than mere true belief (the reply to [swamping](#swamping-problem); Riggs, Greco, Sosa).

- **Weakest: premise 2 as an identity.** *Ability without knowledge:* barn county. *Knowledge without
  much credit:* Lackey ("Why We Don't Deserve Credit for Everything We Know", 2007; 2009): Morris, new
  at a Chicago train station, asks a passer-by for the Sears Tower and is told, correctly, two blocks
  east; he knows, but the credit seems the passer-by's (returns in 4.4).
- **Relativizing abilities to environments** handles barn county but must say which environment counts:
  the generality problem again.

*Lessons:* [1.3](lessons/01-03-why-knowledge.md), [2.5](lessons/02-05-virtue-epistemology.md), [4.4](lessons/04-04-testimony.md)

### Anti-luck virtue epistemology

**Ability is necessary but not sufficient; add safety.** Duncan Pritchard, "Anti-Luck Virtue
Epistemology" (*Journal of Philosophy*, 2012): S knows iff S's *safe* true belief is the product of her
relevant cognitive abilities, to a significant degree creditable to her.

- **Why keep ability: Temp.** Temp reads temperatures off a thermometer that is broken and fluctuating
  at random, but a hidden agent adjusts the thermostat so the room always matches the reading. Safe,
  but the success owes nothing to ability: no knowledge.
- **Why add safety:** barn county (unsafe, so no knowledge, against Sosa's verdict).
- **Cost:** premise 6 of the credit argument. If knowledge is not identical to achievement, the
  achievement answer to the value problem no longer covers all knowledge.

*Lessons:* [2.5](lessons/02-05-virtue-epistemology.md)

### Responsibilist virtue epistemology

**Virtues as character traits a person can be praised for: open-mindedness, intellectual courage,
humility, carefulness.** Contrast reliabilist virtue epistemology (Sosa, Greco: virtues as stable
truth-conducive faculties).

- **Zagzebski's analysis** (*Virtues of the Mind*, 1996): an act of intellectual virtue requires
  (i) a virtuous motive, (ii) doing what a virtuous person would characteristically do, (iii) reaching
  the truth because of (i) and (ii); knowledge is belief from such acts. Clause (iii) entails truth, so
  it escapes her own inescapability argument. Cost: a skilled but unmotivated perceiver, or a small
  child, seems to know without virtuous motive.
- **Non-analysts:** Code (*Epistemic Responsibility*, 1987), Montmarquet (*Epistemic Virtue and
  Doxastic Responsibility*, 1993), Baehr (*The Inquiring Mind*, 2011) study traits of inquiry without
  analysing knowledge. **Vices:** Cassam (*Vices of the Mind*, 2019): closed-mindedness, wishful
  thinking, epistemic insouciance.
- **Situationist triad** (Mark Alfano, *Philosophical Quarterly*, 2012): (1) we have much knowledge;
  (2) intellectual character traits are not robust across situations; (3) knowledge requires acting
  from such traits. One must go: deny (2), weaken (3) toward faculties, or stop analysing knowledge.
  The epistemic twin of the situationist challenge in [ethics 4.3](../ethics/lessons/04-03-modern-virtue-ethics.md).

*Lessons:* [2.5](lessons/02-05-virtue-epistemology.md)

## Module 3 Skepticism about the external world

One valid argument, three premises that each look true, four exits. Notation: $H$ = *I have hands*;
$B$ or $SK$ = *I am a handless brain in a vat* (built to be incompatible with $H$); $K(p)$ = *I know
that $p$*.

| Response | Skeptical premise P1 $\neg K(\neg SK)$ | Closure | Characteristic cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| [Closure denial](#tracking-account) (Dretske, Nozick) | true | false | [abominable conjunction](#abominable-conjunction); red barn, conjunction elimination |
| [Moore](#moores-proof) and [dogmatism](#dogmatism) (Pryor) | false: $\neg SK$ known via $H$ | true | apparent question-begging; [easy knowledge](#easy-knowledge); White's Bayesian objection |
| Conservatism (Wright) | false: unearned entitlement to $\neg SK$ | true; transmission fails | entitlement looks like a stipulation |
| [Contextualism](#contextualism) (DeRose, Cohen, Lewis) | true in the skeptic's context only | true within a context | [semantic blindness](#semantic-blindness); retraction |

### Closure principle

**Deducing something from what you know, correctly and while still knowing the starting point, is a
way of coming to know it.** Single-premise closure in the careful form Hawthorne defends ("The Case for
Closure"), close to Williamson's "intuitive closure" (*Knowledge and Its Limits*, 2000):

> **SPC.** If S knows $p$, competently deduces $q$ from $p$, and thereby comes to believe $q$, while
> retaining knowledge of $p$ throughout, then S knows $q$.

- **Every clause works:** "competently" rules out lucky guesses about what follows; "thereby" requires
  the belief to rest on the deduction; "retaining" covers losing the premise partway.
- **Why hard to give up:** deduction is how knowledge grows (40 mg/kg times 50 kg gives 2,000 mg); if
  competent deduction from known premises could fail to give knowledge, proof would be an unreliable way
  to extend knowledge.
- **Not** "you know everything $p$ entails", and **not** [KK](#kk-principle). **Not**
  [transmission](#closure-and-transmission). **Not** [multi-premise closure](#multi-premise-closure).
- **Kept by** Moore, dogmatists, conservatives (with transmission failure) and contextualists (within a
  context); **denied by** Dretske and Nozick. **Safety** preserves it in the skeptical case; sensitivity
  does not.
- **Structural cousin:** the transfer principle Beta of the consequence argument
  ([metaphysics 6.1](../metaphysics/lessons/06-01-determinism-and-the-consequence-argument.md)).

*Lessons:* [3.1](lessons/03-01-the-closure-argument.md), [3.2](lessons/03-02-denying-closure.md), [3.3](lessons/03-03-moore-and-the-dogmatist.md), [3.4](lessons/03-04-contextualism-and-its-rivals.md)

### Brain in a vat argument

**Knowing your hands would give you knowledge that you are not envatted; the skeptic asks where that
knowledge came from.** A **skeptical hypothesis** (i) is incompatible with things you ordinarily believe
and (ii) predicts exactly the experiences you are having. The skeptic needs only that you **don't know**
it is false, not that it is likely. Descartes, *Meditations* I (1641): the dream and the "malignant
demon"; the BIV case got its standard form in Hilary Putnam, *Reason, Truth and History* (1981), who
used it against skepticism via semantic externalism (that reply belongs to
[`philosophy-of-language-and-logic`](../philosophy-of-language-and-logic/syllabus.md)).

1. **P1.** $\neg K(\neg B)$: I don't know that I'm not a handless BIV.
2. **P2.** $K(H) \to K(\neg B)$: if I know I have hands, I know I'm not a handless BIV (SPC, given the obvious deduction).
3. **C.** $\neg K(H)$ (modus tollens).

- **Why P1:** everything you could cite against $B$ would look the same if $B$ were true; the belief
  that you are not envatted is [insensitive](#sensitivity).
- **The hypothesis must contradict the target belief.** "I am dreaming" does not contradict "I am by
  the fire" (you could doze in the chair); Descartes's own "dreaming while lying in bed" does.
- **Scope:** it threatens beliefs about the world, not arithmetic. Descartes's deceiving God about sums
  is a doubt about his own reasoning (higher-order evidence), not a closure argument.
- **Weakest premises, by camp:** closure-keepers attack P1 (3.3, 3.4); closure-deniers attack P2 (3.2).
  Assigning the vat a tiny probability does not refute P1, since high probability is not knowledge.
- **A second, independent route:** the [underdetermination argument](#underdetermination-argument).

*Lessons:* [3.1](lessons/03-01-the-closure-argument.md), [3.2](lessons/03-02-denying-closure.md), [3.3](lessons/03-03-moore-and-the-dogmatist.md), [3.4](lessons/03-04-contextualism-and-its-rivals.md)

### Closure and transmission

**Closure says what warrant exists; transmission says where it comes from.**

- **Closure:** if you are warranted in (know) $p$ and $p$ entails $q$, warrant for (knowledge of) $q$
  is available by deduction. Silent on what the knowledge of $q$ rests on.
- **Transmission (stronger):** the *grounds* for $p$, plus recognizing the entailment, are a way to
  *acquire* warrant for $q$, possibly for the first time.

Peter Klein (*Certainty*, 1981) separated them; Crispin Wright drew the distinction in his 1985 British
Academy lecture; Wright and Martin Davies (1998) developed [transmission failure](#transmission-failure).
Your visual experience of hands is not evidence against $B$ (B predicts it): transmission fails there,
which does not show closure fails.

*Lessons:* [3.1](lessons/03-01-the-closure-argument.md), [3.3](lessons/03-03-moore-and-the-dogmatist.md)

### Underdetermination argument

**When your evidence cannot break a tie between your belief and a rival, you are not entitled to the
belief.** Anthony Brueckner, "The Structure of the Skeptical Argument" (1994); Duncan Pritchard,
*Epistemic Luck* (2005).

1. **UP.** If S's evidence does not favour $p$ over some incompatible hypothesis $q$, S is not justified in believing $p$.
2. **P1-prime.** My evidence does not favour $H$ over $B$ (Descartes's "no certain marks" between waking and sleep).
3. **P2-prime.** Knowing requires being justified.
4. **C.** $\neg K(H)$.

- **Distinct from the closure argument:** no entailment, no closure; only an incompatible rival.
  Brueckner: the closure argument's P1 tacitly relies on it; Pritchard treats the two as separate.
  **Denying closure leaves it standing.**
- **Target: P1-prime.** E.g. deny that your evidence is the same in the good and vat cases ([E = K](#e-equals-k)).

*Lessons:* [3.1](lessons/03-01-the-closure-argument.md)

### Relevant alternatives

**To know $p$ you must rule out the alternatives live in the context, not every possibility
incompatible with $p$.** Fred Dretske, "Epistemic Operators" (*Journal of Philosophy*, 1970).

- **Operators:** *fully penetrating* (if $p$ entails $q$, $O(p)$ entails $O(q)$: "it is true that");
  *non-penetrating* ("it is strange that"); "knows that" is **semi-penetrating**: it carries over to
  some consequences (from $p \wedge q$ to $q$; from $p$ to $p \vee q$) but not to those denying
  irrelevant alternatives, such as the [zebra case](#zebra-case)'s disguised mules.
- **Gives closure failure without sensitivity's counterexamples** (if it can be made principled).
- **Lewis's contextualist rules** are a relevant-alternatives theory with the relevance set by the
  attributor's conversation ([rules of relevance](#rules-of-relevance)).

*Lessons:* [3.2](lessons/03-02-denying-closure.md)

### Tracking account

**Your belief must co-vary with the truth in nearby worlds.** Robert Nozick, *Philosophical
Explanations* (1981). S knows $p$ via method $M$ iff:

1. $p$ is true;
2. S believes $p$ via $M$;
3. **[sensitivity](#sensitivity):** if $p$ were false, S would not believe $p$ via $M$;
4. **adherence:** if $p$ were true, S would believe $p$ via $M$.

**The closure-denier's argument:**

1. Sensitivity is necessary for knowledge.
2. Your belief that you have hands is sensitive and adherent (the closest handless worlds are accidents; you would see stumps).
3. Your belief that you are not a handless BIV is insensitive.
4. You know you have hands and that having hands entails not being a handless BIV.
5. You do not know you are not a handless BIV (1, 3).
6. **C.** Single-premise closure is false.

- **Verdict on the skeptic:** valid argument, P1 *true*, closure premise false: grant everything about
  the vat, concede nothing about the hands. Not "the skeptic is wrong about the vat".
- **Mechanism:** when $p$ entails $q$, the closest $\neg q$ worlds can be much farther and stranger than
  the closest $\neg p$ worlds.
- **Weakest: premise 1** (the garbage chute is insensitive knowledge); swap in safety and closure survives.
- **Failures spread:** the belief in $H \wedge \neg B$ is sensitive while $\neg B$ is not (a failure of
  conjunction elimination, which Dretske accepted); [Kripke's red barn](#red-barn-objection). Confining
  failures to "heavyweight" conclusions needs added structure (method-relativity, relevant alternatives).
- **Most deductions still yield knowledge;** closure is not *universal*.

*Lessons:* [1.2](lessons/01-02-sensitivity-and-safety.md), [3.2](lessons/03-02-denying-closure.md)

### Zebra case

**Dretske (1970):** at a zoo you look at striped animals in a pen marked "zebras". You know they are
zebras; "they are zebras" entails "they are not mules cleverly disguised by the zoo authorities"; you
have not checked, and your evidence would look the same if they were. Dretske: you don't know the
second, so closure fails. On Nozick's conditions (method: looking at the animal in its pen) "zebra" is
sensitive (closest not-zebra worlds: empty pen, a gazelle) and "not a disguised mule" is not.

*Lessons:* [3.2](lessons/03-02-denying-closure.md)

### Abominable conjunction

**"I know I have hands but I don't know I'm not a handless BIV."** Keith DeRose, "Solving the Skeptical
Problem" (*Philosophical Review*, 1995). Closure denial makes it true; it sounds like a contradiction,
not a discovery. The closure-denier: sounding wrong is not being wrong, but owes an explanation of why
it sounds so wrong. Contextualism keeps closure, so it is never true in a single context.

*Lessons:* [3.1](lessons/03-01-the-closure-argument.md), [3.2](lessons/03-02-denying-closure.md), [3.4](lessons/03-04-contextualism-and-its-rivals.md)

### Multi-premise closure

**If S knows $p_1, \dots, p_n$ and competently deduces their conjunction, S knows it.** Risk
accumulates: independent premises at probability $c$ give a conjunction at $c^n$
([conjunction risk](#conjunction-risk)), so on any view tying knowledge to a probability threshold the
conjunction eventually falls below it. Hawthorne, *Knowledge and Lotteries* (2004), treats the tension
at length. Independent of skepticism: many who accept single-premise closure doubt this one. It feeds
the [lottery](#lottery-paradox) and [preface](#preface-paradox) paradoxes, where the belief version is
[conjunction closure](#conjunction-closure).

*Lessons:* [3.2](lessons/03-02-denying-closure.md), [5.1](lessons/05-01-credences-and-probabilism.md)

### Moores proof

**Here is one hand, here is another; so at least two external objects exist.** G. E. Moore, "Proof of
an External World" (British Academy lecture, 1939). He claimed it met the three conditions of a
rigorous proof: (1) the premises differ from the conclusion; (2) he *knew* the premises; (3) the
conclusion follows. He offered no proof of "here is a hand"; he claimed to know it. "Four Forms of
Scepticism": he is more certain he knows this is a pencil than he is of any premise of the skeptical
argument.

- **As a closure argument:** skeptic and Moore share $K(H) \to K(\neg SK)$; the skeptic runs modus
  tollens, Moore modus ponens (philosophical-method 3.4's standoff). Neither reasons invalidly.
- **The dispute moves to:** to be justified in $H$ from an experience as of a hand, do you first need
  justification for $\neg SK$?

| View | Is $H$ justified? | Does warrant transmit to $\neg SK$? | The proof |
|---|---|---|---|
| Skeptic | no: required prior warrant for $\neg SK$ missing | nothing to transmit | premise unwarranted |
| Conservative (Wright) | yes, given the entitlement to $\neg SK$ | no: the premise's warrant presupposes the conclusion | sound, not cogent |
| Dogmatist (Pryor) | yes, immediately, absent defeaters | yes, by deduction | good; dialectically useless against a doubter |

- **"Question-begging" here is epistemic,** not logical: the premise's *warrant* depends on the
  conclusion's; the propositions differ.

*Lessons:* [3.3](lessons/03-03-moore-and-the-dogmatist.md)

### Dogmatism

**A perceptual seeming that $p$ gives immediate, prima facie justification for $p$.** James Pryor,
"The Skeptic and the Dogmatist" (*Nous*, 2000). *Immediate*: not resting on justification for any
other proposition, including $\neg SK$. *Prima facie*: defeasible by a reason to suspect something is
wrong; no positive reason to think nothing is wrong is required.

- **The perceptual special case of** [phenomenal conservatism](#phenomenal-conservatism).
- **Makes Moore's proof transmit;** its fault is only dialectical (for someone who already doubts
  $\neg SK$, the doubt is a defeater). Pryor's diagnosis.
- **Contrast conservatism** (Wright, "Some Reflections on the Acquisition of Warrant by Inference",
  2003): perceptual warrant for $H$ needs prior warrant for $\neg SK$, supplied by an **entitlement**, an
  unearned warrant to presuppose what any inquiry must take for granted. Labels clash: Wright's is
  "conservative", Pryor's "dogmatist" or "liberal"; learn them as names.
- **Costs:** [easy knowledge](#easy-knowledge) (the red wall) and **White's Bayesian objection**
  (Roger White, "Problems for Dogmatism", *Philosophical Studies*, 2006): if $\mathrm{cr}(E \mid SK) = 1$,
  looking cannot raise $\mathrm{cr}(\neg SK)$, and caps $\mathrm{cr}(H \mid E)$ at the prior
  $\mathrm{cr}(\neg SK)$ ([White's inequality](#whites-inequality)). Conservative argument: (1) $E$
  warrants $H$ only given prior, independent warrant for $\neg SK$; (2) warrant that depends on prior
  warrant for the conclusion does not transmit; (3) so Moore's proof does not transmit. The dogmatist
  denies (1).
- **Weakest assumption of White's form:** that perception gives a proposition $E$ to conditionalize on
  with a prior already in place. The dogmatist's price: either justification and rational credence
  come apart here, or you were entitled to a high prior in $\neg SK$ all along (conservatism renamed).
- **Replies to the red wall:** bite the bullet (dialectical diagnosis; justification grows while the
  credence falls), or retreat to immediate justification for the perceptual belief only (which is the
  conservative's transmission failure).

*Lessons:* [3.3](lessons/03-03-moore-and-the-dogmatist.md)

### Transmission failure

**Closure holds but the inference cannot be a route to its conclusion, because the premise's warrant
presupposes prior warrant for the conclusion** (information-dependence).

- **Wright's soccer case:** you see a player put the ball in the net and conclude a goal was scored,
  which entails a game of soccer is in progress. What you saw supports "goal" only given that a game is
  on (not a practice or a film shoot), so the inference cannot be how you learn a game is on.
- **Red wall** (Wright 2003): "the wall is red" entails "it is not a white wall lit by concealed red lights".
- **The conservative's charge against Moore:** sound but not cogent. Not closure failure: there *is*
  warrant for $\neg SK$ (the entitlement); the proof is just not where it comes from.
- Wright also turned it on McKinsey's and Putnam's arguments from semantic externalism
  ([`philosophy-of-language-and-logic`](../philosophy-of-language-and-logic/syllabus.md)).

*Lessons:* [3.1](lessons/03-01-the-closure-argument.md), [3.3](lessons/03-03-moore-and-the-dogmatist.md)

### Contextualism

**"Knows" works like "tall": what it takes to count depends on the conversation in which the word is
used.**

> **(C)** "S knows that $p$", uttered in context $c$, is true iff S believes $p$, $p$ is true, and S's
> epistemic position with respect to $p$ meets the standard $\sigma_c$ in force in $c$.

$c$ is the **attributor's** (speaker's) context; $\sigma_c$ rises with the conversation's stakes,
salient error possibilities and purposes. What varies is the truth of the *attribution*, not S's
evidence. DeRose (1992, 1995), Cohen (1999), Lewis (1996: [rules of relevance](#rules-of-relevance)).

**DeRose's solution** ("Solving the Skeptical Problem", 1995):

1. "Knows" is context-sensitive as (C) says (the [bank cases](#bank-cases)).
2. Raising a skeptical hypothesis raises the standard. **Rule of sensitivity:** when it is asserted that S knows or doesn't know $p$, the standard tends to rise until S's belief must be sensitive to count; belief in $\neg BIV$ is not sensitive.
3. So in the skeptic's context "I don't know $\neg BIV$" is true.
4. Closure holds within any one context.
5. So in the skeptic's context "I don't know $h$" is true too.
6. In ordinary contexts "I know $h$" is true (and so is "I know $\neg BIV$", though asserting it raises the standard).
7. **C.** The skeptic's conclusion and the ordinary claim express different propositions and do not contradict.

- **Keeps closure** (no abominable conjunction in one context); **concedes the skeptic a truth**, but
  only in her own context: ordinary knowledge claims are not false.
- **Weakest: premise 1 with 7.** [Semantic blindness](#semantic-blindness); the skeptic says she meant
  ordinary "know" (changing the subject); retraction (MacFarlane 2005); disquotation.
- **It is the sentence, not Priya's knowledge, that varies;** SSI makes the subject's situation matter.

*Lessons:* [3.4](lessons/03-04-contextualism-and-its-rivals.md)

### Bank cases

**DeRose, "Contextualism and Knowledge Attributions" (1992).** Friday; DeRose and his wife decide to
deposit their paychecks Saturday morning; he was at the bank two weeks ago on a Saturday and it was open.

- **Case A (low stakes):** "I know the bank is open on Saturdays": seems true.
- **Case B (high stakes):** a large, important check will bounce unless the paychecks are in before
  Monday, and his wife notes banks sometimes change their hours: "I don't know; I'd better make sure"
  seems true.

Same evidence. **Cohen's airport case** ("Contextualism, Skepticism, and the Structure of Reasons",
1999) is the third-person twin: Smith reads "stops in Chicago" off his itinerary and says he knows; Mary
and John, who must make a business contact in Chicago, wonder about a misprint and agree Smith doesn't
really know. All three views (contextualism, SSI, classical invariantism) accommodate the bank
intuitions; they part on cases that separate attributor from subject.

*Lessons:* [3.4](lessons/03-04-contextualism-and-its-rivals.md)

### Rules of relevance

**S knows $p$ iff S's evidence eliminates every possibility in which not-$p$, except those being
properly ignored.** David Lewis, "Elusive Knowledge" (1996).

| Rule | Kind | Says |
|---|---|---|
| Actuality | prohibitive | the actual world is never properly ignored |
| Belief | prohibitive | nor any possibility S believes, or ought to believe, obtains |
| Resemblance | prohibitive | a possibility saliently resembling one that may not be ignored may not be ignored |
| Reliability | permissive | may ignore failures of perception, memory, testimony |
| Method | permissive | may ignore unrepresentative samples, false best explanations |
| Conservatism | permissive | may ignore what everyone conventionally ignores |
| Attention | (overrides) | a possibility actually attended to is not being ignored |

- **Attention makes knowledge elusive:** mention the demon and it can no longer be ignored.
- **Resemblance handles lotteries:** my ticket's winning resembles the actual world (some ticket wins),
  so it may not be ignored, and I don't know my ticket lost.

*Lessons:* [3.4](lessons/03-04-contextualism-and-its-rivals.md)

### Semantic blindness

**If "knows" were context-sensitive, competent speakers would recognize it, as with "tall"; they don't,
and feel contradicted by the skeptic.** Pressed by Stephen Schiffer ("Contextualist Solutions to
Scepticism", 1996: contextualism needs an error theory of speakers); label from John Hawthorne,
*Knowledge and Lotteries* (2004). Cousins: **retraction** (after the skeptic, speakers say "I was wrong,
I didn't know", which contextualism counts false; MacFarlane 2005, who predicts it with
**relativism**: truth relative to the *assessor's* standards) and **disquotation** ("Smith said he
knows" reported freely in the reporter's own words).

*Lessons:* [3.4](lessons/03-04-contextualism-and-its-rivals.md)

### Subject-sensitive invariantism

**"Knows" has one meaning, but whether S knows depends partly on S's own practical stakes.** Hawthorne
(2004); Jason Stanley (*Knowledge and Practical Interests*, 2005: "interest-relative invariantism");
Jeremy Fantl and Matthew McGrath (2002; *Knowledge in an Uncertain World*, 2009). Engine: the
[knowledge-action principle](#knowledge-action-principle). In Case B, relying on "open Saturday" is
inappropriate, so DeRose doesn't know, evidence unchanged.

| View | Standard tracks | High-stakes attributor, low-stakes subject | Low-stakes attributor, high-stakes subject |
|---|---|---|---|
| Contextualism | attributor | false | true |
| SSI | subject | true | false |
| Classical invariantism + WAM | neither (fixed, moderate) | true, but unassertable | true |

- **Classical invariantism with a warranted assertability manoeuvre** (Rysiew, Brown): stakes are
  irrelevant to truth; "I don't know" in Case B is right to *say* because claiming knowledge would imply
  that no further checking is worth doing. Skeptical invariantism is its one-high-standard mirror.
- **Relativism** (MacFarlane): truth of an attribution depends on the *assessor's* standards, so an
  earlier claim is false as now assessed (predicts retraction); about knowledge attributions only.
- **Open question for SSI:** do *actual* or *believed* stakes fix the standard? Actual: knowledge turns
  on facts the subject cannot access. Believed: imagining high stakes destroys knowledge.

*Lessons:* [3.4](lessons/03-04-contextualism-and-its-rivals.md)

### Knowledge-action principle

**Roughly: it is appropriate to treat $p$ as a reason for acting iff you know $p$.** Hawthorne and
Stanley, "Knowledge and Action" (2008); Fantl and McGrath (2002, 2009). Drives SSI's verdict that high
stakes can destroy knowledge with evidence fixed. The [knowledge norm of assertion](#knowledge-norm-of-assertion)
extended to action; its decision-theoretic cousin is that whether to treat $p$ as settled depends on the
cost of being wrong.

*Lessons:* [1.3](lessons/01-03-why-knowledge.md), [3.4](lessons/03-04-contextualism-and-its-rivals.md)

## Module 4 Sources reason induction and other people

Knowledge that does not come from looking: from reflection, from the past projected forward, from
what others tell you, and what to do when an equally good thinker tells you the opposite.

| Answer to Hume (4.3) | Keeps | Concedes |
|---|---|---|
| [Inductive justification](#inductive-justification-of-induction) (Braithwaite, Black) | rational standing via a rule-circular argument | moves only those who already induce; counterinduction self-supports too |
| [Analytic dissolution](#analytic-dissolution) (Strawson) | "reasonable" by definition | no tie to truth |
| [Pragmatic vindication](#pragmatic-vindication) (Reichenbach) | a long-run policy | no finite-stage guarantee; [asymptotic rules](#asymptotic-rules) do as well |

### A priori and a posteriori

**Epistemic: how a proposition is justified.** A belief is justified **a priori** when its justification
does not depend on experience beyond whatever experience was needed to understand the proposition;
otherwise **a posteriori**.

- **Enabling versus evidential role:** experience taught you the word "prime" (enabling) but Euclid's
  proof is your reason (no evidential role).
- **It classifies routes, not propositions.** Theo multiplies $17 \times 23 = 391$ on paper (a priori);
  Priya reads it off a calculator (a posteriori). Same necessary truth. So the useful notion is
  **knowable a priori**: reachable by some a priori route.
- **Not "certain" or "unrevisable":** moderate rationalism makes a priori justification defeasible;
  Quine's attack works only against the indefeasibility notion (Kitcher made that requirement explicit).
- **Three axes** ([necessary and contingent](#necessary-and-contingent): metaphysical;
  [analytic and synthetic](#analytic-and-synthetic): semantic). The classical empiricist aligned them
  (a priori = analytic = necessary); Kant and Kripke broke the alignment.

| | Necessary | Contingent |
|---|---|---|
| **A priori** | no largest prime (the classical home) | stick S is one metre at $t_0$ ([Kripke, Evans](#contingent-a-priori)) |
| **A posteriori** | Lewis Carroll is Charles Dodgson ([Kripke](#necessary-a-posteriori)) | it rained in Leeds today |

- **Hume's fork** is a forerunner ([relations of ideas and matters of fact](#relations-of-ideas-and-matters-of-fact)).

*Lessons:* [4.1](lessons/04-01-a-priori-knowledge.md), [4.2](lessons/04-02-humes-problem-of-induction.md)

### Necessary and contingent

**Metaphysical: could it have been false?** Necessary = true at every possible world; contingent = true
at some but not all. A property of the proposition, not of how it is known. Kinds of necessity and
possible worlds: [metaphysics 3.1](../metaphysics/lessons/03-01-kinds-of-necessity.md).

*Lessons:* [4.1](lessons/04-01-a-priori-knowledge.md)

### Analytic and synthetic

**Semantic: true in virtue of meaning alone, or not.** "Every grandparent is a parent" is analytic.

- **Kant's synthetic a priori:** arithmetic; of $7 + 5 = 12$, analysing the concept of the sum never
  yields "twelve" (*Critique of Pure Reason*, Introduction). A logicist in Frege's tradition says
  analytic (definitions plus logic). Say which view you use.
- **Kripke:** "Carroll is Dodgson" is necessary but not true by meaning.
- **Quine,** "Two Dogmas of Empiricism" (1951): beliefs face experience as a web; any statement can be
  held come what may, and none is immune to revision, logic included (excluded middle and quantum
  mechanics). If a priori means unrevisable, nothing is.
- **Boghossian,** "Analyticity Reconsidered" (1996): concedes **metaphysical** analyticity (truth in
  virtue of meaning) to Quine, keeps **epistemic** analyticity (understanding a sentence suffices for
  being justified in believing it), which explains the a priori by grasp of meaning, not insight.

*Lessons:* [4.1](lessons/04-01-a-priori-knowledge.md)

### Contingent a priori

**Known without investigation via reference-fixing, yet it could have been false.** Kripke, *Naming
and Necessity* (lectures 1970, published 1972): fix "one metre" as *the length of stick S at $t_0$*;
you know a priori that S is one metre long at $t_0$, but S could have been heated and stretched, and
"one metre" rigidly names that length. Gareth Evans, "Reference and Contingency" (1979): introduce
"Julius" for whoever invented the zip; "if anyone uniquely invented the zip, Julius did" is a priori
and contingent.

*Lessons:* [4.1](lessons/04-01-a-priori-knowledge.md)

### Necessary a posteriori

**Necessary, but knowable only through experience.** True identities between rigid names: "Lewis
Carroll is Charles Dodgson" is necessary (nothing could fail to be itself), but no reflection on the
names reveals it; you need archives. Why such identities are necessary:
[metaphysics 3.1](../metaphysics/lessons/03-01-kinds-of-necessity.md). **Contrast** Priya's calculator
belief: an a posteriori route chosen to a truth *also* knowable a priori. A Kripkean identity is
knowable *only* a posteriori. Apriority is not necessity, and (if Kripke is right about names)
neither is analyticity.

*Lessons:* [4.1](lessons/04-01-a-priori-knowledge.md)

### Rational insight

**An immediate, non-inferential, apparent grasp that a proposition is necessarily true.** Laurence
BonJour, *In Defense of Pure Reason* (1998). Moderate rationalism: *apparent* insight justifies,
fallibly and defeasibly, like a perceptual seeming; insight is a source of basic beliefs on the
[modest foundationalist](#modest-foundationalism) model (Ross's moral intuitionism is the same model,
[ethics 6.6](../ethics/lessons/06-06-moral-knowledge-and-disagreement.md)).

**BonJour's anti-empiricist argument:**

1. Most of what we justifiably believe goes beyond what we directly observe.
2. Such a belief is justified only by inference from what is observed.
3. An inference confers justification only if the thinker has some reason to think its premises make its conclusion true or likely.
4. That reason cannot come from observation alone (an observational case would itself be an inference needing such a reason).
5. **C.** Either some justification is a priori, or almost nothing we believe is justified.

- **Weakest premises:** the externalist attacks 3 (a reliable inference justifies without the thinker's
  reason; [4.3](lessons/04-03-answering-hume.md)'s externalist answer); the Quinean attacks 4
  (holistic confirmation). Replies cost: fixing "justification" the internalist way; leaving insight an
  unexplained faculty facing the access worry of [metaphysics 1.2](../metaphysics/lessons/01-02-abstract-objects.md).
- **Hard case: the parallel postulate.** Seemed self-evident for two thousand years; general relativity
  made physical space non-Euclidean. Rationalist: the insight was mathematical, the error the a
  posteriori claim about space. Quinean: nothing told the two apart before 1915. What the rationalist
  owes: a way to tell genuine insight from conviction before experience sorts them.
- **Applied to induction** (BonJour): a long unbroken regularity is a priori, though fallibly, better
  explained by a stable law than by chance (attacks Hume's P3, [4.2](lessons/04-02-humes-problem-of-induction.md)).

*Lessons:* [4.1](lessons/04-01-a-priori-knowledge.md), [4.2](lessons/04-02-humes-problem-of-induction.md)

### Relations of ideas and matters of fact

**Hume's fork** (*Enquiry* section IV Part I). **Relations of ideas** (geometry, arithmetic) are
knowable by thought alone and their denial is contradictory. **Matters of fact** can be denied without
contradiction ("the sun will not rise tomorrow" is as intelligible as that it will). A forerunner of
the a priori / a posteriori line, close but not exact.

*Lessons:* [4.2](lessons/04-02-humes-problem-of-induction.md)

### Demonstrative and probable reasoning

**Hume's division of reasoning** (section 30). **Demonstrative** reasoning works from relations of
ideas; **probable** ("moral") reasoning concerns matters of fact and rests on cause and effect, known
only by experience.

- **Not deduction versus induction.** The word "induction" does not occur in the *Enquiry*. On Samir
  Okasha's reading ("What Did Hume Really Show About Induction?", *Philosophical Quarterly*, 2001) the
  difference is the source of the premises: demonstrative = deductively valid from a priori premises;
  probable = relies on an empirical premise.
- **Adding the uniformity principle as a premise** makes the bread inference deductively valid and
  gains nothing: the premise is a matter of fact needing support.
- **Inference to the best explanation, prediction of a case, statistical estimation** all fall under
  probable reasoning.

*Lessons:* [4.2](lessons/04-02-humes-problem-of-induction.md)

### Uniformity principle

**Unobserved cases resemble observed ones; the future resembles the past.** What Hume says every
inference from experience presupposes ("all inferences from experience suppose, as their foundation,
that the future will resemble the past", section 32). The label is attributed to no one.

- **Too weak or too strong:** nature is not uniform in every respect, so a true principle must say
  "uniform in the right respects", and specifying them is the hard part ([grue](#grue)).
- **Probabilistic form:** a prior that correlates past and future cases. Indifference over outcome
  *patterns* never learns; indifference over the *rate* gives Laplace's rule
  ([Laplace and pattern indifference](#laplace-and-pattern-indifference)). Choosing the prior is Hume's
  question relocated ([5.4](lessons/05-04-the-problem-of-priors.md)).

*Lessons:* [4.2](lessons/04-02-humes-problem-of-induction.md), [4.3](lessons/04-03-answering-hume.md), [5.4](lessons/05-04-the-problem-of-priors.md)

### Humes problem of induction

**No non-circular reasoning supports inference from the observed to the unobserved.** Hume, *An
Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding* (1748) section IV Part II. Outline: every probable inference
presupposes the [uniformity principle](#uniformity-principle); the principle is not demonstrable (its
contrary is conceivable, so implies no contradiction) and cannot be supported by probable reasoning
without presupposing itself; so the inferences that rely on it inherit no support from reasoning. (The
lesson states it in standard form; the boss problem asks you to reconstruct it.)

- **Not a demand for certainty:** it targets *probable* arguments; a 90 percent claim about the
  unobserved still needs the bridge. Fallibility is not the complaint.
- **Covers IBE and statistics,** not just enumerative generalization.
- **Hume does not doubt the practice:** experience is "the great guide of life"; "As an agent, I am
  quite satisfied ... but as a philosopher ... I want to learn the foundation". Section V: *custom*
  produces the expectation. Owen and Garrett read section IV as cognitive psychology (not produced by
  reasoning); epistemology argues about the normative version.
- **Russell's chicken** (*The Problems of Philosophy*, 1912, ch. VI): fed daily, then its neck wrung;
  dramatizes the conclusion, adds nothing to the argument.
- **Attacks:** on the presupposition premise (inference is a basic rule needing no premise; the
  externalist's reliable rule; the principle too weak or too strong); on the non-demonstrability
  premise (BonJour: a priori need not be demonstrative). "Just use probability" relocates the problem
  into the prior. Four answers: [4.3](lessons/04-03-answering-hume.md) (table above).
- **Same shape as** the regress of 2.1 applied to one principle; the circularity charge Moore faces (3.3).

*Lessons:* [4.2](lessons/04-02-humes-problem-of-induction.md), [4.3](lessons/04-03-answering-hume.md)

### Premise and rule circularity

**Premise-circular:** the conclusion appears among the premises (vicious). **Rule-circular:** the
argument uses rule R to conclude that R is reliable; its premises are not the conclusion. R. B.
Braithwaite (*Scientific Explanation*, 1953) and Max Black (1958) held rule-circularity benign;
defenders add that deduction can only be defended deductively.

- **Salmon's counterinductive mirror:** from "most observed A's are B" conclude "the next A is probably
  not B"; counterinduction has failed almost every time, so by its own rule it will succeed next time.
  If rule-circular self-support counts for induction, it counts for counterinduction.
- **The externalist** says rule-circularity matters only if justification requires already knowing your
  rule is good ([externalist answer](#externalist-answer-to-induction)).

*Lessons:* [4.3](lessons/04-03-answering-hume.md)

### Inductive justification of induction

**Black, "Self-Supporting Inductive Arguments" (1958):** in most past uses of the inductive rule, in a
wide variety of conditions, it led from true premises to true conclusions; so, by the inductive rule,
its next use will probably succeed. The premise is a record of past successes, not "induction is
reliable": rule-circular only. **Concedes:** it reassures those who already induce and cannot move one
who doesn't; Salmon's counterinductive mirror.

*Lessons:* [4.3](lessons/04-03-answering-hume.md)

### Analytic dissolution

**"Reasonable", applied to beliefs about the unobserved, *means* proportioned to inductive evidence,
so asking whether induction is reasonable is like asking whether the legal system is legal.** P. F.
Strawson, *Introduction to Logical Theory* (1952). **Concedes:** nothing about whether inductive
conclusions tend to be true; Hume's question can be re-asked in other words.

*Lessons:* [4.3](lessons/04-03-answering-hume.md)

### Pragmatic vindication

**Induction is the best policy if anything works.** Hans Reichenbach (*Experience and Prediction*,
1938; *The Theory of Probability*, 1949). The **straight rule** posits the observed frequency $m/n$ as
the limiting relative frequency ([straight rule arithmetic](#straight-rule-and-asymptotic-rules)).

1. Either the relative frequency of outcome $O$ converges to a limit $L$, or it has no limit.
2. If it converges to $L$, the straight rule's posits $m_n/n$ converge to $L$ (true by definition).
3. If there is no limit, no method can find it.
4. Any method that does find the limit (clairvoyant, oracle, theory) shows it by its track record, which the straight rule, applied to that record, will detect.
5. **C.** If any method of finding limiting frequencies succeeds, the straight rule succeeds (directly, or by learning to defer).

- **A conditional about the long run;** says nothing about the next case or any finite stage.
- **Weakest: premise 2 is true but not *only* of the straight rule** ([asymptotic rules](#asymptotic-rules)).
  Added constraints to select it are arbitrary or reintroduce a preference for the observed frequency.

*Lessons:* [4.3](lessons/04-03-answering-hume.md)

### Asymptotic rules

**Rules positing $m/n + c_n$ with $c_n \to 0$ as $n \to \infty$** (Salmon). They converge to the same
limit as the straight rule whenever one exists, yet disagree with it, and each other, at every finite
$n$. Example: posit $(m + 900)/(n + 1000)$, as if 1,000 phantom trials at frequency 0.9 were added.
Replacing 900 by 1,000 times any target gives a convergent rule positing nearly that target early on,
so vindication alone permits nearly any finite-stage posit. Formal relative: the
[no-free-lunch theorem](#no-free-lunch-theorem).

*Lessons:* [4.3](lessons/04-03-answering-hume.md)

### Externalist answer to induction

**If induction is in fact reliable, inductive beliefs are justified, and so is the belief that
induction is reliable, even reached rule-circularly.** James Van Cleve, "Reliability, Justification,
and the Problem of Induction" (1984): [process reliabilism](#process-reliabilism) applied to an
inference rule; rule-circularity matters only if justification requires prior knowledge that the rule
is good.

- **Concedes:** conditional on a fact (induction's reliability) that cannot be shown to the skeptic.
  A counterinductivist in a counterinductive world would be justified the same way: the
  [new evil demon](#new-evil-demon-problem) in a new setting.
- **Critics:** it changes the question. **Defenders:** the old question demanded what no source,
  perception included, could supply. Its self-support is [bootstrapping](#bootstrapping) at the scale
  of a whole method.

*Lessons:* [4.3](lessons/04-03-answering-hume.md)

### Grue

**A predicate that fits every observed emerald exactly as well as "green" but projects the opposite
way for unobserved ones.** Nelson Goodman, *Fact, Fiction, and Forecast* (1955). Any answer to Hume must
already favour some predicates, or "follow the observed frequency" gives contradictory forecasts. Owned
by [`philosophy-of-science`](../philosophy-of-science/syllabus.md) 1.2.

- **In 5.4:** a prior symmetric between green and grue conditionalizes the same data into opposite
  forecasts; only the prior can break the tie. Conditionalization does not solve it.

*Lessons:* [4.3](lessons/04-03-answering-hume.md), [5.4](lessons/05-04-the-problem-of-priors.md)

### No free lunch theorem

**Averaged over all possible targets, no learner beats any other off the training data.** Taught in
[statistical-learning 1.4](../statistical-learning/lessons/01-04-no-free-lunch-and-inductive-bias.md).
Hume's problem with a constant: averaged over all labellings, every learner is a coin flip on unseen
points (pattern indifference's one half); success needs an inductive bias (the rate prior's
correlation). The formal relative of Salmon's asymptotic rules.

*Lessons:* [4.2](lessons/04-02-humes-problem-of-induction.md), [4.3](lessons/04-03-answering-hume.md)

### Reductionism about testimony

**Testimonial justification reduces to perception, memory and inference: the hearer needs positive,
non-testimonial reasons to trust this telling.** Testimony is evidence the way a smoke alarm is.

- **Global:** reasons to think testimony *in general* reliable. Hume, *Enquiry* section X Part I:
  confidence in reports "derived from no other principle than our observation of the veracity of human
  testimony". 
- **Local:** reasons to trust *this speaker on this occasion* (competence, sincerity, kind of report).
  Elizabeth Fricker ("Against Gullibility", 1994; critical notice in *Mind*, 1995); a mature hearer
  must monitor the speaker.
- **Coady's argument against global reductionism** (*Testimony: A Philosophical Study*, 1992):
  (1) global reductionism requires non-testimonial reasons that testimony is generally reliable;
  (2) these could only come from one's own observed matches of reports to facts; (3) each hearer's
  sample is small, unrepresentative and itself leans on testimony; (4) so no ordinary hearer has them;
  (5) ordinary hearers are justified in many testimonial beliefs; (6) so global reductionism is false.
  **Weakest: premise 3** (coherence among reports and successful plans are non-testimonial evidence),
  and the conclusion does not touch local reductionism.
- **Against local:** Reid's children learn from parents with no reasons about reliability; the local
  reductionist calls their justification weaker or restricts the requirement to mature hearers, either
  of which concedes some testimonial justification without positive reasons.
- **Not the view that testimony is unreliable;** a view about the *source* of its justification.

*Lessons:* [4.4](lessons/04-04-testimony.md)

### Anti-reductionism about testimony

**Testimony is a basic source: a hearer is justified by default in accepting what she is told, absent
defeaters.** No positive reasons required. Thomas Reid, *Inquiry into the Human Mind* (1764). Tyler
Burge, "Content Preservation" (1993): the **acceptance principle**, roughly, one is entitled to accept
as true what is presented as true and intelligible, unless there are stronger reasons not to; the
entitlement is a priori (intelligible assertion comes from a rational source).

- **Objection: gullibility** (E. Fricker): default trust in a stranger looks no better than a guess.
- **Not "believe everything":** acceptance *absent defeaters*; Reid's credulity is restrained by experience.
- **Same shape as** modest foundationalism and phenomenal conservatism: default plus defeaters.

*Lessons:* [4.4](lessons/04-04-testimony.md)

### Principles of veracity and credulity

**Natural dispositions to speak the truth (veracity) and to believe what we are told (credulity).**
Reid, *Inquiry* ch. VI section XXIV, "Of the Analogy between Perception, and the Credit we give to Human
Testimony". Credulity is "unlimited in children, until they meet with instances of deceit and
falsehood", then restrained by experience; "the balance of human judgment is by nature inclined to the
side of belief". Belief is the scale's resting position; only a defeater moves it. "Credulity" names a
trained disposition, not a vice. The developmental point: trust is not the product of reasoning.

*Lessons:* [4.4](lessons/04-04-testimony.md)

### Transmission and generation views

**Can testimony create knowledge, or only pass it on?**

- **Transmission:** testimony works like memory. Necessity thesis: if a hearer knows $p$ by testimony,
  the speaker knew $p$. Michael Dummett (1994).
- **Generation:** Jennifer Lackey ("Testimonial Knowledge and Transmission", 1999; *Learning from
  Words*, 2008). **Stella**, a devout creationist schoolteacher, teaches her fourth-graders, accurately
  and because it is her job, that *Homo sapiens* evolved from *Homo erectus*, which she does not believe.
  The pupils seem to know; Stella does not. Lackey: what matters is the reliability of the *statement*,
  not the speaker's state of mind.
- **Transmission replies:** deny the pupils know (the classroom is a paradigm of learning); relocate
  the knowledge to the scientists behind the textbook (the view stops tracking the speaker in front of you).
- **Independent of** the reductionism question (whether pupils need positive reasons).

*Lessons:* [4.4](lessons/04-04-testimony.md)

### Testimonial injustice

**A credibility deficit owing to the hearer's identity prejudice.** Miranda Fricker, *Epistemic
Injustice* (2007). Identity prejudice tracks social identity (race, gender, class) and resists
counter-evidence. Examples: the jury in *To Kill a Mockingbird* disbelieving Tom Robinson; Herbert
Greenleaf in *The Talented Mr Ripley* dismissing Marge Sherwood's well-grounded suspicion. The speaker
is wronged as a knower; the hearer separately loses knowledge. The *deficit* is the central case. Not
any disbelief of a true speaker: a judge misled by the evidence errs but commits no testimonial
injustice. (Hermeneutical injustice is not covered.)

*Lessons:* [4.4](lessons/04-04-testimony.md)

### Epistemic peer

**Shared evidence plus equal competence on the question** (equally intelligent, careful, unbiased,
alert on this occasion). Idealized, and fixed *before* and independently of the dispute: "she got this
one wrong, so she isn't my peer here" is what Independence forbids. The **restaurant check** (David
Christensen, "Epistemology of Disagreement: The Good News", *Philosophical Review*, 2007): five
diners split evenly with a 20 percent tip; Christensen gets 43 dollars each, an equally reliable friend
45. Almost everyone: he should become much less confident.

*Lessons:* [4.5](lessons/04-05-peer-disagreement.md)

### Equal weight view

**On learning a peer disagrees, give her view the same weight as yours.** Adam Elga, "Reflection and
Disagreement" (*Nous*, 2007): your probability that you are right should equal your *prior conditional*
probability of being right, prior to thinking the question through, conditional on the circumstances
of the disagreement; for a peer, 1/2. The principle doing the work is **Independence** (Christensen):
reasons for discounting a peer must be independent of the disputed reasoning. First taught, with the
spinelessness objection and Elga's self-exemption, in [ethics 6.6](../ethics/lessons/06-06-moral-knowledge-and-disagreement.md).

**The conciliationist argument (restaurant check):**

1. Beforehand you had no reason to expect yourself more reliable than your friend.
2. Independence: discounting her must rest on reasons not depending on the disputed calculation.
3. Setting the calculation aside leaves only symmetric prior reasons.
4. So the probability that you erred is about 1/2.
5. **C.** Be about as confident in 45 as in 43, and much less confident than before.

- **Weakest: premise 2.** Lackey's elementary math (2010): a respected colleague says $2 + 2 \ne 4$;
  you should not split the difference. Conciliationist: your independent confidence in your own sanity
  outruns your trust in her. Critic: that line is what is in dispute.
- **Splitting the difference** is its simplest credal form ([linear pooling](#linear-pooling), 5.5).
- **Objections:** [bootstrapping](#bootstrapping) of two wrong peers (Kelly, crediting Bronfman); the
  [self-undermining objection](#self-undermining-objection). Conciliationist reply to bootstrapping:
  splitting is necessary, not sufficient, for rationality; Kelly: then a peer who was right becomes
  irrational for doing what the view requires.

*Lessons:* [4.5](lessons/04-05-peer-disagreement.md), [5.5](lessons/05-05-bayesian-disagreement-and-higher-order-evidence.md)

### Total evidence view

**First-order and higher-order evidence both count, with case-dependent weights; neither always wins.**
Thomas Kelly, "Peer Disagreement and Higher-Order Evidence" (in Feldman and Warfield (eds.),
*Disagreement*, 2010), revising his steadfast view ("The Epistemic Significance of Disagreement",
2005, later labelled **right reasons**: whoever read the evidence correctly may stand firm).

- **Agrees with conciliation in symmetric cases** (thin first-order evidence: the restaurant check,
  Kelly's horse race); lets a correct reader of rich evidence move only a little.
- **Swamping:** with many independent reliable peers agreeing, higher-order evidence dominates, and
  agreement can make a badly reasoned belief reasonable (Kelly concedes the cost).
- **Gives up a rule you can apply from the inside** without knowing which reading was correct.
- **Not the steadfast view:** it often requires conciliation, even from someone who reasoned correctly.

*Lessons:* [4.5](lessons/04-05-peer-disagreement.md)

### Higher-order evidence

**Evidence about how reliably you assessed your evidence, which bears on $p$ only via a claim about the
reasoner.** Peer disagreement is one instance. The **hypoxic pilot** (cases of this shape from Elga and
from Christensen, "Higher-Order Evidence", *PPR*, 2010): her fuel calculation checks out, but at this
altitude hypoxia impairs reasoning undetectably and pilots get such sums right about half the time.

- **Not evidence against $p$:** the fuel data favour the calculated answer as much as before; what
  changes is her evidence that she read it reliably.
- **Can force falling short of some rational ideal** (Christensen 2010).
- **Formal treatment in 5.5:** [calibrationism](#calibrationism), [level-splitting](#level-splitting),
  [epistemic akrasia](#epistemic-akrasia).
- **Also impairment, drugs, fatigue;** Descartes's deceiving God about sums (3.1) is a doubt of this kind.

*Lessons:* [3.1](lessons/03-01-the-closure-argument.md), [4.5](lessons/04-05-peer-disagreement.md), [5.5](lessons/05-05-bayesian-disagreement-and-higher-order-evidence.md)

### Self-undermining objection

**Conciliationism is itself disputed among peers, so it tells you to doubt itself.** From
[ethics 6.6](../ethics/lessons/06-06-moral-knowledge-and-disagreement.md).

- **Elga's self-exemption** ("How to Disagree About How to Disagree", 2010): a rule for responding to
  evidence must be dogmatic about its own correctness, or it gives inconsistent advice. Structural
  worry: "conciliate about everything except this rule"; why should the exemption stop there?
- **Christensen,** "Epistemic Modesty Defended" (2013): conciliationism can put you in a bind where you
  must violate *some* rational ideal, but every principle of epistemic modesty generates such binds, so
  producing one is no refutation. Defence or concession: open.

*Lessons:* [4.5](lessons/04-05-peer-disagreement.md)

Belief in degrees: why credences should be probabilities, how they should change, where they should
start, and how to combine them. Notation: $\mathrm{cr}(A)$ is credence in $A$; $\mathrm{cr}_0$, $\mathrm{cr}_1$
before and after a learning experience; $\mathrm{Bel}(p)$ is flat-out belief; $\Pr$ is objective chance
in 5.4. The arithmetic for every entry is in [Formulas](#formulas).

### Credence

**How confident you are that something is true, from 0 (certain it is false) to 1 (certain it is
true), written $\mathrm{cr}(A)$,** defined on a set $\mathcal{F}$ of propositions closed under
$\neg, \wedge, \vee$. Most epistemologists treat it as a mental state that betting prices *measure*,
not define ([betting interpretation](#betting-interpretation)). The bridge to flat-out belief is the
[Lockean thesis](#lockean-thesis).

*Lessons:* [5.1](lessons/05-01-credences-and-probabilism.md)

### Betting interpretation

**$\mathrm{cr}(A)$ is the agent's fair price for a ticket paying 1 if $A$ and 0 otherwise: the price at
which she would buy or sell it.** Frank Ramsey, "Truth and Probability" (written 1926, published 1931);
Bruno de Finetti (1937). Reading credence off preference, and why that needs a utility scale:
[decision-theory 2.2](../decision-theory/lessons/02-02-probability-from-preference.md).

**Four objections** (why the price is a measure, not the definition):

1. **Utility is not linear in money:** a risk-averse agent's prices understate her confidence.
2. **Reluctance to bet:** someone who finds gambling distasteful has credences but no prices.
3. **No two-sided price:** a buy-below, sell-above agent has no single fair price.
4. **Observer effects:** betting on $A$ can change whether $A$ (a bet that you finish the marathon).

Some propositions can never be settled ("humanity dies out"), so their bets never pay. Used for the
books in [5.2](lessons/05-02-dutch-books-and-accuracy.md) and [5.3](lessons/05-03-conditionalization.md).

*Lessons:* [5.1](lessons/05-01-credences-and-probabilism.md), [5.2](lessons/05-02-dutch-books-and-accuracy.md)

### Probabilism

**Rational credences are probabilities.** For all $A, B \in \mathcal{F}$:

1. **Non-negativity:** $\mathrm{cr}(A) \ge 0$.
2. **Normality:** $\mathrm{cr}(\top) = 1$ for any tautology $\top$.
3. **Finite additivity:** if $A$ and $B$ are mutually exclusive, $\mathrm{cr}(A \vee B) = \mathrm{cr}(A) + \mathrm{cr}(B)$.

The Kolmogorov axioms of [prob-stat-refresher 1.1](../prob-stat-refresher/lessons/01-01-sample-spaces-events-axioms.md)
reread as norms on a mind. Consequences: [complement and entailment rules](#probability-axioms-as-norms).

- **Constrains how credences hang together, not what they are:** 0.01 in rain and 0.99 in no rain is
  coherent whatever the sky. Which coherent function: [5.4](lessons/05-04-the-problem-of-priors.md).
- **Argued for in 5.2** twice (Dutch book; accuracy). Imprecise credence relaxes the one-number
  assumption ([decision-theory 3.2](../decision-theory/lessons/03-02-models-of-ambiguity.md)).

*Lessons:* [5.1](lessons/05-01-credences-and-probabilism.md), [5.2](lessons/05-02-dutch-books-and-accuracy.md), [5.3](lessons/05-03-conditionalization.md)

### Lockean thesis

**Believing is being confident enough.** For a fixed threshold $t$ with $\tfrac12 \le t < 1$:

$$\mathrm{Bel}(p) \iff \mathrm{cr}(p) > t$$

Named by Richard Foley (1992), after Locke's degrees of assent. Two halves: **sufficiency** (if
$\mathrm{cr}(p) > t$, believe $p$) and **necessity** (believe $p$ only if $\mathrm{cr}(p) > t$).

- **Sufficiency drives both paradoxes** ([lottery](#lottery-paradox), [preface](#preface-paradox)). Read as a
  claim about rationality it is the weakest premise: many deny you are justified in believing your ticket
  lost at 0.999 while you may believe a newspaper's report at lower credence. The preface pulls the
  other way (believing each well-checked claim looks plainly rational).
- **Stakes and confidence** return from 3.4's knowledge-action principle.

*Lessons:* [5.1](lessons/05-01-credences-and-probabilism.md)

### Lottery paradox

**Any fixed threshold short of certainty is beaten by a big enough lottery.** Henry Kyburg (1961). A
fair $N$-ticket lottery with one winner; $L_i$ = "ticket $i$ loses".

1. **P1 (Lockean sufficiency).** Believe $p$ whenever $\mathrm{cr}(p) > t$.
2. **P2 ([conjunction closure](#conjunction-closure)).** If $\mathrm{Bel}(p)$ and $\mathrm{Bel}(q)$, then $\mathrm{Bel}(p \wedge q)$.
3. **P3 (no contradictions).** Never believe a proposition you are certain is false.
4. **P4 (setup).** $\mathrm{cr}(L_i) = 1 - \tfrac1N$ for each $i$, and $\mathrm{cr}(\neg(L_1 \wedge \dots \wedge L_N)) = 1$.
5. **C.** If $1 - \tfrac1N > t$, P1-P4 are inconsistent: $N$ applications of P2 yield belief in a contradiction.

Threshold arithmetic: [Lockean threshold arithmetic](#lockean-threshold-arithmetic).

| Response | Gives up | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| Kyburg | conjunction closure (P2) | reasoning to a conjunction stops being automatically safe |
| Stability theory (Hannes Leitgeb, "The Stability Theory of Belief", 2014) | the *fixed*-threshold Lockean thesis; keeps closure and a Lockean link, threshold set by stability under learning anything compatible with $p$ | answers depend on how the claims are partitioned |
| Normic support (Martin Smith, *Between Probability and Certainty*, 2016) | the threshold: evidence must make $p$'s falsity *abnormal*, needing explanation | lottery beliefs fail however probable (echoes safety, 1.2) |
| Eliminate belief (Richard Jeffrey) | flat-out belief; only credences have norms | Modules 1-4 were stated for belief |
| $t = 1$ | anything short of certainty | close to Descartes |

- **Lewis's version:** the Resemblance rule forbids ignoring the winning possibility ([rules of relevance](#rules-of-relevance)).
- **Pressures sufficiency,** where the preface pressures consistency.

*Lessons:* [1.2](lessons/01-02-sensitivity-and-safety.md), [5.1](lessons/05-01-credences-and-probabilism.md)

### Conjunction closure

**For belief: if $\mathrm{Bel}(p)$ and $\mathrm{Bel}(q)$ then $\mathrm{Bel}(p \wedge q)$.** The premise Kyburg
rejects in the lottery paradox. The belief analogue of [multi-premise closure](#multi-premise-closure)
for knowledge; probabilism makes a conjunction's credence no higher than either conjunct's, so a
Lockean believer who agglomerates eventually crosses below $t$.

*Lessons:* [3.2](lessons/03-02-denying-closure.md), [5.1](lessons/05-01-credences-and-probabilism.md)

### Preface paradox

**A rational author believes each of her claims and that at least one is false.** David Makinson
(1965). With $n$ independent claims each at credence $c$, the conjunction has credence $c^n$; once
$c > t$ and $1 - c^n > t$, Lockean sufficiency *alone* (no closure step) has her believe each claim and
that the book contains an error: a jointly inconsistent set that seems rational.

- **Kyburg's escape does not touch it.** Either rational belief sets can be inconsistent (Makinson's
  option, among others) or she should disbelieve some of her own well-checked claims (which one, when
  all are on a par?).
- **Pressures consistency;** the lottery pressures sufficiency. Arithmetic: [conjunction risk](#conjunction-risk).

*Lessons:* [5.1](lessons/05-01-credences-and-probabilism.md)

### Dutch book

**A set of bets, each fair at the agent's own prices, that together guarantee a loss in every world.**
With credence 0.6 in rain and 0.6 in no rain, she buys both 1-dollar tickets for 1.20 and collects
exactly 1. The bookie trades against the direction of the violation: where her prices for a
partition's cells sum to more than 1 he sells her every ticket; where less, he buys every ticket from
her. Payoff tables: [Dutch book payoffs](#dutch-book-payoffs). The money pump is its cousin on the
preference side ([decision-theory 1.4](../decision-theory/lessons/01-04-what-a-representation-theorem-shows.md)).

- **It does not predict that incoherent people go broke:** a real agent can decline; it shows her
  credences sanction a losing package.

*Lessons:* [5.2](lessons/05-02-dutch-books-and-accuracy.md)

### Dutch book theorem

**Any violation of the axioms can be turned into a sure loss built only from bets the agent calls
fair.** If $\mathrm{cr}$ on a finite set of propositions violates non-negativity, normality or finite
additivity, some finite set of bets at her prices has negative total payoff in every world. Sketched by
Ramsey (1926), developed by de Finetti (1937).

- **Weakest premise: the bridge from betting to belief.** Christensen, "Dutch-Book Arguments
  Depragmatized" (1996): the sure loss is a symptom; incoherent credences evaluate one package as both
  fair and a certain loss, an inconsistency in the credences themselves. Critic: that evaluation needs
  the [package principle](#package-principle).

*Lessons:* [5.2](lessons/05-02-dutch-books-and-accuracy.md)

### Converse Dutch book theorem

**Coherent credences cannot be booked:** if $\mathrm{cr}$ satisfies the axioms, no finite set of bets at
her prices guarantees a loss. Proved independently by John Kemeny (1955) and R. Sherman Lehman (1955).
Without it the argument could be turned on the coherent agent too. Diachronic converse: Skyrms (1987).

*Lessons:* [5.2](lessons/05-02-dutch-books-and-accuracy.md)

### Package principle

**The value of a set of bets is the sum of their values.** Assumed by every Dutch book; fails when
utility is not linear in money or when bets interact (the assumption decision-theory 2.2 flags). The
term is used without attribution in the course.

*Lessons:* [5.2](lessons/05-02-dutch-books-and-accuracy.md)

### Brier score

**Inaccuracy as squared distance between credences and truth-values; lower is better, 0 is
omniscience.** Glenn Brier (1950), for scoring weather forecasters. With $w(A_i) = 1$ if $A_i$ is true
at world $w$ and 0 if not:

$$B(\mathrm{cr}, w) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \big(\mathrm{cr}(A_i) - w(A_i)\big)^2$$

Strictly proper; the **absolute score** $\sum_i |\mathrm{cr}(A_i) - w(A_i)|$ is not, and under it a
coherent point never strictly dominates an incoherent one in the two-cell case (every pair's two scores
total 2: ties, not dominance). Cross-entropy is strictly proper and the hinge loss is not, which is
the property [statistical-learning 2.2](../statistical-learning/lessons/02-02-logistic-regression-and-classification.md)
needs for classifier outputs to be probabilities.

*Lessons:* [5.2](lessons/05-02-dutch-books-and-accuracy.md)

### Accuracy dominance

**Incoherence guarantees avoidable inaccuracy; coherence never does.** (i) Every incoherent
$\mathrm{cr}$ is *strictly dominated*: some coherent $\mathrm{cr}'$ has lower inaccuracy in every world.
(ii) No coherent $\mathrm{cr}$ is even weakly dominated. De Finetti (1974) for the Brier score; James
Joyce, "A Nonpragmatic Vindication of Probabilism" (1998), for a broad class of inaccuracy measures (he
did not assume Brier); Predd and five coauthors (2009) for every continuous, strictly proper measure.

- **Two-cell proof:** orthogonal projection onto the line $x + y = 1$; the foot $p$ beats $c$ by
  $2\delta^2$ in both worlds; part (ii) uses [strict propriety](#strict-propriety)
  ([two-cell projection](#two-cell-projection)).
- **Not the only improvement, and not any coherent point:** only a narrow band of coherent points
  dominates a given incoherent one.
- **Says nothing about which coherent credences to hold:** $(1, 0)$ is coherent and maximally
  inaccurate if $\neg A$.
- **Weakest premise: the score.** "Expected inaccuracy by your own lights" already uses probabilities,
  so the premise selecting the score may presuppose coherence; and dominance reasoning fails when
  credences affect what they are about (Caie 2013, Greaves 2013).

*Lessons:* [5.2](lessons/05-02-dutch-books-and-accuracy.md)

### Strict propriety

**An inaccuracy measure is strictly proper if every coherent credence function expects itself to be
strictly more accurate than any alternative.** Brier yes, absolute score no. Step (ii) of the dominance
proof spends it. Joyce (2009) motivates it as the demand that a coherent credence never recommend
abandoning itself.

*Lessons:* [5.2](lessons/05-02-dutch-books-and-accuracy.md)

### Strict conditionalization

**Learn $E$ for certain, and nothing else: your new credence in anything is your old credence in it
given $E$.** With $\mathrm{cr}_0(E) > 0$:

$$\mathrm{cr}_1(H) = \mathrm{cr}_0(H \mid E) = \frac{\mathrm{cr}_0(H \wedge E)}{\mathrm{cr}_0(E)}$$

Sweep the probability off the ruled-out region and rescale; move nothing *within* the surviving region.
Odds form: [odds form update](#odds-form-update).

- **A diachronic norm, not Bayes' theorem:** the theorem is a synchronic identity within one function;
  the rule links two functions and needs its own argument ([diachronic Dutch book](#diachronic-dutch-book);
  accuracy-based arguments try to drop the bookie).
- **Consequences:** [rigidity](#rigidity); **commutativity** (E then F equals F then E, both being
  conditioning on $E \wedge F$); **certainty is permanent** (credence 1 stays 1; credence 0 is never
  revived), hence the case for **regularity** (no credence 0 for any contingent proposition), and the
  complaint that it idealizes real evidence.
- **If E = K,** you conditionalize on what you come to know (1.3). White's objection to dogmatism
  presupposes it (3.3).

*Lessons:* [5.3](lessons/05-03-conditionalization.md), [5.4](lessons/05-04-the-problem-of-priors.md)

### Rigidity

**The proportions inside each cell of the partition the experience acts on do not move.**

$$\mathrm{cr}_1(H \mid E_i) = \mathrm{cr}_0(H \mid E_i)$$

Holds for strict updates; on a partition it is equivalent to Jeffrey's rule. **Fails when the
experience bears on $H$ other than through the partition** (the assayer says smudging happens mostly on
ore-bearing samples); the fix is to refine the partition, which is a judgment the rule does not supply.

*Lessons:* [5.3](lessons/05-03-conditionalization.md)

### Diachronic Dutch book

**A plan to move off your conditional credence lets a bookie who knows the plan hedge the first half
of the trade against the second and charge you for the hedge.** Constructed by David Lewis, first
reported by Paul Teller (1973); Lewis's own version 1999. Suppose $\mathrm{cr}_0(E) = e$ with $0 < e < 1$,
$\mathrm{cr}_0(H \mid E) = p$, and the plan is to adopt $\mathrm{cr}_1(H) = r \ne p$ on learning $E$.
The guaranteed loss at stake $S$ is $e\,|p - r|\,S$ ([diachronic book arithmetic](#diachronic-book-arithmetic)).

- **Each snapshot is coherent;** the fault is in the plan connecting them.
- **Converse:** no such strategy books a conditionalizer; Skyrms (1987) extended the converse to Jeffrey's rule.
- **Objections:** van Fraassen (the argument assumes the rule is fixed at $t_0$; an agent with no plan
  gives the bookie nothing); Christensen and others (exploitability is a practical exposure, not an
  epistemic defect); J. Dmitri Gallow (2019: with non-partitional evidence, conditionalizing can itself
  be booked). Defenders: a plan is a disposition, and one guaranteeing loss by your own lights is defective.
- **Cousin of** dynamic inconsistency in [decision-theory 1.4](../decision-theory/lessons/01-04-what-a-representation-theorem-shows.md).

*Lessons:* [5.3](lessons/05-03-conditionalization.md)

### Jeffrey conditionalization

**Experience shifts your credences over a partition without making anything certain; reweight the
cells and leave their insides alone.** Richard Jeffrey, *The Logic of Decision* (1965), with the cloth
seen by candlelight. Partition $\{E_1, \dots, E_n\}$, new cell credences $q_i$ summing to 1:

$$\mathrm{cr}_1(H) = \sum_{i} q_i \, \mathrm{cr}_0(H \mid E_i)$$

- **Equivalent to [rigidity](#rigidity) on the partition;** reduces to strict conditionalization when some $q_i = 1$.
- **Does not commute in general:** two experiences setting $\mathrm{cr}(E)$ to 0.75 and then 0.5 end at
  0.5; the other order ends at 0.75 (last input wins on one partition). Recording each experience as a
  likelihood-ratio multiplier on the odds, not a target value, restores commutativity.
- **The input $q_i$ sits outside the formalism:** nothing says what a look at a smudge should produce.
- **Converse Dutch book:** Skyrms (1987).

*Lessons:* [5.3](lessons/05-03-conditionalization.md)

### Subjective and objective Bayesianism

**How much more than coherence must a prior satisfy?**

- **Subjective** (de Finetti, Savage; Jeffrey, van Fraassen): any coherent prior is permissible;
  rationality governs how you move, not where you start. Not "anything goes": coherence, the principal
  principle and conditionalization constrain everything after the start, and shared evidence pulls
  agents together ([merging](#merging-of-opinions)).
- **Objective** (Harold Jeffreys, Carnap, E. T. Jaynes's maximum entropy, Jon Williamson 2010):
  coherence is too weak; spread credence as evenly as the evidence permits ([indifference](#principle-of-indifference))
  and respect known chances (calibration). Science's objectivity cannot rest on permission to start anywhere.
- **The standoff:** subjectivists say disagreement surviving shared evidence is disagreement about
  possibility, which no update rule could settle; objectivists say a posterior *today* then depends on a
  prior nothing fixed.

*Lessons:* [5.4](lessons/05-04-the-problem-of-priors.md)

### Principle of indifference

**With no reason to prefer one possibility over another, give them equal credence.** Named by Keynes
(*A Treatise on Probability*, 1921); descends from Laplace's classical theory (also called the principle
of insufficient reason).

$$\mathrm{cr}(A_i) = \tfrac{1}{n}$$

when the evidence favours none of $A_1, \dots, A_n$; for a continuous quantity, uniform over its range.

- **Partition- and parameter-dependent:** Bertrand's chord paradoxes (*Calcul des probabilités*, 1889);
  the [cube factory](#cube-factory); an urn of unknown colours gets $\tfrac12$ for "red" under red/not-red
  and $\tfrac13$ under red/white/black; the Laplace rule's verdict changes when a column is split
  ([decision-theory 3.3](../decision-theory/lessons/03-03-decisions-under-ignorance.md)); Keynes's urn and
  Ellsberg ([decision-theory 3.1](../decision-theory/lessons/03-01-the-ellsberg-paradox.md)).
- **Neutral only relative to a choice of variable**, which ignorance does not supply. Jaynes tries to
  fix it by symmetry (invariance under the problem's transformations).
- **In 4.2:** indifference over outcome *patterns* never learns ($\tfrac12$ after any run); over the
  *rate* it gives Laplace's rule ([Laplace and pattern indifference](#laplace-and-pattern-indifference)).
- **The fine-tuning argument's** uniform distribution over a constant's range is an indifference move
  ([`philosophy-of-religion`](../philosophy-of-religion/syllabus.md)).

*Lessons:* [4.2](lessons/04-02-humes-problem-of-induction.md), [5.4](lessons/05-04-the-problem-of-priors.md)

### Cube factory

**One event, three equally "ignorant" descriptions, three credences.** Bas van Fraassen, *Laws and
Symmetry* (1989). Cubes with side $s$ between 0 and 1 foot; nothing else known. Indifference over side
length, face area or volume gives $\mathrm{cr}(s \le \tfrac12) = \tfrac12$, $\tfrac14$ or $\tfrac18$
([cube factory arithmetic](#cube-factory-arithmetic)). Applied to every description, the principle
demands credences no probability function can have. The standard test of whether a symmetry fixing
the variable is always available.

*Lessons:* [5.4](lessons/05-04-the-problem-of-priors.md)

### Principal principle

**If you knew the chance of $A$ were $x$, your credence in $A$ should be $x$, absent information that
goes around the chance.** David Lewis, "A Subjectivist's Guide to Objective Chance" (1980). For a
reasonable initial credence function and **admissible** $E$ (bearing on $A$ only by bearing on its chance):

$$\mathrm{cr}\big(A \mid \Pr(A) = x \wedge E\big) = x$$

A reliable report of the outcome is inadmissible. Accepted by subjectivists and objectivists alike;
constrains priors only where chances are in play and known, so it leaves the cube factory untouched.

*Lessons:* [5.4](lessons/05-04-the-problem-of-priors.md)

### Merging of opinions

**Agents who agree on what is possible and see the same growing data become certain their predictions
will converge.** David Blackwell and Lester Dubins, "Merging of Opinions with Increasing Information"
(1962): priors over infinite data sequences that are **mutually absolutely continuous** (each gives
probability zero to exactly the events the other does), conditionalizing on shared data. Haim Gaifman
and Marc Snir (1982): related results for rich languages. Countable additivity assumed.

- **Concrete bound:** beta priors' posterior means stay within $\max(a,b)/(a+b+n)$ of the observed
  frequency ([beta posterior means](#beta-posterior-means)).
- **Hard edge:** credence 0 is permanent under conditionalization, so an agent who zeroes the true
  hypothesis never recovers and mutual continuity fails.
- **Limits:** requires prior agreement on possibility and (at the level of hypotheses) shared
  likelihoods; delivers agreement only in the limit, each agent certain of merging *by her own lights*.
  Not "priors don't matter".

*Lessons:* [5.4](lessons/05-04-the-problem-of-priors.md)

### Linear pooling

**Group credence is a weighted arithmetic average of individual credences, proposition by
proposition.** Splitting the difference is the equal-weight case. Weights $w_i \ge 0$ summing to 1:

$$L(A) = \sum_i w_i\,\mathrm{cr}_i(A)$$

- **Satisfies marginalization** (pool on a fine partition then add cells = pool on the coarse partition).
- **Not externally Bayesian:** pool-then-update reweights the posteriors toward whoever found $E$ more
  probable ([pooling formulas](#pooling-formulas)); guaranteed to agree with update-then-pool only when
  every agent gave $E$ the same credence or one agent has all the weight.
- **Defence:** reweighting toward the better predictor is arguably what a group *should* do (the
  evidence is higher-order evidence about the peers). Reply: then the order of learning and deliberating,
  which is arbitrary, changes the verdict.
- **Russell, Hawthorne and Buchak,** "Groupthink" (2015): take conditionalization as a constraint on
  group credence, prove impossibility results for averaging, examine geometric rules.
- **Averaging never registers agreement as evidence** (contrast [upco](#upco-rule)). The social-utility
  analogue is Harsanyi's weighted sum ([decision-theory 5.1](../decision-theory/lessons/05-01-harsanyis-aggregation-theorem.md)).

*Lessons:* [4.5](lessons/04-05-peer-disagreement.md), [5.5](lessons/05-05-bayesian-disagreement-and-higher-order-evidence.md)

### Geometric pooling

**Take a weighted geometric mean of the credences each agent gives each world, then renormalize.**

$$G(\omega) = \frac{\prod_i \mathrm{cr}_i(\omega)^{w_i}}{\sum_{\omega'} \prod_i \mathrm{cr}_i(\omega')^{w_i}}$$

- **Externally Bayesian** (proof: conditionalizing multiplies every agent's credences by the same 0-1
  factor, which a geometric mean passes straight through).
- **Costs:** fails marginalization (the pooled credence in a proposition can change when you merely
  split one of its cells); any one agent's zero vetoes a world.

*Lessons:* [5.5](lessons/05-05-bayesian-disagreement-and-higher-order-evidence.md)

### External Bayesianity

**Pool-then-conditionalize equals conditionalize-then-pool, when everyone learns the same evidence.**
Albert Madansky's term (1964). Geometric pools have it; linear pools do not. Genest, McConway and
Schervish (1986): under mild conditions the externally Bayesian pools are the geometric kind (Genest
and Zidek's 1986 survey is the standard map). Whether a conciliating pair must behave like one Bayesian
agent is the premise in dispute.

*Lessons:* [5.5](lessons/05-05-bayesian-disagreement-and-higher-order-evidence.md)

### Upco rule

**Treat a peer's credence as evidence: her odds act as a likelihood ratio on yours.** Easwaran,
Fenton-Glynn, Hitchcock and Velasco, "Updating on the Credences of Others" (2016). With $p$ your
credence in $A$, $q$ hers, $\mathrm{cr}^{+}$ yours after hearing hers:

$$\mathrm{cr}^{+}(A) = \frac{pq}{pq + (1-p)(1-q)}$$

- **Exactly Bayesian** when both started at even odds and drew on independent evidence.
- **Synergy:** two peers at 0.6 each move to $9/13 \approx 0.69$; agreement is evidence, which
  averaging can never register. The logic of Condorcet's jury theorem
  ([political-philosophy 5.1](../political-philosophy/lessons/05-01-why-democracy.md)); both depend on independence.
- **Another way to give equal weight,** which can move both peers *beyond* their shared credence.

*Lessons:* [5.5](lessons/05-05-bayesian-disagreement-and-higher-order-evidence.md)

### Calibrationism

**Treat your own judgment as an instrument and set your credence by how often an instrument in your
condition gets it right.** Roger White, "On Treating Oneself and Others as Thermometers" (2009);
developed by Christensen. Model ([calibration formula](#calibration-formula)): $\mathrm{cr}(p) = (1-h)c + hb$.

- **Cost:** when the first-order reasoning was in fact flawless, it requires a credence that
  misrepresents what the evidence supports; Christensen accepts that higher-order evidence can force
  falling short of *some* rational ideal.
- **Agent-relative:** the same fact about the pilot's blood oxygen discounts his credence and not the
  credence of a controller who rechecked the numbers. The formula is silent on where $b$ comes from.

*Lessons:* [5.5](lessons/05-05-bayesian-disagreement-and-higher-order-evidence.md)

### Level-splitting

**First-order credence stays where the first-order evidence puts it; higher-order evidence lowers only
your credence that you reasoned well.** Maria Lasonen-Aarnio, "Higher-Order Evidence and the Limits of
Defeat" (2014); also Williamson and Weatherson. Keeps $\mathrm{cr}(p) = c$ whenever $c$ is what the
evidence supports. **Cost:** [epistemic akrasia](#epistemic-akrasia). Reply: the alternative makes rational
credence depend on misleading evidence about what rationality requires.

*Lessons:* [5.5](lessons/05-05-bayesian-disagreement-and-higher-order-evidence.md)

### Epistemic akrasia

**High confidence that $p$ together with confidence that your evidence does not support $p$.** Sophie
Horowitz, "Epistemic Akrasia" (2014), argues it is irrational: the akratic agent who turns out right
must regard herself as lucky to have believed what she did. The price of level-splitting.

*Lessons:* [5.5](lessons/05-05-bayesian-disagreement-and-higher-order-evidence.md)

### Margin-for-error arithmetic

**Toy model of 1.3:** true quantity $x$, discrimination margin $m$, claim "$L > y$" about the true value $L$.

$$K(L > y) \iff x - m > y$$

$$KK(L > y) \iff x - 2m > y$$

So for $x - 2m \le y < x - m$ the subject knows without knowing that she knows (a KK failure).
Assumes the same margin at both levels. *Serves:* [KK principle](#kk-principle),
[margin for error principle](#margin-for-error-principle). *From* [1.3](lessons/01-03-why-knowledge.md)

### Lottery odds

**A fair $N$-ticket draw:**

$$\Pr(\text{my ticket loses}) = 1 - \frac{1}{N}$$

High probability, yet insensitive and (on Pritchard's reading) unsafe; error-chance ratios between
beliefs compare probabilities, not closeness. *Serves:* [lottery belief](#lottery-belief).
*From* [1.2](lessons/01-02-sensitivity-and-safety.md)

### Witness odds formula

**Conditionally independent reports multiply likelihood ratios.** $E_1, E_2$ = witness 1 (2) reports
$A$; $L_i = \Pr(E_i \mid A) / \Pr(E_i \mid \neg A)$.

$$\frac{\Pr(A \mid E_1 \wedge E_2)}{\Pr(\neg A \mid E_1 \wedge E_2)} = \frac{\Pr(A)}{\Pr(\neg A)} \times L_1 \times L_2$$

- $L_1 = L_2 = 1$: posterior = prior (agreement creates no credibility).
- Prior 0.1 (odds $1:9$), $L = 3$: one witness gives odds $3:9$ (0.25); two agreeing give $9:9$ (0.5).

*Serves:* [coherence impossibility results](#coherence-impossibility-results). Odds form from
[philosophical-method 2.4](../philosophical-method/lessons/02-04-weighing-evidence-in-odds-form.md).
*From* [2.3](lessons/02-03-coherentism.md)

### Reliability of a process type

**Reliability = proportion of true beliefs the type yields.** Illustrative (2.4): one token falls under
"vision" (high), "birds of prey by sight" (870 of 1,000 = 0.87), "small falcons at dusk beyond 150 m"
(46 of 100 = 0.46), "this token only" (1 if true, 0 if false); against a threshold of 0.8 that is
three different verdicts. *Serves:* [generality problem](#generality-problem).
*From* [2.4](lessons/02-04-reliabilism.md)

### Conjunction risk

**Independent premises each at probability (credence) $c$:**

$$\Pr(p_1 \wedge \dots \wedge p_n) = c^n$$

| $n$ | $0.99^n$ |
|---|---|
| 1 | 0.990 |
| 10 | 0.904 |
| 50 | 0.605 |
| 100 | 0.366 |
| 300 | 0.049 |

$0.99^n$ first falls below one half at $n = 69$. Preface version (5.1): at $c = 0.98$, $t = 0.9$, the
author believes the whole book only while $n \le 5$, and believes it contains an error from $n = 114$
on; $0.98^{200} \approx 0.0176$. *Serves:* [multi-premise closure](#multi-premise-closure),
[preface paradox](#preface-paradox). *From* [3.2](lessons/03-02-denying-closure.md), [5.1](lessons/05-01-credences-and-probabilism.md)

### Whites inequality

**If the skeptical hypothesis predicts the experience, looking cannot raise credence that you are not
deceived.** With $\mathrm{cr}(E \mid SK) = 1$ and $\mathrm{cr}(E) > 0$:

$$\mathrm{cr}(SK \mid E) = \frac{\mathrm{cr}(SK)}{\mathrm{cr}(E)} \ge \mathrm{cr}(SK)$$

$$\mathrm{cr}(H \mid E) \le \mathrm{cr}(\neg SK \mid E) \le \mathrm{cr}(\neg SK)$$

(the second line because $H$ entails $\neg SK$). Illustrative prior (3.3): $\mathrm{cr}(H)$ rises from
0.80 to $12/13 \approx 0.923$ while $\mathrm{cr}(\neg SK)$ falls from 0.95 to $12/13$, hitting the
ceiling exactly. *Serves:* [dogmatism](#dogmatism). *From* [3.3](lessons/03-03-moore-and-the-dogmatist.md)

### Laplace and pattern indifference

**Same data, same Bayes' theorem, opposite verdicts.** $N_i$ = "case $i$ succeeds"; $n$ successes in $n$ trials.

- **Indifference over patterns** (every success/failure sequence equally likely):

$$\Pr(N_{n+1} \mid N_1 \wedge \dots \wedge N_n) = \frac{1}{2}$$

- **Indifference over the rate** (rate uniform on $[0,1]$), Laplace's rule of succession:

$$\Pr(N_{n+1} \mid n \text{ successes}) = \frac{n+1}{n+2}$$

$n = 1, 10, 100$ give $2/3$, $11/12$, $101/102$. The second prior builds in a correlation between past
and future cases: a probabilistic [uniformity principle](#uniformity-principle). *Serves:*
[principle of indifference](#principle-of-indifference). *From* [4.2](lessons/04-02-humes-problem-of-induction.md)

### Straight rule and asymptotic rules

**$m$ successes in $n$ trials.** Straight rule posit:

$$\hat{L}_n = \frac{m}{n}$$

Asymptotic rival (Salmon), any $c_n \to 0$:

$$\hat{L}_n = \frac{m}{n} + c_n$$

Example $(m + 900)/(n + 1000)$: on 9 of 20 it posits $909/1020 \approx 0.891$ (straight rule 0.45); with
the frequency held at 0.45 it posits 0.675 at $n = 1{,}000$, about 0.491 at $n = 10{,}000$, about 0.4504
at $n = 10^6$. Same limit, different finite-stage posits. *Serves:* [pragmatic vindication](#pragmatic-vindication),
[asymptotic rules](#asymptotic-rules). *From* [4.3](lessons/04-03-answering-hume.md)

### Probability axioms as norms

**Non-negativity, normality, finite additivity** ([probabilism](#probabilism)). Two consequences that do
most of the work:

$$\mathrm{cr}(\neg A) = 1 - \mathrm{cr}(A)$$

$$A \models B \implies \mathrm{cr}(A) \le \mathrm{cr}(B)$$

In particular $\mathrm{cr}(A \wedge B) \le \mathrm{cr}(A)$: a conjunction never earns more confidence
than a conjunct. *From* [5.1](lessons/05-01-credences-and-probabilism.md)

### Lockean threshold arithmetic

**A fixed threshold $t$ meets the lottery paradox when**

$$1 - \frac{1}{N} > t \iff N > \frac{1}{1-t}$$

At $t = 0.95$: $N = 20$ gives $\mathrm{cr}(L_i) = 0.95$, not greater than $t$ (suspend; no paradox);
$N = 21$ gives $20/21 \approx 0.952$ (believe every $L_i$; with closure, a contradiction). Strict
inequality matters. *Serves:* [Lockean thesis](#lockean-thesis), [lottery paradox](#lottery-paradox).
*From* [5.1](lessons/05-01-credences-and-probabilism.md)

### Dutch book payoffs

**A bet on $A$ at price $\pi$ with stake $S$ pays the agent**

$$S\,(\mathbb{1}_A - \pi)$$

($\mathbb{1}_A$ = 1 if $A$ is true, else 0; $S > 0$ buys, $S < 0$ sells; fair at $\pi = \mathrm{cr}(A)$
either way).

| Violation | Bookie's move | Agent's sure loss |
|---|---|---|
| Overcount: $\mathrm{cr}(R) + \mathrm{cr}(\neg R) > 1$ | sells her both tickets | (sum $- 1$) $\times S$ |
| Undercount: $\mathrm{cr}(R) + \mathrm{cr}(\neg R) < 1$ (or $\mathrm{cr}(\top) < 1$) | buys both tickets from her | ($1 -$ sum) $\times S$ |
| Additivity: $\mathrm{cr}(A \vee B) > \mathrm{cr}(A) + \mathrm{cr}(B)$, $A, B$ exclusive | sells her $A \vee B$, buys $A$ and $B$ from her | the price gap $\times S$ |
| Negativity: $\mathrm{cr}(A) < 0$ | buys the $A$ ticket at her negative price | at least $\lvert\mathrm{cr}(A)\rvert \times S$ |

Lesson instances (stake 10 dollars per ticket): $(0.6, 0.6)$ loses 2 in every world; $(0.3, 0.4)$ loses
3; $0.2, 0.3$ with disjunction at 0.6 loses $6 - 5 = 1$ (the ticket payouts cancel in every world).
*Serves:* [Dutch book](#dutch-book), [Dutch book theorem](#dutch-book-theorem).
*From* [5.2](lessons/05-02-dutch-books-and-accuracy.md)

### Two-cell projection

**Brier dominance on the agenda $\{A, \neg A\}$.** Credence pair $c = (x, y)$; worlds $w_A = (1,0)$,
$w_{\neg A} = (0,1)$; Brier score $B(c, w) = |c - w|^2$; coherent line $x + y = 1$.

$$\delta = \frac{x + y - 1}{2}$$

$$p = (x - \delta,\ y - \delta) = \left(\tfrac{1 + x - y}{2},\ \tfrac{1 - x + y}{2}\right)$$

$$B(c, w) = B(p, w) + 2\delta^2 \quad \text{for both worlds}$$

$c - p = (\delta, \delta)$ is orthogonal to the line, so Pythagoras gives the identity. Part (ii): for
coherent $p = (t, 1-t)$, $p$'s expected inaccuracy of any $q$ equals $|q - p|^2$ plus $p$'s own expected
inaccuracy, so nothing distinct from $p$ weakly dominates it. Lesson instance: $c = (0.9, 0.3)$,
$\delta = 0.1$, $p = (0.8, 0.2)$; scores $0.10$ vs $0.08$ if $A$, $1.30$ vs $1.28$ if $\neg A$; only
coherent points with $\mathrm{cr}(A)$ from about 0.78 to 0.81 dominate it. Absolute score: any pair's
two scores total 2, so no strict dominance. *Serves:* [accuracy dominance](#accuracy-dominance),
[Brier score](#brier-score). *From* [5.2](lessons/05-02-dutch-books-and-accuracy.md)

### Odds form update

**Strict conditionalization in odds: new odds = old odds times yesterday's likelihood ratio.**

$$\frac{\mathrm{cr}_1(H)}{\mathrm{cr}_1(\neg H)} = \frac{\mathrm{cr}_0(H)}{\mathrm{cr}_0(\neg H)} \times \frac{\mathrm{cr}_0(E \mid H)}{\mathrm{cr}_0(E \mid \neg H)}$$

The tool of [philosophical-method 2.4](../philosophical-method/lessons/02-04-weighing-evidence-in-odds-form.md),
now a rule for change. Lesson instance (5.3): cells $H \wedge E = 0.24$, $\neg H \wedge E = 0.16$,
$H \wedge \neg E = 0.06$, $\neg H \wedge \neg E = 0.54$; prior odds $3:7$, likelihood ratio 3.5,
posterior odds $3:2$, so $\mathrm{cr}_1(H) = 0.6 = 0.24/0.40$. *Serves:*
[strict conditionalization](#strict-conditionalization). *From* [5.3](lessons/05-03-conditionalization.md)

### Jeffrey and rigidity formulas

**Partition $\{E_i\}$, new cell credences $q_i$:**

$$\mathrm{cr}_1(H) = \sum_i q_i\, \mathrm{cr}_0(H \mid E_i)$$

$$\mathrm{cr}_1(H \mid E_i) = \mathrm{cr}_0(H \mid E_i)$$

Lesson instance (5.3): $q_E = 0.75$, $\mathrm{cr}_0(H \mid E) = 0.6$, $\mathrm{cr}_0(H \mid \neg E) = 0.1$
gives $0.75 \times 0.6 + 0.25 \times 0.1 = 0.475$; cells become $0.45, 0.30, 0.025, 0.225$.
*Serves:* [Jeffrey conditionalization](#jeffrey-conditionalization), [rigidity](#rigidity).
*From* [5.3](lessons/05-03-conditionalization.md)

### Diachronic book arithmetic

**Plan $\mathrm{cr}_1(H) = r \ne p = \mathrm{cr}_0(H \mid E)$, with $e = \mathrm{cr}_0(E)$, stake $S$.**
Case $r < p$ (for $r > p$ swap buyer and seller in bets 1 and 3):

| Bet | When | Agent's position | Fair by |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | $t_0$ | buys conditional bet on $H$ given $E$ for $pS$ (refunded if $\neg E$) | $\mathrm{cr}_0(H \mid E) = p$ |
| 2 | $t_0$ | buys a bet paying $(p - r)S$ if $E$, for $e(p - r)S$ | $\mathrm{cr}_0(E) = e$ |
| 3 | $t_1$, only if $E$ | sells a bet paying $S$ if $H$, for $rS$ | $\mathrm{cr}_1(H) = r$ |

$$\text{net in every world} = -e\,|p - r|\,S$$

Lesson instance: $p = 0.6$, $r = 0.5$, $e = 0.4$, $S = 100$ dollars: loses 4 in every world.
*Serves:* [diachronic Dutch book](#diachronic-dutch-book). *From* [5.3](lessons/05-03-conditionalization.md)

### Cube factory arithmetic

**Side $s \in (0, 1]$ foot; uniform on side, face area or volume:**

$$\mathrm{cr}(s \le \tfrac12) = \tfrac12 \quad \text{(side)}$$

$$\mathrm{cr}(s^2 \le \tfrac14) = \tfrac14 \quad \text{(area)}$$

$$\mathrm{cr}(s^3 \le \tfrac18) = \tfrac18 \quad \text{(volume)}$$

In general $\mathrm{cr}(s \le a)$ is $a$, $a^2$ or $a^3$; the three priors agree only at the endpoints.
*Serves:* [cube factory](#cube-factory). *From* [5.4](lessons/05-04-the-problem-of-priors.md)

### Beta posterior means

**Prior $\mathrm{Beta}(a, b)$, $a, b > 0$ ($a$ phantom successes, $b$ phantom failures); $h$ successes in $n$ trials.**

$$\text{prior mean} = \frac{a}{a+b}$$

$$\text{posterior mean } m = \frac{a + h}{a + b + n}$$

$$\left| m - \frac{h}{n} \right| \le \frac{\max(a,b)}{a + b + n}$$

$\mathrm{Beta}(1,1)$ is uniform (Laplace's rule is its $h = n$ case). Lesson instance: $\mathrm{Beta}(1,1)$
and $\mathrm{Beta}(9,1)$ at 60 percent success differ by 0.4, 0.109, 0.015, 0.0016 at $n = 0, 20, 200, 2{,}000$.
**Zero stays zero:** $\mathrm{cr}(H) = 0$ implies $\mathrm{cr}(H \mid E) = 0$ for every $E$ with
$\mathrm{cr}(E) > 0$. *Serves:* [merging of opinions](#merging-of-opinions). *From* [5.4](lessons/05-04-the-problem-of-priors.md)

### Pooling formulas

**Weights $w_i \ge 0$, $\sum_i w_i = 1$; worlds $\omega$.**

$$L(A) = \sum_i w_i\,\mathrm{cr}_i(A)$$

$$G(\omega) \propto \prod_i \mathrm{cr}_i(\omega)^{w_i}$$

Linear pool-then-update reweights the posteriors toward whoever found $E$ likelier:

$$L(A \mid E) = \sum_i \frac{w_i\,\mathrm{cr}_i(E)}{\sum_j w_j\,\mathrm{cr}_j(E)}\;\mathrm{cr}_i(A \mid E)$$

Update-then-pool keeps the weights $w_i$; the two agree for all $A$ only if every $\mathrm{cr}_i(E)$ is
equal (or one agent has all the weight). Geometric: pool-then-update = update-then-pool. Upco:

$$\mathrm{cr}^{+}(A) = \frac{pq}{pq + (1-p)(1-q)}$$

(two at 0.6 go to $9/13$). Splitting 0.8 and 0.2 gives 0.5; Kelly's bootstrapping instance: 0.6 and 0.9
average to 0.75 though the evidence supports 0.2. *Serves:* [linear pooling](#linear-pooling),
[geometric pooling](#geometric-pooling), [external Bayesianity](#external-bayesianity), [upco rule](#upco-rule).
*From* [4.5](lessons/04-05-peer-disagreement.md), [5.5](lessons/05-05-bayesian-disagreement-and-higher-order-evidence.md)

### Calibration formula

**$c$ = credence your first-order reasoning outputs; $h$ = credence you are impaired; $b$ = rate at
which impaired reasoners' conclusions of this kind are true.** If unimpaired outputs are calibrated and
impairment is independent of $p$ (law of total probability):

$$\mathrm{cr}(p) = (1-h)\,c + h\,b$$

Level-splitting keeps $\mathrm{cr}(p) = c$. Lesson instance: $c = 0.99$, $h = 0.5$, $b = 0.5$ gives
0.745. *Serves:* [calibrationism](#calibrationism), [level-splitting](#level-splitting).
*From* [5.5](lessons/05-05-bayesian-disagreement-and-higher-order-evidence.md)

## Pitfalls

### Knowledge versus justification

- **"Externalists say reasons don't matter."** Reliabilism counts reasoning as one process among many; it
  denies only that *having accessible reasons* is necessary. *([1.4](lessons/01-04-internalism-and-externalism.md))*
- **"The internalism dispute is about knowledge."** Nearly everyone is an externalist about knowledge (truth
  is external); the dispute is whether *justification* is. *([1.4](lessons/01-04-internalism-and-externalism.md))*
- **"Sensitivity and safety assess the believer's evidence."** They assess the world around the belief:
  Henry and a twin with identical experiences in a facade-free county differ in both. *([1.2](lessons/01-02-sensitivity-and-safety.md))*
- **"A Gettier case needs an inference."** Fake barns put all the luck in the environment; "Gettier case"
  names the lucky-truth structure. *([1.1](lessons/01-01-repairing-jtb.md))*
- **"Defeasibility asks what S could have found out."** The defeater need not be available to S at all;
  DEF quantifies over every truth. *([1.1](lessons/01-01-repairing-jtb.md))*
- **"Zagzebski shows knowledge is unanalysable."** Her conclusion is a dilemma: refutable or infallibilist;
  denying P3 and giving up analysis are both live. *([1.1](lessons/01-01-repairing-jtb.md))*
- **"Infallibilism means feeling certain."** It is about whether the condition entails truth; the factive
  version is not skeptical. *([1.1](lessons/01-01-repairing-jtb.md))*
- **"Swamping shows knowledge has no extra value."** It shows that an add-on valuable only as a means to
  truth cannot explain the extra value: an argument against reliabilism-shaped theories. *([1.3](lessons/01-03-why-knowledge.md))*
- **"E = K makes evidence infallible."** It makes evidence *true*; you can be wrong about what your
  evidence is. *([1.3](lessons/01-03-why-knowledge.md))*
- **"Norman has evidence against clairvoyance."** BonJour stipulates none either way; with counterevidence
  even reliabilists see a defeater. *([1.4](lessons/01-04-internalism-and-externalism.md))*
- **"Aptness is accuracy plus adroitness."** The Gettier victim has both; aptness is accuracy *because of*
  adroitness. *([2.5](lessons/02-05-virtue-epistemology.md))*
- **"Virtue epistemology is reliabilism renamed."** The reliabilist family adds the *because* clause, which
  handles Gettier cases and grounds the value answer. *([2.5](lessons/02-05-virtue-epistemology.md))*
- **"Responsibilists all offer rival analyses of knowledge."** Only some (Zagzebski) do; Code and Montmarquet
  do not, which matters for Alfano's triad. *([2.5](lessons/02-05-virtue-epistemology.md))*
- **"Reliabilism requires knowing your process is reliable."** That is exactly what it denies; requiring it
  restores the access condition and the regress. *([2.4](lessons/02-04-reliabilism.md))*
- **"Proper functionalism is reliabilism plus God."** Its conditions are naturalistically stateable, and it
  splits from reliabilism on the lesion and Swampman whatever one thinks of theism. *([2.4](lessons/02-04-reliabilism.md))*

### Propositional versus doxastic

- **Having a reason is not believing because of it.** The detective with good clues who believes from
  dislike has propositional, not doxastic, justification. *([1.4](lessons/01-04-internalism-and-externalism.md))*
- **"Infinitism requires infinitely many thoughts."** It requires an infinite chain of *available* reasons;
  what S supplies is finite, and that fixes only her (degreed) doxastic justification. *([2.1](lessons/02-01-the-regress-problem.md))*
- **Reliabilism is a theory of doxastic justification:** it evaluates beliefs by how they were actually
  formed. *([2.4](lessons/02-04-reliabilism.md))*

### Structure of justification

- **"A basic belief is an unjustified one."** It is *non-inferentially* justified; showing the base is not
  arbitrary is the foundationalist's whole job. *([2.2](lessons/02-02-foundationalism.md))*
- **"Foundationalism says basic beliefs are certain."** Only the classical version; modest basic beliefs
  are fallible and defeasible. *([2.1](lessons/02-01-the-regress-problem.md), [2.2](lessons/02-02-foundationalism.md))*
- **"Defeasible foundations make modest foundationalism coherentist."** Defeat is negative dependence;
  coherentism needs positive support from other beliefs. *([2.2](lessons/02-02-foundationalism.md))*
- **"Foundationalism is an internalist theory."** It is a claim about structure; a reliabilist with
  non-inferential basic beliefs is a foundationalist. *([2.2](lessons/02-02-foundationalism.md))*
- **"Coherentism is circular reasoning."** Serious versions reject the linear picture on which "circle"
  makes sense; the real objection is isolation. *([2.1](lessons/02-01-the-regress-problem.md), [2.3](lessons/02-03-coherentism.md))*
- **"Coherence is consistency."** Consistency is only the floor; coherence needs positive support among
  beliefs. *([2.3](lessons/02-03-coherentism.md))*
- **"The Bayesian results refute coherentism."** They show coherence cannot create credibility from zero
  and no measure tracks truth in all cases; whether coherentism needs either claim is open. *([2.3](lessons/02-03-coherentism.md))*
- **"The generality problem is a quibble about wording."** Without a typing rule the theory issues no
  verdict, or several. *([2.4](lessons/02-04-reliabilism.md))*

### Closure versus KK versus transmission

- **Closure is not "you know everything $p$ entails"** and not KK: SPC covers competent deduction actually
  performed with the premise retained, and says nothing about knowing that you know. *([3.1](lessons/03-01-the-closure-argument.md))*
- **"Rejecting KK means you never know that you know."** It means knowing does not *guarantee* it; near the
  margin they come apart. *([1.3](lessons/01-03-why-knowledge.md))*
- **"My experience of hands isn't evidence against being a BIV, so closure fails."** That is transmission
  failure; closure lets knowledge of not-BIV come from knowledge of hands whatever grounds the latter. *([3.1](lessons/03-01-the-closure-argument.md))*
- **"Transmission failure is closure failure."** The conservative keeps closure: there *is* warrant for
  not-SK (the entitlement); the proof is just not where it comes from. *([3.3](lessons/03-03-moore-and-the-dogmatist.md))*
- **"Question-begging means the premise restates the conclusion."** Moore's premise and conclusion differ;
  the charge is that the premise's *warrant* depends on the conclusion's. *([3.3](lessons/03-03-moore-and-the-dogmatist.md))*
- **"The skeptic claims you are probably envatted."** She needs only that you don't know you aren't, plus
  closure; a tiny probability for the vat does not refute P1. *([3.1](lessons/03-01-the-closure-argument.md))*
- **A closure argument needs a hypothesis that contradicts the target belief.** "I am dreaming" does not
  contradict "I am by the fire". *([3.1](lessons/03-01-the-closure-argument.md))*
- **Denying closure leaves the underdetermination argument standing.** *([3.1](lessons/03-01-the-closure-argument.md))*
- **"Closure-denial says the skeptic is wrong about the vat."** It concedes the vat premise in full and
  rejects only the bridge. *([3.2](lessons/03-02-denying-closure.md))*
- **"Closure failure means deduction can't extend knowledge."** On Dretske's and Nozick's views most
  deductions still do; closure is not *universal*. *([3.2](lessons/03-02-denying-closure.md))*
- **"Sensitivity and safety disagree because they rank worlds differently."** They share one similarity
  ordering and look in different directions. *([3.2](lessons/03-02-denying-closure.md))*
- **"Safety is sensitivity rewritten."** Counterfactuals do not contrapose; the BIV belief passes one and
  fails the other. *([1.2](lessons/01-02-sensitivity-and-safety.md))*
- **"Contextualism says Priya's knowledge comes and goes."** It says the *sentence* expresses different
  propositions; SSI makes the subject's situation matter. *([3.4](lessons/03-04-contextualism-and-its-rivals.md))*
- **"Contextualism concedes the skeptic's victory."** She wins only in her own context; ordinary knowledge
  claims are true. *([3.4](lessons/03-04-contextualism-and-its-rivals.md))*
- **"The bank cases prove contextualism."** All three views accommodate them; they differ where attributor
  and subject come apart. *([3.4](lessons/03-04-contextualism-and-its-rivals.md))*
- **"White shows experience can't justify belief in hands."** $\mathrm{cr}(H)$ rises; what experience
  can't do is raise $\mathrm{cr}(\neg SK)$, which caps $\mathrm{cr}(H \mid E)$. *([3.3](lessons/03-03-moore-and-the-dogmatist.md))*

### Sources

- **"A posteriori means contingent."** Priya's calculator belief and "Carroll is Dodgson" are a posteriori
  and necessary (a route chosen; the only route). *([4.1](lessons/04-01-a-priori-knowledge.md))*
- **"A priori means certain or unrevisable."** Moderate rationalism makes it defeasible; Quine's argument
  needs the indefeasibility notion. *([4.1](lessons/04-01-a-priori-knowledge.md))*
- **"Independent of experience" does not mean knowable with no experience:** experience may be needed for
  the concepts, just not as evidence. *([4.1](lessons/04-01-a-priori-knowledge.md))*
- **"Hume tells you to stop trusting experience."** It is the great guide of life; custom produces the
  expectation; the complaint is about the foundation. *([4.2](lessons/04-02-humes-problem-of-induction.md))*
- **"The problem is that induction is fallible."** A fallible conclusion can be well supported; Hume denies
  it is supported by reasoning at all. *([4.2](lessons/04-02-humes-problem-of-induction.md))*
- **"Hume's conclusion is plainly normative."** Contested: Owen and Garrett read a thesis in cognitive
  psychology. *([4.2](lessons/04-02-humes-problem-of-induction.md))*
- **"Just use probability"** relocates the uniformity principle into the prior. *([4.2](lessons/04-02-humes-problem-of-induction.md))*
- **"Black's argument is premise-circular."** Its premise is a record of past successes; the circularity is
  in the rule. *([4.3](lessons/04-03-answering-hume.md))*
- **"Strawson shows induction is reliable."** Only that calling it reasonable is analytic. *([4.3](lessons/04-03-answering-hume.md))*
- **"The externalist answer says we know induction is reliable."** It says *if* it is, our inductive
  beliefs are justified. *([4.3](lessons/04-03-answering-hume.md))*
- **"Anti-reductionism says believe everything."** Acceptance *absent defeaters*; credulity is restrained
  by experience. *([4.4](lessons/04-04-testimony.md))*
- **"Reductionism says testimony is unreliable."** It is a view about the *source* of testimonial
  justification. *([4.4](lessons/04-04-testimony.md))*
- **"Testimonial injustice is any disbelief of a true speaker."** The central case needs a credibility
  deficit *caused by identity prejudice*. *([4.4](lessons/04-04-testimony.md))*
- **The reductionism question and the transmission question are independent** (Stella settles the second only). *([4.4](lessons/04-04-testimony.md))*
- **"A peer is anyone who disagrees reasonably."** Peerhood is fixed before and independently of the dispute. *([4.5](lessons/04-05-peer-disagreement.md))*
- **"The total evidence view is the steadfast view."** It often requires conciliation, even from someone who
  reasoned correctly. *([4.5](lessons/04-05-peer-disagreement.md))*
- **"Higher-order evidence is evidence against $p$."** It is evidence about your assessment of the evidence;
  the same fact discounts the pilot and not the controller. *([4.5](lessons/04-05-peer-disagreement.md), [5.5](lessons/05-05-bayesian-disagreement-and-higher-order-evidence.md))*

### Belief versus credence

- **"The lottery and the preface are the same paradox."** The lottery presses on sufficiency, the preface on
  consistency; Kyburg's escape (drop closure) does not touch the preface. *([5.1](lessons/05-01-credences-and-probabilism.md))*
- **"Probabilism says what your credences should be."** It constrains only how they hang together. *([5.1](lessons/05-01-credences-and-probabilism.md))*
- **"The betting interpretation defines credence."** Prices can come apart from confidence (four
  objections); most treat betting as a measure. *([5.1](lessons/05-01-credences-and-probabilism.md))*
- **Mind the strict inequality** in $\mathrm{cr}(p) > t$: at $t = 0.95$, a 20-ticket lottery yields no
  belief, a 21-ticket one does. *([5.1](lessons/05-01-credences-and-probabilism.md))*
- **"The Dutch book predicts incoherent people go broke."** It shows their credences sanction a losing
  package; a real agent can decline. *([5.2](lessons/05-02-dutch-books-and-accuracy.md))*
- **"The projection is the only coherent improvement," or "any coherent point will do."** Neither: a narrow
  band dominates. *([5.2](lessons/05-02-dutch-books-and-accuracy.md))*
- **"Accuracy dominance says which coherent credences to hold."** It rules out incoherence only. *([5.2](lessons/05-02-dutch-books-and-accuracy.md))*
- **"Conditionalization is Bayes' theorem."** The theorem is synchronic; conditionalization is a diachronic
  norm needing its own argument. *([5.3](lessons/05-03-conditionalization.md))*
- **"The diachronic book shows incoherence at some moment."** Every snapshot is coherent; the fault is the
  plan, and the bookie must know it. *([5.3](lessons/05-03-conditionalization.md))*
- **"Jeffrey's rule lets experience change anything."** Only the partition credences; conditional
  credences on each cell stay fixed, and the rule fails when experience bears on $H$ otherwise. *([5.3](lessons/05-03-conditionalization.md))*
- **"Indifference is a neutral default."** Neutral only relative to a chosen variable or partition. *([5.4](lessons/05-04-the-problem-of-priors.md))*
- **"Merging means priors don't matter."** Only for agents who agree on what is possible, only in the
  limit; a zero never recovers. *([5.4](lessons/05-04-the-problem-of-priors.md))*
- **"Subjectivism lets any belief count as rational."** Its permissiveness is about the starting point only. *([5.4](lessons/05-04-the-problem-of-priors.md))*
- **"Splitting the difference *is* the equal weight view."** It is one way to give equal weight; upco is
  another and can move both peers beyond their shared credence. *([5.5](lessons/05-05-bayesian-disagreement-and-higher-order-evidence.md))*
- **"Geometric pooling simply wins."** It loses marginalization and gives any one zero a veto. *([5.5](lessons/05-05-bayesian-disagreement-and-higher-order-evidence.md))*

### Probability is not safety

- **"A highly probable belief is a safe one."** Safety is similarity, not frequency; a one-in-a-million
  event can happen in a world almost like ours. *([1.2](lessons/01-02-sensitivity-and-safety.md))*
- **High probability is not knowledge** (the lottery), which is why a tiny probability for the vat does not
  answer the skeptic. *([1.2](lessons/01-02-sensitivity-and-safety.md), [3.1](lessons/03-01-the-closure-argument.md))*
- **Normic support and Lewis's Resemblance rule** deny lottery knowledge however probable, echoing safety. *([3.4](lessons/03-04-contextualism-and-its-rivals.md), [5.1](lessons/05-01-credences-and-probabilism.md))*
- **Risk accumulates across premises** ($c^n$), so knowledge tied to a probability threshold is not closed
  under multi-premise deduction. *([3.2](lessons/03-02-denying-closure.md))*

## Conventions

- **Verdict-neutral.** Every entry states a position, its conditions, its verdicts on the standard cases,
  and the premise its critics attack. The card never says which view is right.
- **Where the build's checks corrected the syllabus, the card follows the lessons:** Lehrer and Cohen
  (1983, that author order); Kripke's *Naming and Necessity* dated lectures 1970, published 1972; Evans's
  "Julius" (not Leverrier) for the contingent a priori; Leitgeb's stability theory *keeps* closure and a
  Lockean link (it drops only the fixed threshold); Joyce (1998) did not assume the Brier score; Sosa
  bites the bullet on barn county (animal knowledge); Wright's soccer example (not an election case);
  Kelly's 2005 view is labelled "right reasons" only by later discussion; red barn facades are green in
  1.2 and merely "never red" in 3.2.
- **Boss problems are not pre-solved here.** Entries give positions and arguments at the level the lessons
  state them; the cross-tabulations and reconstructions the boss problems ask for are left to you.
- **Headings are anchors.** Lessons link `../reference.md#slug`; apostrophes and punctuation are dropped
  from headings (Agrippas trilemma, Humes problem of induction, Moores proof, Whites inequality) so the
  slugs are stable.
- **No prose dollar signs;** money is written out ("43 dollars").
