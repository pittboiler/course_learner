# Philosophy of Debt · Reference Card

> Open book. This card is meant to be open while you work — including during
> quizzes. Nothing here is something you're expected to recall cold.

The course asks what it is to owe, then argues about debt's hard cases: whether charging
for a loan can be just, what makes a loan exploitative or a pledge void, whether guilt grew
out of debt, who may cancel a debt and at whose expense, and what generations, successor
states and members of a society can owe. It takes no side. Every position below is stated
as its best defenders state it, with the premise its critics attack, and no entry records
a verdict. Use the card mid-problem to find an argument's skeleton and weakest premise, a
text's exact words and translation, who holds what, or the arithmetic of a rate. Three
habits carry the course: keep historical, conceptual and normative claims apart
([Three kinds of claim](#three-kinds-of-claim)); ask which of a debt's four marks a case
has ([Debt](#debt)); and find the premise an argument stands or falls on. Where the build's
checks against the sources corrected the syllabus, the card follows the correction
([Conventions](#conventions)). The Islamic prohibition of *riba* is named in the course as
a gap and covered nowhere in the library.

## The discipline

### Three kinds of claim

**Sort every claim before you argue with it.** A claim about what happened or what will
happen is settled by evidence; a claim about what something *is* is settled by analysis; a
claim about what is owed or right is settled by argument. Most bad arguments about debt
slide from one kind to another without the bridge premise that would license the move
([1.1](lessons/01-01-the-grammar-of-owing.md)).

| Kind | Settled by | Examples from the course |
|---|---|---|
| **Historical / empirical** | records, evidence, prediction | Lent money comes back larger (Aristotle grants it, [2.1](lessons/02-01-aristotle-money-is-barren.md)). Interest became legal and ordinary ([2.2](lessons/02-02-aquinas-selling-what-does-not-exist.md)). Merchants charged high credit prices, and a competitive market would (or would not) have charged the same ([3.1](lessons/03-01-what-exploitation-is.md)). A cap reprices or rations ([3.2](lessons/03-02-predatory-lending-and-usury-caps.md)). Expected relief changes behaviour, by some amount ([5.2](lessons/05-02-moral-hazard-and-fairness-to-those-who-paid.md)). Half of Buffon's adults are dead in 18 years 8 months ([6.1](lessons/06-01-the-earth-belongs-to-the-living.md)). |
| **Conceptual** | analysis of what a thing or relation is | A debt has four marks ([1.1](lessons/01-01-the-grammar-of-owing.md)). A loan is, or is not, an exchange ([2.1](lessons/02-01-aristotle-money-is-barren.md)). A *mutuum* transfers ownership ([2.2](lessons/02-02-aquinas-selling-what-does-not-exist.md)). A loan of capital is not a sale ([2.4](lessons/02-04-marx-interest-bearing-capital.md)). A debt is an exchange not yet completed ([4.3](lessons/04-03-graebers-thesis-examined.md)). A discharge is not a release ([5.3](lessons/05-03-bankruptcy-as-a-moral-institution.md)). What a "generation" is when births are continuous ([6.1](lessons/06-01-the-earth-belongs-to-the-living.md)). |
| **Normative** | argument about what is owed, just or permitted | Interest is unjust in itself ([2.2](lessons/02-02-aquinas-selling-what-does-not-exist.md)). The state may cap rates ([3.2](lessons/03-02-predatory-lending-and-usury-caps.md)). Paying one's debts is not the essence of morality ([4.3](lessons/04-03-graebers-thesis-examined.md)). Relief is unfair to those who paid ([5.2](lessons/05-02-moral-hazard-and-fairness-to-those-who-paid.md)). No generation can bind the next ([6.1](lessons/06-01-the-earth-belongs-to-the-living.md)). A benefit you never chose cannot make you a debtor ([6.1](lessons/06-01-the-earth-belongs-to-the-living.md)). |

- **Arguments need all three.** Böhm-Bawerk's case for interest has conceptual (premise 3),
  empirical (1–2) and normative (4) premises ([2.3](lessons/02-03-justifying-interest.md)); Marx's account splits into an
  explanatory claim, a conceptual one and a normative question he answers guardedly
  ([2.4](lessons/02-04-marx-interest-bearing-capital.md)); Graeber's steps are tagged by kind in [4.3](lessons/04-03-graebers-thesis-examined.md)'s table.
- **A historical claim can bear on a normative one only through a stated bridge.** See
  [genetic fallacy](#genetic-fallacy) and [genealogy](#genealogy) for when the bridge is
  available and when it is only assumed.
- **Explanation is not justification.** Hume's three circumstances say what sets the rate,
  not whether taking it is just ([2.3](lessons/02-03-justifying-interest.md)); "we owe it to ourselves" is accounting, not a
  verdict ([6.2](lessons/06-02-public-debt-and-the-unborn.md)).

## What it is to owe

### Debt

**A directed obligation with four marks, whatever its source.** The marks describe a
debt's shape, not where it came from: debt is "narrower than owing and wider than
promising".

1. **A quantity:** a sum of money, or so much of something counted, weighed or measured.
   Any units of the right kind will do.
2. **A determinate creditor:** at every moment a rule settles who holds the claim, even when
   the debtor cannot say who that is. A bearer bond is owed to whoever holds it.
3. **Transferability:** the creditor can sell or give the claim away ([assignment](#assignment)).
   The debtor cannot swap in a new debtor without the creditor's agreement.
4. **Discharge by anyone's payment or the creditor's [release](#release).** Short of the
   law's own power to cancel (Module 5), only the creditor can end it without payment.

**1.1's argument.** (1) To owe something to someone is to be under an obligation directed to
her. (2) A directed obligation is a debt if and only if it has the four marks. (3) Some
directed obligations lack the marks: a promise to help a friend move owes your own help, not
a quantity; gratitude cannot be priced or sold. (4) Some obligations with all four marks
arose from no promise: a damages judgment against a careless cyclist; the duty to return
money paid to you by mistake (*Institutes* III.14.1). ∴ Not everything owed is a debt
(1–3), and not every debt rests on a promise (2, 4).

- **Attacked at:** the "if" half of premise 2, applied to morality. Premise 4's examples are
  legal debts, so a critic can deny that the four marks make a *moral* debt. On the
  strictest view only a promise will do; on a voluntarist view any act of the debtor's own
  will serves, a wrong freely done included. The hard case for both is the mistaken
  payment; the voluntarist replies that the moral debt begins when the recipient learns of
  the mistake and keeps the money. The "only if" half is pressed by claims the law forbids
  selling, such as many personal-injury claims before judgment.
- **Timing.** The cyclist owes the pedestrian repair the morning after (a directed,
  unliquidated claim) and a debt of 8,000 dollars after judgment. If the court *measures* a
  debt that already existed, quantity can be a moral fact the law discovers; if it *creates*
  it, what morality supplies underneath is a duty of repair with no number attached.
- **Sorting.** Four sources feed in (a promise or contract, a wrong, a benefit received
  unasked, the law alone) and none of the three sorting questions asks which: *owed to
  someone who could release it? a quantity? transferable and ended by anyone's payment?*
- **Shape is not force.** That a payday loan or a tyrant's loan has all four marks is
  conceptual; whether it binds is normative ([3.2](lessons/03-02-predatory-lending-and-usury-caps.md), [6.3](lessons/06-03-whose-debt-is-it.md)).
- **The marks at work later:** a *mutuum* owes a quantity, not a thing ([2.2](lessons/02-02-aquinas-selling-what-does-not-exist.md)); a creditor
  who pays himself has released ([4.2](lessons/04-02-owing-the-ancestors-owing-god.md)); state succession is where politics tries to
  substitute a new debtor without the creditors' agreement ([6.3](lessons/06-03-whose-debt-is-it.md)); gratitude is owed but
  lacks quantity, transfer and discharge by payment ([6.4](lessons/06-04-debts-of-gratitude.md)).

Lessons: [1.1](lessons/01-01-the-grammar-of-owing.md), [1.4](lessons/01-04-contract-debt-and-the-duty-to-repay.md), [4.2](lessons/04-02-owing-the-ancestors-owing-god.md), [6.3](lessons/06-03-whose-debt-is-it.md), [6.4](lessons/06-04-debts-of-gratitude.md)

### Directed obligation

**Owed *to* someone, who can demand it as hers, complain in her own right, and release
it.** The duty is the correlative of her Hohfeldian claim-right
([`philosophy-of-law`](../philosophy-of-law/syllabus.md) 6.1). Break it and you do not just
act wrongly; you wrong *her*.

- **Undirected duty:** concerns others without being owed to any of them. Kant's imperfect
  duty of beneficence is the standard case ([`ethics` 2.2](../ethics/lessons/02-02-the-formula-of-universal-law.md)).
- **Quick test:** *who could release me?* Ana can cancel the 50 dollars you owe her; no one
  can cancel your duty to be generous.
- **The creditor is not whoever benefits.** Promise me that you will pay my mother 100
  dollars and the promise is directed to me; whether she can also sue varies by legal
  system, and the will and interest theories of rights give the rival rationales
  ([`philosophy-of-law`](../philosophy-of-law/syllabus.md) 6.2).
- **The complaint against Hume** ([1.2](lessons/01-02-hume-promises-as-artificial-obligation.md)): his account explains why a defaulter is blamed
  without showing that she owes anything *to* the lender. On the practice view the first
  wronged are all who keep the practice going; on the assurance and authority views, the
  promisee ([1.3](lessons/01-03-promising-after-hume.md)).

Lessons: [1.1](lessons/01-01-the-grammar-of-owing.md), [1.2](lessons/01-02-hume-promises-as-artificial-obligation.md), [1.3](lessons/01-03-promising-after-hume.md)

### Obligatio

**Justinian's definition of a legal bond.** *Institutes* III.13 (533; J. B. Moyle's
translation, 5th ed., 1913): "An obligation is a legal bond, with which we are bound by a
necessity of performing some act according to the laws of our State."

- **A knot and its untying.** "Legal bond" renders *iuris vinculum*; "performing" renders a
  form of *solvere*, to untie and to pay, and III.29's word for performance is *solutio*.
  The definition speaks from the debtor's side and leaves the creditor implicit.
- **Whose law.** "According to the laws of our State" makes it a definition of *legal*
  obligation. III.20.1 sets beside it a "natural" obligation, such as a slave's, which a
  surety may still guarantee.
- **Four classes by source** (III.13.2): "contractual, quasicontractual, delictal, and
  quasidelictal". Contract is concluded "by delivery, by a form of words, by writing, or by
  consent", so three of the four species need more than bare agreement. The "quasi" classes
  are obligations not quite contracts or delicts, treated as if they were; quasi-delict is
  not "liability without wrong" (IV.5 pr: the unjust judge "cannot but be held to have done
  a wrong").
- **Discharge** (III.29): performance extinguishes the obligation whoever performs, the
  debtor released "whether he knows of it or not, and even when it is against his will";
  performance of *something else* needs the creditor's assent. Acceptilation, "an
  acknowledgement of a fictitious performance", is the creditor's own act of release.
- Whether the legal bond also binds in conscience is the question of [1.2](lessons/01-02-hume-promises-as-artificial-obligation.md)–[1.4](lessons/01-04-contract-debt-and-the-duty-to-repay.md).

Lessons: [1.1](lessons/01-01-the-grammar-of-owing.md)

### Quasi-contract

**An obligation that no one agreed to but the law treats as if it were a contract.** The
absent man whose business another managed is bound "even though he knows nothing" (*Institutes*
III.27.1); whoever receives a payment by mistake must repay it (III.14.1). Both are debts
with no promise behind them.

- **Bourgeois's social debt** ([6.4](lessons/06-04-debts-of-gratitude.md)): Léon Bourgeois (*Solidarité*, 1896, paraphrased)
  grounds the *dette sociale* in a quasi-contract, the agreement one *would* have made if
  asked beforehand; it is discharged by contribution (taxes, social insurance) and
  enforceable.
- **A quasi-contract is not a contract.** A hypothetical agreement binds no one merely by
  being hypothetical, so Bourgeois needs a further argument that its terms are fair: the
  fair-play debate ([`political-philosophy` 1.3](../political-philosophy/lessons/01-03-fair-play-gratitude-and-associative-obligation.md)),
  including Nozick's neighbours who set up a public address system.
- **Against Seneca** ([6.4](lessons/06-04-debts-of-gratitude.md)): Roman law already made some benefits collectible, and modern
  law lets some rescuers recover their costs. The defender replies that what is recovered
  is the expense, not the benefit.

Lessons: [1.1](lessons/01-01-the-grammar-of-owing.md), [6.4](lessons/06-04-debts-of-gratitude.md)

### Release

**Ending a debt without payment, by the one who holds the claim.** Anyone may pay a debt,
even against the debtor's will (*Institutes* III.29 pr), but short of the law's own power to
cancel, only the creditor can release it. Roman acceptilation was the creditor's declaration
of a payment he had not received (III.29.1).

- **A Hohfeldian power** ([5.1](lessons/05-01-forgiving-a-debt-forgiving-a-wrong.md)): the creditor holds a claim and a *power* to extinguish
  the debtor's duty by declaring it; the debtor has a *liability* to having it
  extinguished. On Hart's will theory the right-holder is a small-scale sovereign over the
  other's duty (*Essays on Bentham*, 1982). A release needs standing and a recognized form,
  and nothing else: no wrong (you can release a loan not yet due) and no feeling (a release
  signed through gritted teeth is a release).
- **What a third party can do:** pay the creditor, which satisfies the claim so that it ends
  without anyone giving it up; or buy the claim and release it as its new holder. She cannot
  release a claim she does not hold.
- **Promises too:** all three accounts of [1.3](lessons/01-03-promising-after-hume.md) let the promisee release the promisor (by
  the practice's rules, by F's consent clause, by Owens's waiver); none lets the promisor
  release himself.
- **A limit on compulsion is not a release** ([1.4](lessons/01-04-contract-debt-and-the-duty-to-repay.md)): collateral-only remedies, time bars,
  discharge and the cost of suing limit what can be collected. Selling a claim transfers it;
  only releasing it ends it.
- **A discharge is not a release** ([5.3](lessons/05-03-bankruptcy-as-a-moral-institution.md)): the court ends the legal claim over the
  creditor's objection, so it needs a justification a creditor's release never does.
- **A Jubilee is a release no creditor chose** ([5.4](lessons/05-04-jubilee-as-a-moral-principle.md)), so its case must come from justice,
  not forgiveness. Appeals that ask creditors to lighten, defer or cancel run through the
  creditors' own power, so their fairness complaint cannot arise, though that of those who
  repaid can.
- **In ledger terms,** Nietzsche's God who pays himself has released, not been paid ([4.2](lessons/04-02-owing-the-ancestors-owing-god.md));
  Aristotle hands the father the creditor's power to remit his son's unpayable debt
  ("creditors can remit a debt; and a father can therefore do so too", *NE* VIII.14; [6.4](lessons/06-04-debts-of-gratitude.md)).
- **A release is not forgiveness.** It can be pure calculation, when collecting would cost
  more than it recovers, and it leaves any grievance untouched ([forgiveness](#forgiveness)).

Lessons: [1.1](lessons/01-01-the-grammar-of-owing.md), [1.3](lessons/01-03-promising-after-hume.md), [1.4](lessons/01-04-contract-debt-and-the-duty-to-repay.md), [5.1](lessons/05-01-forgiving-a-debt-forgiving-a-wrong.md), [5.3](lessons/05-03-bankruptcy-as-a-moral-institution.md), [5.4](lessons/05-04-jubilee-as-a-moral-principle.md), [6.4](lessons/06-04-debts-of-gratitude.md)

### Natural and artificial obligation

**Hume's line between virtues nature moves us to and virtues a convention makes.** A
*natural* virtue has a motive in human nature that would move us even if nobody called it
a duty: humanity moves us to relieve the miserable, and affection moves a parent. An
*artificial* virtue has none (*Treatise* III.ii.1).

- **The test case.** What moves you to repay a lender who is a miser, or your enemy? Not
  love of him and not love of mankind; only the thought that you must, which presupposes the
  duty it was meant to explain.
- **The first motive.** Hume grants that once a duty exists, a sense of duty can move
  someone to perform it (III.ii.1). His claim concerns the first motive, the one that makes
  the act a duty at all.
- **"Artificial" is not "fake" or "optional":** "Though the rules of justice be artificial,
  they are not arbitrary." He even calls them laws of nature, if "natural" means common to
  the species. He denies only that the obligation exists before and apart from the practice.
- **Hume's second route** (III.ii.5) runs through motives: no act can be required as a duty
  unless human nature holds some motive to it other than the sense of duty, and for keeping
  promises there is none.

Lessons: [1.2](lessons/01-02-hume-promises-as-artificial-obligation.md)

### Hume's case against natural promising

**No one can oblige himself just by willing it, so a promise is a human invention.** The
Source's two propositions: a promise "would not be intelligible, before human conventions
had established it" (conceptual), and "even if it were intelligible, it would not be
attended with any moral obligation" (normative). "All morality depends upon our sentiments",
and we cannot "by a single act of our will, that is, by a promise, render any action
agreeable or disagreeable" (*Treatise* III.ii.5).

1. If promising were natural, "I promise" would express some act of mind, and the obligation
   would depend on it.
2. That act is not a resolution (it never obliges alone), not a desire (we bind ourselves
   while averse), and not willing the performance (the will governs present actions; a
   promise concerns the future). It would have to be [willing an obligation](#willing-an-obligation).
3. To be under an obligation is for the neglect of an act to displease us in a certain way.
4. No one can change a sentiment by an act of will.
5. So by nature a promise is unintelligible, and would oblige no one even if it made sense.
6. People of limited generosity lose the gains of exchanges that cannot be completed at once:
   consent transfers only present, particular goods, not so many bushels of corn in general
   or a day's labour tomorrow, and gratitude is weak security.
7. A [convention](#convention) supplies a known form of words by which the speaker stakes his
   credit, subjecting himself to "the penalty of never being trusted again in case of
   failure": interest is the first obligation.
8. Sympathy with the public interest, helped by education and politicians' praise and blame,
   makes breaches displeasing even to those they do not touch, and general rules carry that
   displeasure to our own conduct: the moral obligation, by the same route as property.

∴ Keeping promises is an artificial virtue: its obligation is real, but a convention makes it.

- **Attacked at premise 2, the inventory:** Reid's [social operations](#social-operations).
  Rationalists attack premise 3 instead; Hume's footnote replies that a will-made
  obligation fares no better if morals are relations.
- **Force** ([1.3](lessons/01-03-promising-after-hume.md)): Hume's wounded man who promises "a competent sum" to a surgeon is bound;
  one who promises a sum to a robber is not, though both promise out of fear. Only "public
  interest and convenience", Hume says, can draw that line. Every successor must draw it
  otherwise or concede the point.
- The same convention reaches debt: Hume made government a further invention, one of whose
  objects is making people keep their promises (III.ii.8).

Lessons: [1.2](lessons/01-02-hume-promises-as-artificial-obligation.md), [1.3](lessons/01-03-promising-after-hume.md)

### Willing an obligation

**"I hereby will that I be bound": the only act a natural promise could express, and one
Hume thinks impossible.** It is what is left once resolution, desire and willing the
performance are struck off (premise 2 of [Hume's case](#humes-case-against-natural-promising)).
It fails because obligation is a matter of sentiment and "we can naturally no more change
our own sentiments, than the motions of the heavens" (III.ii.5).

- **Reid's reply** is that Hume looked for a solitary act of will when a promise is a social
  one ([social operations](#social-operations)).
- **The modern accounts agree the act must be communicated:** a practice's move is made to
  the promisee, assurance must be given, an undertaking must be communicated ([1.3](lessons/01-03-promising-after-hume.md)).

Lessons: [1.2](lessons/01-02-hume-promises-as-artificial-obligation.md), [1.3](lessons/01-03-promising-after-hume.md)

### Convention

**A pattern of conduct kept up because each sees it serves him provided others keep it too,
and knows they see the same.** Not a contract anyone signed: two men rowing a boat keep
time without promising each other anything. Languages, and gold and silver as money, grew
up the same way (*Treatise* III.ii.2). A promise is one more such invention, a form of words
that lets people who do not love each other trade across time.

- **Not a promise to keep promises.** That would be circular; Hume says outright that a
  convention is not a promise, since promises themselves arise from conventions (III.ii.2).
- **Its sanction is reputation:** "the penalty of never being trusted again". That is the
  whole enforcement story of the reputational model of sovereign debt
  ([`economics-of-debt` 6.2](../economics-of-debt/lessons/06-02-reputation-and-eaton-gersovitz.md)–[6.3](../economics-of-debt/lessons/06-03-bulow-rogoff-and-sanctions.md)) and of grim trigger
  ([`grad-game-theory` 3.3](../grad-game-theory/lessons/03-03-repeated-games-finite-infinite.md)),
  where cooperation survives only if the future weighs enough. David Lewis's *Convention*
  (1969) rebuilt the theory with game theory.
- **What it leaves out** ([1.3](lessons/01-03-promising-after-hume.md)): it explains why a defaulter is blamed, not what she owes
  the person she promised.

Lessons: [1.2](lessons/01-02-hume-promises-as-artificial-obligation.md), [1.3](lessons/01-03-promising-after-hume.md)

### Interest and sympathy

**Hume's two sources of the obligation of promises: first reputation, then moral
disapproval.** Interest binds because a breaker loses his credit; sympathy with the public
interest, carried by general rules, makes breaches odious even to bystanders and to
ourselves ([Hume's case](#humes-case-against-natural-promising), premises 7–8).

- **Example 1 of [1.2](lessons/01-02-hume-promises-as-artificial-obligation.md) (the neighbour's loan):** default costs your credit with him and
  everyone who hears (interest); a third villager who lent nothing still disapproves
  (sympathy), and you judge your own conduct by the same rule.
- **The secret loan** (Hume raised it himself, III.ii.1): a traveller borrows her fare from a
  rich stranger in a port she will never revisit. Interest is idle and benevolence weak;
  what remains is the general rule. For Hume that is no evasion, since to be bound just is
  for a breach to meet that disapproval. The critic: it explains the blame without showing
  a [directed obligation](#directed-obligation) to the stranger.
- **Contrasts:** Holmes gives contract a court-imposed sanction without Hume's second stage
  ([1.4](lessons/01-04-contract-debt-and-the-duty-to-repay.md)); Nietzsche agrees that promising is artificial but makes its making slow and
  cruel rather than the lesson of brief experience ([4.1](lessons/04-01-the-animal-with-the-right-to-make-promises.md)).

Lessons: [1.2](lessons/01-02-hume-promises-as-artificial-obligation.md), [1.4](lessons/01-04-contract-debt-and-the-duty-to-repay.md), [4.1](lessons/04-01-the-animal-with-the-right-to-make-promises.md)

### Social operations

**Reid's reply: promising is a natural act of mind that exists only when expressed to
another.** Thomas Reid (*Essays on the Active Powers of Man*, 1788, Essay V ch. 6, paraphrased)
put promising with asking a question, giving a command and testifying. A promise is a will to
be bound, expressed, understood and accepted. It needs a hearer and shared signs, which Reid
thought nature supplies before any compact, but no convention of promising.

- **It denies Hume's premise 2:** Hume searched the acts a person could perform alone and
  found no promise because he searched the wrong list.
- **Reid's two further objections are of different kinds.** The contradiction charge is
  conceptual: to borrow on condition of repaying, with no conception that you must repay,
  is a contradiction, which meets Hume's claim that a promise is unintelligible without a
  practice. The historical challenge (name a people who borrowed with no idea of repaying)
  touches only Hume's conjectural story of origins. Neither touches the normative claim that
  the obligation, once made, is real.
- **The Humean reply:** without the convention there is no borrowing at all, only gifts made
  in hope of gratitude ([1.2](lessons/01-02-hume-promises-as-artificial-obligation.md) P2 tests it on an island with no form of words for binding
  the future).

Lessons: [1.2](lessons/01-02-hume-promises-as-artificial-obligation.md)

### Promise

**The most familiar source of a debt, and the ground most accounts give for the duty to
repay.** For Hume a form of words that stakes the speaker's credit ([1.2](lessons/01-02-hume-promises-as-artificial-obligation.md)); for the modern
accounts a move in a practice, an assurance deliberately given, or a communicated undertaking
that grants the promisee authority ([1.3](lessons/01-03-promising-after-hume.md)).

- **Owed, not a debt:** a promise to help a friend move owes your own help, not a quantity,
  cannot be sold, and is kept by a substitute only if the promisee accepts ([1.1](lessons/01-01-the-grammar-of-owing.md)).
- **The critics' ground against Graeber** ([4.3](lessons/04-03-graebers-thesis-examined.md)): subtract his "mathematics and violence"
  and a promise remains, and none of Module 1's accounts grounds the promise in
  quantification or force.
- **Kant's false promise** is a borrower who never meant to repay; the practice universal
  false promising would destroy ([`ethics` 2.2](../ethics/lessons/02-02-the-formula-of-universal-law.md)).
- **Nietzsche's version** is a standing the promiser earns: the animal that "can promise",
  in Samuel's rendering of *versprechen darf*, may promise ([sovereign individual](#sovereign-individual)).

Lessons: [1.2](lessons/01-02-hume-promises-as-artificial-obligation.md), [1.3](lessons/01-03-promising-after-hume.md), [4.3](lessons/04-03-graebers-thesis-examined.md)

### Practice view

**A promise is a move defined by a social practice's rules, and breaking it free-rides on
everyone who keeps them.** Rawls ("Two Concepts of Rules", 1955; *A Theory of Justice*, 1971):
"I promise" works like a bid at bridge. The rule says promisers perform unless an accepted
excuse applies, and "breaking it would do more good" is not an excuse the practice accepts.
You justify the practice by its benefits, never a single promise by its own.

- **Plus the principle of fairness** (1971): whoever voluntarily takes the benefits of a
  just practice must do his part as its rules define it
  ([`political-philosophy` 1.3](../political-philosophy/lessons/01-03-fair-play-gratitude-and-associative-obligation.md)).
- **Attacked:** Scanlon ("Promises and Practices", 1990) says it names the wrong victim; a
  broken promise wrongs the promisee first. Kolodny and Wallace ("Promises and Practices
  Revisited", 2003) keep the practice as the ground of the obligation and use assurance to
  explain why the promisee is wronged, so the disbelieved Pal is bound by fairness but
  breaks no assurance; they also argue that only a practice breaks the circle in Scanlon's F.
- **Keep two claims apart:** that promising is a social practice (conceptual and historical)
  and that the practice is what makes breaking a promise wrong (normative). Owens grants the
  first and denies the second.
- **Only as firm as the practice:** ours counts duress as an excuse, but Hobbes drew the line
  elsewhere ([Three accounts on the hard cases](#three-accounts-on-the-hard-cases)).

Lessons: [1.3](lessons/01-03-promising-after-hume.md)

### Principle of fidelity

**Scanlon: deliberately give someone an assurance she wants, and you owe her the
performance.** *What We Owe to Each Other* (1998), ch. 7, paraphrased (F): if A voluntarily
and intentionally leads B to expect that A will do X, knows B wants this assurance, aims to
give it and has good reason to think he has, and all this is mutual knowledge, then, absent
special justification, A must do X unless B consents otherwise.

- **Why it holds:** it passes the contractualist test
  ([`ethics` 5.2](../ethics/lessons/05-02-contractualism-what-no-one-could-reasonably-reject.md)),
  since promisees have strong reason to want assurance.
- **No reliance needed.** Losses from reliance fall under a separate principle, which a
  warning or compensation can satisfy; F demands performance. The Guilty Secret: you promise
  an acquaintance not to reveal an embarrassing episode while you visit his campus; he can do
  nothing in reliance, yet breaking the promise adds a wrong to the hurt.
- **The Profligate Pal:** a friend who habitually fails to repay begs one more loan and
  sincerely promises; you lend out of pity, not believing him. F is not triggered, and
  Scanlon accepts that: if the pal owes anything, it is not under F, more likely gratitude
  for a gift politely called a loan.
- **The circle:** for your word to assure her, she must think you already bound, yet under
  F you are bound only once she is assured. Scanlon states it himself; Kolodny and Wallace
  argue his replies fail.

Lessons: [1.3](lessons/01-03-promising-after-hume.md)

### Authority interest

**Owens: a promise hands the promisee a say over what you do, and people want that say for
its own sake.** David Owens (*Shaping the Normative Landscape*, 2012): a promise is a
communicated undertaking of an obligation, and it grants the right to require performance or
to waive it. Breach is a "bare wronging", a wrong to the promisee even if it sets back none of
her other interests.

**[1.3](lessons/01-03-promising-after-hume.md)'s argument**, from practice to authority:
1. A promise is a move defined by a practice's rules (Rawls).
2. Whoever voluntarily takes the benefits of a just practice must do his part (fairness).
   Premises 1–2 are the [practice view](#practice-view).
3. But a broken promise wrongs the promisee first, not the practice (Scanlon, 1990).
4. Scanlon's [Principle of Fidelity](#principle-of-fidelity) explains that wrong with no
   practice.
5. Yet a promise binds even when the promisee is not assured (the Profligate Pal, as Owens
   reads it).
6. So a promise confers authority, not just information (Owens, 2012).

∴ If premises 3 and 5 hold, only the authority view explains both that the wrong is done to
the promisee and that it survives the absence of assurance.

- **Attacked:** premise 5 is a case intuition Scanlon rejects on purpose; critics add that
  social practices may explain what Owens's normative interests are posited to explain.
  Premise 3 is contested too: on Kolodny and Wallace's hybrid a broken promise can involve two
  wrongs, and then the conclusion fails.
- **Strains:** the dead promisee (authority nobody can exercise looks nominal) and the robber
  (to void the extorted promise Owens needs a further premise: the power to bind oneself
  holds only where granting authority serves interests worth protecting). Owens holds that
  the power to promise must be socially recognized to be real.
- It is a Hohfeldian power over a waivable claim, the power [5.1](lessons/05-01-forgiving-a-debt-forgiving-a-wrong.md) studies as release.

Lessons: [1.3](lessons/01-03-promising-after-hume.md), [5.1](lessons/05-01-forgiving-a-debt-forgiving-a-wrong.md)

### Contract as promise

**Fried: a contract is a promise that has acquired legal force, and breaking it abuses a
trust the promisor chose to invite.** Charles Fried (*Contract as Promise*, 1981): by signing,
the borrower deliberately invokes the convention of promising to give the lender grounds to
count on repayment. Expectation damages are the right remedy, because it is fair to make a
promise-breaker hand over its equivalent; paying them remedies the wrong without undoing it.

**The argument from promise** ([1.4](lessons/01-04-contract-debt-and-the-duty-to-repay.md); Fried's premises extended to debt, paraphrased):
- **P1.** Borrowing includes a promise to repay, made by invoking the practice of promising.
- **P2.** A promise binds whether or not a court will enforce it (Hume and all three
  accounts of [1.3](lessons/01-03-promising-after-hume.md) agree, for different reasons).
- **P3.** A promise entitles the promisee to the performance, not to a substitute of the
  promisor's choosing (Shiffrin agrees, about morality).
- **P4.** A limit on what the law will compel is not a [release](#release): collateral-only
  remedies, time bars, discharge and the cost of suing limit collection for reasons of
  liberty, finality, a fresh start or cost.
- **∴ C.** A borrower who can repay, and whom the creditor has not released, owes the whole
  debt, even where the law would let him go for less.

- **Attacked at P4,** for limits both parties could see when the loan was made: on the broad
  option view ([promise in the alternative](#promise-in-the-alternative)) a promise's content
  is fixed by the whole bargain, including the law it was made under, so P4 begs the
  question. Defender: pricing a risk is not consenting to it (restaurants price in no-shows,
  yet a diner who books and never comes has broken his word), and the note says the
  borrower promises to pay. The dispute is over what fixes a promise's content: the words,
  or the words plus every rule both sides could foresee.
- **For a money debt the three positions agree:** the court's remedy is the sum due with
  interest, so "perform or pay" collapses into "pay", and all three positions hold that a
  solvent borrower owes it all. They part only where the remedy falls short of the debt.
- **For Fried, buying a release** is what respects a promise one wants out of (the café, [efficient breach](#efficient-breach)).

Lessons: [1.4](lessons/01-04-contract-debt-and-the-duty-to-repay.md)

### Prediction theory

**Holmes: the legal duty to keep a contract is nothing but a forecast of what a court will
make you pay.** "The Path of the Law" (*Harvard Law Review* 10, 1897): "The duty to keep a
contract at common law means a prediction that you must pay damages if you do not keep
it—and nothing else." At law a contract gives an option, to perform or pay; Holmes credits
the idea to Coke.

- **A claim about law, not morality.** The paragraph opens by attacking the confusion of
  legal with moral ideas, and Holmes takes the "bad man's" view only to isolate the law,
  warning his hearers not to take it for cynicism. Moving from "the law asks only for
  damages" to "I owe only damages" is the slide his own paragraph attacks.
- **Jurisprudence elsewhere:** H. L. A. Hart replied that judges and citizens use legal rules
  as standards, and that being obliged differs from having an obligation
  ([`philosophy-of-law`](../philosophy-of-law/syllabus.md) 1.2). Here the thesis is taken as
  a claim about law.
- **For a loan the option is empty:** the damages *are* the performance.
- Boss problem 1 in the [syllabus](syllabus.md) reads the sentence closely; that reading is
  left to it.

Lessons: [1.4](lessons/01-04-contract-debt-and-the-duty-to-repay.md)

### Efficient breach

**If breaking a contract and paying full damages leaves the promisor better off and the
promisee no worse off, the law should not deter the breach.** The policy law-and-economics
writers from Robert Birmingham to Richard Posner built on Holmes's option: no punitive
damages, no order to perform.

- **1.4's café.** A maker agrees to build 20 tables for 6,000 dollars; a hotel offers 10,000;
  the café can buy equivalent tables for 7,000, so its expectation damages are 1,000. Breach
  and pay, and the maker nets 9,000, 3,000 more than performing, while the café still pays a
  net 6,000.
- **Fried:** a promise was broken, and the 1,000 is a remedy, not performance; what respects
  the promise is buying a release (offer 2,000 and both gain).
- **Macneil (1982), Friedmann (1989):** a bought release also gets the tables to the hotel,
  so breach does not create the 3,000-dollar gain; it decides who keeps it. Defenders:
  bargaining for a release costs time and can fail.
- **Shiffrin:** the law may decline to order delivery for reasons of its own, such as the
  cost of supervising a reluctant performer, but may not rest the rule on the good of
  breaking promises.
- **A money debt cannot be efficiently breached.** Full compensation for an unpaid loan is
  the debt itself; keeping the money puts nothing to better use and only moves wealth from
  lender to borrower.

Lessons: [1.4](lessons/01-04-contract-debt-and-the-duty-to-repay.md)

### Promise in the alternative

**Read a contract backed by expectation damages as a promise to perform or pay, and paying
is one way of keeping it.** Daniel Markovits and Alan Schwartz (2011) read Holmes's option
into the promise itself.

- **The broad option view** extends it: a promise's content is fixed by the whole bargain,
  including the law it was made under. A lender who lends against a house alone, under a
  rule anyone could read, and charges more for the chance that the borrower hands over the
  keys, has sold that chance, and the borrower who takes it performs. This is the attack on
  P4 of the [argument from promise](#contract-as-promise).
- **Where the limit is written into the bargain,** the promise and option views agree: a
  pawn ticket that lets you redeem for a sum *or* let the shop keep the goods, owing nothing
  more, is performed by letting them go.
- **Where the law imposes the limit** (a time bar, the cost of suing, a discharge), the
  promise view says the debt is still owed; the broad option view says the limit was
  accepted when the loan was made.

Lessons: [1.4](lessons/01-04-contract-debt-and-the-duty-to-repay.md)

### Divergence thesis

**Shiffrin: contract law departs from the morality of promising, and some of its reasons
for departing are ones no virtuous agent could accept.** Seana Shiffrin ("The Divergence of
Contract and Promise", 2007) agrees with Fried about morality: absent the promisee's consent,
handing over the cash value does not keep a promise. American contract law does not track
that. It makes damages rather than performance the usual remedy, awards no punitive damages
for deliberate breach, and puts the burden of mitigation on the victim.

- **Her claim is about reasons.** Law should not aim to enforce interpersonal morality as
  such, but its rules and their justifications must be ones a virtuous agent could accept,
  and a rule justified by the economic gains of wrongful breach is not one.
- **She accepts distinctively legal reasons,** such as the burden of supervising a reluctant
  performer. She does not want courts to enforce promissory morality.

Lessons: [1.4](lessons/01-04-contract-debt-and-the-duty-to-repay.md)

### Assignment

**The creditor's transfer of a claim, by sale or gift, so that the debtor comes to owe a
stranger.** It is the third of the four marks of a [debt](#debt). The debtor cannot swap in
a new debtor without the creditor's agreement.

- **A debt can be sold; a promise cannot.** You cannot sell my promise to help you move, but
  you can sell my debt, and I then owe the buyer. A promise view must explain this; the
  natural answer is that the borrower promised inside a practice of transferable credit.
- **Hamilton and Madison (1790):** Hamilton argued that the public's paper promised payment
  to its first holder or anyone he sold it to; Madison objected that discount buyers took a
  windfall, though his plan still paid the full sum, divided differently
  ([`history-of-debt` 4.1](../history-of-debt/lessons/04-01-hamiltons-assumption-plan.md)).
- **Selling a claim transfers it; only releasing it ends it** ([1.4](lessons/01-04-contract-debt-and-the-duty-to-repay.md)). Whether a debt bought
  for pennies is owed in full to the buyer is [1.4](lessons/01-04-contract-debt-and-the-duty-to-repay.md) P2's question.
- **Not universal:** many legal systems restrict assigning a personal-injury claim before
  judgment, and some claims are made unassignable by law. That is where the "only if" half
  of the four-mark analysis is tested ([1.1](lessons/01-01-the-grammar-of-owing.md)).
- **A new debtor needs the creditor's consent:** state succession is where politics tries to
  substitute one anyway ([6.3](lessons/06-03-whose-debt-is-it.md)).

Lessons: [1.4](lessons/01-04-contract-debt-and-the-duty-to-repay.md), [1.1](lessons/01-01-the-grammar-of-owing.md), [6.3](lessons/06-03-whose-debt-is-it.md)

### Strategic default

**Walking away from a loan you can pay, where the law's remedy stops short of the debt.**
1.4's invented figures: a homeowner owes 300,000 dollars on a loan whose only remedy is the
house, now worth 200,000. She can pay but walks away; the lender takes the house and loses
100,000 against repayment, before foreclosure costs.

- **Option view:** if the terms named the house as the whole remedy and the rate priced it
  in, she performed the second branch of a [promise in the alternative](#promise-in-the-alternative).
- **Promise view:** the note records a promise to repay, and the collateral-only rule only
  limits compulsion (P4 of the [argument from promise](#contract-as-promise)).
- **Where the tool strains:** in some jurisdictions the collateral-only rule is set by
  statute, not negotiated, so it is unclear whether it is a term of the deal or a limit
  imposed from outside.
- **It is not an efficient breach:** for a money debt, whatever the borrower keeps the lender
  loses.
- **Elsewhere:** [`theology-of-debt` 6.3](../theology-of-debt/lessons/06-03-the-ethics-of-personal-debt.md)
  sets the dispute inside the Catholic tradition; [`economics-of-debt`](../economics-of-debt/syllabus.md)
  8.1 treats strategic default as an incentive effect. Boss problem 1 runs Hume, Scanlon and
  Owens on the non-recourse homeowner; this card does not.

## The justice of interest

### Proper and secondary use

**A thing's proper use is what it is made for; exchange is a secondary use of most things,
but the proper use of money.** *Politics* I.9: a shoe is for wearing, and trading it is a use
of the shoe too, but a secondary one, since shoes are not made to be bartered ("both are
uses of the shoe"). Money is the odd case: exchange is exactly what it is for.

- **Aquinas's version** (ST II-II q.78 a.1 ad 6): a silver vessel's principal use is not its
  consumption, so its use may be let; but its secondary use may be exchange, "and such use
  may not be lawfully sold". Money's principal use is "sinking it in exchange", yet "[In]
  like manner there may be some secondary use of silver money; for instance, a man might lend
  coins for show, or to be used as security".
- **So the line runs by the use lent, not the object.** The object's principal use only fixes
  the default case, and "principal" leaves room for other uses.

Lessons: [2.1](lessons/02-01-aristotle-money-is-barren.md), [2.2](lessons/02-02-aquinas-selling-what-does-not-exist.md)

### Tokos

**Greek for both "offspring" and "interest": money born of money.** *Politics* I.10
(Jowett, 1921): "this term interest, which means the birth of money from money, is applied
to the breeding of money because the offspring resembles the parent."

- **The text grants that lent money increases.** The charge is that the increase is
  unnatural, not that it is impossible. "Barren" is the later tradition's word: money has no
  natural power to breed, so its breeding is against nature.
- **Echoes:** the Sumerian *máš*, "young goat" ([`history-of-debt` 1.1](../history-of-debt/lessons/01-01-credit-before-coins.md));
  Basil's pun and the Fathers' charge of cruelty rather than sterility
  ([`theology-of-debt` 4.1](../theology-of-debt/lessons/04-01-the-fathers-against-usury.md));
  Antonio's "a breed for barren metal" (*Merchant of Venice* I.iii).
- **Marx** agrees that money does not breed, for a different reason: the return is real, and
  the breeding is only how it looks ([fetish form](#fetish-form)).

Lessons: [2.1](lessons/02-01-aristotle-money-is-barren.md), [2.4](lessons/02-04-marx-interest-bearing-capital.md)

### Chrematistike

**Wealth-getting: natural when it serves the household's use and stops at enough,
unnatural when it seeks more money without limit.** Household management (*oikonomia*) uses
what wealth-getting provides (*Politics* I.8). The text draws two distinctions: using vs
providing (I.8), then natural wealth-getting, part of household management and limited, vs
the kind that works by exchange (retail trade, usury, hire), which is unlimited and censured
(I.9–11).

- **Why a limit is natural:** wealth is "a number of instruments" for living, and no art needs
  unlimited instruments (I.8). Aristotle quotes Solon's line that no bound to riches is fixed
  for man, only to deny it.
- **Why the unlimited kind arises:** people "intent upon living only, and not upon living
  well" want unlimited means (I.9).
- **Natural increase:** "the art of getting wealth out of fruits and animals is always
  natural" (I.10). I.11 lists usury in the art "which consists in exchange"; I.10 calls it the
  most unnatural.

| Circuit | Marx's notation | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| household: shoe → coin → bread | C–M–C | natural; stops at enough |
| retail trade: coin → goods → more coin | M–C–M′ | censured; no limit; gains "from one another" |
| usury: coin → more coin | M–M′ | the most unnatural; has lost the exchange in the middle |

Lessons: [2.1](lessons/02-01-aristotle-money-is-barren.md), [2.4](lessons/02-04-marx-interest-bearing-capital.md)

### Money as convention

**Money is a measure made by agreement, standing in for the need that holds exchange
together.** *Nicomachean Ethics* V.5 (Ross): "money has become by convention a sort of
representative of demand; and this is why it has the name 'money' (*nomisma*)—because it
exists not by nature but by law (*nomos*) and it is in our power to change it and make it
useless." It makes goods commensurable, and for future exchange it is "as it were our
surety".

- **What this does to "unnatural":** the "nature" of money in premise 3 of [Aristotle's
  argument](#aristotle-against-tokos) is an instituted purpose, not a physical nature, so
  "unnatural" means turned from the use money was given.
- **Hume's answer to the barren coin:** what a borrower takes is in effect labour and
  commodities, not metal ([2.3](lessons/02-03-justifying-interest.md)).
- **Coin and credit:** Odd Langholm (*The Aristotelian Analysis of Usury*, 1984) reads the
  whole Aristotelian theory as built on money as coin, and Aquinas as keeping the idea of
  sterility while widening it from coin to anything lent by *mutuum*. Whether credit money
  has a different purpose is a conceptual question the date of a text cannot answer.

Lessons: [2.1](lessons/02-01-aristotle-money-is-barren.md)

### Aristotle against tokos

**Usury is the most unnatural mode of wealth-getting: it makes a tool for exchange breed.**
*Politics* I.10 (1258a38–b8, Jowett as revised for the Oxford *Works of Aristotle*, 1921):
"The most hated sort, and with the greatest reason, is usury, which makes a gain out of money
itself, and not from the natural object of it." Skeleton (*Politics* I.8–10 with *NE* V.5;
premise 5 is implicit in "most"):

1. Acquisition is natural when it serves use, and use sets a limit (I.8).
2. Acquisition for its own sake is unnatural and unlimited; retail trade gains "from one
   another" rather than from nature (I.9–10).
3. Money exists for exchange; it was introduced, by convention, to measure goods and carry
   them between people who need each other's products (I.9; *NE* V.5).
4. Usury gains from money itself: the lender's money comes back increased without being used
   in exchange, the use it was provided for (I.10).
5. Unnaturalness comes in degrees: retail trade departs from nature once, in its end; usury
   twice, in its end and in its means.

∴ Of all modes of getting wealth, usury is the most unnatural and the most reasonably hated.

- **Premises 1–2 concern the purpose of acquisition, 3 the nature of money,** and 4 applies
  both to the lender.
- **No premise needs the borrower to get less than he gave.** The argument condemns the form
  of the gain, not its size or its victim: 101 drachmas for 100, or a rich borrower, changes
  nothing. That separates it from the Fathers' charge of cruelty and from Module 3's
  exploitation arguments. The *Ethics* files petty high-rate lenders under meanness, with
  pimps and gamesters, who take "more than they ought and from wrong sources" (*NE* IV.1).
- **Attacked at premise 4,** at the words "a gain out of money itself": see
  [gain out of money itself](#gain-out-of-money-itself).

Lessons: [2.1](lessons/02-01-aristotle-money-is-barren.md)

### Gain out of money itself

**The contested phrase of Aristotle's premise 4: does usury's gain come from money itself,
or from what the borrower did with it?** The Greek (*ouk eph' hoper eporisthē*, 1258b2–4)
says only "not from that for which it was provided"; "natural" in Jowett's "the natural
object of it" is the translator's word.

- **The critic's case:** a loan *is* an exchange, of present money for future money, and I.11
  itself files usury under the art "which consists in exchange". If money now is worth more
  to its holder than the same sum next year ([2.3](lessons/02-03-justifying-interest.md)), the increase is the price of an
  exchange, and exchange is what money was provided for.
- **The defender's case:** the lender's claim is fixed in money whatever it bought and however
  it fared; nothing he receives depends on an exchange or a harvest, so his gain comes from
  the contract alone.
- **Two readings, tested on a loan for ewes** (the shepherd repays 110 from the spring lambs):
  - *Source reading:* the phrase is about where the increase comes from. Here it comes from
    lambs, so the interest is a share of natural increase and premise 4 fails; pushed, it
    fails for nearly every loan, since every borrower repays out of some harvest, wage or
    trade.
  - *Form reading:* the phrase is about the lender's contract. A fixed larger sum is owed
    whether the ewes lamb or die, so this loan is usury exactly as a loan to a hungry
    neighbour is.
- **The scholastics drew their line where the form reading does:** an investor who shares a
  venture's gains and losses may share its profit; a lender owed a fixed sum may not
  ([`theology-of-debt` 4.5](../theology-of-debt/lessons/04-05-contracts-that-are-not-loans.md)).
  The dispute is over what counts as money's use, and premise 3 does not settle it.

Lessons: [2.1](lessons/02-01-aristotle-money-is-barren.md)

### Consumptibles

**Goods whose use is their consumption, so that their use cannot be granted apart from the
thing.** We drink wine and eat wheat; a house or a silver vessel, by contrast, can be used and
still returned.

- **Money counts by function, not physics.** Coins outlive their spending (a coin can
  circulate for decades); money is a consumptible because using it means parting with it.
  "Consumptible" does not mean destroyed.
- **The Roman source hedges.** The passage Aquinas cites for the category (a.1 ad 3) counts
  coin among things consumed by use only with "and perhaps we may also say coined money"
  (*Institutes* II.4.2, Moyle).
- **Wear is not consumption.** A van wears with every kilometre, so read charitably the line
  is the Roman one: a loan for use returns the very thing lent, a *mutuum* only its
  equivalent, and only where the same thing comes back can ownership stay with the lender
  (*Institutes* III.14.2).

Lessons: [2.2](lessons/02-02-aquinas-selling-what-does-not-exist.md)

### Mutuum

**The loan for consumption: ownership passes to the borrower, and an equal quantity of the
same kind comes back.** *Institutes* III.14: things counted, weighed or measured are lent so
that "meum or mine becomes tuum or thine"; the borrower returns not the same things but an
equal quantity of the same kind, and bears the risk of loss (if the wheat burns, he still owes
wheat; q.78 a.2 ad 5).

- **Contrast the loan for use,** which returns "the same identical thing" and must be
  gratuitous, since a fee makes it hire (III.14.2).
- **It fits the four marks:** the borrower owes a quantity, not a thing ([1.1](lessons/01-01-the-grammar-of-owing.md)).
- **Böhm-Bawerk concedes the transfer** ("full and free possession") and, like Aquinas,
  mocked the jurists' separate "use" of lent money (a coal burned to cinders on New Year's
  Day, yet somehow used all year). He denies only that returning the same kind and amount is
  equal value: a difference of date is a difference of good. That is where the two traditions
  part ([2.3](lessons/02-03-justifying-interest.md)).

Lessons: [2.2](lessons/02-02-aquinas-selling-what-does-not-exist.md), [2.3](lessons/02-03-justifying-interest.md)

### Commutative justice

**Justice in exchanges between persons, measured by equality of thing to thing.** A lender is
repaid justly if he is repaid as much as he lent, and no more (q.78 a.1 ad 5). It is premise
1 of the [consumptibles argument](#consumptibles-argument).

- **Compensating a real loss restores equality rather than exceeding it**, which is why
  Aquinas allows agreed compensation for a loss actually incurred (a.2 ad 1).
- **Böhm-Bawerk keeps the premise and changes what it measures:** equal values, fixed where
  valuations meet in a market (his supplied premise 4, [2.3](lessons/02-03-justifying-interest.md)).
- **The duty to repay** read as commutative justice (CCC 2409–2411) is
  [`theology-of-debt` 6.3](../theology-of-debt/lessons/06-03-the-ethics-of-personal-debt.md)'s.

Lessons: [2.2](lessons/02-02-aquinas-selling-what-does-not-exist.md), [2.3](lessons/02-03-justifying-interest.md)

### Consumptibles argument

**Charging for a loan of money sells what does not exist, so it breaks the equality of
exchange at any rate and to any borrower.** ST II-II q.78 a.1 (English Dominican Province
translation, 1920): "To take usury for money lent is unjust in itself, because this is to sell
what does not exist, and this evidently leads to inequality which is contrary to justice."
Reconstructed from a.1 and its replies (the numbering is [2.2](lessons/02-02-aquinas-selling-what-does-not-exist.md)'s):

- **P1.** Justice in exchange requires equality: a lender is repaid justly if repaid as much
  as he lent, and no more ([commutative justice](#commutative-justice); a.1 ad 5).
- **P2.** Some things are used by being used up (wine, wheat): their use cannot be counted
  apart from the thing ([consumptibles](#consumptibles)).
- **P3.** So granting their use grants the thing: lending them transfers ownership, and an
  equal quantity of the same kind returns (the [*mutuum*](#mutuum)); the borrower bears the
  risk.
- **P4.** Where use is not consumption, as with a house, use and ownership can be split: the
  owner may sell the use (rent) and reclaim the thing.
- **P5.** In a *mutuum* the equal return pays for everything handed over; a further charge
  "for the use" prices the thing a second time or a use that does not exist apart from it.
- **P6.** Money was invented for exchange, so "the proper and principal use of money is its
  consumption or alienation whereby it is sunk in exchange".
- **∴ C.** A loan of money is a *mutuum*; usury sells what does not exist and breaks the
  equality of exchange. It is unjust in itself, and what was taken must be restored.

- **"Unjust in itself":** the wrong lies in the exchange, whatever the rate, the borrower's
  need or the lender's motive. It would hold if the borrower were a millionaire; taking
  unfair advantage of need is a different charge ([exploitation](#exploitation)).
- **What it does not forbid:** agreed compensation for a loss the loan actually causes, but
  not profit the lender might have made, since that sells what he does not yet have (a.2 ad 1);
  profit-sharing by an investor who keeps ownership and risk (a.2 ad 5). See
  [extrinsic titles](#extrinsic-titles). Its whole weight falls on one contract.
- **Attacked at P6:** it is functional, not physical. Objection 6 notes that coin silver and
  vessel silver are one metal; Aquinas answers only by appeal to money's *principal* use, and
  the Roman text he cites hedges with "perhaps".
- **Attacked at P5, the deeper objection:** P5 counts two things a lender might sell, the money
  and its use, and finds the second empty. The critic says she sells neither: she is paid for
  going without her money for a year. P5 excludes that third thing only by assuming that a
  loan transfers nothing but a quantity, which is the question at issue (begging the question,
  [`philosophical-method` 2.3](../philosophical-method/lessons/02-03-informal-fallacies-and-when-they-arent.md)).
  Defenders answer that going without is an absence, not something the lender owns, and that
  where it causes a real loss, P1 itself allows repayment.
- **Why rent survives:** the landlord sells a use of a thing she still owns; the lender, once
  the money has passed, owns only a claim. The critic presses that the landlord too is paid
  for going without, and that the lender bears the risk of non-payment; the dispute becomes
  whether giving up the present use of money is something a person can sell ([2.3](lessons/02-03-justifying-interest.md)).
- **Doctrine and authority** belong to [`theology-of-debt` 4.3](../theology-of-debt/lessons/04-03-aquinas-on-usury-ii-ii-q78.md)
  and [5.4](../theology-of-debt/lessons/05-04-did-the-usury-doctrine-change.md); here the
  argument is tested as philosophy. Boss problem 2 asks for a test case of your own.

Lessons: [2.2](lessons/02-02-aquinas-selling-what-does-not-exist.md)

### Sale of time

**The older argument that the usurer sells time, which is common to every creature and so
no one's to sell.** Usually traced to William of Auxerre's *Summa aurea* early in the
thirteenth century; paraphrased, not quoted.

- **Not q.78's argument.** In q.78 time enters only at a.2 ad 7, where raising a price
  because payment will come later counts as charging for a loan.
- **It proves too much:** a landlord also charges by the year. Unless it adds that the
  landlord sells the use of a thing, with time only measuring it, it condemns rent along with
  interest, and that addition is the consumptibles argument's move.

Lessons: [2.2](lessons/02-02-aquinas-selling-what-does-not-exist.md)

### Extrinsic titles

**Grounds for receiving more than the principal because of something outside the loan
itself.** q.78 already contains the first: a lender may agree to be compensated for a loss
the loan actually causes him, but not for a profit he might have made, since that would sell
what he does not yet have (a.2 ad 1). An investor who keeps ownership and risk may share a
merchant's profit (a.2 ad 5).

- **Later titles:** loss incurred and profit forgone became two of the later titles, and
  later moralists added a separate title for real risk to the principal. Their history and
  canon law belong to [`theology-of-debt` 4.4](../theology-of-debt/lessons/04-04-the-extrinsic-titles.md)
  (whose Source is a.2 ad 1; paraphrase it rather than quote it).
- **They are the argument's own limits,** not exceptions smuggled in: the target is a price
  *for the loan*.
- **The later admission of profit forgone** does not by itself show that P5 or P6 of the
  [consumptibles argument](#consumptibles-argument) is false.
- **Marx quotes Luther** against lenders who charge every borrower for losses they never
  suffered: the titles as a critic saw their abuse ([2.4](lessons/02-04-marx-interest-bearing-capital.md)).

Lessons: [2.2](lessons/02-02-aquinas-selling-what-does-not-exist.md)

### Abstinence theory

**Interest rewards the lender's sacrifice of present enjoyment, as wages reward toil.**
Nassau Senior (*An Outline of the Science of Political Economy*, 1836; paraphrased, nothing of
his quoted) ranked abstinence a third instrument of production beside labour and natural
agents; profit, interest included, is its reward. On this view interest is earned by a cost
the lender bears.

- **Böhm-Bawerk:** saving is a condition of capital, and no pure negation, since people find
  it hard (*Positive Theory* Book II, ch. VI); but it does not explain interest, much of which
  is "pocketed" with no abstinence worth rewarding (VI.9).
- **Marx's mockery** (*Capital* vol. 1, ch. XXIV §3, Moore and Aveling): "If the corn is not all
  eaten, but part of it also sown — abstinence of the capitalist. If the wine gets time to
  mature — abstinence of the capitalist." Setting seed aside and letting wine age are
  conditions of the labour process whoever owns them; every act is "abstinence" from its
  opposite; abstinence explains why an owner has a sum to lend, not why the sum grows. His
  general charge: reasons for dividing the profit pose as reasons there is a profit to divide.
- **Where the defenders of interest part** ([2.3](lessons/02-03-justifying-interest.md)): is interest owed for a cost the lender
  bears (abstinence, forgone profit, risk), or for a difference in value that a market sets,
  whatever this lender feels (the agio)?
- **Its purest form is the $\rho$ layer** of the [rate decomposition](#rate-decomposition).

Lessons: [2.3](lessons/02-03-justifying-interest.md), [2.4](lessons/02-04-marx-interest-bearing-capital.md)

### Agio

**Böhm-Bawerk's premium of present over future goods of like kind and number, which he
identifies with interest.** *The Positive Theory of Capital* (tr. William Smart, 1891), VI.1:
"A loan is nothing else than a real and true exchange of present goods for future goods ... He
must thus pay an 'agio' or premium ..., and this agio is interest."

**Three grounds** (V.2–4):
- **(a) Want and provision:** many people are worse provided now than they expect to be later,
  and a present good can nearly always be kept for later, so it is seldom worth less.
- **(b) Underestimate of the future:** weak imagination, defect of will, and the shortness and
  uncertainty of life. By his own account this values future goods "at a lower figure than
  expresses their true value" (V.3).
- **(c) Technical superiority:** present goods put into longer, roundabout production yield
  more.

- **Risk is separate:** a premium for risk is a deduction for uncertainty, not interest proper
  (V.1).
- **Usury** lies not in any gain on a loan "but in the immoderate extent of that gain" (VI.9),
  as where lenders do not compete.
- **Explanation vs justification:** Böhm-Bawerk blamed much harm on confusing the theoretical
  problem of interest with the socio-political one; his premises 1–3 answer the first, and
  only premise 4 carries the argument into the second ([Böhm-Bawerk's case](#boehm-bawerks-case-for-interest)).
- **Date:** cite Smart's translation (1891). Smart's preface dates the German original to
  Innsbruck, 1888; do not assert 1889.

Lessons: [2.3](lessons/02-03-justifying-interest.md)

### Boehm-Bawerk's case for interest

**A loan exchanges present goods for more valuable future ones, so an equal exchange needs
a premium, and interest as such is not unjust.** *Positive Theory*, Books V–VI, reconstructed:

1. Present goods are, as a rule, worth more than future goods of like kind and number (V.1).
   *(Empirical.)*
2. Three grounds make it so (V.2–4): see [agio](#agio). *(Empirical.)*
3. A loan is an exchange of present goods for future goods (VI.1). *(Conceptual.)*
4. *(Supplied.)* An exchange is just when equal values change hands, value being fixed where
   valuations meet in a market. *(Normative: the scholastics' equality premise, measuring
   something else.)*
5. So a present sum is matched in value only by a larger future sum, and the difference is
   interest (VI.1).
6. A premium for risk is a separate deduction, not interest proper (V.1).

∴ Interest as such is not unjust; usury is its immoderate extent (VI.9).

- **Attacked at premise 4, applied to ground (b):** the part of the agio that exists because
  people misjudge their own futures measures an error, not a value, and a lender who collects
  it profits from the error. Frank Ramsey ("A Mathematical Theory of Saving", 1928; paraphrase
  only) refused to discount later enjoyments in his main model, judging the practice
  ethically indefensible and a failure of imagination ([time preference](#time-preference)).
- **Ground (a) at its sharpest:** Böhm-Bawerk's first proof of the agio is the usurious terms
  people in distress accept (a smallholder after a bad harvest, [2.3](lessons/02-03-justifying-interest.md) Example 2).
- **The defender:** ground (a) for the prosperous and ground (c) involve no error; a just price
  is the common estimate, not each party's ideal valuation; and impatience is not irrational,
  since for Hume preferring one's acknowledged lesser good to one's greater is not contrary to
  reason (*Treatise* II.iii.3).
- **The crux with Aquinas:** "entirely similar, but future" and "but for the fact that the one
  belongs to the present, the other to the future". For Aquinas returning the same kind and
  amount is equality; for Böhm-Bawerk a difference of date is a difference of good.
- **Marx's charge reaches it too:** reasons for dividing a profit posing as reasons for its
  existence ([2.4](lessons/02-04-marx-interest-bearing-capital.md)).

Lessons: [2.3](lessons/02-03-justifying-interest.md), [2.4](lessons/02-04-marx-interest-bearing-capital.md)

### Opportunity cost

**The return a lender forgoes by lending: what the money would earn elsewhere, the safe rate
$r_f$.** Hume tied interest to the profits of trade (*Of Interest*, 1752, Green and Grose):
"High interest arises from three circumstances: A great demand for borrowing; little riches to
supply that demand; and great profits arising from commerce".

- **Hume's reading:** the first two circumstances are habits of prodigality and frugality, how
  a people weighs present against future; the third is what money earns in trade. Since no
  one lends cheap while trade pays well, interest and profits hold each other level. The
  three circumstances are "a clear proof of the small advance of commerce and industry, not
  of the scarcity of gold and silver".
- **A relative justification.** It justifies the going rate only if the going rate is itself
  justified, and the forgone alternative is another loan or a profit, so it passes the
  question down to time preference and productivity. Whether productivity's return belongs to
  the owner is [2.4](lessons/02-04-marx-interest-bearing-capital.md)'s question.
- **Explanation is not justification:** Hume's circumstances say what sets the rate, not
  whether taking it is just.
- **Marx's observation** (ch. XXIII): an owner using his own capital books "interest" on it as
  if lent to himself, and the rest as the return to his activity. Economists call that imputed
  interest an opportunity cost; for Marx a split fixed by who owns what has hardened into two
  supposedly different kinds of income.

Lessons: [2.3](lessons/02-03-justifying-interest.md), [2.4](lessons/02-04-marx-interest-bearing-capital.md)

### Time preference

**Pure time preference ($\rho$): valuing a good less merely because it comes later.** It is
Böhm-Bawerk's ground (b) and Senior's abstinence at its purest.

- **Ramsey's charge** ("A Mathematical Theory of Saving", *Economic Journal* 38, 1928;
  paraphrase only): in his main model of a nation's saving he assumed no discounting of later
  enjoyments, calling the practice ethically indefensible and a product of weak imagination.
  Section II does admit a rate, so say "in his main model".
- **Hume's counter** (*Treatise* II.iii.3): preferring one's acknowledged lesser good to one's
  greater is not contrary to reason.
- **It is a layer of the safe rate, not an addition to it.** In the Euler equation with flat
  consumption the safe rate equals $\rho$ ([`grad-macro` 1.3](../grad-macro/lessons/01-03-euler-transversality.md));
  with consumption growing at $g$, $r = \rho + \sigma g$ ([`grad-macro` 2.3](../grad-macro/lessons/02-03-ramsey-cass-koopmans.md)).
  Adding $\rho$ on top of $r_f$ double-counts (a build correction to the syllabus and Boss 2(c)).
- **Ramsey's charge reaches one layer only:** with $\rho = 0$ a growing economy still has a
  positive safe rate, $r = \sigma g$, and risk and opportunity cost are untouched.
- **In the social discount rate** $r = \rho + \sigma g$ ([6.2](lessons/06-02-public-debt-and-the-unborn.md)), $\rho$ is the value judgment,
  $g$ the forecast, and $\sigma$ partly each.
- The general debate over discounting belongs to
  [`philosophy-of-economics`](../philosophy-of-economics/syllabus.md) 4.1; here the only
  question is whether time preference can ground a lender's claim.

Lessons: [2.3](lessons/02-03-justifying-interest.md), [6.2](lessons/06-02-public-debt-and-the-unborn.md)

### Rate decomposition

**A loan rate stacks layers, each something a defender says the lender gives up, plus a
residual no one has yet justified.** With $i$ the loan rate, $r_f$ the safe rate, $p$ the
chance of default, $\ell$ the share of what is owed lost if it happens, and $\pi$ a residual
premium:

$$1+i=\frac{(1+r_f)(1+\pi)}{1-p\ell}, \qquad 1+b=\frac{1+r_f}{1-p\ell}.$$

*In words:* match the safe return, gross it up so that expected repayment after defaults still
matches it, and call whatever remains the residual. The break-even rate $b$ sets $\pi = 0$.

| Layer (points) | What the lender gives up | Whose ground |
|---|---|---|
| $\rho$ | waiting as such | Böhm-Bawerk's (b); Senior's abstinence at its purest |
| $r_f - \rho$ | a present good's edge when later provision is better | Böhm-Bawerk's (a), with (c) behind it |
| $b - r_f$ | safety | the premium for risk, outside interest proper |
| $i - b$ | nothing yet named | none, without more argument |

- **The layers add to $i$ exactly.** The first two together are the safe rate, the lender's
  [opportunity cost](#opportunity-cost), which Hume's link between interest and profits
  covers jointly. $\rho$ is nested inside the safe rate, never added to it.
- **The additive shortcut** $i \approx r_f + p\ell + \pi$ is close for safe loans and
  understates the risk layer for risky ones.
- **Worked in [2.3](lessons/02-03-justifying-interest.md)** (illustrative; $r_f = 4\%$, $\rho = 1.5\%$, nothing recovered on
  default): a 9% loan to a workshop with $p = 4\%$ splits $1.50 + 2.50 + 4.33 + 0.67$; a 35%
  harvest loan with $p = 10\%$ splits $1.50 + 2.50 + 11.56 + 19.44$. The borrower would pay up
  to 40%, so of the 24.44-point gap between break-even and his ceiling the lender takes 19.44,
  about four-fifths: a question about dividing the gains ([3.1](lessons/03-01-what-exploitation-is.md)).
- **For small loans** ([3.2](lessons/03-02-predatory-lending-and-usury-caps.md)) add a fixed servicing cost per loan, $F/L$ per dollar lent, and
  a markup above the whole stack. The monthly break-even rate is $F/L$ + expected loss + cost
  of funds; the stack stands in for [fair market value](#fair-market-value), only the markup
  raises the exploitation question, and a cap below the stack acts as a minimum loan size.
- Boss problem 2(c) decomposes a 12% loan with figures it supplies; the card does not.

Lessons: [2.3](lessons/02-03-justifying-interest.md), [3.2](lessons/03-02-predatory-lending-and-usury-caps.md)

### Interest-bearing capital

**Marx: the lender hands over something real, money's power to command a profit, but
contributes nothing to producing it; interest is a share of surplus value paid to ownership.**
*Capital* vol. 3, Part V (chs. XXI–XXIV, Untermann's translation, 1909): capital as ownership
set against capital as function. Marx's own example (ch. XXI): A lends B 100 pounds; B's
business earns the average profit of 20 percent, ends the year with 120, repays A 105 and
keeps 15.

1. **Surplus value.** All profit is [surplus value](#surplus-value), value workers produce
   beyond the value of their labour-power (vol. 1).
2. **Money as capital.** Under capitalism a sum of money has an extra use-value: advanced as
   capital, it brings in the average profit (ch. XXI).
3. **Lending is not selling.** The lender parts with that use-value for a time, gets no
   equivalent back, and remains the owner; the borrower returns the sum plus part of the
   profit (ch. XXI).
4. **Interest is a share.** Interest is the part of the profit paid to the owner; the
   remainder, [profit of enterprise](#profit-of-enterprise), stays with the functioning
   capitalist (chs. XXI, XXIII). To call interest the "price" of capital is an irrational
   expression; a given profit is simply split.
5. **No natural rate.** The rate is capped by the rate of profit and tends to move with it,
   has no definable floor, and is set by competition, custom and legal tradition
   (chs. XXI–XXII).
6. **Ownership, not function.** The lender performs no function in production, so his only
   title to interest is ownership (ch. XXIII).

∴ Interest rewards ownership, not a contribution to production, and money that seems to breed
(M→M′) is the form that hides where interest comes from (ch. XXIV).

- **Against the justifications:** "The reasons for dividing the profit among two kinds of
  capitalists thus turn surreptitiously into reasons for the existence of the surplus-value to
  be divided" (ch. XXIII). It fits Senior's [abstinence](#abstinence-theory), and a Marxist can
  press it against Böhm-Bawerk's time preference, which appeared after Marx's death.
- **Attacked at premise 1,** which carries the whole conclusion about where interest comes
  from: most economists abandoned the labour theory of value after the marginalist revolution
  of the 1870s, and Böhm-Bawerk argued that vol. 3's prices of production contradict vol. 1
  (*Karl Marx and the Close of His System*, 1896; English 1898). Defenders: G. A. Cohen (1979)
  argued the charge needs only that workers alone produce the product that has value, while
  owners who do no work receive part of it. The critic: someone had to save the capital, and
  whether only labour counts as a contribution is what is in dispute.
- **Not Aristotle's or Aquinas's view:** Marx agrees money does not breed but grants the
  lender a real use-value to transfer. Proudhon complained that lending at interest sells the
  same article over and over; Marx answered that lending is not selling at all.
- **Consumer credit stalls at premise 2:** a worker who pawns a coat to pay rent borrows for
  necessities, so there is no profit for the interest to share. Marx keeps such lending out of
  this analysis as the older form of usurer's capital (ch. XXXVI) and calls the overcharging
  of workers there a "secondary exploitation".
- **Is interest unjust, for Marx?** He calls Gilbart's appeal to "natural justice" nonsense and
  measures justice by correspondence to the mode of production (ch. XXI); whether his analysis
  still implies an injustice is disputed (Allen Wood, 1972, no; Ziyad Husami, 1978, yes, by a
  standard beyond capitalism). One can accept the explanation and deny the wrong, or the
  reverse.

Lessons: [2.4](lessons/02-04-marx-interest-bearing-capital.md)

### Surplus value

**The value workers produce beyond the value of their own labour-power: on Marx's account
the one fund from which profit, rent and interest are all paid.** The value of a product is
the labour socially necessary to make it; workers are paid the value of their labour-power,
what it costs to maintain them, and work longer than it takes to produce that value
(*Capital* vol. 1).

- **Measures:** with $c$ constant capital, $v$ variable capital (wages) and $s$ surplus value,
  the rate of surplus value is $s/v$ and the rate of profit $s/(c+v)$ ([Marx's value arithmetic](#marxs-value-arithmetic)).
- **Exploitation without an unfair price:** "The rate of surplus-value is therefore an exact
  expression for the degree of exploitation of labour-power by capital, or of the labourer by
  the capitalist" (vol. 1, ch. IX §1, Moore and Aveling). It mentions no price
  ([structural exploitation](#structural-exploitation)).
- **Who takes the interest makes no difference to the workers,** Marx says; only a change in
  the rate of surplus value would.
- **Three kinds of claim:** that interest is a share of surplus value is explanatory
  (empirical); that a loan of capital is not a sale is conceptual; whether any of it makes
  interest unjust is normative, and Marx is guarded on it.

Lessons: [2.4](lessons/02-04-marx-interest-bearing-capital.md), [3.1](lessons/03-01-what-exploitation-is.md)

### Profit of enterprise

**The part of the profit left to the functioning capitalist after interest is paid.** In
Marx's example, 15 of the 20 (chs. XXI, XXIII).

- **How it looks:** like the fruit of the capitalist's own activity, even a wage for managing
  (ch. XXIII); and, in the [fetish form](#fetish-form), like a mere by-product while interest
  looks like capital's first fruit.
- **An owner on his own capital** splits his profit the same way, booking part as "interest"
  and the rest as profit of enterprise (ch. XXIII).

Lessons: [2.4](lessons/02-04-marx-interest-bearing-capital.md)

### Fetish form

**Marx: in interest-bearing capital a relation among people appears as a property of money
itself, and the order of derivation is turned upside down.** *Capital* vol. 3, ch. XXIV
(Untermann): "In the interest-bearing capital, the relations of capital assume their most
externalised and most fetish-like form. ... It becomes a faculty of money to generate value
and yield interest, just as it is a faculty of a pear tree to bear pears."

- **Two illusions.** First, a social relation shows up as a property of a thing, extending
  vol. 1's fetishism of commodities (ch. I §4). Second, interest looks like "the typical fruit
  of capital, the primal thing", and profit of enterprise like a by-product, when on Marx's
  account interest is carved out of profit.
- **Read the verbs:** "appears", "looks ... as though". Marx describes how a loan looks from
  the lender's chair; he does not say money breeds.
- **M→M′ cuts out the middle:** the whole movement is M–C…C′–M′, and the lender's view skips
  everything between.
- **Nothing to uncover in consumer credit:** behind a pawned coat's M→M′ stands no workshop,
  only a wage ([2.4](lessons/02-04-marx-interest-bearing-capital.md) Example 2).

Lessons: [2.4](lessons/02-04-marx-interest-bearing-capital.md)

## Exploitation and the vulnerable borrower

### Exploitation

**Taking unfair advantage: using some feature of another's situation to gain on terms
unfair to her, the unfairness lying in how the deal's gains are split.** Alan Wertheimer's
transactional account (*Exploitation*, 1996, paraphrased; premise 5 from his *Coercion*,
1987):

1. **To exploit is to take unfair advantage.** A exploits B when A gains from a transaction
   with B by using some feature of B's situation, on terms that are unfair to B.
2. **The unfairness lies in the [division of the gains](#division-of-the-gains).**
3. **Fair terms are [fair market value](#fair-market-value).**
4. **Harm is not required.** The exploitation may be [mutually advantageous](#mutually-advantageous-exploitation)
   or harmful; either way B is worse off than fair terms would leave her.
5. **Consent does not cleanse it.** Only a threat coerces; an offer adds an option, however
   desperate B is, so her consent can be valid ([coercion](#coercion)).

∴ A loan can be honest, freely accepted and good for the borrower, and still exploit her, if
its price exceeds fair market value because the lender used her *special* lack of
alternatives. Exploitation is its own wrong, not fraud (consent defeated by deceit), coercion
(consent forced by a threat) or harm (a loss measured against no transaction).

- **"Special" does the work.** Locke (*Venditio*, 1661): a merchant may sell corn in a famine
  town at that town's price, but may not charge a ship in distress many times the usual price
  of an anchor. General scarcity stays in the fair price; this buyer's plight is screened out.
- **Using need is not yet exploiting.** A surgeon who sets your broken leg for the usual fee
  profits from your injury; the moral sense needs *unfair* advantage.
- **Attacked at premise 3, twice.** Where no market exists (the only lender in reach), the
  hypothetical price must be constructed, and constructions can be contested. And a
  competitive price reflects the parties' alternatives, so if poverty or injustice set those,
  the benchmark writes them into the fair price: the charge Ruth Sample and the structural
  theorists press. Wertheimer accepts the gap (a price at fair market value may be unjust all
  things considered and still not exploitative); the critic answers that this shrinks what
  "exploitation" can condemn.
- **Rivals between the poles:** Robert Goodin (1987) makes exploiting a breach of a norm of
  protecting the vulnerable, whatever caused the vulnerability; Ruth Sample (2003) a failure
  of respect, as in profiting from someone's unmet basic needs or from an injustice done to
  her. The structural rival is Marx's ([structural exploitation](#structural-exploitation)).
- **Applied to lending** ([3.2](lessons/03-02-predatory-lending-and-usury-caps.md)): a loan's price exploits only by its markup above the cost
  stack (funds, expected loss, servicing). A 180% APR charged at cost is not exploitative on
  this account; a local monopolist's markup above it is.
- **Applied to Shylock** ([3.3](lessons/03-03-what-may-be-pledged.md)): an interest-free loan gives all the priced gains to the
  borrower, so if the bond wrongs Antonio the wrong is in the security, not the division.
- **Another benchmark:** modern Catholic teaching measures a rate by what the borrower can
  bear rather than by the loan's cost ([`theology-of-debt` 6.1](../theology-of-debt/lessons/06-01-usury-and-finance-in-modern-teaching.md)).
- **Separate from Aquinas's usury charge,** which condemns any agreed surplus on a *mutuum*,
  at 1 percent to a rich borrower, on grounds of equality, not advantage-taking ([2.2](lessons/02-02-aquinas-selling-what-does-not-exist.md)).

Lessons: [3.1](lessons/03-01-what-exploitation-is.md), [3.2](lessons/03-02-predatory-lending-and-usury-caps.md), [3.3](lessons/03-03-what-may-be-pledged.md)

### Mutually advantageous exploitation

**Exploitation that leaves the victim better off than no deal at all, but worse off than a
fair deal.** Measure her against fair terms, not against no transaction. If she ends better
off than with no transaction, the exploitation is mutually advantageous; if worse off,
harmful (premise 4 of [exploitation](#exploitation)).

- **The central cases are mutually advantageous.** Harmful exploitation, worse than no deal,
  usually arrives with fraud or coercion.
- **Consensual exploitation:** both gain, she agrees with open eyes, and it can still be wrong.
- **The village seed loan** ([3.1](lessons/03-01-what-exploitation-is.md)): a farmer whose crop was flattened needs seed now; the
  market town's competing lenders charge 15% for loans this risky but are days away; the
  village lender asks 60%. She gains (without seed she loses the season), she agrees, and the
  rate is four times fair market value because she alone cannot reach another lender.
- **Weight and force:** that such a loan wrongs her does not by itself settle whether the
  state may forbid it ([moral weight and moral force](#moral-weight-and-moral-force)).

Lessons: [3.1](lessons/03-01-what-exploitation-is.md)

### Division of the gains

**A deal creates gains for both sides; exploitation is a complaint about the split, not
about whether the deal happened.** A transaction is unfair to B when A's share of what it
yields exceeds what fair terms would give him (premise 2 of [exploitation](#exploitation)).

- **The range.** The lender's *floor* is the lowest rate at which lending beats his next-best
  use of the money; the borrower's *ceiling* is the highest rate at which the loan still beats
  no loan. Past the ceiling she is worse off than with no loan: harmful exploitation. Where
  the fair rate sits inside the range depends on the benchmark.
- **The village loan** ([3.1](lessons/03-01-what-exploitation-is.md), illustrative): floor 15% (the competitive break-even,
  $1.035/0.90 = 1.15$), ceiling 100%, so 85 points of gain. At 60% the lender takes 45 and
  leaves her 40; the unfair excess over fair market value is 45 points, 450 on a loan of
  1,000 if repaid.
- **Two benchmarks.** *Competitive* (Wertheimer): the fair rate is the competitive one, 15%.
  *Equal split* (the Nash bargaining solution when utility is linear in money,
  [`game-theory-refresher` 4.1](../game-theory-refresher/lessons/04-01-bargaining.md)): the
  midpoint, $(15+100)/2 = 57.5\%$, at which 60% is nearly fair.
- **The scaling objection to the equal split:** make her need greater (a ceiling of 200%
  because the crop would save the farm) and the midpoint jumps to 107.5% while the
  competitive rate stays at 15%. The equal split charges her for her own desperation, "a
  circumstance affecting the buyer" ([just price](#just-price)). Bargaining theorists reply
  that both parties create the gains, so both may share them.

Lessons: [3.1](lessons/03-01-what-exploitation-is.md)

### Fair market value

**Wertheimer's benchmark for fair terms: the price a hypothetical competitive market would
set, with both sides informed and neither taking special advantage of the other.** Precisely,
a market free of information asymmetries and undue pressure, in which neither party takes
special unfair advantage of the other's special vulnerabilities or weaknesses in
decision-making (Wertheimer 1996, p. 232, paraphrased).

- **It keeps** general supply and demand in the price, including general scarcity (Locke's
  famine town). **It screens out** this party's special plight (the ship in distress),
  monopoly power and deception.
- **As the benchmark a usury cap would have to track** ([3.2](lessons/03-02-predatory-lending-and-usury-caps.md)), the cost stack (funds,
  expected loss, servicing) stands in for it: pricing that tracks the stack is risk-based
  pricing; anything above is markup.
- **Its decision-making clause and the rollover** ([3.2](lessons/03-02-predatory-lending-and-usury-caps.md) Example 2): every renewal of a
  payday loan can be priced at the benchmark, so any wrong lies in the chain. A business that
  profits because borrowers predictably expect to repay in a month, and do not, may fail that
  clause with no price above the benchmark. The transactional account grades prices; the
  complaint is about design.
- **A market rate is not a moral finding.** "Merchants charged high credit prices" is
  historical; "a competitive market would have charged the same" is an economic
  counterfactual; "the price was fair" is normative. Premise 3 bridges the second to the
  third, and only evidence about competition bridges the first to the second.

Lessons: [3.1](lessons/03-01-what-exploitation-is.md), [3.2](lessons/03-02-predatory-lending-and-usury-caps.md)

### Coercion

**Only a threat coerces: a proposal to leave someone worse off than she is entitled to be,
when she has no reasonable alternative.** Wertheimer (*Coercion*, 1987, paraphrased). An
offer adds an option, however desperate she is, so her consent to it can be valid:
desperation is not duress.

- **The village lender** threatened nothing, owed her no loan, and offered something she
  lacked: if there is a wrong, it is exploitation, not coercion ([3.1](lessons/03-01-what-exploitation-is.md)).
- **A threat is measured against what she is owed,** not against how badly she needs the
  deal.
- **Why exploitation needs its own account:** the possible-consent test of
  [`ethics` 2.3](../ethics/lessons/02-03-humanity-autonomy-and-the-lie.md) condemns fraud and
  coercion because they make consent impossible; consensual exploitation passes it.
- **Promises under force** are Module 1's version: Hume's surgeon and robber, Hobbes's thief
  ([Three accounts on the hard cases](#three-accounts-on-the-hard-cases)).

Lessons: [3.1](lessons/03-01-what-exploitation-is.md)

### Structural exploitation

**Exploitation located in the system that sets everyone's alternatives, not in the split of
one deal.** In [2.4](lessons/02-04-marx-interest-bearing-capital.md), workers are paid the full value of their labour-power, a fair
exchange by the market's own measure, and are exploited all the same, since they produce
more value than they are paid. Marx's measure mentions no price: the rate of surplus value is
"an exact expression for the degree of exploitation of labour-power by capital" (*Capital*
vol. 1, ch. IX §1, Moore and Aveling).

- **Interest is a share of that surplus,** however fair the loan's terms, so a lender who never
  meets the workers shares in their exploitation, since their surplus is the only fund he is
  paid from ([2.4](lessons/02-04-marx-interest-bearing-capital.md)).
- **Consumer credit:** behind a worker's pawn loan there is no surplus to share, only a price
  to judge. Marx calls the overcharging of workers as consumers a "secondary exploitation",
  running parallel to the primary one in production ([2.4](lessons/02-04-marx-interest-bearing-capital.md) Example 2).
- **The furnishing merchant** ([3.1](lessons/03-01-what-exploitation-is.md) Example 2; [`history-of-debt` 4.3](../history-of-debt/lessons/04-03-debt-peonage-after-slavery.md)):
  sharecroppers bought supplies on credit against a lien on their crop, at credit prices well
  above cash prices. Ransom and Sutch saw territorial monopoly; Higgs and Wright saw
  competition and the cost of lending to poor borrowers against a risky crop. On either
  history the families' alternatives were set by a system (no land, lien laws, exclusion by
  race). Sample: the merchant profited from injustice and so exploited. A Marxian: *was this
  deal fair?* is the wrong question. Wertheimer can grant that the system was unjust while
  denying that each merchant exploited.
- **The crux:** is exploitation a wrong in the split of a deal or in the structure that sets
  its terms? A fair price can still crush a poor borrower, since poverty raises risk and cost
  per dollar; that complaint is about the background, which a transactional account does not
  reach ([3.2](lessons/03-02-predatory-lending-and-usury-caps.md)).

Lessons: [2.4](lessons/02-04-marx-interest-bearing-capital.md), [3.1](lessons/03-01-what-exploitation-is.md)

### Just price

**Aquinas: a seller may charge for what the sale costs him, never for what the thing is
worth to a needy buyer.** ST II-II q.77 a.1 (English Dominican Province, 1920), fraud already
set aside: "the just price will depend not only on the thing sold, but on the loss which the
sale brings on the seller. ... the advantage accruing to the buyer, is not due to the seller,
but to a circumstance affecting the buyer. Now no man should sell what is not his, though he
may charge for the loss he suffers."

- **The ground is entitlement, not harm:** the buyer's gain comes from his own circumstance,
  so pricing it sells "what is not his". The fair price is fixed without reference to this
  buyer's need.
- **"Worth"** is left to "a kind of estimate" (ad 1), which Raymond de Roover (1958) read as the
  going market price. A buyer may still pay more "of his own accord": the gain may be given,
  not exacted.
- **The pair with q.78:** q.77 lets a seller charge for his loss but not for the buyer's need;
  q.78 lets a lender recover a loss but charge nothing for the use of a consumable lent ([2.2](lessons/02-02-aquinas-selling-what-does-not-exist.md)).
- **It is the principle behind the scaling objection** to an equal split of the gains
  ([division of the gains](#division-of-the-gains)).

Lessons: [3.1](lessons/03-01-what-exploitation-is.md)

### Non-worseness claim

**If you had a right not to deal with someone at all, a consensual, mutually advantageous
deal that harms no third party cannot be worse than no deal.** Four conditions: A had a right
not to interact with B; the interaction is consensual; it is mutually advantageous; it harms
no third party. Wertheimer stated the claim (1996); Matt Zwolinski defended it in work on
price gouging (2008, 2009).

- **Bentham's lending version** (*Defence of Usury*, Letter IV, 1787): so long as the
  borrower's net gain comes to anything at all, he is "just so much the better for borrowing,
  even on such comparatively disadvantageous terms".
- **Against a cap:** the 300-percent lender does more for the borrower than the lender who
  stays away, whom no one blames; a cap that ends the loan leaves her with the need that drove
  her to borrow and one option fewer. It bites only where the cap *rations*, and it says
  nothing against a *fairer* loan.
- **Contested.** The *Stanford Encyclopedia* reports that most exploitation theorists doubt it,
  since it would make blaming a price gouger a mistake when we would not blame him for staying
  home. Accepting it need not excuse the gouger: it may show instead that staying home is worse
  than we thought.
- **Weight before force:** it bears first on how wrong the lender is, not on what the state may
  do ([moral weight and moral force](#moral-weight-and-moral-force)).
- **Against voiding labour pledges** ([3.3](lessons/03-03-what-may-be-pledged.md)): for someone with nothing, future labour is the
  only collateral, and voiding its pledge may mean no loan at all. The defender's best reply is
  empirical: once self-pledge is lawful, lenders demand it of everyone in need, so the class is
  worse off even if each loan helps.

Lessons: [3.2](lessons/03-02-predatory-lending-and-usury-caps.md), [3.3](lessons/03-03-what-may-be-pledged.md)

### Moral weight and moral force

**How wrong an exploitation is, and what reasons it gives anyone to act on it, are separate
questions.** Wertheimer's pair: *moral weight* is the wrongness; *moral force* is the reasons
for action it gives the parties or society (blame, refusal to enforce, prohibition). A
mutually advantageous exploitation may be wrong yet not license a ban.

- **The strategic argument** (Wertheimer): banning exploitative terms can make a monopolist
  lend at a fair price, since he would rather lend fairly than not at all; it fails in a
  competitive market, where the price already equals cost.
- **An alternative to a ban:** remove the vulnerability, for instance by redistribution
  (Allen Wood, 1995), since a ban leaves the need untouched.
- **Premise 3 of the [exploitation argument for a cap](#the-exploitation-argument-for-a-cap)**
  assumes that weight carries force.

Lessons: [3.2](lessons/03-02-predatory-lending-and-usury-caps.md)

### The exploitation argument for a cap

**If some lenders take an unfair markup, a cap at the fair price removes the markup and
leaves the loan.** A reconstruction, not any one author's ([3.2](lessons/03-02-predatory-lending-and-usury-caps.md)):

1. **P1.** A lender exploits a borrower when the loan's terms give it an unfair share of the
   gains, measured against fair market value. *(Conceptual.)*
2. **P2.** Some high-cost lenders charge well above that benchmark, because their borrowers
   are desperate, uninformed or without alternatives. *(Empirical.)*
3. **P3.** The state may forbid exploitative terms even when both parties consent and both
   gain. *(Normative: weight carries force.)*
4. **P4.** A cap at the benchmark removes the markup and leaves the loan: it reprices, it does
   not ration. *(Empirical.)*

∴ The state may cap rates at the benchmark.

- **Attacked at P4, with P2 behind it:** a cap reprices only where the lender has a markup to
  lose. The strategic argument works against a monopolist and fails in a competitive market,
  so P4 holds or fails market by market, and a cap set below cost turns C into a ban.
- **Attacked at P3:** only harm or a violated right licenses the state to forbid, and a loan
  both parties want involves neither.
- **Fallbacks that do not need P4:** [paternalism](#paternalism), if borrowers' choices are
  defective, or Smith's social reason ([prodigals and projectors](#prodigals-and-projectors)).
  Rationing is their point. Neither is an exploitation argument.
- **Bentham's list of reasons for a cap** (Letter I), apart from "prevention of usury", sorts
  into three kinds: exploitation (protect the needy from extortion), paternalism (protect
  prodigals and the naive from themselves), social allocation (keep capital from rash
  projectors).
- **[3.2](lessons/03-02-predatory-lending-and-usury-caps.md)'s two lenders** (illustrative, per 100 dollars a month on 300-dollar loans): funds 1,
  expected default 5, servicing 9 (a 27-dollar fixed cost), a stack of 15. A, with rivals,
  charges 15 (APR 180%); B, the only lender in town, charges 25 (APR 300%). A cap of 36% APR
  (3 a month) sits below both lenders' costs: neither lends, P4 fails, and non-worseness bites
  on both sets of borrowers. A cap of 180% leaves A alone and, on the strategic argument, makes
  B lend at A's price.
- **Rival predictions dressed as morals:** "caps hurt the poor" and "caps protect the poor" are
  mostly rival predictions about whether a cap reprices or rations in a given market.
- Boss problem 3(d) asks whether the state should cap the rate; the card gives no verdict.

Lessons: [3.2](lessons/03-02-predatory-lending-and-usury-caps.md)

### Paternalism

**Interfering with someone's choice for her own good: soft if the choice is uninformed or
not fully voluntary, hard if it is informed and voluntary.** Owned by
[`political-philosophy` 3.3](../political-philosophy/lessons/03-03-paternalism-and-legal-moralism.md).

- **A usury cap is hard paternalism toward informed borrowers,** since it binds the fully
  informed too. A warning of how long borrowers typically stay in debt, or instalments unless
  the borrower opts out, aims at misprediction and is soft ([3.2](lessons/03-02-predatory-lending-and-usury-caps.md)).
- **Bentham's Letter I thesis** (1787) is the anti-paternalist statement: an informed adult may
  make whatever bargain about money he thinks fit.
- **Block** calls premise 4 of the inalienability argument paternalism wearing a principle's
  name ([3.3](lessons/03-03-what-may-be-pledged.md)); a non-waivable discharge raises the same question ([5.3](lessons/05-03-bankruptcy-as-a-moral-institution.md); Mill's slavery
  contract).

### Prodigals and projectors

**Smith's case for a usury cap near the safest borrowers' rate: it rations credit away from
the rash, toward sober people.** *Wealth of Nations* II.4 (the lesson cites the chapter
without claiming first-edition wording): the legal rate should be "somewhat above" but "not
much above" the lowest market rate.

- **Smith's two sentences.** A cap "fixed precisely at the lowest market price ... ruins, with
  honest people who respect the laws of their country, the credit of all those who cannot give
  the very best security, and obliges them to have recourse to exorbitant usurers." And at "eight
  or ten per cent. the greater part of the money which was to be lent, would be lent to
  prodigals and projectors, who alone would be willing to give this high interest."
- **Reading them:** the first concedes Bentham's mechanism (a cap at the best borrowers' rate
  cuts everyone else off; "with honest people who respect the laws" means *among law-abiding
  lenders*). The second wants that rationing, aimed at the right people. So Smith's cap is
  built to ration: an allocative argument about third parties, not about the borrower.
- **Bentham, Letter XIII "To Dr. Smith"** (*Defence of Usury*, 1787; quoted from the 4th ed.,
  1818): "these restraints have no power or tendency to pick out bad projects from the good."
  A projector, as Bentham reads Smith, is anyone trying something new, and every new venture
  is riskier than an old trade, so the cap screens out invention with folly.
- **The crux is empirical:** who borrows at high rates? The selection effect Smith saw is
  credit rationing ([`economics-of-debt` 1.2](../economics-of-debt/lessons/01-02-credit-rationing-stiglitz-weiss.md), Stiglitz–Weiss;
  the lemons logic of [`grad-micro` 5.1](../grad-micro/lessons/05-01-adverse-selection-lemons.md)).

Lessons: [3.2](lessons/03-02-predatory-lending-and-usury-caps.md)

### Effective annual rate

**What a year costs at a periodic rate if every fee is itself borrowed.** For periodic rate
$i$ and $n$ periods a year: APR $= n \times i$ (no compounding); EAR $= (1+i)^n - 1$. A borrower
who pays each fee in cash for a year pays the APR's total; one who finances each fee pays the
EAR.

- **[3.2](lessons/03-02-predatory-lending-and-usury-caps.md)'s lenders:** at 15% a month, APR 180% and EAR $1.15^{12} - 1 \approx 435\%$
  (300 dollars grows to about 1,605 owed, against $12 \times 45 = 540$ dollars in fees paid in
  cash); at 25% a month, APR 300% and EAR $\approx 1{,}355\%$.
- **A high APR is not yet exploitation:** on a benchmark account a rate exploits only by
  exceeding fair market value; A's 180% may be fair while a 20% loan from the only lender on an
  island exploits.
- Boss problem 3(a) prices a two-week loan with its own figures; the card does not.

### Hostage collateral

**Security worth little to the creditor and much to the debtor, which works by the threat of
taking it rather than by resale.** A grandmother's watch at a pawnshop fetches little, but its
owner will pay a lot not to lose it. Contrast *value* collateral, such as a car on a car loan,
which repays the lender by sale: "value" means value to the creditor.

- **Shylock's pound of flesh is the limit case** ([Shylock's bond](#shylocks-bond)).
- **A hostage secures only if the debtor believes it may be taken.**
- **What makes any security secure:** the creditor may take it on default without the
  debtor's consent at that time (premise 1 of the [inalienability argument](#inalienability)).

Lessons: [3.3](lessons/03-03-what-may-be-pledged.md)

### Pledging future labor

**A thing can be handed over, and taking it ends the matter; future labor can only be
commanded, day after day.** That is why labor pledges have needed force or criminal law behind
them: *nexum* at Rome ([`history-of-debt` 1.5](../history-of-debt/lessons/01-05-nexum-and-the-roman-plebs.md)),
peonage in the American South ([`history-of-debt` 4.3](../history-of-debt/lessons/04-03-debt-peonage-after-slavery.md)).
Flesh can be taken only against a present will.

- **A claim on income sits near the line, on the property side:** a wage assignment or
  income-share agreement takes a share of what you earn but leaves you to decide whether and
  how to work. A work-effort clause enforceable by compulsion would cross the line.
- **The law's line in the peonage cases:** a debtor may choose to work a debt off, but must
  stay free to quit and be sued instead. The pledge survives only as a debt of money, which is
  Rothbard's line too.
- **Legal lists:** Solon banned loans on the person; Rome made goods, not the body, the
  security; Deuteronomy's creditor may not take a millstone; a US Federal Trade Commission rule
  of 1984 bars covered lenders from taking most assignments of future wages, or a lien on
  household goods the borrower already owns and keeps, wedding rings included.
- **In [5.3](lessons/05-03-bankruptcy-as-a-moral-institution.md):** the future-freedom argument says an unlimited claim on income crosses this
  line; its critic holds to 3.3's line, that a claim on earnings leaves the debtor free.
- **Economists' version** ([`economics-of-debt` 1.3](../economics-of-debt/lessons/01-03-pledgeable-income-collateral-and-monitors.md)): a
  borrower can pledge future returns but not skills, which walk out the door with her. That is
  a claim about what she *can* pledge; this is about what she *may*.

Lessons: [3.3](lessons/03-03-what-may-be-pledged.md), [5.3](lessons/05-03-bankruptcy-as-a-moral-institution.md)

### Inalienability

**Some things may not be transferred even between willing parties; the question is which,
and why.** Guido Calabresi and A. Douglas Melamed (1972) sorted the law's protections of an
entitlement into three rules: a *property rule* (no one takes it without your consent, at your
price), a *liability rule* (others may take it at a price a court sets) and an *inalienability
rule* (no transfer at all). The word is older; the three-rule framework is theirs.

**The inalienability argument** (the assembly is [3.3](lessons/03-03-what-may-be-pledged.md)'s):
1. A pledge lets the creditor take the pledged good on default, without the debtor's consent at
   that time. *(Conceptual.)*
2. A thing can be taken without the debtor's cooperation, and taking it ends the matter. Future
   labor can only be commanded, and flesh taken only against a present will. *(Conceptual.)*
3. So a pledge of labor or of the body gives the creditor a standing power over the debtor's
   person, held for the life of the loan and used at his discretion.
4. No one may give another such a power over himself, even by free consent. *(Normative.)*

∴ A pledge of future labor or of the body is void from the start, however fair its price and
however free the choice.

- **Premise 4's defenders agree on little else:** Mill's self-defeat ([Mill's slavery
  contract](#mills-slavery-contract)); Locke's natural law ("No body can give more power than
  he has himself; and he that cannot take away his own life, cannot give another power over it",
  *Second Treatise* §§23–24; he accepted self-sale into limited service, never into a power of
  life and death); Kant's dignity (a person has dignity, not a price,
  [`ethics` 2.3](../ethics/lessons/02-03-humanity-autonomy-and-the-lie.md)); republican
  [non-domination](#non-domination). Murray Rothbard (*The Ethics of Liberty*, 1982): the will
  cannot be surrendered, so rights over oneself can be waived but not alienated; you may agree
  to work, but no one may compel you to perform.
- **Attacked at "even by free consent":** Walter Block (2003) answers that self-ownership is a
  *right* to control one's person, not a psychological capacity, and rights can be transferred,
  so a self-owner may pledge himself. The harder attack is the poverty objection from
  [non-worseness](#non-worseness-claim).
- **Degrees:** many countries let you give a kidney but not sell one; the peonage cases let a
  debtor work off a debt but forbid holding him to the work.
- **Normative, not empirical:** economists' "inalienable human capital" (after Hart and Moore,
  1994) says a lender *cannot* make you work, because you can walk away; here the claim is that
  the law *should not* help him try. Peonage is the empirical claim made false by criminal law.
- **Revisited:** in [5.3](lessons/05-03-bankruptcy-as-a-moral-institution.md) it is premise P3 of the [future-freedom argument](#future-freedom-argument),
  on which the discharge is inalienability extended across time; in [5.4](lessons/05-04-jubilee-as-a-moral-principle.md) a rule that land
  may not be sold for ever is an inalienability rule.

Lessons: [3.3](lessons/03-03-what-may-be-pledged.md), [5.3](lessons/05-03-bankruptcy-as-a-moral-institution.md), [5.4](lessons/05-04-jubilee-as-a-moral-principle.md)

### Shylock's bond

**An interest-free loan secured by a pledge of the debtor's body: the play's wrong, if any, is
in the security, not the price.** *The Merchant of Venice* I.iii: 3,000 ducats for three months,
no interest, the bond sealed "in a merry sport", the forfeit "an equal pound / Of your fair
flesh, to be cut off and taken / In what part of your body pleaseth me".

- **The shape of a penalty:** "forfeit" and "condition" mean the flesh does not repay the
  ducats, it replaces them. "In what part of your body pleaseth me" gives the creditor not a
  thing but a choice over the debtor's body, at his pleasure. The written bond read in IV.i
  names the place: nearest his heart.
- **IV.i:** Portia holds the bond forfeit and the claim lawful, then reads it to the letter
  ("This bond doth give thee here no jot of blood"), and exactly a pound. Shylock, retreating
  to the money, is refused it; a statute against any *alien* who seeks a citizen's life forfeits
  his goods and puts his life at the Duke's mercy, and the pardon comes with a forced
  conversion.
- **Why Venice cannot simply refuse the bond:** Antonio has said the city's trade depends on
  strangers trusting its courts (III.iii).
- **Jhering** (*The Struggle for Law*, trans. Lalor, 1879): the court should have voided the
  bond from the start as contrary to good morals; once it had upheld it, the blood reading
  cheated Shylock, however humane the motive.
- **Shylock's tu quoque:** Venetians own bought slaves and would never free them on demand; a
  city that lets persons be bought cannot assert premise 4 of the [inalienability
  argument](#inalienability) without condemning itself. A court that decides after the fact
  which bonds to honour, under a statute that binds only aliens, holds the discretionary power
  the argument forbids to creditors; a defender replies that this is a reason to state the rule
  in advance and for everyone.
- Portia's mercy speech belongs to [`philosophy-of-law`](../philosophy-of-law/syllabus.md) 5.4.
  Boss problem 3(c) close-reads her judgment; the card leaves that reading to it.

### Mill's slavery contract

**An engagement to sell oneself as a slave is void, because it uses liberty to end liberty.**
*On Liberty* (1859), ch. V: we respect voluntary choice out of regard for liberty, and self-sale
abdicates all future liberty in one act. "The principle of freedom cannot require that he
should be free not to be free. It is not freedom, to be allowed to alienate his freedom."

- **Limited by the necessities of life,** which require consenting to limits on freedom. Mill
  reports Humboldt's view that engagements of personal service should not bind beyond a limited
  time.
- **It shrinks as the pledge shrinks:** a year's service is not a life. The republican objection,
  by contrast, grows with the creditor's discretion, whatever is pledged ([non-domination](#non-domination)).
- Paternalism and the slavery contract as political philosophy belong to
  [`political-philosophy` 3.3](../political-philosophy/lessons/03-03-paternalism-and-legal-moralism.md).

Lessons: [3.3](lessons/03-03-what-may-be-pledged.md)

### Non-domination

**Republican freedom: not being subject to another's power to interfere at will. The wrong is
the power's existence, not its use.** A benevolent master is still a master (Pettit; owned by
[`political-philosophy` 3.1](../political-philosophy/lessons/03-01-three-concepts-of-liberty.md), not this course's 3.1).

- **Applied to pledges** ([3.3](lessons/03-03-what-may-be-pledged.md)): a creditor holding a pledge of the person dominates even if
  he never collects. A borrower who must work a year on the lender's farm if he defaults faces
  the republican objection at full strength.
- **Unlike Mill's objection,** which shrinks as the pledge shrinks, this one grows with the
  creditor's discretion, whatever is pledged.
- **An interest-free advance from an employer**, falling due in full if the worker quits, can
  look like a tether rather than a loan on a reading of "equal in principle" as parity of power
  and exit ([4.3](lessons/04-03-graebers-thesis-examined.md) Example 2).

Lessons: [3.3](lessons/03-03-what-may-be-pledged.md), [4.3](lessons/04-03-graebers-thesis-examined.md)

## Debt, guilt and genealogy

### Genealogy

**A history of how a value or practice originated, told to change how we assess what
descends from it.** It may vindicate or debunk. Nietzsche's *Genealogy of Morals* (1887),
Second Essay, is the course's case; genealogy as a method belongs to
[`modern-philosophy`](../modern-philosophy/syllabus.md) 6.5. Whether the history is true is a
historical question; what it would show about what we owe is a separate, normative one.
Nietzsche himself separates a thing's origin from its later purposes (GM II §12).

**What a genealogy can show without the [genetic fallacy](#genetic-fallacy)** ([4.3](lessons/04-03-graebers-thesis-examined.md)): the
bridge premise, that origin bears on validity, can be supplied in three ways.
- **(i) Against self-evidence.** A maxim presented as obvious and universal loses that support
  if a history shows it is neither; the maxim itself survives.
- **(ii) Debunking.** If the best explanation of why a maxim feels binding never involves its
  truth (it served creditors and conquerors), its felt force stops counting as evidence of its
  truth, provided the causal story is independently supported and does not also sink the
  debunker's own judgments ([`philosophical-method` 3.4](../philosophical-method/lessons/03-04-intuitions-and-reflective-equilibrium.md)).
- **(iii) Origin as a validity condition.** How a *particular* debt arose can decide whether it
  binds: an agreement extracted by force, such as Haiti's 1825 indemnity. That differs from a
  genealogy of debt in general.

None of the three reaches a debt freely contracted between equals without a further premise.

- **A datable cousin:** Anderson's thesis that sin shifted from burden to debt in Second Temple
  Aramaic is a philologist's documented genealogy of the same link
  ([`theology-of-debt` 3.1](../theology-of-debt/lessons/03-01-from-burden-to-debt.md)).
- **The same shape with evolution:** the debunking argument of
  [`ethics` 6.4](../ethics/lessons/06-04-constructivism-and-debunking.md).

Lessons: [4.1](lessons/04-01-the-animal-with-the-right-to-make-promises.md), [4.3](lessons/04-03-graebers-thesis-examined.md)

### Genetic fallacy

**Judging a belief true or false, or an obligation valid or void, by where it came from,
with the bridge premise from origin to validity assumed rather than supplied.** The
bridge-premise test is [`philosophical-method` 2.3](../philosophical-method/lessons/02-03-informal-fallacies-and-when-they-arent.md),
which teaches the method but never names the genetic fallacy; the line between the fallacy and
a legitimate debunking explanation is drawn in [`philosophical-method` 3.4](../philosophical-method/lessons/03-04-intuitions-and-reflective-equilibrium.md).

- **Three claims, three kinds** ([4.1](lessons/04-01-the-animal-with-the-right-to-make-promises.md)): "guilt came from debt" (historical), "guilt is really
  debt" (conceptual) and "the duty to repay is debunked" (normative) are different claims.
- **Owing God** ([4.2](lessons/04-02-owing-the-ancestors-owing-god.md)): GM II §§16–22 are historical and psychological claims about how the
  feeling of owing God arose. By themselves they show neither that nothing is owed to God or
  one's ancestors nor that God does not exist.
- **Violent origins prove too much** ([4.3](lessons/04-03-graebers-thesis-examined.md)): if they voided obligations, they would void
  property, states and, on GM II §3, the very memory that makes promising possible.
- **It runs both ways.** Origin is no more proof of validity than of invalidity: "discharge
  began as a creditors' device, so it serves creditors" is the same slide ([5.3](lessons/05-03-bankruptcy-as-a-moral-institution.md)). And crying
  "genetic fallacy" is itself a mistake when the bridge is supplied ([genealogy](#genealogy)
  (iii): how a particular debt arose).
- **Hume's promises** ([1.2](lessons/01-02-hume-promises-as-artificial-obligation.md)): explaining where a duty came from does not dissolve it.

Lessons: [4.1](lessons/04-01-the-animal-with-the-right-to-make-promises.md), [4.2](lessons/04-02-owing-the-ancestors-owing-god.md), [4.3](lessons/04-03-graebers-thesis-examined.md)

### Schuld

**German for both guilt and debt, the pun Nietzsche's derivation turns on.** *Schulden* are
debts and a *Schuldner* is a debtor; duty is *Pflicht*, a different word. GM II §4 (Samuel,
1913): "the cardinal moral idea of 'ought' originates from the very material idea of 'owe'",
where "ought" renders *Schuld* and "owe" *Schulden*.

- **Samuel's renderings shift.** *Schuld* is "ought" in §§4 and 8 and "guilt" in §6;
  *Schulden* is "owe" or "owing"; *Schuldner* is "ower"; his footnote to §4 explains that he
  "occasionally" uses owe/ought for the double meaning. In §§20–21 he renders *Schuld* and its
  compounds as "debt", "owing", "'ought' (owe)" and "guilt" by turns, so an English reader can
  miss that §21's moralization turns on one word.
- **Read "ought from owe" as guilt from debts.** Never quote "guilt" or "debt" as Samuel's §4
  words, and do not read his "ought" as duty.
- **§6** calls the law of contract the cradle of guilt, conscience and duty.
- **The word's history is history:** *credere*, *ought* from *owe* and *Schuld* show how words
  got their weight, not whether a debt binds ([1.1](lessons/01-01-the-grammar-of-owing.md)).

Lessons: [4.1](lessons/04-01-the-animal-with-the-right-to-make-promises.md), [4.2](lessons/04-02-owing-the-ancestors-owing-god.md)

### Mnemotechnics of pain

**Memory, and so the capacity to keep a promise, was first made by pain.** GM II §3 (Samuel's
"system of mnemonics"): "Something is burnt in so as to remain in his memory: only that which
never stops *hurting* remains in his memory." Early pledges and obligations came wrapped in
sacrifice, mutilation and savage penal law, and the harshness of old codes measures how hard
forgetting was to beat.

- **A claim about prehistory,** about how memory was *first* made, not about what works now.
  The process ends in a person who keeps his word from strength of will, not fear of pain
  ([sovereign individual](#sovereign-individual)).
- **Contrast Hume,** for whom the point of promising is plain after brief experience and its
  sanction reputational ([1.2](lessons/01-02-hume-promises-as-artificial-obligation.md)).
- **In [4.3](lessons/04-03-graebers-thesis-examined.md):** if violent origins voided obligations, §3 would void promising itself, which is
  one of the critics' replies to Graeber.

Lessons: [4.1](lessons/04-01-the-animal-with-the-right-to-make-promises.md), [4.3](lessons/04-03-graebers-thesis-examined.md)

### Sovereign individual

**The end product of the breeding: the one who *may* promise, because his will is strong
enough to stand surety for his future self.** GM II §2: the "ripest fruit" of the morality of
custom, autonomous and "super-moral", "competent to promise"; his conscience is pride in that
power, not guilt.

- **"Can promise" is a standing, not a bare ability.** Samuel's "an animal that *can promise*"
  (§1) renders *versprechen darf*, may promise; §2's "competent to promise" renders the same
  words. Do not put "the right to make promises" in quotation marks as Samuel's words.
- **The promiser earns the standing** on Nietzsche's picture, where [1.3](lessons/01-03-promising-after-hume.md)'s accounts ask what
  the *promisee* gets.
- **In the promiser's surety for himself** (§1: he "could guarantee himself *as a future*"), a
  promise already has a debtor's shape: he pledges his future self as a debtor pledges in §5.

Lessons: [4.1](lessons/04-01-the-animal-with-the-right-to-make-promises.md)

### Community as creditor

**Nietzsche's model of punishment: the community advances peace and protection, and the
criminal is a defaulting debtor who also attacks his creditor.** GM II §9: the breach counts for
more than the damage, so the community forecloses. It withdraws its peace and treats the
offender as an enemy: outlawry, the "peaceless" state in which anyone may harm him.

- **§8 behind it:** pricing and exchange are the oldest human relation, and the first justice
  was settling accounts between rough equals.
- **§10 after it:** a secure community prices offences, shields the offender from the injured
  party's anger, and may let him go ([mercy as the privilege of the powerful](#mercy-as-the-privilege-of-the-powerful)).
- **The creditor's seat moves** ([4.2](lessons/04-02-owing-the-ancestors-owing-god.md)): §19 moves it from the living community to the dead
  founders, and §20 from the founders to the gods.
- **Returns** as "paying one's debt to society" ([6.4](lessons/06-04-debts-of-gratitude.md)).

Lessons: [4.1](lessons/04-01-the-animal-with-the-right-to-make-promises.md), [4.2](lessons/04-02-owing-the-ancestors-owing-god.md)

### Mercy as the privilege of the powerful

**As a community grows strong, its penal law softens, and justice that held everything
payable ends by letting non-payers go.** GM II §10: composition, shielding the offender,
separating the offender from his act; weakness brings the harsh forms back. Samuel's words are
"Grace", "the privilege of the strongest, better still, their super-law" (*Gnade*); his
"self-destruction" of justice renders *Selbstaufhebung*. The heading's phrase is the syllabus's
paraphrase, not a quotation.

- **Two readings** ([4.1](lessons/04-01-the-animal-with-the-right-to-make-promises.md) Example 2). As a historical generalization (law softens as
  communities grow secure), cases can test it, and Deuteronomy 15 pushes back: its release binds
  every creditor, however modest, forbids refusing the poor a loan as the year approaches, and
  is framed as a duty to a brother, not a display of surplus power. As an account of what mercy
  *is*, no case refutes it (any release can be credited to some powerful party, even the Lord of
  15:2), and it must earn its place by what it explains.
- **§10 is about penal law.** Applying it to loans takes the model at its word that punishment
  and debt are one relation.
- **In [5.1](lessons/05-01-forgiving-a-debt-forgiving-a-wrong.md):** if punishment is a debt owed to a creditor who may waive it, mercy is a release
  and the [paradox of mercy](#paradox-of-mercy) dissolves, at the price that identical offenders
  may fare differently.

Lessons: [4.1](lessons/04-01-the-animal-with-the-right-to-make-promises.md), [5.1](lessons/05-01-forgiving-a-debt-forgiving-a-wrong.md)

### The genealogy of the promising animal

**Nietzsche's story of how a forgetful animal was made able to promise, through debt:
memory burnt in by pain, guilt born of debts, punishment as collection, mercy as a strong
creditor's luxury.** *Genealogy* II §§1–10 (Samuel, 1913; the numbering is [4.1](lessons/04-01-the-animal-with-the-right-to-make-promises.md)'s):

1. **Promising requires a memory of the will** (§1): a past "I will" kept alive against active
   forgetting, which is the healthy default, until the deed is done.
2. **It requires a calculable self** (§§1–2): before anyone could vouch for his future self, the
   morality of custom made people regular and predictable.
3. **The first memory was made by pain** (§3; [mnemotechnics of pain](#mnemotechnics-of-pain)).
4. **The oldest enforced promise was a debtor's** (§§4–5): to make repayment credible he pledged
   what he still controlled (his body, his freedom, his wife, even his rest in the grave); on
   default the creditor could take an equivalent in pain, such as flesh cut in proportion to the
   debt.
5. **Guilt descends from debts** (§§4–6): the idea that every injury has an equivalent, payable
   even in pain, grew from this contract; suffering could repay, Nietzsche conjectures (§6),
   because making someone suffer was a pleasure. [4.1](lessons/04-01-the-animal-with-the-right-to-make-promises.md) compresses §§4–6 here; Boss problem
   4(a) reconstructs them step by step, and the card leaves that to it.
6. **The [community is a creditor](#community-as-creditor)** (§§8–9).
7. **Power softens the creditor** (§10).

∴ **Justice ends in grace** (§10), and at the end of the breeding stands the
[sovereign individual](#sovereign-individual).

- **Weakest: premises 4 and 6,** which rest on §8's historical claim that creditor and debtor,
  buyer and seller, are the oldest relation there is, older than any social organization. The
  evidence is etymology and legal antiquities; Nietzsche calls his account of why suffering could
  repay conjectural (§6). His Roman exhibit, the Twelve Tables' clause on cutting shares of a
  defaulter, is usually read literally, but Gellius's jurist, who reports it, had never heard of
  its use ([`history-of-debt` 1.5](../history-of-debt/lessons/01-05-nexum-and-the-roman-plebs.md)).
- **The critic:** contract presupposes the community it is meant to explain; to lend, pledge and
  collect, people must already share rules about promises, pledges and defaults, so guilt and
  debt may both descend from an older social bond. That is Graeber's line ([4.3](lessons/04-03-graebers-thesis-examined.md)).
- **The defender:** the philosophical point survives a wrong chronology; morality's vocabulary
  bears the marks of an accounting relation however it began.
- **Description, not prescription.** Nietzsche describes the creditor's cruelty, calls modern
  squeamishness hypocrisy, and in §7 says life was brighter before humanity grew ashamed of it;
  readers dispute how far he admires it, but nothing in §§1–10 proposes reviving it, and §10 makes
  harshness the symptom of a weak community. (§7 also contains a racist aside about who feels
  pain; nothing in the argument depends on it.)

Lessons: [4.1](lessons/04-01-the-animal-with-the-right-to-make-promises.md)

### Bad conscience

**Cruelty turned inward: instincts that can no longer discharge outward turn on their
owner.** GM II §16 (Samuel; Nietzsche calls it a "hypothesis"): once man is penned inside
society and peace, "All instincts which do not find a vent without, *turn inwards*", and the
inner life begins.

- **Not yet guilt, and it needs no debt.** By §15 Nietzsche has argued that punishment did not
  produce guilt (the punished felt that something had gone wrong, not that they ought not to have
  done it), so §16 is a second source, fused with debt only in §21
  ([moralization of *Schuld*](#moralization-of-schuld)).
- The essay's structure depends on keeping bad conscience and guilt apart.

Lessons: [4.2](lessons/04-02-owing-the-ancestors-owing-god.md)

### Debt to the ancestors

**The clan owes its founders a legal debt for its existence, and the debt, and the fear, grow
with the clan's power until the founder becomes a god.** GM II §19: the living believe the clan
exists only through the founders' sacrifices, and owe them a legal debt, not a sentiment, repaid
in sacrifice and obedience; for that age, Nietzsche says, "there is no gratis". The ancestors
live on as potent spirits and are credited with every new advantage, so the debt accumulates;
fear of them rises with the clan's victories and falls with its decline; at the limit the
founder of the mightiest clan is "transfigured into a god".

- **§20:** subject peoples take over their masters' gods with the debts attached; universal
  empires bring universal gods, and the Christian "record god" brings the record amount of guilt.
- **Critics:** Nietzsche calls the step "this crude kind of logic", not evidence, and backs the
  ancestor cult with one example, the sacrifice of the first-born. Gratitude to a benefactor
  predicts the same curve (offerings rise with a clan's fortunes), so only data the debt reading
  alone predicts (the suspicion that the creditor has never been paid enough; the "wholesale
  ransom" periodically extorted) favour it ([4.2](lessons/04-02-owing-the-ancestors-owing-god.md) Example 1). The religious critic adds that
  "there is no gratis" asserts, rather than shows, that a benefit must be a loan.
- **The Thomist counterpart:** after God, a man owes most to his parents and his country (ST
  II-II q.101 a.1), and what he renders God is due yet "cannot be equal" (q.80 a.1); religion and
  piety answer with worship and service ([grace as gift](#grace-as-gift)).
- **Translation trap:** Samuel's §19 sentence on "the middle period" is ambiguous and reads most
  naturally as extending the difficulty for piety; the German concedes that "but also from piety"
  is right all the more for the middle period of the noble clans, who repaid their ancestors "with
  interest" (*mit Zins*). Do not cite it as Nietzsche denying piety.

Lessons: [4.2](lessons/04-02-owing-the-ancestors-owing-god.md)

### Moralization of Schuld

**The unpayable debt is pushed inward, where it becomes a guilt that can never be expiated.**
GM II §21: *Schuld* and *Pflicht*, debt-guilt and duty, are pushed back into the bad
conscience; an unpayable debt becomes an unpayable penalty ("eternal punishment") and turns on
the debtor, then on whatever can be blamed for his existence (Adam, Nature, existence itself).
Then Christianity's "stroke of genius": "God personally immolating himself for the debt of man
... the creditor playing scapegoat for his debtor, from *love* (can you believe it?), from love
of his debtor!", a "temporary alleviation". §22: "Owing something to God: this thought becomes
his instrument of torture."

- **Samuel's additions.** The German has the creditor *sacrificing* himself (*sich opfernd*), not
  "playing scapegoat"; "the creditor sacrificing himself for his debtor" is Kaufmann's
  copyrighted wording, not Samuel's. "God paying himself personally out of a pound of his own
  flesh" is the translator's echo of Shylock; the German says only that God makes himself paid by
  himself. Build nothing on it.
- **In ledger terms** payer and payee are one person, so nothing passes from debtor to creditor:
  a release, not a payment. Nietzsche's incredulity falls on the motive, since love is no
  creditor's reason.
- **§21 corrects §20.** §20 forecast that the consciousness of owing would fade with belief (a
  triumphant atheism as "a kind of second innocence"); §21 says the moralization cut guilt loose
  from its creditor. The defender: §21 predicts a specific shape for surviving guilt,
  inexpiable, self-directed, owed to no one who could accept payment. The Strawsonian: most guilt
  unbelievers feel is remorse toward a particular person they wronged ([4.2](lessons/04-02-owing-the-ancestors-owing-god.md) Example 2).
- **Samuel's §20 "centuries"** renders *mehrere Jahrtausende*, several millennia; do not quote the
  time span as Nietzsche's claim.

Lessons: [4.2](lessons/04-02-owing-the-ancestors-owing-god.md)

### Guilt before God as debt turned inward

**Nietzsche's last movement: the clan's creditor grows into a god, and the unpayable debt
sinks into the bad conscience.** *Genealogy* II §§16–22 in six steps ([4.2](lessons/04-02-owing-the-ancestors-owing-god.md); quoted phrases
Samuel's):

1. **Penned in, the instincts turn inward** (§16): the [bad conscience](#bad-conscience).
2. **The clan owes its founders a legal debt, not a sentiment** (§19).
3. **The debt, and the fear, grow with the clan's power**, until the founder is "transfigured
   into a god" (§19; [debt to the ancestors](#debt-to-the-ancestors)).
4. **The debt scales with the god** (§20): the "record god" brings the record amount of guilt.
5. **Moralization fuses the strands** (§21; [moralization of *Schuld*](#moralization-of-schuld)).
6. **The creditor pays, and the relief is temporary** (§§21–22).

∴ Guilt before God is the old debt relation, moralized and fused with self-directed cruelty.

- **The conclusion is historical and psychological.** The text draws no normative verdict,
  though §20's pairing of atheism with "a kind of second innocence" leans toward one.
- **Weakest: step 3,** which carries the whole passage from creditor to god on "crude logic";
  gratitude predicts the same rise in offerings. The religious critic adds that step 2 assumes a
  benefit must be a loan.
- **Three replies, each aimed at one step:** gratitude, not fear (step 3); the
  [reactive attitudes](#reactive-attitudes) (step 5); [grace as gift](#grace-as-gift) (step 6).

Lessons: [4.2](lessons/04-02-owing-the-ancestors-owing-god.md)

### Grace as gift

**The religious reply: the creature was never God's debtor in the creditor's sense, because
nothing it could pay was not first given.** "Or who hath first given to him, and recompense
shall be made him?" (Romans 11:35, Douay–Rheims). Paul sets grace against debt: a worker's
reward is reckoned "according to debt", not grace (Romans 4:4; the Douay reads "reward"). The
"handwriting" against us, a note of hand on one reading, is blotted out and fastened to the
cross (Colossians 2:14; [`theology-of-debt` 2.4](../theology-of-debt/lessons/02-04-the-bond-and-the-ransom.md)).

- **Even the satisfaction theories make the payment a gift.** For Anselm, Christ's death is the
  one gift made "not of debt but freely" (*Cur Deus Homo* II.11;
  [`theology-of-debt` 3.3](../theology-of-debt/lessons/03-03-anselm-cur-deus-homo.md)). For
  Aquinas, God could have forgiven sin without any satisfaction and wronged no one (ST III q.46
  a.2 ad 3), and chose satisfaction as the "more copious mercy" (a.1 ad 3;
  [`theology-of-debt` 3.4](../theology-of-debt/lessons/03-04-aquinas-satisfaction-and-the-debt-of-punishment.md)).
- **Aquinas grants §19's debts in his own terms:** after God, man owes most to parents and
  country (II-II q.101 a.1), and what he renders God "cannot be equal" (q.80 a.1). Religion and
  piety, the virtues of justice that answer these debts, issue in worship and service, not
  self-torment.
- **A modern statement:** David Bentley Hart, "A Gift Exceeding Every Debt" (*Pro Ecclesia*,
  1998), an essay on Anselm, cited by title.
- **On this view** Nietzsche describes the Cross accurately and inverts its effect. His answer is
  already in §22: whatever the doctrine calls a gift, the man of bad conscience makes his
  instrument of torture.
- **The reply needs [5.1](lessons/05-01-forgiving-a-debt-forgiving-a-wrong.md)'s distinction,** since it says the Cross both releases a debt and
  forgives a wrong. Doctrine is ceded to `theology-of-debt` 2.4, 3.3 and 3.4.

Lessons: [4.2](lessons/04-02-owing-the-ancestors-owing-god.md)

### Reactive attitudes

**Strawson's name for resentment, indignation, gratitude and their kin: responses to the
goodwill or ill will people show one another.** P. F. Strawson, "Freedom and Resentment"
(1962), a paper about free will that says nothing of Nietzsche
([`metaphysics` 6.2](../metaphysics/lessons/06-02-compatibilism.md)).

- **The secular reply to Nietzsche built from it** ([4.2](lessons/04-02-owing-the-ancestors-owing-god.md)): guilt, on a Strawsonian account, is
  the demand for regard turned on oneself, so its object is a person wronged, not a sum unpaid.
  You can feel guilt toward a stranger you humiliated and owe nothing, and owe a great deal
  without guilt on a mortgage paid on time. Debt may have lent guilt a vocabulary without giving
  it its object.
- **The Nietzschean rejoinder:** Strawson describes the finished product; a genealogy is about
  how it was made.
- **In [5.1](lessons/05-01-forgiving-a-debt-forgiving-a-wrong.md):** forgiving as *forswearing* resentment is Strawson's formula, not Butler's.
- "Self-reactive attitudes" is not attributed to Strawson in the course (unverified in a primary
  text).

Lessons: [4.2](lessons/04-02-owing-the-ancestors-owing-god.md), [5.1](lessons/05-01-forgiving-a-debt-forgiving-a-wrong.md)

### Three moral principles

**Graeber: communism, exchange and hierarchy run side by side through everyday life, and
exchange is only one of them.** David Graeber, *Debt: The First 5,000 Years* (2011),
paraphrased, never quoted at length.

| Principle | Rule | Everyday scene |
|---|---|---|
| **Communism** | from each according to ability, to each according to need; no accounts kept | someone asks you to pass the salt |
| **Exchange** | equivalence: each gives as much as he gets, and once accounts balance either can walk away | you buy a coffee, pay and leave |
| **Hierarchy** | unequals bound by precedent: what was given before is owed again, with no expectation of balance | the coffee a junior once brought the boss becomes expected |

- **Baseline communism** is not a regime: among people who are not enemies, it is the default
  whenever the need is great enough or the cost small enough, and Graeber makes it the
  foundation of all sociability.
- It is premise P1 of [Graeber's case](#graebers-case-against-the-maxim).

Lessons: [4.3](lessons/04-03-graebers-thesis-examined.md)

### Debt as unfinished exchange

**Graeber: a debt is an exchange not yet completed, a precise sum owed between parties
equal in principle, and paying it ends the relationship.** Until then hierarchy rules: the
debtor is, for now, the creditor's inferior. Precision sets a debt apart from every other
obligation, and it takes money to state it.

- **Where precision came from:** to put a number on what a person owes, or on the person, you must
  first tear them out of the web of relations that makes each one unique, and historically, he
  argues, violence did the tearing, with slavery as its paradigm.
- **His compressed formula** (paraphrase only, never quote): a debt is a promise corrupted by
  mathematics and violence.
- **The critics' use of it:** subtract the mathematics and the violence and a promise remains,
  which is what an ordinary borrower gave ([4.3](lessons/04-03-graebers-thesis-examined.md)).
- **"Equal in principle" is where a hard case turns:** formal equality, or parity of power and
  exit ([4.3](lessons/04-03-graebers-thesis-examined.md) Example 2, an employer's advance that falls due if the worker quits).

Lessons: [4.3](lessons/04-03-graebers-thesis-examined.md)

### Primordial debt theory

**The theory that we are born owing society for our existence, the sovereign takes over that
debt, and taxes measure it.** Graeber attributes it to the French theorists of money Michel
Aglietta, André Orléan and Bruno Théret; it draws on the Vedic debts to gods, sages, ancestors
and humanity. It is a cousin of the chain [4.2](lessons/04-02-owing-the-ancestors-owing-god.md) follows from ancestors to God.

- **Graeber rejects it** as a second myth, twin to barter: it cannot say how an infinite debt
  becomes a sum; in the ancient states it cites, taxes fell on conquered peoples, not citizens;
  "society" as one bounded creditor is a nation-state idea; the Vedic debts are too vast and
  diffuse to pay anyone. Barter made the market look natural; primordial debt does the same for
  the state.
- **His reading of Nietzsche:** Nietzsche took Adam Smith's premise further (exchange older than
  society, GM II §8), and granting that priority leads to something like primordial debt.
  Graeber allows that Nietzsche may have meant to shock the bourgeois with their own assumptions,
  but calls that a game played inside them. His own order runs the other way: sociality first,
  exchange later and narrower. Which came first is Module 4's crux.
- **Graeber is not a primordial-debt theorist,** though many readers took him for one, as Bill
  Maurer's review notes (*Anthropological Forum*, 2013).

Lessons: [4.3](lessons/04-03-graebers-thesis-examined.md)

### Graeber's case against the maxim

**Graeber: the felt self-evidence of "one must pay one's debts" is exchange's rule wearing the
authority of morality as a whole; debts are human arrangements a democratic public may
rearrange.** The book opens with a lawyer's reply to his case for cancelling poor countries'
debts, that surely one has to pay one's debts, which persuades, Graeber argues, because it is a
moral statement, not an economic one. Reconstructed in [4.3](lessons/04-03-graebers-thesis-examined.md) (the steps are his claims,
paraphrased; the joins are the lesson's):

- **P1.** Economic life runs on [three moral principles](#three-moral-principles), and exchange is
  only one of them. *(Conceptual.)*
- **P2.** A debt is an exchange awaiting completion: a precise sum, owed between parties equal in
  principle, which payment settles and ends. *(Conceptual.)*
- **P3.** Precision requires stripping people and things of their contexts, and historically force
  did the stripping: war, conquest, slavery. *(Historical, resting on a conceptual claim.)*
- **P4.** The language of debt has again and again made relations founded on force look moral,
  because it casts the victim as the one in the wrong. *(Historical.)*
- **P5.** So when the maxim feels self-evident, that is exchange's rule wearing the authority of
  morality as a whole. *(A diagnosis: why the belief feels true.)*
- **∴ C.** Paying one's debts is not the essence of morality; debts are human arrangements, and a
  democratic public may rearrange them, for instance by a [Jubilee](#jubilee-as-principle).
  *(Normative.)*

- **Weakest: P5.** The duty to repay is not borrowed from anything: it has its own ground in the
  [promise](#promise), and none of Module 1's accounts rests on quantification or force.
  Graeber's own formula concedes as much, since subtracting the mathematics and the violence
  leaves a promise. On this view P3 and P4, however true, indict how debts are counted and
  enforced, not the obligation to repay; and violent origins would void too much. Maurer adds
  that the book runs together a timeless beginning and a datable event, blurring the conceptual
  and the historical where the argument needs them apart.
- **The defender:** C never needed to void promises. Its target is the maxim's claim to be
  self-evident and supreme, and P4 shows how often what were called debts were never free
  promises between equals.
- **At full strength: Haiti's indemnity** ([4.3](lessons/04-03-graebers-thesis-examined.md) Example 1; [`history-of-debt` 4.4](../history-of-debt/lessons/04-04-haitis-independence-debt.md)).
  In 1825 France offered recognition for 150 million francs, delivered with warships. The move
  from origin to validity needs no grand history here: its bridge is the familiar rule that force
  voids an agreement, which Hume cited as evidence that promising is a convention.
- **Not "no one need repay."** The conclusion concerns morality's *essence*, and the remedy is
  collective: a Jubilee a democratic public agrees on.

**The claims, sorted** ([4.3](lessons/04-03-graebers-thesis-examined.md)):

| Claim (paraphrased) | Kind | Where it is tested |
|---|---|---|
| No barter economy preceded money; credit came first | Historical | [`history-of-debt` 1.1](../history-of-debt/lessons/01-01-credit-before-coins.md): the chronology holds; that money began as credit *rather than* as a medium of exchange is harder to defend |
| Violence, slavery above all, made persons and obligations countable | Historical and conceptual | Graeber's own evidence; not tested in the debt thread |
| Debt language has repeatedly dressed force as obligation | Historical (a pattern) | Haiti ([`history-of-debt` 4.4](../history-of-debt/lessons/04-04-haitis-independence-debt.md)), peonage ([`history-of-debt` 4.3](../history-of-debt/lessons/04-03-debt-peonage-after-slavery.md)) |
| Three principles; debt is exchange awaiting completion | Conceptual | the lesson's cases |
| A debt is a promise corrupted by mathematics and violence | Conceptual | Module 1's accounts of promising |
| Paying one's debts is not the essence of morality | Normative | [4.3](lessons/04-03-graebers-thesis-examined.md) |
| A democratic public may cancel debts by a Jubilee | Normative | [5.4](lessons/05-04-jubilee-as-a-moral-principle.md) |

- Boss problem 4(b) sets the Nietzsche–Graeber crux over which came first; the card does not
  settle it.

## Forgiveness, relief and the fresh start

### Resentment

**Butler: settled resentment answers to injury, a moral wrong, not to suffering or loss.**
Joseph Butler, *Fifteen Sermons Preached at the Rolls Chapel* (1726; quoted from the London
edition of 1841), Sermon VIII, "Upon Resentment": "it is not natural, but moral evil; it is not
suffering, but injury, which raises that anger or resentment, which is of any continuance."

- **Sudden vs settled.** Sudden anger can be mere instinct against harm, like closing your eyes
  when something falls toward them. "Of any continuance" limits the claim to settled
  resentment, and someone who harms you innocently is no fit target for it.
- **Its abuses** (Sermon VIII): imagining an injury, magnifying it, resenting an innocent cause,
  indignation out of proportion, and inflicting harm to gratify the feeling, which is revenge.
- **What the command to forgive forbids** (Sermon IX, on Matthew 5:43–44): "only the excess and
  abuse of this natural feeling, in cases of personal and private injury". A limit, not an
  abolition; indignation on behalf of others is outside its scope.
- **Among the [reactive attitudes](#reactive-attitudes)** in Strawson's later terms.

Lessons: [5.1](lessons/05-01-forgiving-a-debt-forgiving-a-wrong.md)

### Forgiveness

**The wronged person's response to a wrong, not a loss: a change in how she stands toward the
wrongdoer, keeping the judgment that what he did was wrong.** Three accounts:

- **Butler's** (Sermon IX): the command forbids only resentment's excess and abuse, so to forgive
  is to give up revenge and malice and keep good-will, with a resentment proportionate to the
  injury. His measure: feel toward the offender what good people with no stake in the case would
  feel, judging the fault as justly, and still wish him well. Even a public execution does not
  cancel the duty of good-will toward the criminal.
- **Forswearing or overcoming resentment:** forswearing is P. F. Strawson's formula ("Freedom and
  Resentment", 1962); overcoming it for moral reasons is Jeffrie Murphy's account (Murphy and
  Hampton, *Forgiveness and Mercy*, 1988).
- **Normative-power accounts:** Brandon Warmke ("The Normative Significance of Forgiveness", 2016) likens
  forgiving to promising or cancelling a debt. A sincere "I forgive you" releases the wrongdoer
  from what the wrong left him owing, such as further apology and amends (Dana Nelkin, 2013), and
  binds the forgiver not to go on blaming, so a forgiver who keeps blaming wrongs the one forgiven.

- **"She forgave him but still resents it"** is coherent on Butler's account and on the power
  view, and a contradiction only on the forswear-or-overcome definitions.
- **The crux:** is forgiveness essentially a change in the victim's stance, or a change in the norms
  between two people? Critics of the power view: one can forgive the dead, or a wrongdoer who has
  already made full amends, when nothing is left to release; and many sincerely say "I forgive
  you" while still trying to.
- **Forgiveness leaves the claim standing,** so "I forgave him" never answers "does he still owe
  you?" ([the separation argument](#the-separation-argument)). It is not [condonation](#condonation).
- **The word's history settles nothing:** the Lord's Prayer names God's forgiveness of sin with the
  verb the Greek Old Testament uses for releasing loans in Deuteronomy 15
  ([`theology-of-debt` 2.2](../theology-of-debt/lessons/02-02-forgive-us-our-debts.md)); whether
  forgiving a wrong *is* a release is conceptual, and whether anyone ought to forgive is normative.

Lessons: [5.1](lessons/05-01-forgiving-a-debt-forgiving-a-wrong.md)

### Condonation

**Treating a wrong as though it were not one.** Forgiveness keeps the judgment that the act was
wrong; condonation drops it ("everyone fudges what a loan is for"). In a looser sense,
condoning is failing to resist a wrong enough, which allows condoning by forgiving too readily.

Lessons: [5.1](lessons/05-01-forgiving-a-debt-forgiving-a-wrong.md)

### Standing to forgive

**Only the one wronged can forgive, as only the one owed can cancel a debt, on the standard
view.** Glen Pettigrove draws the analogy ("Standing to Forgive", 2009); some philosophers,
Pettigrove among them, with Govier and Walker, extend standing to suitably related third parties.

- **What a third party can do with a debt:** pay it, which satisfies the claim, or buy the claim
  and release it as its new holder. She cannot release a claim she does not hold, and on the
  standard view cannot forgive a wrong done to someone else.
- **A judge** holds no claim and suffered no wrong, so a lighter sentence is neither a release nor
  forgiveness; that is where the [paradox of mercy](#paradox-of-mercy) lives.

Lessons: [5.1](lessons/05-01-forgiving-a-debt-forgiving-a-wrong.md)

### Paradox of mercy

**If a lighter sentence is what justice requires, it is not mercy; if it is less than justice
requires, it is unjust.** Owned by [`philosophy-of-law`](../philosophy-of-law/syllabus.md) 5.4
and cited, not re-derived.

- **A creditor escapes it:** waiving what is owed to *her* wrongs no one, so her mercy is neither
  required nor unjust. Portia's plea to Shylock asks a creditor for exactly that ([3.3](lessons/03-03-what-may-be-pledged.md)).
- **An official does not,** since he administers claims not his own, unless punishment is a debt
  owed to someone who may waive it, the victim or the community. Nietzsche's §10 reads a
  community's leniency this way, as the luxury of a creditor grown rich ([4.1](lessons/04-01-the-animal-with-the-right-to-make-promises.md)). The price: two
  identical offenders may fare differently because one creditor waived.
- **Anselm denies the premise for God,** whom he treats as guardian of the order rather than a
  private creditor, so God cannot fittingly remit sin by mercy alone
  ([`theology-of-debt` 3.3](../theology-of-debt/lessons/03-03-anselm-cur-deus-homo.md)).
- **Whether a judge is more like the creditor or the guardian,** the release/forgive distinction
  cannot decide.

Lessons: [5.1](lessons/05-01-forgiving-a-debt-forgiving-a-wrong.md)

### The separation argument

**Releasing a debt and forgiving a wrong are different acts on different objects, so either
can happen without the other.** [5.1](lessons/05-01-forgiving-a-debt-forgiving-a-wrong.md)'s reconstruction, from Butler and the analysis of rights:

1. **To release a debt is to exercise a power over a claim:** the holder extinguishes the debtor's
   duty by declaring it ([release](#release)).
2. **A release requires standing, but neither a wrong nor any particular feeling.**
3. **Forgiveness answers to injury, not loss** (Sermon VIII): where no one did wrong there is
   nothing to forgive, only something to release.
4. **To forgive is to keep one's resentment within what the injury warrants and give up revenge
   and ill-will, while still judging the act wrong** (Sermon IX).
5. **Standing to forgive belongs to the one wronged** ([standing to forgive](#standing-to-forgive)).

∴ Releasing a debt and forgiving a wrong are distinct acts: either can occur without the other,
and they can have different agents. A creditor who was also wronged holds two things, a claim and
a grievance, and may give up one, both or neither.

- **Weakest: premise 4 as an account of what forgiving *is*** (the normative-power accounts). But
  the conclusion survives: on the power view, releasing the money and forgiving the lie release
  *different* claims.
- **[5.1](lessons/05-01-forgiving-a-debt-forgiving-a-wrong.md)'s cousin Teo** (borrowed 4,000 dollars "for a deposit", spent it on a holiday): Rosa can
  release and still resent; forgive and still collect; be paid by her mother, which satisfies the
  claim without releasing or forgiving anything; or shrug it off, which is condonation.

| What is given up | Who acts | Result |
|---|---|---|
| the claim | the creditor | release: the duty ends, whatever she feels; the grievance is untouched |
| the claim | a third party | pays the creditor, or buys the claim and releases it |
| the grievance | the one wronged | forgiveness: revenge given up, the judgment kept; the claim is untouched |
| the grievance | a third party | contested: victims only, or suitably related others? |

Lessons: [5.1](lessons/05-01-forgiving-a-debt-forgiving-a-wrong.md)

### Moral hazard

**The change in behaviour when someone else bears part of the cost of your risk.** The
economists' term for hidden action under insurance ([`grad-micro` 5.4](../grad-micro/lessons/05-04-moral-hazard-principal-agent.md)).
As an objection to relief it is a prediction about future behaviour, true or false whatever
anyone deserved. **Argument A** ([5.2](lessons/05-02-moral-hazard-and-fairness-to-those-who-paid.md)):

- **A1.** Relief of this kind will be expected, as a standing rule or a precedent likely to be
  repeated. *(Empirical.)*
- **A2.** Expected relief moves part of the cost of a bad outcome off the person who controls the
  risk, onto the creditor or the taxpayer. *(Conceptual: expected relief works like insurance.)*
- **A3.** People who bear less of a risk's cost take more of that risk, or less care to avoid it.
  *(Empirical.)*
- **A4.** The losses that follow (more default, dearer or scarcer credit for everyone else)
  outweigh what the relief achieves. *(Empirical, plus a weighing.)*
- **∴** The relief does more harm than good.

- **Weakest: A1 and A4.** The argument proves too much: insurance, bankruptcy and fire brigades all
  move costs off risk-takers, so an effect's existence settles nothing and only its size matters.
  Size depends on design: a rule or a one-off (a promise never to repeat it may not be believed,
  the time-inconsistency problem of [`economics-of-debt` 8.1](../economics-of-debt/lessons/08-01-forgiveness-commitment-and-the-fresh-start.md));
  conditions attached; who bears the loss (a write-down charged to the creditor makes lenders more
  careful; a bailout paying creditors in full makes them less so). The defender: a one-off is
  rarely credible, since every rescue is a precedent.
- **Its existence is close to conceptual; its size is empirical.** A philosopher can check whether
  an argument has shown A1 and A4 or only asserted them.
- **Not a synonym for "undeserved".** Moral hazard can hold of relief for the wholly blameless (a
  standing rule repaying furnace-repair loans after a cold snap). Using it for "unearned" smuggles
  a desert verdict into what sounds like economics, as the 1834 report's "bounty on indolence and
  vice" does ([less eligibility](#less-eligibility)).
- **HIPC** ([5.2](lessons/05-02-moral-hazard-and-fairness-to-those-who-paid.md) Example 1): the creditors' objection, as [`history-of-debt` 5.5](../history-of-debt/lessons/05-05-jubilee-2000-and-hipc.md)
  reports it, was that forgiving once teaches every future borrower and lender that debts can be
  escaped. The design answered it: relief became irrevocable only at a "completion point" after
  agreed reforms, and the multilateral creditors took losses on their own claims. Whether that
  shrank the hazard enough is A4, an empirical question.
- **In [5.4](lessons/05-04-jubilee-as-a-moral-principle.md):** a surprise release rewards borrowing already done and creates no hazard unless
  another is expected; a scheduled release has a known, dated hazard, which is why lenders stop
  lending as the date nears ([scheduled and surprise release](#scheduled-and-surprise-release)).

Lessons: [5.2](lessons/05-02-moral-hazard-and-fairness-to-those-who-paid.md), [5.4](lessons/05-04-jubilee-as-a-moral-principle.md)

### Fairness to payers

**The desert objection to relief: those who repaid, or never borrowed, stood where the relieved
stood, and relief treats them worse for doing what was asked.** It looks back, and holds even if
no one changes behaviour. **Argument B** ([5.2](lessons/05-02-moral-hazard-and-fairness-to-those-who-paid.md)):

- **B1.** Those who repaid, or never borrowed, stood where the relieved stood: similar debts,
  similar freedom to choose. *(Factual, plus a judgment of relevant similarity.)*
- **B2.** Like cases should be treated alike. *(Normative.)*
- **B3.** Relief treats them unalike: one group's debt vanishes, the other's was paid, and the
  payers may fund the relief as taxpayers. *(Factual.)*
- **B4.** Nothing justifies the difference; if anything the payers have the better claim, having
  borne the cost of their choices. *(Normative: desert.)*
- **∴** The relief is unfair to the payers.

- **Weakest: B1.** The groups may not be alike: some of the relieved suffered
  [brute luck](#brute-and-option-luck); others were wronged by their lenders, and then relief is
  [compensation](#relief-as-compensation), not a reward. Where B1 holds, denying relief makes no
  payer better off ([leveling down](#leveling-down-objection)), so a purely comparative complaint
  grounds at most leveling up.
- **The defender:** the complaint was never about equality for its own sake. The payers sacrificed
  in reliance on the rule that debts are paid; changing it afterwards makes their sacrifice look
  like a mistake, and funding the change from common money makes them pay twice.
- **Three groups, three complaints** ([5.2](lessons/05-02-moral-hazard-and-fairness-to-those-who-paid.md) Example 2, a one-time student-debt cancellation):
  those who repaid have a reliance complaint, those who chose cheaper paths a comparative one, and
  non-borrowers who fund the plan a distributive one, whose force turns on who in fact benefits.
  Leveling up can answer the first by crediting what they repaid; the third had no debt to credit.
- **HIPC:** the relieved are citizens who signed nothing, so B1 fails at the root unless citizens
  answer for their governments' choices, which is [6.3](lessons/06-03-whose-debt-is-it.md)'s question; short of that, the case
  against HIPC is an incentive case.

Lessons: [5.2](lessons/05-02-moral-hazard-and-fairness-to-those-who-paid.md)

### The two objections crossed

**The incentive objection looks forward and the desert objection looks back, so each can hold
without the other.** [5.2](lessons/05-02-moral-hazard-and-fairness-to-those-who-paid.md)'s map:

| | **Desert objection holds** (the relieved chose the risk; payers treated unalike) | **Desert objection fails** (brute luck, or a wrong done to the borrower) |
|---|---|---|
| **Incentive objection holds** (relief expected; costs shift off whoever controls the risk) | a standing pledge to cancel the loan of any small business that fails | a standing rule that the city repays furnace-repair loans after a cold snap |
| **Incentive objection fails** (relief unexpected, or charged to whoever caused the loss) | a one-off, never-repeated vote clearing water bills residents had stopped paying | a lender ordered to cancel an illegal hidden fee and refund those who already paid it |

Critics of relief place programs top left, defenders bottom right; the work is showing which
cell a real program occupies, and one program can put different borrowers in different cells.
Boss problem 5(c) builds its own come-apart case; the card does not.

Lessons: [5.2](lessons/05-02-moral-hazard-and-fairness-to-those-who-paid.md)

### Less eligibility

**The 1834 rule that relief must never make a pauper's lot as worth choosing as the lowest
independent labourer's.** The Royal Commission on the Poor Laws, whose members included Nassau
Senior and Edwin Chadwick (*Report*, London: B. Fellowes, 1834, p. 228; it became the Poor Law
Amendment Act that year): the pauper's "situation on the whole shall not be made really or
apparently so eligible as the situation of the independent labourer of the lowest class."
"Eligible" means worth choosing.

- **Sort the passage.** The rule ("really or apparently" is already incentive talk: people choose by
  how things look); an empirical claim about wages and work; an incentive conclusion, moral hazard
  before the name. Then a verdict arrives in the vocabulary: "proper position" could be the place
  that keeps incentives right or the place the pauper deserves; "a bounty on indolence and vice"
  judges character.
- **The principle can be defended on incentives alone or on desert alone,** and the passage never
  says which carries the weight.
- **Its evidence:** Mark Blaug, "The Myth of the Old Poor Law and the Making of the New" (*Journal of
  Economic History*, 1963), is the classic challenge.

Lessons: [5.2](lessons/05-02-moral-hazard-and-fairness-to-those-who-paid.md)

### Brute and option luck

**Option luck is how a deliberate gamble you could have foreseen and declined turns out; brute
luck is how risks fall that were no such gamble.** Ronald Dworkin ("Equality of Resources";
*Sovereign Virtue*, 2000; paraphrased). Available insurance links them: declined cover makes a
bad outcome option luck. Luck egalitarians compensate brute luck only (owned by
[`political-philosophy` 2.6](../political-philosophy/lessons/02-06-luck-relations-priority-and-sufficiency.md)).

- **Against the desert objection** ([5.2](lessons/05-02-moral-hazard-and-fairness-to-those-who-paid.md)): B1 fails for borrowers whose losses were brute luck.
- **The hard case, student debt** ([5.2](lessons/05-02-moral-hazard-and-fairness-to-those-who-paid.md) Example 2): a degree is a deliberate gamble, so its bad
  outcomes look like option luck; but it is often taken at eighteen, at prices others set, with
  the bankruptcy exit all but closed by statute. Does income-linked repayment count as the cover
  whose refusal makes a bad outcome option luck?
- **Bankruptcy** ([5.3](lessons/05-03-bankruptcy-as-a-moral-institution.md)): the desert account of discharge restates the old line between failure
  as sin and failure as misfortune: release for brute bad luck, not for gambles one chose.
- **The Jubilee** ([5.4](lessons/05-04-jubilee-as-a-moral-principle.md)): Dworkin would fault a periodic release for ignoring whether a loss
  came from choice.

Lessons: [5.2](lessons/05-02-moral-hazard-and-fairness-to-those-who-paid.md), [5.3](lessons/05-03-bankruptcy-as-a-moral-institution.md)

### Leveling-down objection

**Removing an advantage that benefits no one cannot be an improvement, so equality is not
valuable for its own sake.** Owned by [`political-philosophy` 2.6](../political-philosophy/lessons/02-06-luck-relations-priority-and-sufficiency.md).

- **Applied to relief** ([5.2](lessons/05-02-moral-hazard-and-fairness-to-those-who-paid.md)): denying relief to others makes no payer better off, so a purely
  comparative complaint grounds at most *leveling up*, such as crediting the payers.
- **The payers' reply:** their complaint is reliance and paying twice, not equality for its own
  sake ([fairness to payers](#fairness-to-payers)).

Lessons: [5.2](lessons/05-02-moral-hazard-and-fairness-to-those-who-paid.md)

### Relief as compensation

**Relief for a borrower whom the lender wronged is compensation owed in corrective justice by
the wrongdoer, not a reward.** Fraud and predatory lending ([3.2](lessons/03-02-predatory-lending-and-usury-caps.md)) turn relief into repair.

- **It defeats the desert objection** (the wronged borrower did not simply choose a risk), but not
  the complaint of taxpayers asked to fund it, unless the state shares responsibility or recovers
  the cost from the wrongdoer.
- **Bottom right of the map:** a lender ordered to cancel an illegal fee and refund those who paid
  it meets neither objection ([the two objections crossed](#the-two-objections-crossed)).

Lessons: [5.2](lessons/05-02-moral-hazard-and-fairness-to-those-who-paid.md)

### Creditors' bargain

**Bankruptcy's collective proceeding is what creditors would agree to in advance, before
knowing their place in the race to collect.** Thomas H. Jackson, paraphrased: first set out in
"Bankruptcy, Non-Bankruptcy Entitlements, and the Creditors' Bargain" (91 *Yale L.J.* 857, 1982),
then *The Logic and Limits of Bankruptcy Law* (1986). The pool saves going-concern value; beyond
that, bankruptcy should respect the rights creditors held outside it.

- **It justifies collective collection, not the discharge.** Release takes value from each
  creditor and gives it to someone outside the bargain, so creditors bargaining among themselves
  would not agree to it. Jackson defended the discharge separately (1986, ch. 10, "The Fresh-Start
  Policy in Bankruptcy Law"), drawing on psychology: borrowers systematically misjudge the risks
  they load onto their future selves, so the right must be one they cannot sign away.
- **The race and the pool** ([5.3](lessons/05-03-bankruptcy-as-a-moral-institution.md) Example 1, illustrative): three creditors owed 50,000 dollars
  each; the shop fetches 90,000 sold whole, 60,000 piecemeal. In the race the first to levy takes
  50,000, the second 10,000, the third nothing, so each expects
  $(50{,}000 + 10{,}000 + 0)/3 = 20{,}000$; the pool pays $90{,}000/150{,}000 = 0.6$ of each
  claim, 30,000 each for certain.
- **Taken alone,** the bargain's rule of respecting outside rights would have let Hunt's Illinois
  wage lien stand; the Court overrode it for the fresh start ([fresh start](#fresh-start)).
- **A different contract could produce a discharge:** a borrower choosing before she knows her luck
  might buy one as insurance, paid for in higher rates
  ([`economics-of-debt` 8.1](../economics-of-debt/lessons/08-01-forgiveness-commitment-and-the-fresh-start.md)). That reading grants the incentive
  objection in full and says lenders price it.
- **For states:** bondholders suing one by one face the same common-pool problem, and a collective
  action clause is the bargain written into the contract in advance
  ([`history-of-debt` 6.3](../history-of-debt/lessons/06-03-holdouts-and-the-law-of-sovereign-restructuring.md)).
- A mutual-advantage argument of the kind in [`ethics` 5.1](../ethics/lessons/05-01-contractarianism-morality-as-mutual-advantage.md);
  as mechanism design, [`economics-of-debt` 7.3](../economics-of-debt/lessons/07-03-designing-bankruptcy.md).

Lessons: [5.3](lessons/05-03-bankruptcy-as-a-moral-institution.md)

### Fresh start

**The discharge's purpose: a clear field for the honest debtor who surrenders what he owns.**
*Local Loan Co. v. Hunt*, 292 U.S. 234 (1934), Sutherland J. for the Court. Hunt had assigned
part of his future wages as security for a 300-dollar loan; after his discharge the lender sued
his employer to collect from later wages. Having quoted an earlier decision on relieving "the
honest debtor" so that he may "start afresh", the Court wrote:

> This purpose of the act has been again and again emphasized by the courts as being of public as
> well as private interest, in that it gives to the honest but unfortunate debtor who surrenders
> for distribution the property which he owns at the time of bankruptcy, a new opportunity in life
> and a clear field for future effort, unhampered by the pressure and discouragement of
> pre-existing debt.

And on the wage assignment: "The power of the individual to earn a living for himself and those
dependent upon him is in the nature of a personal liberty quite as much if not more than it is a
property right. ... From the viewpoint of the wage-earner there is little difference between not
earning at all and earning wholly for a creditor."

- **"Fresh start" is not in the opinion.** "Start afresh" is, inside its quotation of *Williams v.
  U.S. Fidelity & Guaranty Co.* (236 U.S. 549), which spoke of "business misfortunes"; the
  Court's own gloss is the sentence above. Keep the two layers apart.
- **The second passage turns purpose into principle:** earning power as a liberty is premise P1 of
  the [future-freedom argument](#future-freedom-argument).
- **Against the Jubilee** ([5.4](lessons/05-04-jubilee-as-a-moral-principle.md)): a discharge releases one debtor at a time, after the fall, and
  can be refused to one who cheated. If the end of debt bondage and the discharge already prevent
  permanent dependence, bankruptcy satisfies the [Jubilee principle](#jubilee-as-principle) with
  no calendar. Defenders reply that dependence now runs through lost credit, lost housing and
  inherited poverty, which discharge does not reach: a dispute about present facts.
- **Not forgiveness and not a release:** the court ends the legal claim over the creditor's
  objection ([release](#release)). Whether a moral debt survives the discharge is argued inside
  the Catholic tradition in [`theology-of-debt` 6.3](../theology-of-debt/lessons/06-03-the-ethics-of-personal-debt.md).
- Boss problem 5(a) close-reads the purpose statement; the card leaves that reading to it.

Lessons: [5.3](lessons/05-03-bankruptcy-as-a-moral-institution.md), [5.4](lessons/05-04-jubilee-as-a-moral-principle.md)

### Future-freedom argument

**The discharge protects the debtor's future self, which no one may pledge away.** [5.3](lessons/05-03-bankruptcy-as-a-moral-institution.md)'s
reconstruction from *Local Loan* and [3.3](lessons/03-03-what-may-be-pledged.md); P3 to P5 are the lesson's, not the Court's:

- **P1.** Earning power is a liberty, not only an asset: "in the nature of a personal liberty".
- **P2.** An unlimited claim on future earnings commands that power: past some point, owing
  everything you will earn is a kind of servitude.
- **P3.** The conditions of future agency are [inalienable](#inalienability): no one may sell or
  pledge them, and the law should not enforce such a pledge even when freely made.
- **P4.** For an insolvent debtor, a surviving debt is such a claim: once present property is gone,
  only future earnings remain.
- **P5.** Honest surrender satisfies the creditor's rightful claim: he is entitled to what the
  debtor has, not to what P3 forbids anyone to pledge.
- **∴ C.** The law should discharge the honest debtor who surrenders her property, and the right to
  that discharge should not be waivable.

- **Weakest: P4, with P2 behind it.** A critic grants P3 and holds to [3.3](lessons/03-03-what-may-be-pledged.md)'s own line: a share
  of future income may be pledged, because a claim on earnings leaves the debtor free to choose
  whether and how to work ([pledging future labor](#pledging-future-labor)). Income tax already
  takes a share of everyone's earnings, and no one calls it servitude. So the argument earns a floor
  (exempt property, a cap on garnished wages, a time limit), not discharge of the whole balance.
- **The Court's reply on size:** the amount assigned "may here be small, but the principle, once
  established, will equally apply where both are very great." The critic: a cap is exactly the
  line that stops the slide. The defender: an unpayable debt harms by its horizon, not its monthly
  bite; the "pressure and discouragement" runs for life under any cap.
- **It does not condemn every surviving debt:** a capped, time-limited claim may pass P2, and child
  support sets the debtor's future against a child's, a conflict inside the argument rather than a
  counterexample to it.
- **Three accounts of the discharge compared:** desert grants it for the blameless only; the
  [creditors' bargain](#creditors-bargain) gives no reason for it; future freedom grants it to every
  honest debtor.
- **For a state it loses its debtor:** a sovereign can surrender almost nothing a court could sell,
  and its "future effort" is its citizens', many unborn when the debt was contracted ([5.3](lessons/05-03-bankruptcy-as-a-moral-institution.md)
  Example 2).

Lessons: [5.3](lessons/05-03-bankruptcy-as-a-moral-institution.md)

### Non-dischargeable debt

**Debts the law refuses to discharge, and the rival accounts that explain them.** US law
(11 U.S.C. § 523(a)) excepts from discharge, among others, child support and alimony, certain
taxes, debts obtained by fraud, debts for willful and malicious injury, fines owed to the
government, and student loans unless excepting them would impose "undue hardship" (the history is
[`history-of-debt` 6.5](../history-of-debt/lessons/06-05-student-and-consumer-debt.md)).

- **No single account explains the list.** Fraud and malicious injury fit desert: the debt was
  born of a wrong. Support, taxes and fines fit the *pricing argument*: a card issuer charged for
  the risk of discharge in its rate, but a child, a tax office or a court never chose to extend
  credit, so never priced it. Student loans sit worst with future freedom: the loan bought the
  very earning power a discharge would shield, and a new graduate has almost nothing to surrender.
- **The debts a law refuses to discharge show which reason it holds** ([5.3](lessons/05-03-bankruptcy-as-a-moral-institution.md)).
- Gambling debts are not a statutory category of non-dischargeable debt.

Lessons: [5.3](lessons/05-03-bankruptcy-as-a-moral-institution.md)

### Jubilee as principle

**No claim may strip a household of its means of independence for good, and periodically those
means come back.** Leviticus 25 abstracted into a limiting half and a restoring half. A sale of
farmland is really a lease running to the fiftieth year, and an Israelite who sells himself works
as a hired man until then (25:39–41): strictly, nothing is forgiven, because the claim was never
permanent. The law protects livelihoods, not property as such: a house in a walled city,
unredeemed after a year, is sold for good, while fields and village houses go back (25:29–31).

**What comes back, three readings:** *restoration* (each family recovers its own holding,
however large: the letter of Leviticus, backward-looking and not equalizing); *sufficiency* (no
one stays at another's mercy for good); *a level start* (everyone begins again with equal means;
[starting-gate equality](#starting-gate-equality)).

**The argument** ([5.4](lessons/05-04-jubilee-as-a-moral-principle.md)):
1. **Some goods are conditions of independence** (in Leviticus a family's fields and its members'
   freedom; in Paine a share of the earth's natural value).
2. **Left alone, debt moves those goods from debtors who fall behind to their creditors, and the
   loss passes to their children.** *(Empirical, and it has to hold now.)*
3. **No private title to those goods is absolute.** *Religious form:* the land "shall not be sold
   for ever: because it is mine" (Lev 25:23), so a seller only ever had a tenancy to sell.
   *Secular form:* no one made the earth, so no one may hold it in perpetuity against those born
   later, and private right covers the improvement, not the ground (Paine).
4. **So no claim, whether a debt, a sale or a pledge, may make that loss permanent.**
5. **A restoration fixed in advance honours 4 without taking anything from later claimants:**
   anyone who lends or buys after the rule is set pays only for a claim that runs out, as Leviticus
   prices land by the harvests left (25:15–16).

∴ Justice requires a periodic, scheduled restoration of the means of independence: in Leviticus,
land and persons return in the fiftieth year; in Paine, every twenty-one-year-old receives a stake.

- **Weakest: premise 3, in both forms,** against Nozick's [entitlement theory](#entitlement-theory).
  The religious form answers him only for those who accept God's title, and they keep its premise
  rather than its calendar: Catholic teaching treats the calendar as a lapsed judicial precept but
  holds owners to be stewards of goods destined for all
  ([`theology-of-debt` 1.2](../theology-of-debt/lessons/01-02-the-seventh-year-deuteronomy-15.md),
  [1.3](../theology-of-debt/lessons/01-03-the-jubilee-the-land-is-mine.md)). The secular form meets
  Nozick on shared ground, Locke's proviso, which is Paine's baseline and Paine's remedy; they part
  over a fact (Nozick: market economies readily satisfy the proviso; Paine: millions are worse off
  than if born before civilization began).
- **Premise 5 moves the fight to the transition:** a release written into titles wrongs no later
  buyer, but someone owned land outright when the law passed. Paine collects at death, when "the
  bequeather gives nothing: the receiver pays nothing"; the critic replies that the power to
  bequeath was part of what the owner held.
- **Other objections:** discharge already stops the slide (against premise 2); lenders price or
  evade the date (against premise 5).
- **The Jubilee does not cancel debts.** Leviticus 25 returns land and persons; debts are released
  in Deuteronomy's seventh year ([`theology-of-debt` 1.2](../theology-of-debt/lessons/01-02-the-seventh-year-deuteronomy-15.md)).
  Modern "Jubilee" campaigns merge the two.
- **Graeber's remedy** ([4.3](lessons/04-03-graebers-thesis-examined.md)): a Biblical-style Jubilee of international and consumer debt,
  agreed democratically. Collective, not a claim that no debt binds.
- **Jefferson's rule** ([6.1](lessons/06-01-the-earth-belongs-to-the-living.md)): voiding public debts 19 years after their date is a scheduled
  release for public debt, meant to put lenders on their guard and bridle war; Madison's warning
  that values fall as the expiry nears is the advance-notice problem.
- **The thread's showcase:** Leviticus 25 as law ([`history-of-debt` 1.3](../history-of-debt/lessons/01-03-release-laws-in-ancient-israel.md)),
  as theology ([`theology-of-debt` 1.3](../theology-of-debt/lessons/01-03-the-jubilee-the-land-is-mine.md)),
  as a mechanism lenders price ([`economics-of-debt` 8.1](../economics-of-debt/lessons/08-01-forgiveness-commitment-and-the-fresh-start.md)–[8.2](../economics-of-debt/lessons/08-02-the-scheduled-release.md)),
  and here as a candidate principle of justice.
- Boss problem 5(d) asks about a seven-year consumer Jubilee known in advance; the card gives no
  verdict.

Lessons: [4.3](lessons/04-03-graebers-thesis-examined.md), [5.4](lessons/05-04-jubilee-as-a-moral-principle.md), [6.1](lessons/06-01-the-earth-belongs-to-the-living.md)

### Starting-gate equality

**Equal resources at the start, laissez-faire afterwards: Dworkin's name for a view he
rejected.** Ronald Dworkin ("What Is Equality? Part 2", *Philosophy & Public Affairs*, 1981)
called it arbitrary, since equality owed at one moment is owed at others. It is not a position he
held.

- **Paine's stake at 21** is a one-time starting gate; a periodic Jubilee is a starting gate that
  comes round again, though one Dworkin would fault for ignoring whether a loss came from choice
  ([`political-philosophy`](../political-philosophy/syllabus.md) 2.5–2.6).

### Ground-rent

**Paine: only the improvement is private property, so every landholder owes the community a
rent for the ground itself.** *Agrarian Justice* (written 1795–96, published 1797; Conway's
edition): "it is the value of the improvement only, and not the earth itself, that is individual
property. Every proprietor, therefore, of cultivated land, owes to the community a *ground-rent*
... for the land which he holds"; man "had no right to locate as his property in perpetuity any
part of it; neither did the creator of the earth open a land-office"; "it is a right, and not a
charity, that I am pleading for."

- **"In perpetuity"** is Leviticus's "for ever" in secular dress; the **"land-office"** marks the
  difference (Leviticus's God keeps the title; for Paine no one ever issued a private one).
- **"The value of the improvement only"** concedes the Lockean point that the farmer owns what his
  labour added, so the remedy is a rent, not a share-out of farms, which Paine rejects as unjust.
- **"Owes" turns the Jubilee around:** landholders owe the community, and "a right, and not a
  charity" makes paying it justice, not relief.
- **The plan:** a national fund pays everyone fifteen pounds sterling at twenty-one, as partial
  compensation for the lost natural inheritance, and ten pounds a year for life from fifty, rich
  and poor alike; it is filled by a tenth of each estate passing at death, and a further tenth on
  estates going to distant relatives ([Paine's plan in figures](#paines-plan-in-figures)).
- **Later in *Agrarian Justice*,** whoever accumulates personal property beyond what his own hands
  produce owes part of it back to society: the *social debt* [6.4](lessons/06-04-debts-of-gratitude.md) examines.

Lessons: [5.4](lessons/05-04-jubilee-as-a-moral-principle.md)

### Entitlement theory

**Holdings are just if justly acquired and justly transferred, judged by their history, not by
their pattern.** Robert Nozick (*Anarchy, State, and Utopia*, 1974;
[`political-philosophy` 2.4](../political-philosophy/lessons/02-04-nozick-entitlement-and-the-challenge-to-patterns.md)), with rectification of past
injustice. Extremes of wealth prove no injustice by themselves. A Lockean proviso: an
appropriation wrongs anyone it leaves worse off than if the land had stayed in common, and the
remedy is compensation, not surrender; Nozick held that market economies readily satisfy it.

- **Against a Jubilee** ([5.4](lessons/05-04-jubilee-as-a-moral-principle.md)): a law that turns sales into leases takes part of what owners
  hold, including the power to sell for ever and to bequeath. It bites hardest on the transition.
- **Nozick's public address system** ([6.4](lessons/06-04-debts-of-gratitude.md)): neighbours who set one up and enjoy the broadcasts
  are not thereby obliged to take a turn running it; benefits thrust on people do not bind them,
  the objection Bourgeois's social debt must meet.

Lessons: [5.4](lessons/05-04-jubilee-as-a-moral-principle.md), [6.4](lessons/06-04-debts-of-gratitude.md)

### Scheduled and surprise release

**A release fixed in advance takes nothing from later lenders but creates a dated hazard; a
surprise takes claims held in good faith but creates no hazard, unless another is expected.**
[5.4](lessons/05-04-jubilee-as-a-moral-principle.md) compares an invented state, Marenia, that cancels all consumer debts on one unannounced
day and promises never to repeat it, with a release written into every loan in advance.

| | Surprise | Scheduled |
|---|---|---|
| Fairness to creditors | takes claims held on terms that never included a release | takes nothing from those who lent after it was set: they priced it in |
| Moral hazard | none, unless people expect another release | known and dated: a loan made just before the date need never be repaid, so lending freezes as the date nears |
| Credibility | "never again" is hard to believe, so lenders price the next surprise into every loan | "on this date, whatever happens": creditors will press for delay when it comes |
| Who bears the cost | lenders who lent in good faith, and future borrowers through a premium | borrowers who need credit near the date |

- **The freeze** is what Deuteronomy 15:9 forbids and Hillel's *prosbul* engineered around
  ([`history-of-debt` 1.3](../history-of-debt/lessons/01-03-release-laws-in-ancient-israel.md));
  **the pressure for delay** is Jeremiah 34, a release of slaves proclaimed under siege, then revoked.
- **National day vs personal clock:** under a personal clock each debt lapses a fixed term after its
  date, as in Jefferson's 1789 proposal. It avoids the freeze, but bounds each *claim*, not each
  household, since refinancing restarts the clock.
- **What those shut out of credit are owed:** Deuteronomy's duty to lend anyway; a charitable lender
  like the *monti di pietà* ([`history-of-debt` 2.2](../history-of-debt/lessons/02-02-jewish-lenders-and-the-monti-di-pieta.md));
  or Paine's stake, which makes borrowing less necessary.
- **A third route:** the Catholic appeals on international debt
  ([`theology-of-debt` 6.2](../theology-of-debt/lessons/06-02-international-debt-and-the-jubilee-call.md);
  the call is theology-of-debt's, not `catholic-social-teaching`'s) hold that debts bind but may not
  be exacted at the price of a people's hunger, and ask creditors to lighten, defer or cancel, so
  relief runs through the creditors' own [release](#release).
- The models of rules against discretion are [`economics-of-debt`](../economics-of-debt/syllabus.md)
  8.1's.

Lessons: [5.4](lessons/05-04-jubilee-as-a-moral-principle.md)

## Generations, nations and society

### Usufruct of the living

**Jefferson: each generation holds the earth as a life tenant holds an orchard, so none may
spend its successors' harvests.** Jefferson to Madison, 6 September 1789 (*Papers of Thomas
Jefferson* 15:392–97, as printed in *The Founders' Constitution*): "I set out on this ground,
which I suppose to be self evident, 'that the earth belongs in usufruct to the living': that the
dead have neither powers nor rights over it." A *usufruct* (Justinian, *Institutes* II.4) is the
right to use another's property and take its fruits without impairing its substance, ending when
the holder dies.

**Jefferson's argument** ([6.1](lessons/06-01-the-earth-belongs-to-the-living.md), in the letter's order):
1. **Usufruct.** The earth belongs in usufruct to the living; the dead have no powers or rights
   over it.
2. **Succession is conventional.** At death a holding reverts to society, and heirs or creditors
   take it only by society's law.
3. **The reductio.** If anyone could by natural right charge his successors with his debts, "he
   might, during his own life, eat up the usufruct of the lands for several generations to come,
   and then the lands would belong to the dead, and not to the living".
4. **Aggregation.** The whole has no more rights than its members have individually.
5. **No umpire.** Between generations there is no common superior, "no umpire but the law of
   nature": they stand as independent nations do.

∴ **C1.** No generation can bind the next to pay its debts; it may contract only what it can pay
in its own time. (Premises 6–7 and C2 are the [nineteen-year rule](#nineteen-year-rule).)

- **"By natural right" is the qualifier everything depends on.** Jefferson grants that a society
  may make an heir pay a decedent's debts; that requisition is "municipal only, not moral", the
  society's own law. His objection holds only between whole generations, which have no society
  above them.
- **"Self evident"** marks premise 1 as an axiom, not a conclusion. And a usufruct is a right over
  property *not one's own*: Jefferson never names the owner. Leviticus does
  ([`theology-of-debt` 1.3](../theology-of-debt/lessons/01-03-the-jubilee-the-land-is-mine.md)).
- **Weakest: not premise 1** (Madison accepts it for the natural earth) **but the step to C1.**
  Premises 1–5 show that the dead have no *authority* over the living; C1 needs that nothing else
  can bind the living to what the dead borrowed. Madison names two other routes, accepting the
  benefits ([benefit principle](#benefit-principle)) and [tacit consent](#tacit-consent). A
  Jeffersonian replies that neither reaches the unborn: a benefit you cannot refuse does not make
  you a debtor, and silence is not consent when you were born into the arrangement (fair play and
  tacit consent: [`political-philosophy`](../political-philosophy/syllabus.md) 1.2–1.3).
- **Burke and Paine:** Burke's partnership of the living, the dead and the unborn, and Paine's
  reply that the dead have no authority over the living ("governing beyond the grave"), belong to
  [`history-of-political-thought` 5.3](../history-of-political-thought/lessons/05-03-burke-prescription-against-abstract-rights.md),
  which quotes Paine; [6.1](lessons/06-01-the-earth-belongs-to-the-living.md) paraphrases.
- **Modern states refinance rather than repay,** so their debts can outlive the voters who ran
  them up ([6.2](lessons/06-02-public-debt-and-the-unborn.md)).

Lessons: [6.1](lessons/06-01-the-earth-belongs-to-the-living.md), [6.2](lessons/06-02-public-debt-and-the-unborn.md)

### Nineteen-year rule

**Jefferson's arithmetic: a debt, law or constitution binds only while a majority of the
adults alive at its date are still alive, which Buffon's table puts at about nineteen years.**

- **The fiction:** a generation comes of age together at 21 and dies together 34 years later (the
  average further life the bills of mortality gave a 21-year-old), so it could bind itself for 55
  minus its age: 34 years at 21, 33 at 22, one at 54.
- **Real, overlapping generations:** births and deaths are continuous, so Jefferson fixes one term.
  **Premise 6** (implicit in the letter): the will of the majority binds the whole, so an act binds
  until a majority of the adults alive at its date are dead. **Premise 7:** from Buffon's table,
  half of those aged 21 and over are dead within "18. years 8. months, or say 19. years as the
  nearest integral number".
- **∴ C2.** Public debts are void as to whatever remains unpaid 19 years from their date, and every
  law and constitution expires at 19 years; enforcing one longer is force, not right. The power of
  repeal is no substitute, since faction, bribery and unequal representation obstruct it.
- **A consequentialist side argument:** voiding debts after 19 years would put lenders on their
  guard and bridle the spirit of war, which makes the rule a scheduled
  [jubilee](#jubilee-as-principle) for public debt, and its success an empirical question.
- **Nineteen is not the principle.** The principle is that a generation binds only itself; 19 is the
  output of one mortality table, and a different table gives a different term.
- **Madison's objections to expiry:** for a constitution, a government revised every 19 years would
  lose the reverence antiquity lends it, breed faction and risk an interregnum; for ordinary laws,
  property rights resting on law would lapse, values would fall as the expiry approached, and
  industry would slacken.
- **Hume's precursor** (a paragraph added to "Of the Original Contract" in 1777): if generations
  succeeded each other all at once, "as is the case with silk-worms and butterflies", each might
  found its own polity; since real society is in perpetual flux, each new brood must conform to the
  established constitution. Jefferson's defenders: the 19-year cohort is only a clock, marking when
  an act's authors have stopped being the people it binds.
- **Part of the quarrel is factual:** Jefferson thought America's debts payable within the lives of
  those then alive; Madison doubted 19 years would discharge them.
- **A personal clock** ([5.4](lessons/05-04-jubilee-as-a-moral-principle.md)): each debt lapsing a fixed term after its date is exactly
  Jefferson's proposal for future debts, and refinancing restarts it.

Lessons: [6.1](lessons/06-01-the-earth-belongs-to-the-living.md)

### Benefit principle

**Madison: the dead may bill the living, but never for more than they left them.** Madison to
Jefferson, 4 February 1790 (*Papers of James Madison* 13:18–21): the living's title covers the
earth in its natural state only; improvements made by the dead are a charge on the living who take
their benefit. Some debts serve the unborn, such as debts for repelling a conquest, and some serve
mainly posterity (perhaps, he says, the present American debt), and 19 years may not pay them.

> All that is indispensable in adjusting the account between the dead & the living is to see that
> the debits against the latter do not exceed the advances made by the former.

- **Madison's own caution:** few of the burdens nations had inherited would pass even this test; he
  would welcome Jefferson's principle as a curb on the living's power to burden their successors
  unjustly. (He received the 6 September letter only in early 1790, enclosed with Jefferson's letter
  of 9 January.)
- **The clean case does not separate the two men:** a loan the living simply spent, like Jefferson's
  Genoese example, leaves advances of zero, so the successors owe nothing on either view. Only a
  debt that leaves something behind can.
- **A war of defence** ([6.1](lessons/06-01-the-earth-belongs-to-the-living.md) Example 2): Jefferson's rule makes the war generation pay within 19
  years (a level 8.27 million a year on 100 million at 5 percent, against 5 million a year in
  perpetuity), and its successors inherit the independence free; Madison: the charge should follow
  the advance. His principle strains in turn: what is independence worth to the unborn, and who
  keeps the account? The borrowers, who have every reason to find their debts were for posterity.
  Jefferson's rule needs no accountant; Madison's needs one who does not work for the borrower.
- **As a rule for public borrowing** ([6.2](lessons/06-02-public-debt-and-the-unborn.md)): borrow for what will still serve the later taxpayer,
  and charge her no more than it leaves her. Where the advance is an evil averted, the ledger needs
  a comparison with what she would otherwise have had, and for people born long after the
  borrowing the [non-identity problem](#non-identity-problem) says there is none.
- **As a ground of collective liability** ([6.3](lessons/06-03-whose-debt-is-it.md)): a debt binds up to the benefit the people
  received and no further, which can split a debt rather than accept or reject it whole.

Lessons: [6.1](lessons/06-01-the-earth-belongs-to-the-living.md), [6.2](lessons/06-02-public-debt-and-the-unborn.md), [6.3](lessons/06-03-whose-debt-is-it.md)

### Tacit consent

**Assent to established laws, inferred where no positive dissent appears.** Locke's version is
[`history-of-political-thought` 4.2](../history-of-political-thought/lessons/04-02-locke-limited-government-and-toleration.md);
Madison made it his remedy in 1790.

- **Madison's argument:** majority rule itself rests on compact, not on the law of nature, so it
  binds new adults only by their tacit assent; exclude tacit assent, and every law would need
  unanimous re-enactment or an express assent from each new member. [6.1](lessons/06-01-the-earth-belongs-to-the-living.md)'s argument map sets it
  against Jefferson's majority premise ([nineteen-year rule](#nineteen-year-rule)).
- **The critics** (Hume's peasant and ship; [`political-philosophy`](../political-philosophy/syllabus.md)
  1.2): silence is not consent without a real option to leave. The Jeffersonian: silence is not
  consent when you were born into the arrangement.

Lessons: [6.1](lessons/06-01-the-earth-belongs-to-the-living.md)

### Burden of public debt

**Does public debt burden the future, or only move money around inside it? Two books, both
accurate, answering different questions.**

- **The nation's books** (Abba Lerner, "The Burden of the National Debt", 1948; slogan: "we owe it
  to ourselves"): if a country borrows from its own savers, the later taxes go to bondholders in the
  same country in the same year, so it is not a cent poorer; a war's real cost (steel, soldiers'
  time) was paid when the resources were used, and later output cannot be sent back to the
  battlefield. Lerner excepted debt owed abroad, whose repayment ships goods out.
- **The persons' books** (James Buchanan, *Public Principles of Public Debt*, 1958): only persons
  bear burdens. The savers swapped cash for bonds because they preferred the bonds, so they
  sacrificed nothing; the worker taxed thirty years later pays and gets nothing back.
- **Neither book says whether anyone is *wronged*.** That turns on an empirical question (does the
  borrowing also displace private investment?), a conceptual one (can a policy wrong someone whose
  existence it decided?) and a normative one dressed as arithmetic (what does a distant burden
  weigh today?).
- **Smith** (*Wealth of Nations* V.iii, 1776) reported the transfer defence ("it is the right hand
  which pays the left ... the nation is not a farthing the poorer") and called it mercantile
  sophistry: he accepted the transfer but denied it was harmless, since the taxes that pay the
  interest fall on landlords and owners of capital, so land goes unimproved and capital leaves. An
  empirical claim that foreshadows crowding out.
- **Hume** (*Of Public Credit*, 1752): the "modern expedient" is "to mortgage the public revenues,
  and to trust that posterity will pay off the incumbrances contracted by their ancestors", and
  posterity trusts its own posterity in turn: a rollover with no last payer (where Hume thought the
  chain ends: [`history-of-debt` 3.4](../history-of-debt/lessons/03-04-humes-prophecy-carrying-a-great-debt.md)).
- **"We owe it to ourselves" settles no moral question,** and "the taxpayer bears the burden" does
  not yet say she is wronged either.

Lessons: [6.2](lessons/06-02-public-debt-and-the-unborn.md)

### Uncompensated-burden argument

**A generation that borrows to consume sends the bill to people who never sat at the table.**
[6.2](lessons/06-02-public-debt-and-the-unborn.md)'s reconstruction; Buchanan supplies P2:

- **P1.** Borrowing now creates claims that later taxpayers must service, unless the state inflates
  the debt away or repudiates it. *(Empirical.)*
- **P2.** The bondholders are compensated, having chosen to trade cash for later payments; the later
  taxpayers pay and receive nothing. *(Conceptual: burdens are counted person by person.)*
- **P3.** When the money pays for present consumption, those taxpayers get no offsetting benefit,
  and none of them consented; most could not have, being children or unborn. *(Empirical, then
  normative.)*
- **P4.** Imposing on a person a cost she did not consent to, and that no benefit offsets, wrongs
  her. *(Normative.)*
- **∴ C.** Borrowing to fund present consumption wrongs the later taxpayers who service the debt.

| Position | Presses | Kind of claim |
|---|---|---|
| Lerner: internal debt is a transfer inside the future | P2 | conceptual |
| Ricardian equivalence: bequests cover heirs' taxes ([`grad-macro` 3.4](../grad-macro/lessons/03-04-social-security-transfers.md)) | P2 | empirical |
| $r<g$: the debt rolls over as its ratio to income falls ([`economics-of-debt` 5.2](../economics-of-debt/lessons/05-02-when-r-is-less-than-g.md)) | P1 | empirical |
| Crowding out: less private capital (Diamond, 1965; [`grad-macro` 3.1](../grad-macro/lessons/03-01-olg-model.md)) | strengthens P2: a loss even on the nation's books (if $r>g$) | empirical |
| Madison's debits and advances ([6.1](lessons/06-01-the-earth-belongs-to-the-living.md)) | P3 | normative and empirical |
| Parfit's non-identity problem | P4: a wrong to whom? | conceptual |

- **Weakest: P4, which proves too much.** Every generation inherits costs it never agreed to (laws,
  pension promises, bridges needing repair) with benefits it never paid for, and nobody thinks each
  such cost wrongs the heir. The premise needs a baseline, says a critic in Madison's line or
  Burke's: the heir is wronged only if the whole bequest leaves her worse off than it should.
  Defenders can restrict P4 to net burdens on people who exist either way, or make "worse off"
  non-comparative.
- **A borrowed rebate** ([6.2](lessons/06-02-public-debt-and-the-unborn.md) Example 1): today's young adults, still working when the bonds are
  serviced, are borrowing from their future selves; the transfer between generations falls on
  today's children and the unborn. Today's children exist however the rebate is financed, so P4's
  comparison is available for them, and the argument goes through unless an empirical escape holds
  (bequests, or $r<g$). What remains open is economics, not philosophy.

Lessons: [6.2](lessons/06-02-public-debt-and-the-unborn.md)

### Non-identity problem

**A policy that decides who will exist cannot leave those people worse off than they would
otherwise have been, since otherwise they would not have existed.** Derek Parfit (*Reasons and
Persons*, 1984, ch. 16): almost any large policy changes who meets whom and when children are
conceived, so within a few generations none of the people alive would have existed had the choice
gone the other way. His case: a community depletes its resources, making life slightly better for
two centuries and much worse after, yet worse for no one.

- **On public debt** ([6.2](lessons/06-02-public-debt-and-the-unborn.md) Example 2): the taxpayer a century after a war debt is not worse off
  than otherwise, so a person-affecting reading of P4 of the
  [uncompensated-burden argument](#uncompensated-burden-argument) finds no victim.
- **It cuts both ways:** Madison's claim that the war's benefit, a conquest averted, descends to her
  fails on the same comparison.
- **It arises only when the alternatives compared contain different people;** for people who exist
  either way (today's children) the comparison stands.
- **Replies:** *impersonal* (Parfit's "Same Number Quality Claim": with the same number of people
  either way, it is worse if those who live are worse off than those who would have lived; its
  price is population ethics, [`decision-theory`](../decision-theory/syllabus.md) 5.3–5.4);
  *non-comparative* (Seana Shiffrin: a burden can harm without making anyone worse off than
  otherwise; James Woodward: a choice can violate a right without harming; the price is a
  defensible threshold or list of rights); *standpoints* (contractualists such as Rahul Kumar ask
  whether a principle could be reasonably rejected from the standpoint of a *type* of person, such
  as whoever pays this tax in a century, [`ethics` 5.2](../ethics/lessons/05-02-contractualism-what-no-one-could-reasonably-reject.md));
  *bite the bullet* (David Boonin: such choices, if they leave lives worth living, wrong no one, so
  objections must rest on people who exist either way).
- **Catholic social teaching** extends the common good to future generations and calls
  intergenerational solidarity a matter of justice (*Laudato Si'* 159, 2015, paraphrased;
  [`catholic-social-teaching`](../catholic-social-teaching/syllabus.md) 6.1), a ground that, like the
  impersonal and standpoint replies, needs no victim made worse off.
- **It does not license burdening the far future:** it removes one explanation of the wrong, the
  person-affecting one, and leaves the others standing.

### Discounting

**Weighing a burden due in the future by a discount factor, a value judgment in arithmetic's
clothing.** At rate $r$, a burden $B$ due in $T$ years is worth $B/(1+r)^T$ today; $1/(1+r)^T$ is
the discount factor.

- **A century away** ([6.2](lessons/06-02-public-debt-and-the-unborn.md), illustrative): 1 billion dollars due in 100 years is worth 370 million
  today at 1 percent (factor 0.370), 52.0 million at 3 percent (0.0520) and 7.60 million at 5 percent
  (0.00760), a spread of about 49 times.
- **A rolled-over debt:** borrow 1 billion and roll it at 3 percent for 100 years, and the bill is
  $1.03^{100} \approx 19.2$ billion; discounted at 3 percent it is worth exactly the billion
  borrowed; at 1 percent, 7.1 billion; at 5 percent, 0.15 billion. In general a debt $B$ compounding
  at $i$ and discounted at $r$ is worth $B\,[(1+i)/(1+r)]^T$ today, more than was borrowed exactly
  when $r<i$.
- **Discounting at the borrowing rate is not neutral bookkeeping:** it builds in the answer, since a
  century of burden then weighs exactly the original loan, whatever the century holds. (It is also
  why [6.1](lessons/06-01-the-earth-belongs-to-the-living.md)'s lender was indifferent between repayment schedules at the loan's own rate.)
- **Which $r$?** The split [2.3](lessons/02-03-justifying-interest.md) took from [`grad-macro` 2.3](../grad-macro/lessons/02-03-ramsey-cass-koopmans.md):
  $r = \rho + \sigma g$, with $\rho$ pure [time preference](#time-preference), $g$ the growth rate of
  consumption per head, and $\sigma$ the curvature of utility (how fast an extra dollar's value falls
  as people get richer). Illustratively $\rho = 0$, $\sigma = 1.5$, $g = 2\%$ give 3 percent; adding
  $\rho = 2\%$ gives 5 percent. $g$ is a forecast; $\rho$ is a value judgment, the target of Ramsey's
  charge; $\sigma$ is partly each.
- The ethics of setting them belongs to [`philosophy-of-economics`](../philosophy-of-economics/syllabus.md)
  Module 4.

Lessons: [6.2](lessons/06-02-public-debt-and-the-unborn.md)

### Collective liability

**Three grounds on which citizens can owe what their state borrowed: they authorized it,
they benefited from it, or they belong to the community that contracted it.** [6.3](lessons/06-03-whose-debt-is-it.md)'s firm: a
treasurer who borrows with authority binds the firm even after she and its shareholders have gone;
one who forged her authority and pocketed the money binds it to nothing, and a lender who never
asked to see her authority bears the loss; if the money went on the firm's roof, the firm may owe
something anyway.

- **Authorization:** the officials who borrowed acted in the citizens' name, with authority they
  gave ([author and actor](#author-and-actor)).
- **Benefit:** the citizens received what the money bought (Madison's test, [benefit principle](#benefit-principle)).
- **Membership:** citizens of a democratic state are co-authors of its acts through institutions
  they share, so they may be held liable for its costs even when they voted against them (Anna
  Stilz, *Liberal Loyalty*, 2009, and later work, paraphrased). On this view the ground reaches
  dissenters in a democracy but not the subjects of a dictatorship. Without that limit a creditor
  could say that every national, as a member, owes every national debt.
- **Behind all three** sits the creditors' default rule, [state continuity](#state-continuity).
  [Odious debt](#odious-debt) is the claim that some debts fall outside every ground at once.
- **The grounds can split a debt** ([6.3](lessons/06-03-whose-debt-is-it.md) Example 2, an invented colonial railway): a claim up to
  the railway's continuing benefit and none for the rest. The strain: "benefit" needs a baseline
  (benefit compared with what?), which is where [6.2](lessons/06-02-public-debt-and-the-unborn.md)'s problem returns; and the remainder must
  fall on someone, the lenders or the imperial power that was the real author. In 1898 the American
  negotiators left the Cuban debt with Spain on much this reasoning
  ([`history-of-debt` 5.5](../history-of-debt/lessons/05-05-jubilee-2000-and-hipc.md)).
- **In relief debates** ([5.2](lessons/05-02-moral-hazard-and-fairness-to-those-who-paid.md)): the desert objection to relieving poor countries' debts fails at
  the root unless citizens answer for their governments' choices.

Lessons: [6.3](lessons/06-03-whose-debt-is-it.md), [5.2](lessons/05-02-moral-hazard-and-fairness-to-those-who-paid.md)

### State continuity

**The creditors' default rule: the debtor is the state, a legal person that persists through
changes of government, so a successor may not choose which of its predecessors' debts to keep.**

- **The Tinoco arbitration** ([6.3](lessons/06-03-whose-debt-is-it.md) Example 1; *Great Britain v. Costa Rica*, sole arbitrator
  William Howard Taft, 1923): Federico Tinoco seized power in Costa Rica in 1917 and fell in 1919;
  the restored government annulled his regime's acts. On continuity Taft sided with the claimants:
  Tinoco's regime had been the government in fact, so its acts could bind Costa Rica whatever other
  states had recognized. On the Royal Bank of Canada's claim he sided with Costa Rica: the bank had
  paid out the funds knowing they were going to Tinoco personally, and could not show it had
  furnished money for the government's legitimate use.
- **So Sack's third condition can operate inside a continuity framework,** not only against it,
  four years before Sack named it.
- **The continuity theorist's attack on the odious-debt argument:** liability does not run through
  the people at all; the three grounds explain only why the state's debts may fairly be charged to
  its taxpayers, not *whether* it owes.
- **A debtor cannot substitute a new debtor without the creditor's agreement** ([1.1](lessons/01-01-the-grammar-of-owing.md)): state
  succession is where politics tries to do it anyway.

Lessons: [6.3](lessons/06-03-whose-debt-is-it.md)

### Author and actor

**Hobbes's channel by which one person's covenant binds another: an actor speaks and acts in
another's name, and the author owns those words and acts.** *Leviathan* (1651), ch. XVI:

> From hence it followeth, that when the Actor maketh a Covenant by Authority, he bindeth thereby
> the Author, no lesse than if he had made it himselfe; and no lesse subjecteth him to all the
> consequences of the same. … And therefore he that maketh a Covenant with the Actor, or
> Representer, not knowing the Authority he hath, doth it at his own perill. For no man is obliged
> by a Covenant, whereof he is not Author; nor consequently by a Covenant made against, or beside
> the Authority he gave.

- **The channel has limits** ("against, or beside the Authority he gave"), and it puts the risk of
  unverified authority on the other party: in debt terms, a lender who does not know whether an
  official may borrow for the people lends "at his own perill".
- **Hobbes is not on the repudiating successor's side.** For Hobbes, subjects are authors of
  everything their sovereign does, whether it came to power by agreement or by conquest (chs. XVIII
  and XX; [`history-of-political-thought` 3.4](../history-of-political-thought/lessons/03-04-hobbes-the-sovereign-and-the-church.md)),
  so his own theory makes nearly every effective government authorized and favours continuity.
  Odious-debt theorists borrow his model and replace his theory of sovereignty.
- **Modern agency law's apparent authority** is not in the passage.

Lessons: [6.3](lessons/06-03-whose-debt-is-it.md)

### Odious debt

**Sack: a debt does not bind a people when it was contracted without their consent, not spent
for their benefit, and lent by creditors who knew both.** Alexander Sack (1927, paraphrased). Built
on the grounds of [collective liability](#collective-liability), [6.3](lessons/06-03-whose-debt-is-it.md)'s argument:

1. **P1.** Citizens owe a debt their state contracted only if they authorized the borrowing,
   benefited from it, or are bound through membership in a legitimate community that contracted
   it. *(Normative: the grounds are exhaustive.)*
2. **P2.** Authorization reaches only as far as the authority given: an actor who borrows "against,
   or beside" it binds no author. *(Conceptual, from Hobbes.)*
3. **P3.** A regime that holds power by force against the people's will has no authority from them
   to borrow. *(Normative.)*
4. **P4.** Money spent against the people, on their repression or the rulers' pockets, confers no
   benefit that could ground a claim on them. *(Empirical case by case, normative in principle.)*
5. **P5.** Membership binds citizens to the acts of a community whose institutions they share, not
   to the acts of a regime that shuts them out. *(Normative.)*
6. **P6.** A creditor who knew that P3 and P4 held lent at his own peril, and cannot claim the good
   faith that would shift the loss to the people. *(Normative.)*

∴ When no consent, no benefit and creditor knowledge hold together, no ground binds the people,
and the loss stays where it fell: on the lender.

| Sack's condition | What it defeats | Why |
|---|---|---|
| No consent | authorization; membership, for a regime that excluded the people | no authority was given, and no institutions were shared |
| No benefit | benefit | nothing was received that the people must restore |
| Creditor knowledge | the creditor's good-faith reliance, which state continuity protects | Hobbes: "at his own perill" |

Two conditions knock out grounds; the third removes the creditor's defence.

- **Weakest: P1, from two directions.** The [state continuity](#state-continuity) theorist denies
  that liability runs through the people at all. And creditors add the incentive objection: if a
  successor may repudiate after the fact by finding the three conditions, every lender to every
  poor state prices in the risk, and legitimate borrowers pay more. Michael Kremer and Seema
  Jayachandran (2003, paraphrased; the models are [`economics-of-debt`](../economics-of-debt/syllabus.md)'s)
  answered with timing: let an independent body declare a regime odious *before* further loans,
  so that only knowing lenders bear the loss and the successor's claim is not self-serving.
- **Not settled law.** The doctrine is often invoked and, as [`history-of-debt` 5.5](../history-of-debt/lessons/05-05-jubilee-2000-and-hipc.md)
  reports (Cuba 1898, Iraq 2004), never squarely applied by a tribunal. That is a historical claim;
  whether such debts bind is normative, and the absence of a ruling settles nothing either way.
- **Contrast Jefferson,** who allows even innocent buyers of perpetual grants no right to
  reimbursement, only generosity; Sack's conditions turn partly on what the creditor knew ([6.1](lessons/06-01-the-earth-belongs-to-the-living.md)).
- Boss problem 6 applies all this to a junta that built a port; the card does not.

Lessons: [6.3](lessons/06-03-whose-debt-is-it.md)

### Kant's fourth preliminary article

**No national debts for the external affairs of the state: a limit by purpose, not by
consent.** *Perpetual Peace* (1795; M. Campbell Smith's translation): "No national debts shall be
contracted in connection with the external affairs of the state."

- **What is allowed:** borrowing for the economic administration of the country (the improvement of
  roads, the settlement and support of new colonies, granaries against scarcity) is "above
  suspicion".
- **What is not:** a credit system used by the powers against one another is a war chest and a danger
  to peace, because "the final inevitable bankruptcy of the state in question must involve in the
  loss many who are innocent".
- **It forbids *contracting* such debts; it does not declare them void once contracted.** The remedy
  it names is other states uniting against the borrower, not a successor walking away. It says
  nothing about what a successor owes, so it does not license repudiating war debts.

Lessons: [6.3](lessons/06-03-whose-debt-is-it.md)

### Gratitude

**Owed to someone, but without a debt's other marks: no quantity, no transfer, no discharge by
payment.** A neighbour who drives your feverish child to hospital at two in the morning is owed
something, since ingratitude would slight *her*, but nothing she could price, sell or be paid off
for; a bank that lends you 2,000 dollars holds a claim with a sum, a date and a remedy ([1.1](lessons/01-01-the-grammar-of-owing.md),
[6.4](lessons/06-04-debts-of-gratitude.md)). So "owe" is wider than "debt".

- **When calling it a debt matters:** "Is gratitude really a debt?" is often a quarrel about a word
  ([`philosophical-method` 3.2](../philosophical-method/lessons/03-02-distinctions-and-verbal-disputes.md)).
  It stops being one when someone draws a debt's consequences from gratitude: that it can be
  demanded, priced, or paid off and closed. Each of those, applied to a parent or benefactor, changes
  the relation it describes ([6.4](lessons/06-04-debts-of-gratitude.md)).
- **Aristotle** (*NE* VIII.14, Ross): to gods and parents "no one could ever return ... the
  equivalent of what he gets"; so a son may not disown his father: "being in debt, he should repay,
  but there is nothing by doing which a son will have done the equivalent of what he has received,
  so that he is always in debt. But creditors can remit a debt; and a father can therefore do so
  too." The debt model run all the way through: a creditor, a measure the son can never reach, and
  the creditor's power of [release](#release). Yet the measure quietly changes: "friendship asks a
  man to do what he can, not what is proportional to the merits of the case".
- **The giver's purpose fixes the category:** a benefit given with a view to a return should be
  settled "just as if we had been benefited on fixed terms" (*NE* VIII.13); one given freely creates
  gratitude, not a debt.

**Four answers to what grown children owe** ([6.4](lessons/06-04-debts-of-gratitude.md)):

| View | Holder | Claim |
|---|---|---|
| Debt | Aristotle, *NE* VIII.14 | "always in debt"; the father may remit; explains why the duty lasts, at the cost of a permanent debtor who cannot pay |
| Gratitude | Seneca, *On Benefits* III.29–31 | owed but not collectible; a son can repay and even outdo the gift of life (whoever once healed the father would otherwise be owed an unpayable debt too) |
| Friendship | Jane English, "What Do Grown Children Owe Their Parents?" (1979), paraphrased | "owe" is the wrong word: favours asked for create debts, but parents' love is no favour asked for; duties track the present relationship and present needs, and fade if the friendship has ended |
| Special goods | Simon Keller, "Four Theories of Filial Duty" (2006), paraphrased | duties protect goods only the parent–child relationship can provide, such as being known and cared for by one's own child |

- **Nietzsche's darker version** of owing everything to those who came before is the
  [debt to the ancestors](#debt-to-the-ancestors) ([4.2](lessons/04-02-owing-the-ancestors-owing-god.md)).

Lessons: [1.1](lessons/01-01-the-grammar-of-owing.md), [6.4](lessons/06-04-debts-of-gratitude.md)

### Benefit and loan

**Seneca: a benefit that can be sued for has become a loan, so ingratitude should be hated, not
made actionable.** *On Benefits* III.6–8 (Aubrey Stewart's translation). Ingratitude is "this most
odious vice", and yet "With the exception of Macedonia, no nation has ever established an action at
law for ingratitude." Reconstructed ([6.4](lessons/06-04-debts-of-gratitude.md)):

1. **P1.** What makes a benefit a benefit is that the giver leaves its return to the receiver's free
   choice.
2. **P2.** A return that can be sued for is secured, so a benefit that can be sued for is a loan: "if
   I call him before a judge, it begins to be not a benefit, but a loan".
3. **P3.** Gratitude is admirable only when it is not compelled: "There is no credit in being
   grateful, unless it is safe to be ungrateful."
4. **P4.** A court needs a definite object it can measure, and a benefit's worth turns on the giver's
   cost, timing and spirit (the same sum is a trifle from a rich man and everything from a poor one,
   III.8), which no judge can weigh.

∴ Ingratitude is to be condemned, "only to be hated", but not made actionable: gratitude is owed,
but not as a debt. Keep the moral verdict; refuse the legal instrument.

- **Weakest: P2 and P4, from the law's own practice.** Roman law already turned some benefits into
  debts ([quasi-contract](#quasi-contract): the absent man must repay expenses "even though he knows
  nothing"), and modern law lets some rescuers recover their costs, so a benefit can be made
  collectible, at least for its cost, without being ruined. Seneca's defender: what is recovered is
  the expense, not the benefit. A critic presses P4 instead: some claims measure need, not benefit;
  a law letting a destitute parent claim support from an adult child asks what the parent lacks, and
  survives as a question about P2 and P3.
- **Seneca does not excuse ingratitude:** he calls it the most odious vice and denies only that it
  should be actionable, a claim about institutions, not about whether ingratitude is wrong.
- **Society as creditor** ([6.4](lessons/06-04-debts-of-gratitude.md) Example 2): Léon Bourgeois (*Solidarité*, 1896, paraphrased) holds
  that everyone is born into an inheritance of language, science, tools and public works built by
  others, so begins life a debtor to the association of past and present members, the *dette
  sociale*, grounded in a [quasi-contract](#quasi-contract), discharged by contribution and
  enforceable. That applies the debt model to society at full strength, exactly what Seneca refused
  for private benefits. A country that wanted its publicly trained surgeons to repay could have said
  so in advance with a service bond, a genuine debt; making the claim after the fact is Seneca's
  benefit turned into a loan.
- **"Debt" is literal where all four marks hold,** as with loans, taxes and fines; contested where a
  mark is missing or disputed; and a model to be argued for, not assumed, for guilt, gratitude and
  society ([6.4](lessons/06-04-debts-of-gratitude.md), looking back over the course; [debt](#debt)).

### Small loans, APR and EAR

| Quantity | Formula | Variables and note |
|---|---|---|
| APR | $n \times i$ | $i$ periodic rate, $n$ periods a year; no compounding (fees paid in cash) |
| Effective annual rate | $(1+i)^n - 1$ | the year's cost if each fee is itself financed |
| Examples ([3.2](lessons/03-02-predatory-lending-and-usury-caps.md)) | $1.15^{12} - 1 \approx 435\%$; $1.25^{12} - 1 \approx 1{,}355\%$ | APRs 180% and 300%; 300 dollars at 15% a month grows to about 1,605 owed, against $12 \times 45 = 540$ in cash fees |
| Monthly cost stack | $F/L + \text{expected loss} + \text{cost of funds}$ | $F$ fixed cost per loan, $L$ loan size; [3.2](lessons/03-02-predatory-lending-and-usury-caps.md)'s stack: $9 + 5 + 1 = 15$ per 100 a month ($27/300 = 9\%$) |
| Smallest loan a cap allows | $L \ge \dfrac{F}{c - \text{loss} - \text{funds}}$ | $c$ the cap as a monthly rate (an APR cap divided by 12); below it the cap rations |

*From* [3.2](lessons/03-02-predatory-lending-and-usury-caps.md)

### Marx's value arithmetic

| Surplus value | $s$ = new value added $- \; v$ | $v$ variable capital (wages); $c$ constant capital (materials, wear) |

### Paine's plan in figures

### Jefferson's arithmetic

| Quantity | Formula or figure | Note |
|---|---|---|
| The fiction | a generation may bind itself for $55 - \text{age}$ years | all come of age at 21 and die at 55: 34 years at 21, one at 54 |
| The real term | half of those aged 21 and over dead in 18 years 8 months, "or say 19" | from Buffon's table (23,994 deaths); the principle, not the number, is Jefferson's point |
| The Genoese terms | $1.05^{19} = 2.52695$; $0.05 \times 2.52695 = 12.635\%$ | nothing for 19 years, then 12⅝ per cent a year for ever, is 5 percent with the interest capitalized (12⅝ = 12.625) |
| Level repayment within 19 years | $A = P\,\dfrac{r}{1-(1+r)^{-19}}$ | $P = 100$ million at $r = 5\%$: $A \approx 8.27$ million a year, against 5 million a year in perpetuity; both are worth 100 million at 5 percent |

### Discounting a distant burden

| Quantity | Formula | Variables and note |
|---|---|---|
| Discount factor | $\dfrac{1}{(1+r)^T}$ | $r$ discount rate, $T$ years; at $T = 100$: 0.370 (1%), 0.0520 (3%), 0.00760 (5%) |
| Present value of a burden | $\dfrac{B}{(1+r)^T}$ | 1 billion due in 100 years: 370, 52.0 or 7.60 million |
| A rolled-over debt | $B(1+i)^T$ due; worth $B\left[\dfrac{1+i}{1+r}\right]^T$ today | $1.03^{100} \approx 19.2$; worth more than was borrowed exactly when $r<i$ |

### Splitting a state's debt

When part of a state secedes by agreement ([6.3](lessons/06-03-whose-debt-is-it.md) P3), candidate rules for its share of an
authorized debt: **by population** (its population share $\times$ debt); **by tax base** (its
share of the tax base $\times$ debt); **by benefit** (debt spent on assets it alone keeps, plus its
population share of debt spent on everyone). A debtor cannot substitute a new debtor without the
creditor's agreement ([1.1](lessons/01-01-the-grammar-of-owing.md)).

## Pitfalls

### Historical claims doing normative work

- **"Aquinas was refuted because practice moved on."** That interest became legal and ordinary is
  historical; whether a charge on a *mutuum* prices anything is conceptual; whether it is unjust is
  normative. A common practice can still be unjust, and the later admission of profit forgone does
  not by itself show P5 or P6 false. Neither the scholastic nor the economic view is refuted by the
  fact that practice moved on. *([2.2](lessons/02-02-aquinas-selling-what-does-not-exist.md), [2.3](lessons/02-03-justifying-interest.md))*
- **"My savings account pays interest, so Aristotle was wrong that money cannot breed."** He grants
  that lent money grows ("the birth of money from money"); his charge is that the growth is
  unnatural, a normative claim about money's use. *([2.1](lessons/02-01-aristotle-money-is-barren.md))*
- **"The argument is dated because Aristotle knew only coins."** Whether credit money has a different
  purpose is a conceptual question the date of a text cannot answer. *([2.1](lessons/02-01-aristotle-money-is-barren.md))*
- **"An explanation of interest is a justification of it."** Hume's three circumstances say what sets
  the rate; Böhm-Bawerk's premises 1–3 answer the theoretical problem, and only premise 4 carries it
  into the socio-political one. *([2.3](lessons/02-03-justifying-interest.md))*
- **"The market rate is a moral finding."** "Merchants charged high prices" is historical, "a
  competitive market would have charged the same" is an economic counterfactual, "the price was
  fair" is normative. *([3.1](lessons/03-01-what-exploitation-is.md))*
- **"'Caps hurt the poor' and 'caps protect the poor' are rival moral views."** Mostly they are
  rival predictions about whether a cap reprices or rations in a given market; "every civilized
  society has capped usury" settles neither. *([3.2](lessons/03-02-predatory-lending-and-usury-caps.md))*
- **"Refuting the barter myth weakens the duty to repay."** If credit came before coins, debt is older
  and more ordinary than the textbook says, which is no reason to think it binds less. *([4.3](lessons/04-03-graebers-thesis-examined.md))*
- **"The Jubilee argument stands or falls with ancient history."** Its premise 2 is empirical and has
  to hold *now*: whether discharge and the end of debt bondage already prevent permanent dependence
  is a dispute about present facts. *([5.4](lessons/05-04-jubilee-as-a-moral-principle.md))*
- **"Odious debt is settled international law."** It is often invoked and never squarely applied by a
  tribunal; that is historical, and the absence of a ruling settles the normative question neither
  way. *([6.3](lessons/06-03-whose-debt-is-it.md))*
- **"The two men disagree only about principle."** Part of the Jefferson–Madison quarrel is factual
  (could America's debts be paid in 19 years? would expiry depress property and credit?), part
  conceptual (what is a "generation" when births are continuous?), part normative (can a benefit
  bind someone who never consented?). Settling one leaves the others open. *([6.1](lessons/06-01-the-earth-belongs-to-the-living.md))*

### The genetic fallacy both ways

- **Origin to invalidity without a bridge:** "debt was born in violence, so nobody is obliged to
  repay"; "if guilt came from debt, guilt is really debt, or the duty to repay is debunked"; "the
  Second Essay shows nothing is owed to God". Each needs the unstated premise that origin fixes
  validity, and violent-origin arguments prove too much (property, states, GM II §3's memory).
  *([4.1](lessons/04-01-the-animal-with-the-right-to-make-promises.md), [4.2](lessons/04-02-owing-the-ancestors-owing-god.md), [4.3](lessons/04-03-graebers-thesis-examined.md))*
- **Origin to validity, the other way round:** "discharge began as a creditors' device, so it serves
  creditors"; "the law came to see failure as misfortune, so discharge is for the unfortunate".
  *([5.3](lessons/05-03-bankruptcy-as-a-moral-institution.md))*
- **Crying "genetic fallacy" when the bridge is supplied** is also a mistake: how a *particular* debt
  arose can decide whether it binds (Haiti's indemnity, extracted by warships, with the rule that
  force voids an agreement as the bridge), and a debunking explanation removes a belief's felt
  force as evidence when its causal story is independently supported. *([4.3](lessons/04-03-graebers-thesis-examined.md))*
- **"Promising is a social practice, so the practice is what makes breach wrong."** The first claim
  is conceptual and historical, the second normative; Owens grants the first and denies the second.
  *([1.3](lessons/01-03-promising-after-hume.md))*
- **"Explaining where a duty came from dissolves it."** Hume made the obligation of promises a human
  invention and held it no less binding for that. *([1.2](lessons/01-02-hume-promises-as-artificial-obligation.md))*
- **Citing `philosophical-method` 2.3 for the genetic fallacy by name.** It teaches the bridge-premise
  test and never names the fallacy; the fallacy/debunking line is drawn in its 3.4. *([4.1](lessons/04-01-the-animal-with-the-right-to-make-promises.md), [4.3](lessons/04-03-graebers-thesis-examined.md))*

### Incentive and desert

- **"Moral hazard means undeserved."** It names an incentive effect, and it can hold of relief for
  the wholly blameless (a standing rule repaying furnace loans after a cold snap). Using it for
  "unearned" smuggles a desert verdict into what sounds like economics. *([5.2](lessons/05-02-moral-hazard-and-fairness-to-those-who-paid.md))*
- **The 1834 report's "bounty on indolence and vice"** dresses a normative verdict as an empirical
  prediction; less eligibility can be defended on incentives alone or on desert alone, and the text
  never says which. *([5.2](lessons/05-02-moral-hazard-and-fairness-to-those-who-paid.md))*
- **Running the two objections together.** An unexpected, never-repeated relief leaves the incentive
  objection nothing to grip while the payers' complaint stands; a standing rule for the blameless
  keeps the hazard with no desert complaint. Each needs its own evidence. *([5.2](lessons/05-02-moral-hazard-and-fairness-to-those-who-paid.md))*
- **"Economics has settled the incentive objection."** That some effect exists is close to
  conceptual; its size, and whether it outweighs the relief, is empirical and depends on design (rule
  or one-off, conditions, who bears the loss). *([5.2](lessons/05-02-moral-hazard-and-fairness-to-those-who-paid.md))*
- **Treating a loss as a wrong.** Relief is compensation only for a borrower someone *wronged*; a bad
  outcome of a freely chosen gamble is option luck. *([5.2](lessons/05-02-moral-hazard-and-fairness-to-those-who-paid.md))*
- **Forgetting what advance notice does.** A scheduled release takes nothing from those who lend
  after it is set but creates a dated hazard and a credit freeze; a surprise takes claims held in
  good faith and creates no hazard unless another is expected. *([5.4](lessons/05-04-jubilee-as-a-moral-principle.md))*

### Release, forgiveness and discharge

- **"Butler defines forgiving as ceasing to resent."** Sermon IX forbids "only the excess and abuse"
  of resentment; forswearing it is Strawson's formula and overcoming it Murphy's. "She forgave him but
  still resents it" is no contradiction for Butler. *([5.1](lessons/05-01-forgiving-a-debt-forgiving-a-wrong.md))*
- **"A release shows good-will," or "forgiveness ends the debt."** A release can be pure calculation;
  forgiveness leaves the claim standing, so "I forgave him" never answers "does he still owe you?"
  *([5.1](lessons/05-01-forgiving-a-debt-forgiving-a-wrong.md))*
- **"A third party can forgive a debt."** She can pay it (satisfying the claim) or buy it and release
  it; she cannot release a claim she does not hold or, on the standard view, forgive a wrong done to
  someone else. *([5.1](lessons/05-01-forgiving-a-debt-forgiving-a-wrong.md))*
- **"A judge's leniency is a release or forgiveness."** He holds no claim and suffered no wrong: that
  is the paradox of mercy. *([5.1](lessons/05-01-forgiving-a-debt-forgiving-a-wrong.md))*
- **"A discharge is forgiveness,"** or a creditor's release. The court ends the legal claim over the
  creditor's objection; no grievance is in question. *([5.3](lessons/05-03-bankruptcy-as-a-moral-institution.md))*
- **"A limit on compulsion is a release,"** or "selling a debt ends it." Collateral-only rules, time
  bars and the cost of suing limit collection; a sale transfers the claim; only the creditor's release
  ends it. *([1.4](lessons/01-04-contract-debt-and-the-duty-to-repay.md))*
- **"The Jubilee cancels debts."** Leviticus 25 returns land and persons; debts are released in
  Deuteronomy's seventh year. Check which release an argument needs. *([5.4](lessons/05-04-jubilee-as-a-moral-principle.md))*
- **"Release separates the accounts of promising."** All three let the promisee release the promisor;
  none lets the promisor release himself. *([1.3](lessons/01-03-promising-after-hume.md))*

### Legal and moral obligation

- **"Holmes licenses breach."** His sentence is a claim about what a legal duty is; moving from "the
  law asks only for damages" to "I owe only damages" is the slide his own paragraph attacks. *([1.4](lessons/01-04-contract-debt-and-the-duty-to-repay.md))*
- **"Efficient breach covers an unpaid debt."** Full compensation for a money debt is the debt itself;
  keeping the money moves wealth from lender to borrower. *([1.4](lessons/01-04-contract-debt-and-the-duty-to-repay.md))*
- **"Shiffrin wants courts to enforce promissory morality."** She denies it; her target is the reasons
  given for contract law's divergence, and she accepts distinctively legal ones. *([1.4](lessons/01-04-contract-debt-and-the-duty-to-repay.md))*
- **"Justinian defines moral obligation."** *Obligatio* is a bond "according to the laws of our State";
  whether a legal debt also binds in conscience is Module 1's question, and the four-mark argument is
  weakest exactly there. *([1.1](lessons/01-01-the-grammar-of-owing.md))*
- **"Jefferson denies that heirs must pay a dead man's debts."** He grants a society may require it by
  "municipal" law; his objection is only between whole generations. *([6.1](lessons/06-01-the-earth-belongs-to-the-living.md))*
- **"Seneca excuses ingratitude."** He calls it the most odious vice and denies only that it should be
  actionable: a claim about institutions. *([6.4](lessons/06-04-debts-of-gratitude.md))*
- **"Kant licenses repudiating war debts."** The article forbids *contracting* them and justifies other
  states in resisting a state that does; it says nothing about what a successor owes. *([6.3](lessons/06-03-whose-debt-is-it.md))*
- **"Hobbes is on the repudiating successor's side."** He supplies the structure (author, actor, the
  lender's peril), but his subjects are authors of whatever their sovereign does, so his theory favours
  continuity. *([6.3](lessons/06-03-whose-debt-is-it.md))*

### Owing is wider than debt

- **"Classifying something as a debt settles whether it must be paid."** The four marks fix shape, not
  force. *([1.1](lessons/01-01-the-grammar-of-owing.md))*
- **"To owe is to be in debt."** Gratitude is owed to someone yet has no quantity, no transfer and no
  discharge by payment: owed, not a debt (a build correction to the syllabus's list of debts without
  promises). *([1.1](lessons/01-01-the-grammar-of-owing.md), [6.4](lessons/06-04-debts-of-gratitude.md))*
- **"The creditor is whoever benefits."** A promise to pay my mother is directed to me, and my say-so
  can let you off. *([1.1](lessons/01-01-the-grammar-of-owing.md))*
- **"A quasi-contract is a kind of contract."** It is the agreement one *would* have made, and a
  hypothetical agreement binds no one merely by being hypothetical. *([6.4](lessons/06-04-debts-of-gratitude.md))*
- **"'Is gratitude really a debt?' is only about a word."** It stops being verbal when someone draws a
  debt's consequences: demanding, pricing, paying off and closing. *([1.1](lessons/01-01-the-grammar-of-owing.md), [6.4](lessons/06-04-debts-of-gratitude.md))*

### Interest, exploitation and pledges

- **"The consumptibles argument condemns only steep rates or loans to the poor."** Its ground is
  equality, not harm: any agreed surplus on a *mutuum*, even 1 percent from a rich borrower. *([2.2](lessons/02-02-aquinas-selling-what-does-not-exist.md))*
- **"Consumptible means destroyed."** Coins outlive their spending; the claim is that using money means
  parting with it. *([2.2](lessons/02-02-aquinas-selling-what-does-not-exist.md))*
- **"The four justifications of interest compete."** They stack as layers of one rate; opportunity cost
  is relative and passes the question down. *([2.3](lessons/02-03-justifying-interest.md))*
- **"Ramsey's charge condemns interest"; "add pure time preference on top of the safe rate."** It
  reaches only the $\rho$ layer, which is nested inside $r_f$; adding it again double-counts. *([2.3](lessons/02-03-justifying-interest.md))*
- **"Marx argues interest is unjust," or "Marx sides with Aristotle and Aquinas."** He calls the appeal
  to natural justice nonsense and grants the lender a real use-value; he agrees only that money does
  not breed. *([2.4](lessons/02-04-marx-interest-bearing-capital.md))*
- **"Exploitation means the loan left her worse off," or "a desperate borrower's consent is coerced."**
  The central cases are mutually advantageous, and only threats coerce. *([3.1](lessons/03-01-what-exploitation-is.md))*
- **"'Predatory' means expensive."** A rate exploits only by exceeding fair market value; a fair price
  can still crush a poor borrower, which is a complaint about the background. *([3.2](lessons/03-02-predatory-lending-and-usury-caps.md))*
- **"The non-worseness claim shows exploitation is harmless."** It compares a loan with no loan, says
  nothing against a fairer loan, and bears on weight before force. *([3.2](lessons/03-02-predatory-lending-and-usury-caps.md))*
- **"A usury cap is hard paternalism."** Only toward informed borrowers; remedies aimed at misprediction
  are soft (a build correction). *([3.2](lessons/03-02-predatory-lending-and-usury-caps.md))*
- **"Shylock's bond wrongs Antonio by exploiting him."** The loan carries no interest; if there is a
  wrong, it is in the security. *([3.3](lessons/03-03-what-may-be-pledged.md))*
- **"Inalienable means you cannot part with it at all,"** or "economists' inalienable human capital is
  this claim." Inalienability comes in degrees, and the economists' claim is empirical, this one
  normative. *([3.3](lessons/03-03-what-may-be-pledged.md))*

### Reading and translation traps

- **Aristotle:** the Greek of *Politics* 1258b2–4 says "not from that for which it was provided";
  "natural" in "the natural object of it" is Jowett's word. "Barren" is the later tradition's word:
  Aristotle grants that lent money breeds and calls the breeding unnatural, which is how 2.1 and 2.4 put it. Quote
  the 1921 revision of Jowett, not his 1885 or 1905 printings. *([2.1](lessons/02-01-aristotle-money-is-barren.md), [2.4](lessons/02-04-marx-interest-bearing-capital.md))*
- **Aquinas:** the sale-of-time argument is not in q.78; time enters only at a.2 ad 7. The New Advent
  text's "On like manner" is a slip for "In like manner". *([2.2](lessons/02-02-aquinas-selling-what-does-not-exist.md))*
- **Böhm-Bawerk:** cite Smart's translation (1891); Smart dates the German to 1888, so do not assert
  1889. **Senior** is paraphrased; nothing of his is quoted. *([2.3](lessons/02-03-justifying-interest.md))*
- **Non-domination** is cited to `political-philosophy` 3.1, not this course's 3.1; **Calabresi and
  Melamed** gave the three-rule framework, not the word "inalienability". In I.iii Shylock leaves the
  part of the body to his choice; the written bond read in IV.i says nearest the heart. *([3.3](lessons/03-03-what-may-be-pledged.md))*
- **Samuel's Nietzsche:** his "ought" and "owe" render *Schuld* and *Schulden*, so read guilt from
  debts, not duty; his §1 phrase is "an animal that *can promise*" (*versprechen darf*), so never put
  "the right to make promises" in quotation marks as his; §10 is "Grace ... the privilege of the
  strongest"; §21's "the creditor playing scapegoat for his debtor" is Samuel's, while "sacrificing
  himself" is Kaufmann's copyrighted wording; "a pound of his own flesh" is Samuel's addition; §20's
  "centuries" renders *Jahrtausende*, millennia; his §19 "middle period" sentence reverses the German,
  which concedes piety for that period. *([4.1](lessons/04-01-the-animal-with-the-right-to-make-promises.md), [4.2](lessons/04-02-owing-the-ancestors-owing-god.md))*
- **Graeber:** paraphrase him, never quote the formula about mathematics and violence; he names
  Aglietta, Orléan and Théret as primordial-debt theorists, not Ingham; `history-of-debt` 1.1 tested
  only his history of money, not his claims about violence and slavery; the lawyer's "surely one has
  to pay one's debts" is given in indirect speech. *([4.3](lessons/04-03-graebers-thesis-examined.md))*
- **Strawson's paper** is about free will, not Nietzsche; the reply to Nietzsche is only "built from"
  it. *([4.2](lessons/04-02-owing-the-ancestors-owing-god.md))*
- **Local Loan** never says "fresh start": "start afresh" is in its quotation of *Williams*, and the
  Court's own gloss is the "honest but unfortunate debtor" sentence. **Jackson's** creditors' bargain
  was first set out in 1982 and justifies collective collection, not the discharge. *([5.3](lessons/05-03-bankruptcy-as-a-moral-institution.md))*
- **Dworkin** named starting-gate equality in order to reject it. **The Catholic call** for debt relief
  is `theology-of-debt` 6.2's; `catholic-social-teaching` cedes international debt to it. **Paine's**
  "about three to one" does not match his own figures (2.25 to 1). *([5.4](lessons/05-04-jubilee-as-a-moral-principle.md))*
- **Jefferson's** Genoese terms are 12⅝ per cent (the web text's "125/8" is a flattened fraction), and
  "18. years 8. months, or say 19." is his rounding. Premise 6, majority rule, is implicit in the
  letter: it is Madison's outline of Jefferson, not Jefferson's words. *([6.1](lessons/06-01-the-earth-belongs-to-the-living.md))*

## Conventions

- **One card per course**, covering all 22 lessons. The linter checks that every lesson file is cited
  somewhere on this card.
- **Headings are anchors.** Renaming a `###` breaks inbound lesson links. Headings are ASCII only, so
  Böhm-Bawerk appears as "Boehm-Bawerk" in headings and *Schuld* without italics.
- **No prose dollar signs** (see CLAUDE.md): money is written "300 dollars".
- **Verdict-neutral.** Entries state each position as its defenders state it, give the lessons'
  skeletons, and name the premise its critics attack. None records a verdict for or against interest,
  forgiveness, caps, discharge, a Jubilee or repudiation.
- **Corrections over syllabus.** Where the build's checks against the sources corrected the syllabus or
  the lesson specs, the card follows the correction: gratitude is owed but not a debt (1.1, 6.4); the
  sale of time is not q.78's argument (2.2); pure time preference is a layer of the safe rate, not an
  addition (2.3, Boss 2(c)); Samuel's renderings of GM II §§1, 4, 10, 19, 20 and 21 (4.1–4.2);
  `philosophical-method` 2.3 does not name the genetic fallacy (4.1–4.3); Butler forbids only
  resentment's excess and abuse (5.1); *Local Loan* never says "fresh start" (5.3); Jackson's bargain
  is 1982 and justifies collection, not discharge (5.3); starting-gate equality is a view Dworkin
  rejected (5.4); the Catholic call for relief is `theology-of-debt` 6.2's (5.4); Jefferson's figure is
  12⅝ per cent (6.1); the *Politics* text is Jowett as revised in 1921 (2.1).
- **Boss problems are reserved.** The card gives the lessons' own skeletons, including those a boss
  problem also asks for, and nothing beyond them: no Hume, Scanlon or Owens verdict on the non-recourse
  homeowner (Boss 1), no seed-grain or machine test of q.78 and no 12 percent decomposition or
  critic-by-component map (Boss 2), no payday computation with the boss's figures and no reading of
  Portia's judgment (Boss 3), no step-by-step reconstruction of GM II §§4–6 and no settled
  Nietzsche–Graeber crux (Boss 4), no close reading of *Local Loan*'s purpose statement and no verdict on
  the ill borrower and the gambler (Boss 5), no verdict on the junta and its port (Boss 6), and no essay
  verdicts.
- **Paraphrase rule.** Public-domain texts are quoted in the translations the lessons confirmed. Modern
  authors (Scanlon, Owens, Fried, Shiffrin, Wertheimer, Graeber, Stilz, Parfit and the rest) are
  paraphrased and cited, never quoted at length.
