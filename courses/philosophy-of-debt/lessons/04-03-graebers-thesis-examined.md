# Philosophy of Debt · Lesson 4.3: Graeber's thesis examined

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 4: *Schuld*: debt, guilt and genealogy · Builds on: [4.1 The animal with the right to make promises](04-01-the-animal-with-the-right-to-make-promises.md), [4.2 Owing the ancestors, owing God](04-02-owing-the-ancestors-owing-god.md) · Unlocks: [5.1 Forgiving a debt, forgiving a wrong](05-01-forgiving-a-debt-forgiving-a-wrong.md), [5.4 Jubilee as a moral principle](05-04-jubilee-as-a-moral-principle.md)

## Why this matters

The anthropologist David Graeber opens *Debt: The First 5,000 Years* (2011) with a lawyer's reply to his case for cancelling poor countries' debts: surely one has to pay one's debts. The sentence persuades, he argues, because it is not an economic statement but a moral one, and the book sets out to show that the morality is confused. It is a genealogy like Nietzsche's in [4.1](04-01-the-animal-with-the-right-to-make-promises.md) and [4.2](04-02-owing-the-ancestors-owing-god.md), but one that ends in a normative conclusion. [`history-of-debt` 1.1](../../history-of-debt/lessons/01-01-credit-before-coins.md) tested his history of money. This lesson takes up the question both Nietzsche lessons left here: grant the history, and what does it prove?

## The idea

Three scenes. Someone at dinner asks you to pass the salt, and nobody thinks you are owed anything. You buy a coffee, pay and leave; neither of you owes the other a thing. A junior who once brought the boss a coffee finds, a month later, that the coffee is expected. Graeber reads these as [three moral principles](../reference.md#three-moral-principles) that run side by side through everyday life, ours included:

- **Communism.** From each according to ability, to each according to need, with no accounts kept. Not a regime: among people who are not enemies, it is the default whenever the need is great enough or the cost small enough. Graeber calls this *baseline communism* and makes it the foundation of all sociability.
- **Exchange.** Equivalence. Each side gives as much as it gets, and once the accounts balance, either can walk away.
- **Hierarchy.** Unequals bound by precedent. What was given before is owed again, and nobody expects the gifts to balance.

A debt, on this account, is [an exchange not yet completed](../reference.md#debt-as-unfinished-exchange). It holds between parties who are equals in principle, it is a precise sum, and paying it ends the relationship. Until then hierarchy rules: the debtor is, for now, the creditor's inferior. Precision sets a debt apart from every other obligation, and it takes money to state it. Graeber's sharpest claim concerns where precision came from. To put a number on what a person owes, or on the person, you must first tear them out of the web of relations that makes each one unique, and historically, he argues, violence did the tearing, with slavery as its paradigm. His own compression: a debt is a promise corrupted by mathematics and violence.

## Source

Nietzsche, *Genealogy of Morals* II §8 (Horace B. Samuel's translation, 1913; public domain), the passage Graeber argues with:

> The feeling of "ought," of personal obligation (to take up again the train of our inquiry), has had, as we saw, its origin in the oldest and most original personal relationship that there is, the relationship between buyer and seller, creditor and ower: here it was that individual confronted individual, and that individual *matched himself against* individual. … Sale and purchase, together with their psychological concomitants, are older than the origins of any form of social organisation and union

Two claims share the passage. One is about **origin**: guilt and obligation (Samuel's "ought" renders *Schuld*) began between creditor and debtor. The other is about **priority**: exchange is older than society. Graeber reads Nietzsche as taking Adam Smith's premise further. Grant the priority claim, he argues, and you reach something like [primordial debt](../reference.md#primordial-debt-theory), the theory he attributes to the French theorists of money Michel Aglietta, André Orléan and Bruno Théret: we are born owing society for our existence, the sovereign takes over that debt, and taxes measure it. It is a cousin of the chain 4.2 followed from ancestors to God. Graeber rejects the priority premise outright. He allows that Nietzsche may have meant to shock the bourgeois with their own assumptions, but calls that a game played inside them. His own order runs the other way: sociality first, exchange later and narrower. Which came first is the module's crux.

## The argument

**[Graeber's case against the maxim](../reference.md#graebers-case-against-the-maxim)**, reconstructed. The steps are his claims, paraphrased and tagged by kind; the joins are this lesson's.

- **P1.** Economic life runs on three moral principles, and exchange is only one of them. *(Conceptual.)*
- **P2.** A debt is an exchange awaiting completion: a precise sum, owed between parties equal in principle, which payment settles and ends. *(Conceptual.)* *In words:* the rule that debts must be paid is exchange's rule.
- **P3.** Precision requires stripping people and things of their contexts, and historically force did the stripping: war, conquest, slavery. *(Historical, resting on a conceptual claim.)*
- **P4.** The language of debt has again and again made relations founded on force look moral, because it casts the victim as the one in the wrong. *(Historical.)* *In words:* tribute, indemnity and the peon's account all present force as a bill.
- **P5.** So when the maxim that one must pay one's debts feels self-evident, that is exchange's rule wearing the authority of morality as a whole. *(A diagnosis: why the belief feels true.)*
- **∴ C.** Paying one's debts is not the essence of morality. Debts are human arrangements, and a democratic public may rearrange them, for instance by a [Jubilee](../reference.md#jubilee-as-principle). *(Normative.)* *In words:* that something is a debt does not settle that it must be paid; look at the relation behind it.

**Why not primordial debt?** It makes debt the essence of morality from the other side, and Graeber calls it a second myth, twin to barter. It cannot say how an infinite debt becomes a sum. In the ancient states it cites, taxes fell on conquered peoples, not citizens. "Society" as one bounded creditor is a nation-state idea. And its Vedic texts name debts to gods, sages, ancestors and humanity, too vast and diffuse to pay anyone. Barter made the market look natural; primordial debt does the same for the state.

**What a [genealogy](../reference.md#genealogy) can show.** The [genetic fallacy](../reference.md#genetic-fallacy) is judging a belief or practice valid or invalid by where it came from. Run the bridge-premise test of [`philosophical-method` 2.3](../../philosophical-method/lessons/02-03-informal-fallacies-and-when-they-arent.md) on it: the move fails when its bridge, that the origin bears on validity, is simply assumed. The bridge can be supplied in three ways, and each has a place in Graeber's case. (i) *Against self-evidence:* the lawyer's "surely" presents the maxim as obvious and universal, and a history showing it is neither removes that support, though not the maxim. (ii) *Debunking:* if the best explanation of why the maxim feels binding is that it served creditors and conquerors, its felt force stops counting as evidence of its truth, provided the causal story is independently supported and does not also sink the debunker's own judgments ([`philosophical-method` 3.4](../../philosophical-method/lessons/03-04-intuitions-and-reflective-equilibrium.md)). (iii) *Origin as a validity condition:* how a particular debt arose can decide whether *it* binds, as [1.1](01-01-the-grammar-of-owing.md)'s critic held, and that is a different thing from a genealogy of debt in general. None of the three reaches a debt freely contracted between equals without a further premise.

**Where the argument is weakest.** Critics attack P5. The duty to repay, they say, is not borrowed from anything: it has its own ground in the [promise](../reference.md#promise), and none of Module 1's accounts of why promises bind rests on quantification or force. Graeber's own formula concedes as much, since subtracting the mathematics and the violence leaves a promise, which is what an ordinary borrower gave. On this view P3 and P4, however true, indict how debts are counted and enforced, not the obligation to repay. And if violent origins voided obligations, they would void too much: property, states and, on Nietzsche's §3, the very memory that makes promising possible. Bill Maurer, an admiring anthropologist reviewer (*Anthropological Forum*, 2013), adds that the book runs together two senses of origin, a timeless beginning and a datable event. In this lesson's terms, the conceptual and the historical blur where the argument needs them apart. A defender replies that C never needed to void promises. Its target is the maxim's claim to be self-evident and supreme, and P4 shows how often what are called debts were never free promises between equals.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean: Haiti's indemnity).** In 1825 France offered Haiti recognition in return for 150 million francs, to be distributed to the former colonists, and delivered the offer with warships ([`history-of-debt` 4.4](../../history-of-debt/lessons/04-04-haitis-independence-debt.md)). Sort first. *Historical:* the squadron, and who the money went to. *Conceptual:* it had the shape of a debt in [1.1](01-01-the-grammar-of-owing.md)'s sense, a sum owed to a determinate creditor and discharged by payment. *Normative:* did Haiti owe it? Run P4 and the case fits: a demand backed by warships became an obligation, and from then on non-payment would make Haiti the party in the wrong. The move from origin to validity needs no grand history of debt here, because its bridge is the familiar rule that force voids an agreement. Hume cited that rule as evidence that promising is a convention (*Treatise* III.ii.5; [1.2](01-02-hume-promises-as-artificial-obligation.md)). The history of *this* debt bears on it directly, which is the thesis at full strength.

**Example 2 (hard: the advance from the boss).** An invented case. A warehouse lends a new hire 3,000 dollars, interest-free, for a car to reach the job, repaid from her pay over a year; if she quits, the balance falls due at once. It is exchange held open: a precise sum between legal equals, ended by payment. It is also hierarchy: the creditor is her boss, and the debt makes quitting expensive. Does she owe the balance if she leaves in month four? A promise account says yes: she agreed freely and the lender relied on it ([1.4](01-04-contract-debt-and-the-duty-to-repay.md)). Graeber's frame says look at the relation, and now P2's *equal in principle* carries the verdict. Read it as formal equality and this is a debt like any other. Read it as parity of power and exit, and the loan starts to look like a tether ([3.3](03-03-what-may-be-pledged.md)). The scheme does not say which reading is right, and P3–P4 are idle either way. The verdict turns on present questions of domination and exploitation ([3.1](03-01-what-exploitation-is.md)), not on where debt came from.

## Watch out

- **You might think refuting the barter myth weakens the duty to repay.** That is a historical claim about money's origin. If credit came before coins, debt is older and more ordinary than the textbook says, which is no reason to think it binds less; nothing [`history-of-debt` 1.1](../../history-of-debt/lessons/01-01-credit-before-coins.md) found is a normative result.
- **You might think Graeber is a primordial-debt theorist.** Many readers took him for one, as Maurer's review notes. He calls it a myth, the state's counterpart to the market's myth of barter.
- **You might think Graeber concludes that no one need repay.** His conclusion is that paying one's debts is not the *essence* of morality, and his remedy is collective: a Jubilee that a democratic public agrees on.

## One-liner

> Graeber's history can show that *pay your debts* is neither timeless nor the whole of morality; whether a particular debt binds still turns on the promise behind it and the power around it.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢) *(Exegetical.)*** An invented podcast on debt makes six claims. Label each historical, conceptual or normative, with one clause of reason. Two of them mix kinds: split those and label each part.

(a) "Most of the oldest Mesopotamian tablets record who owed what to whom."
(b) "A gift you must return at a fixed value is not a gift at all; it is a loan."
(c) "Most people today believe it is wrong to default on a loan you could afford to repay."
(d) "A lender who knew the borrower could never repay has no right to be repaid."
(e) "Coinage spread with armies and slave markets, so a debt counted in money can never be a moral obligation."
(f) "A debt is only an exchange left open, so it has no more moral weight than the exchange it came from."

**P2 (🟡) *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)*** Three invented relations.

- **The family meal.** Four adult siblings share a monthly Sunday dinner. Each brings what they can and nobody keeps track. The brother who lost his job brings nothing for a year, and no one mentions it.
- **The bar tab.** A neighbourhood bar lets regulars run a tab until payday. The owner writes every drink in a book and charges no interest. When a regular settles up, the owner pours them one on the house.
- **The feudal due.** Forty years ago, after a bumper harvest, a manor's tenants brought their lord two geese at Michaelmas, and they have done so every year since. This year one tenant, short of geese, offers their price in coin. The steward refuses the coin and enters the tenant in the manor roll as in default.

(a) For each relation, name the principle or principles at work and the feature that marks each. (b) Using Graeber's definitions, say why the geese are not a debt and what the steward's word "default" does. Then say whether the case supports P4, or whether a defender of the maxim that debts must be paid can accept all of it. 100 words or fewer for (b).

**P3 (🔴, optional) *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)*** An invented op-ed paragraph, not the words of any real person:

> Debt was born in violence. The first accounts recorded what peasants owed to temples that could seize their children. The first coins paid the soldiers who took slaves. The first prices ever put on human beings were set in slave markets. Every loan today, your mortgage and your student loan included, carries that birthmark. Scholars have shown that "pay your debts" is a moral confusion. So stop feeling guilty: nobody is obliged to repay anything.

(a) Sort the paragraph's claims by kind, name the bridge premise that carries it from its history to its last sentence, and say how its conclusion differs from Graeber's. (b) Build a counterexample: a loan the paragraph's historical claims, even if all true, would not reach. Name the ground of the duty to repay that your case relies on and what the op-ed would have to add to reach it. If instead you think the op-ed can bite your case, say what that costs. 150 words or fewer for (b).

<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical — strict.)*

**Must hit, strict:**
- (a) **Historical.** It reports what surviving records contain, and looking at the tablets settles it.
- (b) **Conceptual.** It concerns what counts as a gift and what as a loan. The "must" describes the arrangement's terms; the claim is about what to call it.
- (c) **Historical** (empirical, about the present). It reports what people believe about morality, and a survey would settle it. Its topic is moral, but it asserts nothing about what is right.
- (d) **Normative.** It says what a lender is entitled to.
- (e) **Mixed.** "Coinage spread with armies and slave markets" is historical; "a debt counted in money can never be a moral obligation" is normative. The "so" needs an unstated bridge: that an origin bears on validity.
- (f) **Mixed.** "A debt is only an exchange left open" is conceptual; "no more moral weight than the exchange it came from" is normative.

**Wrong turns:** calling (c) normative because it is about morality. Reading the "must" in (b) as a moral claim. Calling (e) historical because its evidence is historical, which misses that its conclusion is a claim about obligation. Calling (f) purely conceptual because it opens with a definition.

**Model answer:** (a) Historical: a claim about what the tablets say. (b) Conceptual: about the meanings of "gift" and "loan". (c) Historical or empirical: a report of present beliefs, true or false by survey. (d) Normative: about a lender's entitlement. (e) Historical premise, normative conclusion. (f) Conceptual premise, normative conclusion. In (e) and (f) the "so" is where a bridge premise hides.

---

**P2** *(Exegetical (a) — strict · Evaluative (b) — any verdict.)*

**Must hit, strict (a):**
- **Family meal: communism.** No accounts are kept; each gives according to ability, and the out-of-work brother takes according to need. Keeping score would itself be the offence.
- **Bar tab: exchange, held open as a debt.** Every drink is recorded as an exact sum, the parties are equals, and paying the tab settles the account, after which either could walk away. The drink on the house is not exchange, since nothing is recorded and nothing is owed back. Accept it as communism or a gift that keeps the settled relation from closing, or as incipient hierarchy if regulars have come to *expect* it (precedent), provided the marking feature is named.
- **Feudal due: hierarchy.** Lord and tenant are unequals, and the geese are owed because they were given before (precedent), not as the return of anything received.

**Must hit, any verdict (b):**
- Why not a debt: no equality in principle, no equivalent received, and payment does not end the relation but renews it next year. The steward even refuses the equivalent sum.
- What "default" does: it lends a customary levy exchange's moral charge, casting the tenant as someone who took and failed to give back.
- A verdict on bearing, with a reason: the case illustrates P4's mechanism, *or* a defender of the maxim can grant it, since the maxim concerns genuine debts and misnaming a due is a separate wrong. Say whether one case can support a claim about a historical pattern.

**Wrong turns:** calling the geese a debt because the quantity is fixed. Custom fixes quantities too, and a fixed quantity is not an exchange between equals. Reading the steward's refusal of coin as a sign that the due is exchange; it shows the reverse, since exchange would accept an equivalent.

**Model answer (b), one of several:** The geese are owed by precedent between unequals, not as the return of anything received, and paying renews the due instead of ending it; the steward will not even take the equivalent in coin. So on Graeber's definitions this is hierarchy. "Default" lends a customary levy exchange's moral charge: the tenant looks like someone who took and did not pay back. That is P4's mechanism in miniature. But a defender of the maxim can grant all of it, since the maxim is about real debts. One manor illustrates the mechanism; P4 needs a pattern.

---

**P3** *(Exegetical (a) — strict · Evaluative (b) — any verdict.)*

**Must hit, strict (a):**
- **Historical:** "born in violence" and the three "first …" sentences (accounts, coins, prices of persons). "Scholars have shown" is a claim about scholarship, and "shown" overstates one book's argument.
- **The disguised step:** "every loan carries that birthmark" sounds historical but asserts that the origin attaches to each present loan. That is the bridge in figurative form.
- **Normative:** "stop feeling guilty" and "nobody is obliged to repay anything".
- **Bridge premise:** that if a kind of obligation arose through violence, no instance of it can bind now (origin fixes validity). This is the premise the [genetic fallacy](../reference.md#genetic-fallacy) leaves unstated.
- **Versus Graeber:** his conclusion is that paying one's debts is not the *essence* of morality and that a democratic public may rearrange debts, for instance by a Jubilee. It is collective, and it does not claim that no debt binds.

**Must hit, any verdict (b):**
- A precise loan with no coercion, deception or domination in its own making.
- The ground of the duty named: a free promise the lender relied on, or the fairness of returning what was lent (Module 1).
- Why the historical claims do not bear on that ground: they concern the practice's early forms, not this obligation's validity conditions.
- What the op-ed would need: a claim about the present loan, such as coercion or an inequality that makes it hierarchy dressed as exchange, with its kind noted (a claim about now, not history). *Or*, biting the bullet, the cost: the argument voids too much, since property, law and (on *Genealogy* II §3) promising itself have violent origins.

**Wrong turns:** "the op-ed's history is false, so its conclusion fails". Refuting a premise does not refute a conclusion, and the counterexample should work even granting the history. Choosing a coerced or exploitative loan, which the op-ed's author would happily agree does not bind, for reasons that have nothing to do with the history. Attributing the op-ed's words to Graeber.

**Model answer (b), one of several:** Two friends, equal in means. One lends the other 2,000 dollars, interest-free, for a month's rent, to be repaid by spring. No temple, soldier or slave market figures in this loan. The duty to repay rests on the borrower's free promise, which the lender relied on, and on the fairness of returning what was lent. The op-ed's history, even if all true, is about how the practice began, not about this promise, so it touches neither ground. To reach the case, the op-ed would need a claim about the present loan: that it was coerced, or struck between unequals. That is a claim about now, and here it is false. Biting the bullet costs too much: the same reasoning voids every promise, since on Nietzsche's §3 memory itself was burnt in by pain.

## Flashback

**From Lesson [4.1](04-01-the-animal-with-the-right-to-make-promises.md) (The animal with the right to make promises):** *(Exegetical.)* *Genealogy* II §10 (Samuel, 1913):

> The creditor has always grown more humane proportionately as he has grown more rich; finally the amount of injury he can endure without really suffering becomes the criterion of his wealth. It is possible to conceive of a society blessed with so great a *consciousness of its own power* as to indulge in the most aristocratic luxury of letting its wrong-doers go *scot-free*.—"What do my parasites matter to me?" might society say. "… I am strong enough for it."

(a) On the passage, what makes a creditor lenient, and what does his leniency measure? Quote the deciding words. **Two sentences.** (b) Does the society that lets its wrong-doers go judge that they owe it nothing? Quote the deciding words, and say what, in §9, makes a wrong-doer the society's debtor in the first place. **Three sentences.**

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):** wealth and power, not pity and not a verdict on the offenders: the creditor grows "more humane proportionately as he has grown more rich", the society acts from "consciousness of its own power", and its reason is "I am strong enough for it". Leniency measures wealth: "the amount of injury he can endure without really suffering becomes the criterion of his wealth".

**Must hit, strict (b):** **no.** It still calls them "wrong-doers" and "parasites", and what it endures is "injury"; going "scot-free" means going unpunished, not owing nothing, so the society forgoes collecting a debt it still reckons. On §9 the community has advanced its members peace, protection and the advantages of common life, against their pledges, and an offender is an "ower" who defaults on those advances and attacks his creditor besides: punishment is collection, and letting him go is a creditor's waiver.

**Wrong turns:** reading the leniency as compassion or moral progress: the stated reason is strength, and "parasites" is contempt. Reading "scot-free" as acquittal. Taking the scot-free society as a historical report, when the passage only says it is "possible to conceive" of one. Answering (b)'s last part from the private creditor of §§4–6 rather than §9's community as creditor.

**Model answer:** (a) The creditor is lenient because he is rich and strong: he grows "more humane proportionately as he has grown more rich", and the society's reason is "I am strong enough for it". His leniency measures his wealth, since "the amount of injury he can endure without really suffering becomes the criterion of his wealth". (b) No: it still calls them "wrong-doers" and "parasites" and counts what they did as "injury", so going "scot-free" means going unpunished, not owing nothing. The society waives collection of a debt it still reckons. They are its debtors because, on §9, the community advanced its members peace and protection against their pledges, and an offender defaults on those advances and attacks his creditor besides.
