# Philosophy of Debt · Lesson 5.2: Moral hazard and fairness to those who paid

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 5: Forgiveness, relief and the fresh start · Builds on: [5.1 Forgiving a debt, forgiving a wrong](05-01-forgiving-a-debt-forgiving-a-wrong.md), [3.2 Predatory lending and usury caps](03-02-predatory-lending-and-usury-caps.md) · Unlocks: [5.3 Bankruptcy as a moral institution](05-03-bankruptcy-as-a-moral-institution.md), [5.4 Jubilee as a moral principle](05-04-jubilee-as-a-moral-principle.md)

## Why this matters

Every proposal to cancel debts meets the same reply, usually in one breath: it will teach the next borrower not to pay, and it is unfair to those who did. HIPC's creditors ([`history-of-debt` 5.5](../../history-of-debt/lessons/05-05-jubilee-2000-and-hipc.md)) pressed the first; student-debt relief ([`history-of-debt` 6.5](../../history-of-debt/lessons/06-05-student-and-consumer-debt.md)) draws the second. These are two objections. One is a prediction about behaviour, true or false whatever anyone deserved. The other is a verdict about desert, which stands or falls however anyone behaves. They need different evidence, and much of the debate runs them together.

## The idea

Take the complaint "relief rewards people for not paying."

Read forward, it is a **prediction**. If people expect relief, some will borrow more, take more risk or repay less eagerly, and lenders will lend less carefully, because part of the cost of a bad outcome has moved to someone else. That is [moral hazard](../reference.md#moral-hazard), the economists' name for the change in behaviour that follows when someone else bears part of the cost of your risk ([`grad-micro` 5.4](../../grad-micro/lessons/05-04-moral-hazard-principal-agent.md)). Read backward, it is a **verdict**: the relieved chose their debts, the payers bore the cost of theirs, and relief treats them unequally. That is the complaint of [fairness to payers](../reference.md#fairness-to-payers).

Two cases pull them apart. A city council, in one unannounced vote that everyone believes will never be repeated, clears every water bill that residents had simply stopped paying. No one can act on a gift no one expects, so the incentive objection has nothing to grip. Yet the neighbour who paid on time was treated worse for doing what was asked. Now a standing rule: the city repays the repair loan of anyone whose furnace fails in a cold snap. Those it relieves did nothing wrong, so the desert objection is idle. But once the rule is known, fewer owners service their furnaces. **The incentive objection looks forward; the desert objection looks back.**

## Source

The Royal Commission on the Poor Laws, whose members included Nassau Senior ([2.3](02-03-justifying-interest.md)'s abstinence theorist) and Edwin Chadwick, reported in 1834, and its recommendations became the Poor Law Amendment Act that year. Opening its remedies for the relief of the able-bodied, it laid down [less eligibility](../reference.md#less-eligibility) as the first condition of relief (*Report*, London: B. Fellowes, 1834, p. 228):

> …that his situation on the whole shall not be made really or apparently so eligible as the situation of the independent labourer of the lowest class. Throughout the evidence it is shown, that in proportion as the condition of any pauper class is elevated above the condition of independent labourers, the condition of the independent class is depressed; their industry is impaired, their employment becomes unsteady, and its remuneration in wages is diminished. Such persons, therefore, are under the strongest inducements to quit the less eligible class of labourers and enter the more eligible class of paupers. The converse is the effect when the pauper class is placed in its proper position, below the condition of the independent labourer. Every penny bestowed, that tends to render the condition of the pauper more eligible than that of the independent labourer, is a bounty on indolence and vice.

"Eligible" means worth choosing. Sort the sentences. The first states a rule, and "really or apparently" is already incentive talk: relief must not even *look* better, since people choose by how things look. The second is empirical, a claim about wages and work. The third draws the incentive conclusion, moral hazard before the name. Then the verdict arrives in the vocabulary. A "proper position" below the labourer could be the place that keeps incentives right or the place the pauper deserves. "Bounty" meant a subsidy, and a subsidy increases what it pays for. "Indolence and vice" is a judgment of character. The principle can be defended on incentives alone or on desert alone, and the passage never says which carries the weight. Economic historians later disputed its evidence; Mark Blaug's "The Myth of the Old Poor Law and the Making of the New" (1963) is the classic challenge.

## The argument

**Argument A: the incentive objection.**

- **A1.** Relief of this kind will be expected, as a standing rule or a precedent likely to be repeated. *(Empirical.)*
- **A2.** Expected relief moves part of the cost of a bad outcome off the person who controls the risk, onto the creditor or the taxpayer. *(Conceptual.)* *In words:* expected relief works like insurance, and moral hazard is what hidden action does under insurance.
- **A3.** People who bear less of a risk's cost take more of that risk, or less care to avoid it. *(Empirical.)*
- **A4.** The losses that follow (more default, dearer or scarcer credit for everyone else) outweigh what the relief achieves. *(Empirical, plus a weighing.)*
- **∴** The relief does more harm than good. *In words:* forgive today's debts and you subsidize tomorrow's.

**Argument B: the fairness-to-payers objection.**

- **B1.** Those who repaid, or never borrowed, stood where the relieved stood: similar debts, similar freedom to choose. *(Factual, plus a judgment of relevant similarity.)*
- **B2.** Like cases should be treated alike. *(Normative.)*
- **B3.** Relief treats them unalike: one group's debt vanishes, the other's was paid, and the payers may fund the relief as taxpayers. *(Factual.)*
- **B4.** Nothing justifies the difference, and if anything the payers have the better claim, having borne the cost of their choices. *(Normative: desert.)*
- **∴** The relief is unfair to the payers. *In words:* relieving those who didn't pay makes fools of those who did.

**Where the argument is weakest.** For A, it is A1 and A4. The critic says the argument proves too much: insurance, bankruptcy and fire brigades all move costs off risk-takers, so an effect's existence settles nothing and only its size matters. Size depends on design. Is the relief a rule or a one-off? (A promise never to repeat it may not be believed, the time-inconsistency problem that [`economics-of-debt`](../../economics-of-debt/syllabus.md) 8.1 models.) Do conditions attach? Who bears the loss? By the argument's own logic, a write-down charged to the creditor makes lenders more careful, and a bailout paying creditors in full makes them less so. The defender replies that a one-off is rarely credible, since every rescue is a precedent, so A1 holds more often than critics allow. For B, it is B1. The critic says the groups were not alike. Some of the relieved suffered [brute luck](../reference.md#brute-and-option-luck), a risk they could not have anticipated or declined, rather than option luck, the outcome of a deliberate gamble (Dworkin's distinction, cited to [`political-philosophy`](../../political-philosophy/syllabus.md) 2.6). Others were wronged by their lenders ([3.2](03-02-predatory-lending-and-usury-caps.md)), and then [relief is compensation](../reference.md#relief-as-compensation), owed in corrective justice by the wrongdoer, not a reward. And where B1 holds, denying relief makes no payer better off (the [leveling-down objection](../reference.md#leveling-down-objection), also 2.6), so the complaint grounds at most leveling up. The defender replies that the complaint was never about equality for its own sake. The payers sacrificed in reliance on the rule that debts are paid. Changing it afterwards makes their sacrifice look like a mistake, and funding the change from common money makes them pay twice.

## Map of cases

| | **Desert objection holds** (the relieved chose the risk; payers treated unalike) | **Desert objection fails** (brute luck, or a wrong done to the borrower) |
|---|---|---|
| **Incentive objection holds** (relief expected; costs shift off whoever controls the risk) | A standing pledge to cancel the loan of any small business that fails | The standing rule that the city repays furnace-repair loans after a cold snap |
| **Incentive objection fails** (relief unexpected, or charged to whoever caused the loss) | The one-off, never-repeated vote clearing water bills residents had stopped paying | A lender ordered to cancel an illegal hidden fee and refund those who already paid it |

Critics of relief place programs top left, defenders bottom right. The work is showing which cell a real program occupies, and one program can put different borrowers in different cells.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean): HIPC's creditors.** The creditors' objection, as [`history-of-debt` 5.5](../../history-of-debt/lessons/05-05-jubilee-2000-and-hipc.md) reports it, was that forgiving once teaches every future borrower and lender that debts can be escaped. That is Argument A, and the design answered it. Relief became irrevocable only at a "completion point" reached after agreed reforms, so it could not be had simply by borrowing. The multilateral creditors took losses on their own claims, so lenders bore part of the cost of what they had lent. Both shrink the cost of carelessness that A2 says relief shifts onto others; whether they shrank it enough is A4, an empirical question. Now run B. Who is relieved? The citizens of a poor country, who signed nothing, while the governments that borrowed may be long gone. B1 fails at the root unless citizens answer for their governments' choices, which is [6.3](06-03-whose-debt-is-it.md)'s question. A payers' complaint could come from a poor country that serviced its debts by cutting its budget, but that complaint concerns where the eligibility line fell, not whether the relieved deserved anything. The tool's verdict: short of a theory of collective responsibility, the case against HIPC is an incentive case. The Church's documents answer it the same way ([`theology-of-debt` 6.2](../../theology-of-debt/lessons/06-02-international-debt-and-the-jubilee-call.md)): repayment stays the norm, debtors take on duties, lenders take losses.

**Example 2 (hard): a one-time student-debt cancellation.** An invented plan: the state, itself the lender, cancels up to a fixed sum for every borrower under an income cap, once, from general revenue. (The real 2022 plan and its fate are in [`history-of-debt` 6.5](../../history-of-debt/lessons/06-05-student-and-consumer-debt.md).)

- **Incentive.** For loans already taken, A1 fails, since nobody borrowed expecting this. For future loans A1 is open, because a first cancellation is evidence of a second. The hazard also reaches colleges, which set prices that students may not end up paying.
- **Desert.** B1 is where the tools strain. A degree is a deliberate gamble, so its bad outcomes look like option luck. But it is often taken at eighteen, at prices others set, with the bankruptcy exit all but closed by statute. Dworkin makes insurance the bridge between the two kinds of luck: where cover was available and declined, a bad outcome counts as option luck. Does income-linked repayment count as such cover? If it does, a borrower who declined it chose the gamble. If it is too partial, he did not.
- **The payers** are three groups with three complaints. Those who repaid have a reliance complaint, those who chose cheaper paths a comparative one, and non-borrowers who fund the plan a distributive one, whose force turns on who in fact benefits, an empirical question. Leveling up can answer the first group by crediting what they repaid; the third had no debt to credit.

The tools yield no verdict. They say what each side must show: the opponent, that future borrowing and pricing will respond (A1, A3) or that the gamble was freely and fairly taken (B1); the defender, that it was not, or that the state, as the lender that financed the prices, shares the responsibility.

## Watch out

- **You might think "moral hazard" means "undeserved."** It names an incentive effect, and it can hold of relief for the wholly blameless, as the furnace rule shows. Using it for "unearned" smuggles a desert verdict into what sounds like economics, the 1834 report's move with "a bounty on indolence and vice": a normative verdict dressed as an empirical prediction.
- **You might think economics has settled the incentive objection, one way or the other.** That some effect exists is close to conceptual, since any cushion lowers the cost of a risk. Its size, and whether it outweighs the relief, is empirical and depends on design. What a philosopher can check is whether an argument has shown A1 and A4 or only asserted them.

## One-liner

> Moral hazard predicts tomorrow's behaviour and fairness to payers judges yesterday's choices; each can hold without the other, so each must be argued with its own kind of evidence.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢) *(Exegetical.)*** An invented op-ed, not the words of any real person:

> [S1] Cancelling these loans is moral hazard in its purest form. [S2] Moral hazard simply means rewarding people who haven't earned it. [S3] My neighbour worked a second job for six years to clear her loan, while most of the people now being relieved chose not to pay. [S4] It is unjust to everyone who played by the rules. [S5] The government swears it will never happen again, which only proves my point: it is a one-time gift to the undeserving.

(a) In what sense does the author use "moral hazard", what does the term mean in economics, and which of the lesson's two objections is doing the work? (b) Classify S2, S3 and S4 as conceptual, empirical or normative. Which one carries the factual premise the desert objection needs, and which premise of Argument B is it? (c) Suppose the promise in S5 is believed. What happens to each objection, and what would the author need to show to make a genuine moral-hazard argument? 150 words or fewer in total.

**P2 (🟡) *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)*** Two invented borrowers each owe 30,000 dollars to the state, which lent the money and approved both colleges for its loans. Dana enrolled at a for-profit college after it showed her job-placement rates that a regulator later found were falsified; she would not have enrolled had she known. Eli was admitted to a public university and chose a private one costing three times as much, for its prestige; his earnings disappointed. A proposal cancels both debts from general taxes.

(a) For each borrower, say whether the incentive objection and the desert objection apply, whether the loss is brute or option luck, and which cell of the lesson's map the proposal puts them in. Three sentences or fewer per borrower.
(b) Dana's advocate says: "Her relief is compensation for a wrong, so nobody who paid has any complaint." Assess the claim. Who owes Dana compensation? Does the reply answer a taxpayer who repaid her own loan? Does it reach Eli? Any verdict; 150 words or fewer.

**P3 (🔴, optional) *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)*** Matthew 20:12–15 (Douay–Rheims). The labourers hired at dawn complain that those hired at the eleventh hour received the same penny. The Challoner edition's note on "what I will" follows the passage.

> Saying: These last have worked but one hour, and thou hast made them equal to us, that have borne the burden of the day and the heats. But he answering said to one of them: friend, I do thee no wrong: didst thou not agree with me for a penny? Take what is thine, and go thy way: I will also give to this last even as to thee. Or, is it not lawful for me to do what I will? Is thy eye evil, because I am good?
>
> *Note:* Viz., with my own, and in matters that depend on my own bounty.

What the parable teaches about grace is theology's question. Here only the householder's argument is tested.

(a) The householder gives two distinct replies. State each in one sentence and name the words that carry it. Then say, in two sentences, what the note adds to the verse.
(b) A state cancels student loans from general taxes, and a graduate who repaid in full makes the labourers' complaint. Which reply carries over and which does not, and which words decide it? Does the surviving reply answer her? Any verdict; 150 words or fewer.

<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical — strict.)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** the author uses "moral hazard" to mean an *undeserved benefit*, which is a desert verdict. In economics it names an incentive effect: the change in behaviour when someone else bears part of the cost of your risk, a prediction about future conduct ([`grad-micro` 5.4](../../grad-micro/lessons/05-04-moral-hazard-principal-agent.md)). All the work is done by the desert objection, Argument B: "haven't earned it", "played by the rules", "undeserving".
**Must hit, strict (b):** S2 is conceptual, and false as a definition. S3 is empirical: one anecdote plus an unsupported generalization about why the relieved did not pay. S4 is normative, a desert claim. S3 carries the factual premise, and it is **B1**: that the relieved stood where the payers stood and chose freely not to pay.
**Must hit, strict (c):** a believed promise removes **A1**. Nobody expects relief again, so the incentive objection has nothing left. The desert objection is untouched, which shows it was the real objection all along. A genuine moral-hazard argument needs evidence that borrowers or lenders will expect a repeat despite the promise, and that they will change their behaviour enough to outweigh what the relief does (A3–A4).
**Wrong turns:** accepting S2 and then debating whether the relieved "earned" relief, which argues about desert while believing it argues about incentives. Reading S5 as strengthening the moral-hazard case, when a credible one-off is where moral hazard is weakest. Calling S3 normative because it sounds like blame: whether they "chose not to pay" is a question of fact.
**Model answer:** (a) The author means "undeserved"; economists mean the change in behaviour when someone else bears part of your risk's cost. Only the desert objection is at work. (b) S2 is conceptual (and wrong), S3 empirical (an anecdote stretched into a generalization), S4 normative. S3 supplies B1, the claim that the relieved chose as freely as the payers did. (c) A believed promise removes A1 and kills the incentive objection, while the desert objection stands untouched. A real moral-hazard argument would need evidence that future borrowers or lenders will expect a repeat anyway and will act on it enough to matter.

---

**P2** *(Exegetical (a) — strict · Evaluative (b) — any verdict.)*

**Must hit, strict (a):**
- **Eli:** option luck. The dearer university was a deliberate gamble he could have declined, since the cheaper one was on offer, so the desert objection applies: those who chose cheaper paths, or repaid, are treated worse for their prudence. The incentive objection applies to the extent the cancellation is expected to recur (A1): future students would choose dearer programmes and borrow more, and colleges could price for it. **Top left.**
- **Dana:** her loss came from a deception she could not reasonably have detected, not from a risk she could have foreseen and declined. So it is brute luck as to the fraud, or strictly a wrong, and her relief is compensation: the desert objection fails. The incentive objection fails for borrowers, since nobody chooses to be defrauded, but it applies to colleges if taxpayers pay while the college keeps the tuition, to the extent colleges come to expect such relief (A1). As proposed, that puts her **top right**. Charge the college and she moves to bottom right.

**Must hit, any verdict (b):**
- Name who owes compensation: the wrongdoer, the college. Add the state, if you argue that as lender and approver it shares responsibility.
- Separate the two versions of the payers' complaint. The reply defeats the desert version (Dana deserves relief), not the funding version (why should taxpayers who wronged no one pay?).
- Say whether the reply reaches Eli. It does not, since no one wronged him, so his relief needs another ground and faces both objections.
- A verdict with a reason.

**Wrong turns:** treating "compensation" as settling who pays. Extending the reply to Eli because he also lost money: a loss is not a wrong. Calling Dana's loss option luck because she chose to enroll, when she chose on false information.
**Model answer (b), one of several:** Compensation is owed by whoever did the wrong: the college, which falsified its rates, and arguably the state, which lent the money for study at a college it had approved. So the reply fully answers anyone who says Dana doesn't deserve relief. She was deceived, not imprudent. It does not by itself answer the taxpayer who repaid her own loan, since she wronged no one. The advocate must add that the state shares responsibility as lender and gatekeeper, or better, that it will recover the cost from the college. Otherwise the complaint survives in its funding form. My verdict: tax-funded relief for Dana is justified as an advance on the college's debt, provided the state pursues recovery. The reply does not reach Eli. No one wronged him, so his relief must rest on other grounds and meets both objections.

**P3** *(Exegetical (a) — strict · Evaluative (b) — any verdict.)*

**Must hit, strict (a):**
- **Reply 1, no wrong done:** "I do thee no wrong: didst thou not agree with me for a penny? Take what is thine". The complainant received exactly what he was owed, so the others' good fortune is no injustice to him.
- **Reply 2, liberty to give:** "is it not lawful for me to do what I will?" A giver may be generous as he chooses. "Is thy eye evil, because I am good?" recasts the complaint as envy, a wish that the others had less rather than that the speaker had more.
- **The note** supplies the premise that the gift comes from the giver's own resources ("with my own … my own bounty"). The verse alone claims a liberty to do "what I will", which is too strong as a principle, since no one may do what he wills with other people's goods. The note restricts it to his own.

**Must hit, any verdict (b):**
- Reply 1 carries over: the graduate's loan was honoured on its terms, and relief for others breaches nothing owed to her.
- Reply 2 does not, and the deciding words are the note's "with my own" and "my own bounty". Relief from general taxes is paid partly with her money, so it is not the state's "own bounty" in the householder's sense. The complaint survives in its funding form.
- A verdict on whether Reply 1 alone answers her, with a reason. The envy charge may be addressed, but it is not required.

**Wrong turns:** treating the envy question as a third argument about justice, when it is a diagnosis of the complainant's motive. Saying neither reply carries over because the state is not a householder: Reply 1 does not depend on who pays. Reading "what I will" as licensing anything, and missing the note.
**Model answer (b), one of several:** Reply 1 carries over. The graduate got exactly what she contracted for, and cancelling others' loans takes nothing she was owed. Reply 2 fails. The householder gives "with my own", but a state cancelling loans from general taxes gives with common money, some of it hers. Is Reply 1 enough? I think not. Her complaint was never that her contract was breached. It is that, as a taxpayer, she helps fund for others a relief she was never offered, under a rule changed after she relied on it. The householder's answer to that was Reply 2, and it is exactly the one that does not transfer. The envy charge lands only on a complainant who wants others to have less. She may want only not to pay.

## Flashback

**From Lesson [4.3](04-03-graebers-thesis-examined.md) (Graeber's thesis examined):** *(Exegetical.)* Diagnose. An **invented** speech by a finance minister on the eve of a tax deadline, written for this problem and not the words of any real person:

> "Every one of us is born in debt: to the parents who raised us, the teachers who taught us, the soldiers who kept us safe, and the society that made us who we are. Taxes are simply the instalments on that debt, and the state is the one creditor that collects them on behalf of everyone we owe. Nor is this a modern idea. The anthropologist David Graeber showed that credit came before coins, so this is the oldest debt there is."

(a) Name the theory the speech states, say how Graeber classifies it, and say why "credit came before coins" gives the speech no support. (b) Give two of Graeber's objections to the theory, each matched to the words of the speech it hits. **Two sentences per part.**

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):** **Primordial-debt theory**: we are born owing for our existence, the sovereign takes over the debt, and taxes measure it. Graeber calls it a **myth**, the state's counterpart to the market's myth of barter, so the speech enlists him for a theory he rejects. "Credit came before coins" is a **historical** claim about the origin of money. It says nothing about anyone being born in debt, and a finding about money's history is not a normative result, so it makes no debt bind more, just as it makes none bind less.

**Must hit, strict (b):** any two of these, each tied to its words:
- **No sum.** The theory cannot say how an infinite debt becomes a sum, yet "instalments on that debt" presuppose a balance to be paid down.
- **One bounded creditor.** "Society" as a single bounded creditor is a nation-state idea, and "the state is the one creditor that collects them on behalf of everyone we owe" assumes it.
- **Taxes fell on the conquered.** In the ancient states the theory cites, taxes fell on conquered peoples, not on citizens, so "Nor is this a modern idea" gets the history wrong.
- **Too diffuse to pay.** Debts to parents, teachers, soldiers and society, like the Vedic debts to gods, sages, ancestors and humanity, are too vast and diffuse to be paid to anyone, which is why the speech has to appoint one creditor to collect for all of them.

**Wrong turns:** Accepting the appeal to Graeber because he did argue that credit came before coins: that is history about money, and he calls primordial debt a myth. Concluding that taxes are not owed at all: Graeber's objections hit the claim that taxes repay a debt for our existence, not every ground for a duty to pay them. Whether anyone is born owing society is [6.4](06-04-debts-of-gratitude.md)'s question.

**Model answer:** (a) The speech states primordial-debt theory: we are born owing for our existence, and the state takes over that debt and collects it as taxes. Graeber calls the theory a myth, the state's counterpart to the market's myth of barter, and his claim that credit came before coins is history about money, which neither shows that anyone is born owing nor makes any debt bind more. (b) The theory cannot say how an infinite debt becomes a sum, yet "instalments" presuppose a balance to pay down. And "the state is the one creditor" treats society as a single bounded creditor, which Graeber calls a nation-state idea.

</details>
