# Philosophy of Debt - Syllabus

> Philosophy · Tier 2 · ~22 lessons · Prereqs: [`ethics`](../ethics/syllabus.md), [`history-of-debt`](../history-of-debt/syllabus.md) · Roadmap id: `philosophy-of-debt`

## Goal

This course asks the moral and conceptual questions that debt raises and that every other course in the **debt thread** leaves open. What is it to owe something? Is charging for a loan ever just, or ever unjust? What makes a loan exploitative? Did guilt grow out of debt, as Nietzsche claimed? Is forgiving debts fair to those who paid? Can one generation bind the next, or a people be held to what a tyrant borrowed? And is "debt to society" a real debt or a metaphor? You will finish able to tell a debt from a promise and a promise from a contract, and to reconstruct and test the canonical arguments about interest: Aristotle on the sterility of money, Aquinas on selling what does not exist, time preference and opportunity cost, and Marx on interest-bearing capital. You will be able to apply an account of exploitation to a payday loan, close-read the second essay of Nietzsche's *Genealogy* and ask whether Graeber's genealogy of debt has the normative force it claims, and give principled arguments about forgiveness, bankruptcy, the Jubilee, intergenerational public debt, odious debt and gratitude. The other three courses in the thread supply the material this course argues about. [`history-of-debt`](../history-of-debt/syllabus.md) supplies the episodes. `theology-of-debt` reads usury, the Jubilee and sin-as-debt from inside the Catholic tradition. `economics-of-debt` builds the models of default and moral hazard. The course takes no side: religious and secular positions are presented at equal strength, and no rubric rewards a verdict for or against interest, forgiveness or repudiation. It deliberately skips the usury doctrine and its development, the history of debt, formal models, bankruptcy and contract law as doctrine (see the table), and non-Western moral traditions of credit. The Islamic prohibition of *riba* and its arguments are named as a gap, not covered.

## How practice works in this course

Problems follow [HUMANITIES-BUILD-BRIEF.md](../../HUMANITIES-BUILD-BRIEF.md): every part declares its kind (exegetical, evaluative, formal; there are no apologetic parts in this course), and every solution is a rubric (must-hit moves, wrong turns) plus a model answer. The course leans on five archetypes:

- **Close reading (archetype 7), exegetical.** Every passage is public domain: Aristotle, *Politics* I (Jowett) and *Nicomachean Ethics* V and VIII (Ross); Aquinas, *Summa Theologiae* II-II q.78 (English Dominican Province); Justinian's *Institutes* on *obligatio* (Moyle); Hume, *Treatise* III.ii.5 and the essay *Of Interest*; Smith, *Wealth of Nations* II.4; Butler's sermons on resentment and forgiveness; Seneca, *De Beneficiis* (Stewart); Shakespeare, *The Merchant of Venice*; Kant, *Perpetual Peace* (Campbell Smith); Jefferson's letter to Madison and Madison's reply; Paine; Marx, *Capital* vols. 1 and 3 (Moore and Aveling; Untermann); Böhm-Bawerk, *The Positive Theory of Capital* (Smart); Nietzsche, *The Genealogy of Morals* (Horace B. Samuel's translation, 1913); Holmes, "The Path of the Law"; and *Local Loan Co. v. Hunt* (a US Supreme Court opinion). Modern philosophers (Scanlon, Owens, Shiffrin, Wertheimer, Graeber, Stilz and others) are paraphrased and cited, never quoted at length.
- **Reconstruct (archetype 1).** The canonical arguments of the course go into numbered premises with the weakest one flagged: Hume's case that promises are artificial, Aristotle's case against *tokos*, Aquinas's consumptibles argument, Böhm-Bawerk's three grounds, Nietzsche's derivation of guilt from debt, Jefferson's nineteen-year rule, and Sack's odious-debt doctrine.
- **Counterexample and apply to a hard case (archetypes 2-3), evaluative.** Principles about debt are tested on cases written for the problem: a strategic default on an underwater mortgage, a deathbed promise, a loan of a consumable versus a loan of a house, a payday rollover, a debtor who gambled and one who got sick, a war debt contracted by a junta, and a parent's claim on an adult child.
- **Find the crux (archetype 5), exegetical.** The debates here are old, and each lesson maps one: Aquinas and Böhm-Bawerk on what a lender sells, Marx and Senior on what interest rewards, Nietzsche and Graeber on which relation came first, Jefferson and Madison on whether benefits can bind the unborn, and the authorization and benefit views of collective liability.
- **Formal model (archetype 8), formal, and light.** Decomposing a loan rate into expected loss, opportunity cost and pure time preference (2.3); the effective annual rate of a rolled-over payday loan (3.2); Jefferson's generational arithmetic, and what a discount rate does to a burden a century away (6.1-6.2). Anything beyond arithmetic is cited to `economics-of-debt` or [`grad-macro`](../grad-macro/syllabus.md).

Evaluative parts are verdict-neutral. Is interest just? Should student debt be forgiven? Should a successor government repudiate odious debt? Any answer passes if the must-hit moves are made: state the principle precisely, run it on the case, name the premise the strongest objection attacks, and say whether that objection generalizes. Invented op-eds and speeches (archetype 4) are written fresh for the problem and never attributed to real people.

## Dangerous Checklist

When you finish, you can:

- [ ] Say what distinguishes a debt from a promise, a duty and a contract (quantity, transferability, a determinate creditor, discharge), and classify a hard case such as a tort judgment, a tax, a bearer bond or a debt of gratitude
- [ ] Reconstruct Hume's argument that the obligation of promises is artificial, and compare it with the practice, expectation (Scanlon) and authority (Owens) accounts on a promise no one else relies on
- [ ] State the case for and against a moral duty to repay a debt that the law would let you walk away from, using Fried, Holmes's prediction theory and Shiffrin's divergence thesis
- [ ] Reconstruct Aristotle's argument that *tokos* is the most unnatural form of acquisition and Aquinas's argument that charging for a loan of a consumable sells what does not exist, and name the premise each depends on
- [ ] Decompose an interest rate into its justifying components (expected loss, opportunity cost, time preference) and say which component each classical critic of interest attacks and which each defender relies on
- [ ] Explain Marx's account of interest as a share of surplus value, and name the crux between Marx and abstinence theorists about what a lender contributes
- [ ] Apply Wertheimer's account of exploitation to a lending case, distinguishing harmful from mutually advantageous exploitation, and test a usury cap against the non-worseness claim and the paternalism objection
- [ ] Close-read the second essay of Nietzsche's *Genealogy*, reconstruct his derivation of guilt from the creditor-debtor relation, and say whether Graeber's genealogy supports his normative conclusion or commits the genetic fallacy
- [ ] Distinguish forgiving a debt, forgiving a wrong, and a third party's relieving a debt, and assess the fairness-to-payers and moral-hazard objections to relief without confusing incentive claims with desert claims
- [ ] Give the moral case for and against bankruptcy discharge and a periodic Jubilee, and say what separates a scheduled release from an unexpected one
- [ ] Reconstruct Jefferson's argument that the earth belongs to the living and Madison's reply, and apply both to modern public debt and the non-identity problem
- [ ] State the authorization, benefit and membership grounds for holding citizens liable for their state's debts, map Sack's three conditions for odious debt onto them, and apply the result to a successor government's repudiation

## Modules

### Module 1: What is it to owe?

Before asking whether a debt is just, get clear on what a debt is: how it differs from a duty, whether it rests on a promise, and whether a contract is only a promise the state enforces.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1.1 | The grammar of owing | Tell a debt from a duty, a promise and a contract, and classify debts that arise without any promise | *obligatio* as a bond of law (Justinian, *Institutes* III.13), directed vs undirected obligation, the marks of a debt (a quantity, a determinate creditor, transferability, discharge by payment or release), debts without promises (torts, taxes, gratitude), credit as trust (Simmel cited to [`social-theory`](../social-theory/syllabus.md) 5.2) |
| 1.2 | Hume: promises as artificial obligation | Reconstruct Hume's argument that promising cannot bind by nature and must be a human convention | *Treatise* III.ii.5, natural vs artificial virtues, the puzzle of willing an obligation into being, convention and mutual interest, the "form of words", Kant's false promise cited to [`ethics`](../ethics/syllabus.md) 2.2 |
| 1.3 | Promising after Hume: practice, expectation, authority | Compare the three modern accounts of why a promise binds and run them on hard cases | Rawls's practice view ("Two Concepts of Rules"), Scanlon's Principle of Fidelity and the wrong of disappointed reliance, Owens on the promisee's authority interest, the deathbed promise, coerced promises |
| 1.4 | Contract, debt and the duty to repay | Decide whether a legal debt creates a moral duty to repay beyond what the law enforces | Fried's contract as promise, Holmes's prediction theory applied to contract ("The Path of the Law", 1897; the theory of law and Hart's reply cited to [`philosophy-of-law`](../philosophy-of-law/syllabus.md) 1.2), efficient breach, Shiffrin on the divergence of contract and promise, strategic default, owing a stranger who bought your debt (Hamilton's argument cited to [`history-of-debt`](../history-of-debt/syllabus.md) 4.1) |

**Boss problem 1:** A homeowner's house is worth far less than the mortgage. She can afford the payments, but walking away is legal (the loan is non-recourse), and the lender priced that option into the loan. (a) Close-read Hume's *Treatise* III.ii.5: state why Hume thinks a promise "would not be intelligible naturally", and what he thinks the obligation rests on instead. *(Exegetical.)* (b) Close-read Holmes's sentence in "The Path of the Law" identifying the duty to keep a contract with a prediction of damages. State the view, and name the words that make it a claim about law, not about morality. *(Exegetical.)* (c) Say what Hume's convention account, Scanlon's Principle of Fidelity and Owens's authority account each imply about the homeowner, and where each stops giving a verdict. *(Exegetical, then evaluative.)* (d) In 300-500 words: does she wrong the lender by defaulting strategically? Any verdict passes if it distinguishes the moral from the legal obligation, uses one account from 1.3 at full strength, and answers the objection that a sophisticated creditor who priced in default has no complaint. *(Evaluative.)*

### Module 2: The justice of interest

For most of Western history, learned opinion held that charging for a loan was unjust. This module reconstructs the arguments on both sides as philosophy. The doctrine and its development belong to `theology-of-debt`.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2.1 | Aristotle: money is barren | Reconstruct Aristotle's case that usury is the most unnatural form of wealth-getting | *Politics* I.8-10, household management (*oikonomia*) vs wealth-getting (*chrematistike*), natural vs unnatural acquisition, *tokos* (interest as "offspring"), money as a convention and a measure of exchange (*Nicomachean Ethics* V.5), **close reading** |
| 2.2 | Aquinas: selling what does not exist | Reconstruct the consumptibles argument and test whether it begs the question about what a lender sells | *Summa Theologiae* II-II q.78 a.1, consumptible vs non-consumptible goods, the loan (*mutuum*) transfers ownership, charging twice for one thing, the "sale of time" argument, the extrinsic titles (loss incurred vs profit forgone) as the argument's own limits; doctrine and authority ceded to `theology-of-debt` |
| 2.3 | Justifying interest: time, abstinence, opportunity | State the modern justifications of interest and say what each claims a lender gives up | Senior's abstinence, Böhm-Bawerk's three grounds for the premium of present over future goods, opportunity cost and risk, Hume's *Of Interest* (the rate tracks real factors, not the quantity of money), whether pure time preference is rational (Ramsey's charge, paraphrased; Euler equations cited to [`grad-macro`](../grad-macro/syllabus.md) 1.3), decomposing a rate (**formal**) |
| 2.4 | Marx: interest-bearing capital | Reconstruct Marx's account of interest as a claim on surplus value, and name his crux with the abstinence theorists | *Capital* vol. 3, Part V: M-M' as capital's "fetish" form, interest vs profit of enterprise, interest as a division of surplus value, the lender as owner rather than producer, Marx's mockery of abstinence theory in vol. 1; Marx's wider theory cited to [`social-theory`](../social-theory/syllabus.md) Module 2 |

**Boss problem 2:** (a) Close-read the passage at the end of *Politics* I.10 on usury and *tokos* (Jowett), and reconstruct Aristotle's argument in numbered premises. Which premise concerns the nature of money, and which the purpose of acquisition? *(Exegetical.)* (b) Reconstruct Aquinas's argument in ST II-II q.78 a.1 that charging for the use of a consumable sells the same thing twice. Then build a case, such as a loan of seed grain or a loan of money to buy a machine, that tests whether the argument's premise about "use" holds. *(Exegetical, then evaluative.)* (c) A lender charges 12 percent on a one-year loan. Given an expected loss rate, a safe return available elsewhere and a stated rate of pure time preference (supplied), decompose the 12 percent. Say which component Aristotle's argument, Aquinas's argument (with the extrinsic titles) and Marx's account each reject. *(Formal, then exegetical.)* (d) Name the crux between Aquinas and Böhm-Bawerk on what a lender transfers, and say what argument could move either side. *(Exegetical.)* (e) In 300-500 words: can charging interest on a loan to someone in need be just? Any verdict passes if it uses the decomposition in (c), states the strongest version of the opposing tradition, and does not treat either the scholastic or the economic view as refuted by the fact that practice moved on. *(Evaluative.)*

### Module 3: Exploitation, domination and the vulnerable borrower

A loan can be voluntary, mutually beneficial and still wrong. This module asks what exploitation is, whether predatory lending is its paradigm case, and what a borrower may not be allowed to pledge.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3.1 | What exploitation is | State a transactional account of exploitation and separate it from harm, coercion and Marxian exploitation | Wertheimer's account (taking unfair advantage; unfairness in dividing the gains), harmful vs mutually advantageous exploitation, consensual exploitation, the benchmark problem (what a fair price would be), Marxian structural exploitation as the rival (cited to 2.4) |
| 3.2 | Predatory lending and usury caps | Apply the account to high-cost credit and test interest-rate caps against it | payday loans and rollovers, the effective annual rate (**formal**), risk-based pricing vs taking advantage of desperation, the non-worseness claim, Smith on legal rates and "prodigals and projectors" (*Wealth of Nations* II.4), caps as hard paternalism (cited to [`political-philosophy`](../political-philosophy/syllabus.md) 3.3), **diagnose** problems |
| 3.3 | What may be pledged | Decide which things a person may not put up as security, and why | debt bondage and self-sale (*nexum* and peonage cited to [`history-of-debt`](../history-of-debt/syllabus.md) 1.5, 4.3), Shylock's bond and Portia's reading (*The Merchant of Venice* IV.i), Mill's slavery contract (cited to [`political-philosophy`](../political-philosophy/syllabus.md) 3.3), inalienability, debt as domination (non-domination cited to 3.1) |

**Boss problem 3:** A lender offers two-week loans at a flat fee per hundred dollars borrowed, with rollovers allowed. Most revenue comes from borrowers who roll over many times. (a) Given the fee and a typical number of rollovers (supplied), compute the effective annual rate and the total fees paid on one loan. *(Formal.)* (b) Apply Wertheimer's account: is this harmful or mutually advantageous exploitation, if either? Name the benchmark you use for a fair division of the gains, and say where the account stops giving a verdict. *(Exegetical, then evaluative.)* (c) Close-read Portia's judgment on Shylock's bond in *The Merchant of Venice* IV.i. What does the reading accept about the bond's validity, and what does it deny about how it can be enforced? State one principle about what may be pledged that the scene supports, and one it leaves open. *(Exegetical.)* (d) In 300-500 words: should the state cap the rate? Any verdict passes if it states the non-worseness claim at full strength, separates the exploitation objection from the paternalism objection, and says what the borrowers who lose access would be owed on your view. *(Evaluative.)*

### Module 4: *Schuld*: debt, guilt and genealogy

Nietzsche claimed that the moral concept of guilt descends from the material concept of debt. Graeber claimed that the morality of debt is a confusion born of violence. Both are genealogies, so the module asks what a genealogy can show.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4.1 | The animal with the right to make promises | Reconstruct Nietzsche's account of how memory, promising and the creditor-debtor relation formed the moral animal | *Genealogy* II §§1-10 (Samuel, 1913): the mnemotechnics of pain, the sovereign individual, *Schuld* and *Schulden*, cruelty as compensation, the community as creditor, mercy as the privilege of the powerful; the link to Module 1's accounts of promising; genealogy as a method cited to [`modern-philosophy`](../modern-philosophy/syllabus.md) 6.5 |
| 4.2 | Owing the ancestors, owing God | Follow Nietzsche from debt to ancestors to the Christian God, and state the religious replies at full strength | *Genealogy* II §§16-22: bad conscience, debt to the founders and the gods, the moralization of *Schuld* and *Pflicht*, the God who pays the debt himself; replies that treat grace as gift rather than settlement (satisfaction and grace as doctrine ceded to `theology-of-debt`), and secular critics of the derivation |
| 4.3 | Graeber's thesis examined | Separate Graeber's historical, conceptual and normative claims and test whether the history supports the norm | baseline communism, exchange and hierarchy as moral principles, debt as an exchange not yet completed, the primordial-debt theory he rejects, violence and the quantification of obligation, "one must pay one's debts" as a moral confusion, his reading of Nietzsche; the historical claims tested in [`history-of-debt`](../history-of-debt/syllabus.md) 1.1; the genetic fallacy (cited to [`philosophical-method`](../philosophical-method/syllabus.md) 2.3) |

**Boss problem 4:** (a) Close-read *Genealogy* II §§4-6 in Samuel's translation. Reconstruct in numbered premises Nietzsche's argument that the concept of guilt derives from the concept of debt, and say which premise is etymological, which psychological and which historical. *(Exegetical.)* (b) Name the crux between Nietzsche and Graeber: both tie morality to debt, but they disagree about which came first, the creditor-debtor relation or a prior sociality that exchange distorts. Say what kind of evidence or argument would move each. *(Exegetical.)* (c) A critic says both genealogies commit the genetic fallacy: even if guilt descends from debt, that shows nothing about whether guilt or the duty to repay is valid. Steelman a genealogist's reply in 150 words or fewer, saying what a genealogy can undermine without the fallacy. *(Evaluative.)* (d) In 300-500 words: does anything in Module 4 give a reason to doubt that one has a moral duty to pay one's debts? Any verdict passes if it keeps the historical and normative claims apart, uses one account of promising from Module 1, and states the strongest religious or secular response to the genealogists. *(Evaluative.)*

### Module 5: Forgiveness, relief and the fresh start

When debts cannot or should not be paid, who may cancel them, on what grounds, and at whose expense? The history is in [`history-of-debt`](../history-of-debt/syllabus.md). This module argues about it.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5.1 | Forgiving a debt, forgiving a wrong | Distinguish releasing a debt from forgiving an injury, and say who has the standing to do either | release as the creditor's normative power (Hohfeldian powers and waivable claims cited to [`philosophy-of-law`](../philosophy-of-law/syllabus.md) 6.1-6.2), forgiveness as overcoming resentment (Butler, *Fifteen Sermons* VIII-IX), mercy vs justice (the paradox of mercy cited to [`philosophy-of-law`](../philosophy-of-law/syllabus.md) 5.4), whether a third party can "forgive" a debt or only pay it, condonation, the creditor who forgives but still resents |
| 5.2 | Moral hazard and fairness to those who paid | Separate the incentive objection to relief from the desert objection, and test each on cases | moral hazard as an empirical claim (economics ceded to `economics-of-debt`; [`grad-micro`](../grad-micro/syllabus.md) 5.4), the complaint of those who repaid or never borrowed, brute vs option luck (cited to [`political-philosophy`](../political-philosophy/syllabus.md) 2.6), relief as compensation for a wrong (predatory loans, 3.2), student-debt relief as the hard case ([`history-of-debt`](../history-of-debt/syllabus.md) 6.5), the moral-hazard argument as HIPC's actors made it (5.5) |
| 5.3 | Bankruptcy as a moral institution | State the moral case for a discharge and the objections to it | failure as sin vs misfortune ([`history-of-debt`](../history-of-debt/syllabus.md) 4.2), the "honest but unfortunate debtor" and the fresh start (*Local Loan Co. v. Hunt*, 1934), Jackson's creditors' bargain, discharge as protecting future freedom (inalienability revisited from 3.3), non-dischargeable debts, why states cannot go bankrupt ([`history-of-debt`](../history-of-debt/syllabus.md) 6.3) |
| 5.4 | Jubilee as a moral principle | Assess a scheduled, periodic release of debts as a principle of justice, in its religious and secular forms | Leviticus 25 as law ([`history-of-debt`](../history-of-debt/syllabus.md) 1.3) and theology (`theology-of-debt`), read here as an argument: the land as held in trust, the prevention of permanent dependence; secular analogues (Paine's *Agrarian Justice*, starting-gate equality, inheritance taxation); scheduled vs surprise release and the credibility question (models ceded to `economics-of-debt`); Catholic social teaching's call for relief as one position (`catholic-social-teaching`) |

**Boss problem 5:** Two borrowers default on identical loans. One lost her job to an illness. The other lost the money gambling. A proposal would discharge both debts through bankruptcy, and a second proposal would have the state pay both creditors in full. (a) Close-read the Court's statement of the purpose of the discharge in *Local Loan Co. v. Hunt* (1934). Whose interest does it name, and does its reasoning distinguish the two borrowers? *(Exegetical.)* (b) Say what Butler's account of forgiveness implies about whether the creditor can forgive each borrower, and whether the state's payment under the second proposal is forgiveness at all. *(Exegetical.)* (c) Build the strongest moral-hazard objection and the strongest desert objection to discharging the gambler's debt, and show that they can come apart: describe a case where one applies and the other does not. *(Evaluative.)* (d) In 300-500 words: should a society adopt a Jubilee, meaning a scheduled release of all consumer debts every seven years, known to every lender in advance? Any verdict passes if it addresses how advance notice changes the fairness and moral-hazard objections, states one religious and one secular argument for the principle at full strength, and says what the borrowers who can no longer get credit would be owed. *(Evaluative.)*

### Module 6: Generations, nations and society

The hardest debts to understand are owed by or to people who never signed anything: the unborn, citizens of a state that borrowed under a tyrant, and anyone told they owe a debt to society.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 6.1 | The earth belongs to the living | Reconstruct Jefferson's argument that no generation can bind the next, and Madison's reply | Jefferson to Madison (6 September 1789): usufruct, the generational arithmetic behind a nineteen-year limit on debts and laws; Madison's reply (4 February 1790): debts incurred for the benefit of posterity, tacit consent; Burke's partnership and Paine's "governing beyond the grave" (cited to [`history-of-political-thought`](../history-of-political-thought/syllabus.md) 5.3), **close reading** |

| 6.3 | Whose debt is it? **Boss problem 6:** A junta borrowed heavily from foreign banks, spent most of the money on internal repression and a little on a port that still earns revenue, and fell. The elected successor government is deciding whether to repudiate. (a) Close-read Jefferson's letter to Madison of 6 September 1789. Reconstruct his argument in numbered premises, and state the principle Madison's reply uses to limit it. *(Exegetical.)* (b) Map Sack's three conditions onto the authorization, benefit and membership grounds for collective liability. Say which ground each condition defeats, and which condition the port complicates. *(Exegetical.)* (c) Apply Kant's fourth preliminary article and Madison's benefit principle to the case, and say where each stops determining a verdict. *(Exegetical, then evaluative.)* (d) In 300-500 words: may the successor repudiate all, part or none of the debt? Any verdict passes if it uses at least two grounds from 6.3, answers the creditors' strongest argument (state continuity, and the harm to future borrowing by other poor states), and treats the question of who bears the loss as contested. *(Evaluative.)*
