# Constitutional Law — Syllabus

> Politics & Society · Tier 1 · ~26 lessons · Prereqs: [`political-institutions`](../political-institutions/syllabus.md) · Roadmap id: `constitutional-law`

## Goal

This course teaches US constitutional law the way lawyers learn it: by reading the Supreme Court's opinions, majority and dissent, and working out what each one held, how it argued, and what it left open. It covers judicial review and its limits, federalism and the enumerated powers, the separation of powers and the administrative state, the incorporation of the Bill of Rights against the states, due process and equal protection, free speech, the religion clauses, and the theories of interpretation and precedent the justices argue with. You will finish able to state a case's holding precisely and say whether it is still good law. You will also be able to run a current doctrinal test on a fresh fact pattern and say where the test stops deciding it, reconstruct a majority and a dissent as rival arguments, name the crux between them, and say which interpretive method each opinion is using. The course is verdict-neutral: contested cases (*Roe* and *Dobbs*, *Citizens United*, *Smith*, *SFFA*, the removal and deference cases) are taught as the Court and the dissents argued them, and it never argues that a decision was right. It deliberately skips criminal procedure (the Fourth, Fifth and Sixth Amendments as applied to policing and trials), procedural due process, the Takings Clause, the dormant Commerce Clause, war powers and national-security cases, Second Amendment doctrine beyond its use as the showcase for interpretive method (8.1-8.2), state constitutional law, and the courts of other countries. The comparative, normative and jurisprudential questions each have an owner, listed below.

## How practice works in this course

Problems follow [HUMANITIES-BUILD-BRIEF.md](../../HUMANITIES-BUILD-BRIEF.md): every part declares its kind, and every solution is a rubric (must-hit moves, wrong turns) plus a model answer. Supreme Court opinions are works of the US government and in the public domain, so the course can quote them freely, and it does. Five archetypes carry the load:

- **Close reading (archetype 7), exegetical.** The workhorse. A passage of 150 words or fewer from a majority, concurrence or dissent: what does it hold or claim, which words carry the weight, and is a given sentence holding or dicta? Strict.
- **Apply to a hard case (archetype 3).** An invented statute or executive order and a set of facts. Run the current test (*Youngstown*'s three categories, the *Lopez* factors, strict scrutiny, *Brandenburg*, *Smith*) and say what it yields. That part is exegetical, and strict where the doctrine determines an answer. Then say where the test stops deciding, which is evaluative.
- **Find the crux (archetype 5), exegetical.** A majority and a dissent: the single premise, whether about text, history, precedent or consequences, that one side must deny.
- **Reconstruct (archetype 1).** An opinion's argument in numbered premises, with each premise tagged by its mode of argument (text, history, structure, precedent, consequences). The reconstruction is exegetical; the flag on the weakest premise is evaluative.
- **Diagnose (archetype 4), exegetical.** An invented op-ed or campaign speech about a decision: name the doctrine it misstates, or the interpretive method it is really objecting to.

**Exegetical parts are strict about the law.** What a case held, which test it applied, which opinion commanded a majority, and whether it has since been overruled or limited all have right answers. Mistaking a plurality for a holding, or treating a repudiated case as overruled when the Court never said so, is an exegetical error. **Evaluative parts are verdict-neutral.** "Was *Dobbs* rightly decided?" passes on either answer if the student states the other side's best argument in its own terms and meets it. There are no formal problems.

## Dangerous Checklist

When you finish, you can:

- [ ] Read a Supreme Court opinion and separate the holding from dicta, a majority from a plurality, and a concurrence in the judgment from a joining concurrence
- [ ] Reconstruct *Marbury v. Madison* in numbered premises and say which premises rest on text, which on structure, and which on the judicial oath
- [ ] Decide whether a plaintiff has standing, and whether a question is political, under *Lujan* and *Baker v. Carr*
- [ ] Say whether an invented federal statute is within the commerce, taxing or spending power, or commandeers the states, under *Lopez*, *Raich*, *NFIB v. Sebelius*, *Dole*, *New York* and *Printz*
- [ ] Place a presidential action in one of Justice Jackson's three *Youngstown* categories, and say what follows
- [ ] Say whether a removal restriction on an agency head survives under *Humphrey's Executor*, *Morrison*, *Seila Law*, and whatever the Court has decided since, and what *Loper Bright* and the major-questions doctrine change about agency interpretations
- [ ] Explain how the Bill of Rights came to bind the states, clause by clause, and why it happened through due process rather than the Privileges or Immunities Clause
- [ ] Choose the tier of scrutiny an equal-protection or due-process claim triggers, and run it on a fresh case
- [ ] Trace the unenumerated-rights line from *Lochner* through *Griswold*, *Roe*, *Casey*, *Glucksberg*, *Obergefell* and *Dobbs*, and state the method each side used
- [ ] Apply *Brandenburg* and the content-based/content-neutral distinction to an invented speech restriction
- [ ] Explain how Establishment Clause doctrine moved from *Everson* and *Lemon* to *Kennedy v. Bremerton*, and Free Exercise doctrine from *Sherbert* to *Smith* to *Fulton*, without overstating what any case overruled
- [ ] Identify whether an opinion argues from original public meaning, living constitutionalism or precedent, and run the stare decisis factors on a precedent the Court is asked to overrule

## Modules

### Module 1: Judicial review and national power

Chief Justice Marshall's two foundational opinions, then the limits on when and how far the Court's word is final.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1.1 | *Marbury v. Madison* (1803) and how to read an opinion | Reconstruct Marshall's argument for judicial review and learn the parts of an opinion on it | the undelivered commission, the three questions in Marshall's order, Section 13 of the Judiciary Act of 1789 and Article III original jurisdiction, written constitution and the judicial oath; holding vs dicta, U.S. Reports citation; the modes of constitutional argument (text, history, structure, precedent, consequences) |
| 1.2 | *McCulloch v. Maryland* (1819) | Explain implied powers and the Necessary and Proper Clause, and why a state cannot tax a federal bank | the Second Bank, "necessary" as convenient vs indispensable, "a constitution we are expounding", the power to tax as the power to destroy, the Hamilton-Jefferson bank debate as background (cited to [`history-of-political-thought`](../history-of-political-thought/syllabus.md) 5.2 for *The Federalist*) |
| 1.3 | The last word and its limits | Say when the Court's interpretation binds others, and when a federal court may decide at all | *Cooper v. Aaron* (1958) and judicial supremacy; departmentalism (Jackson's 1832 bank veto, Lincoln on *Dred Scott*); Congress and jurisdiction (*Ex parte McCardle*, 1869); standing (*Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife*, 1992); political questions (*Baker v. Carr*, 1962; *Nixon v. United States*, 1993; *Rucho v. Common Cause*, 2019); legitimacy debate cited to [`political-philosophy`](../political-philosophy/syllabus.md) 5.2 |

**Boss problem 1:** (a) Close-read the passage of *Marbury* in which Marshall argues that a legislative act repugnant to the Constitution is void. Reconstruct it in numbered premises and tag each premise by its mode of argument. *(Exegetical.)* (b) A critic replies that nothing in the text gives courts, rather than Congress or the President, the final say, and cites Jackson's bank veto. State the departmentalist position precisely, then say what *Cooper v. Aaron* claimed that *Marbury* did not. *(Exegetical.)* (c) An invented statute creates a federal bank-like entity and forbids states to tax it, and a taxpayer with no other injury sues to challenge it. Decide the standing question under *Lujan*. Then say how *McCulloch* would answer the merits if a proper plaintiff sued. *(Exegetical.)* (d) In 300-500 words: is judicial supremacy, as *Cooper* stated it, the best reading of *Marbury*? Any verdict passes if it engages the strongest departmentalist argument and uses at least two modes of argument. *(Evaluative.)*

It excludes schools that "require religious instruction," and it separately requires every participating school to follow the state's nondiscrimination rules on hiring all staff. A Catholic school applies. (a) Analyze the exclusion under *Locke v. Davey*, *Espinoza* and *Carson*, and say which case the facts are closest to. *(Exegetical.)* (b) Analyze the hiring rule as applied to the school's theology teachers and to its groundskeeper, under *Hosanna-Tabor*, *Our Lady of Guadalupe* and *Smith* as limited by *Fulton*. *(Exegetical.)* (c) Close-read a short passage of Justice Scalia's majority opinion in *Smith* and a short passage of Justice O'Connor's concurrence in the judgment (both public domain), and name the crux between them. *(Exegetical.)* (d) In 300-500 words: should *Smith* be overruled? Any verdict passes if it states what would replace *Smith* and meets the strongest objection to that replacement, including the objection Justice Barrett raised in *Fulton*. *(Evaluative.)*

The methods the justices have been using all course, named and tested on the cases where they collide.

**Boss problem 8:** Take one precedent from the course that the Court has been asked to overrule, or has overruled: *Chevron*, *Roe* and *Casey*, *Smith*, *Humphrey's Executor*, or *Austin*. (a) State the precedent's holding and the method its majority used (text, original meaning, precedent, consequences). *(Exegetical.)* (b) Run the stare decisis factors as the Court stated them in *Dobbs* or *Janus*, and say which factor the actual or likely overruling turned on. *(Exegetical.)* (c) Say how an original-public-meaning originalist and a common-law constitutionalist would each approach the question, and whether they must reach different results. *(Exegetical, then evaluative.)* (d) In 300-500 words: should the precedent stand? ## Notes

- **Lesson count.** The roadmap estimated ~22; the syllabus runs 26 (+18%, inside the band). Constitutional law is taught through a long run of cases, and each lesson is built around one line of cases read closely, so eight doctrinal areas need more than 22 lessons unless lines are cut in half. The Fourteenth Amendment takes seven lessons across two modules, and the religion clauses take four, which reflects both how much that doctrine has moved and the bridge to Catholic Theology. Recommend updating the roadmap estimate to 26.
- **Verdict-neutrality on hot-button cases.** Lessons on *Roe*, *Casey* and *Dobbs*, *Obergefell*, *SFFA*, *Skrmetti*, *Citizens United*, *Kennedy v. Bremerton*, *Smith*, *Trump v. United States* and the removal cases present the majority and the principal dissent at the strength their authors gave them, quoting both. Rubrics never require a view on whether a case was rightly decided.
- **Overruled vs repudiated vs limited: the recurring exegetical trap.** Lessons must use the Court's own words. *Brown* held separate-but-equal had no place in public education; it did not in terms overrule *Plessy* across the board. *Trump v. Hawaii* repudiated *Korematsu* in dicta. *Kennedy* said *Lemon* had long been abandoned. *SFFA*'s effect on *Grutter* is described differently by different opinions in the case. *NFIB*'s commerce reasoning has disputed status as a holding. Express overrulings the syllabus relies on: *Hammer* by *Darby*, *Adkins* by *West Coast Hotel*, *National League of Cities* by *Garcia*, *Palko* by *Benton*, *Bowers* by *Lawrence*, *Gobitis* by *Barnette*, *Whitney* by *Brandenburg*, *Austin* by *Citizens United*, *Abood* by *Janus*, *Apodaca* by *Ramos*, *Roe* and *Casey* by *Dobbs*, *Chevron* by *Loper Bright*.
