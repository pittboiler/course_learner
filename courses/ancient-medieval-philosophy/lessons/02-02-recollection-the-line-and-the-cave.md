# Ancient & Medieval Philosophy · Lesson 2.2: Knowledge: recollection, the Line and the Cave

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 2: Plato · Builds on: [2.1 The Forms, and what goes wrong with them](02-01-the-forms-and-what-goes-wrong-with-them.md) · Unlocks: [2.3 The soul: its parts and its immortality](02-03-the-soul-its-parts-and-immortality.md)

## Why this matters

[2.1](02-01-the-forms-and-what-goes-wrong-with-them.md) gave Plato's reasons for positing Forms: unchanging, never seen, never touched. That raises the obvious question, and Plato knew it: if nothing you perceive is a Form, how does anyone come to know one? His answers, a slave boy who "remembers" geometry, a line cut in four, a cave of shadows, also give philosophy its first theory of what knowledge adds to a lucky true opinion. Every later "true belief plus something" account of knowledge descends from it.

## The idea

Start with a puzzle Meno throws at Socrates when their hunt for a definition of virtue stalls. You can't search for what you already know: you have it. You can't search for what you don't know: you wouldn't know what to look for, or recognise it if you tripped over it. So inquiry is either pointless or impossible.

Plato's answer is that the dichotomy is false. There is a third state: knowledge you *have* but cannot currently *get at*, like a name on the tip of your tongue. You can search for it because it is in you; you need to search because it isn't available. You recognise the answer the way you recognise the forgotten name: it clicks. Learning, on this view, is **recollection** (*anamnesis*), and Socrates backs it with a demonstration. Meno's slave boy, untaught in geometry, is asked only questions and ends up finding the side of a square with double the area of a given one.

The *Republic* then says what the recovered knowledge is *of*, and why most of us never get there. Knowing comes in grades, and each grade has its own kind of object. The **Divided Line** diagrams the grades; the **Cave** dramatises the climb; the **Sun** names what sits at the top. The Form of the Good does for knowledge what the sun does for sight.

## Source

Plato, *Meno* 80d–e (Jowett, public domain):

> MENO: And how will you enquire, Socrates, into that which you do not know? What will you put forth as the subject of enquiry? And if you find what you want, how will you ever know that this is the thing which you did not know?
>
> SOCRATES: I know, Meno, what you mean; but just see what a tiresome dispute you are introducing. You argue that a man cannot enquire either about that which he knows, or about that which he does not know; for if he knows, he has no need to enquire; and if not, he cannot; for he does not know the very subject about which he is to enquire.

Meno asks three questions. Socrates hands back a clean two-horned dilemma, and it is not quite the same argument. That gap is P2.

## The argument

**The paradox of inquiry, in Socrates' form (80e).**

1. For any subject, a person either knows it or does not know it. *In words:* no third option.
2. If he knows it, he has no need to inquire. *In words:* you don't search for what you've got.
3. If he does not know it, he cannot inquire. *In words:* you can't aim a search at a blank.
4. **So inquiry is either needless or impossible.** *In words:* nobody ever genuinely learns by searching.

**Socrates' reply: recollection (81a–86c).**

1. The soul is immortal, has been born many times, and has seen all things "whether in this world or in the world below" (81c). Socrates credits this to priests and poets, not to argument. *In words:* your soul was learning before you were born.
2. "All nature is akin" (81d), so recovering one thing lets a persistent inquirer recover the rest. *In words:* truths are linked, so one thread pulls up others.
3. So a person can hold knowledge he is not now aware of. *In words:* premise 1 of the paradox is false, because "knows" hides a latent and an actual sense.
4. Inquiry recovers latent knowledge: "all enquiry and all learning is but recollection" (81d). *In words:* searching is remembering.
5. The latent knowledge also answers Meno's third question: the found answer is recognised as the one that was in you. *In words:* the click is the criterion.
6. The slave boy (82b–85b), asked only questions, produces true opinions he was never taught. So they were in him already (85c–d). *In words:* here is recollection happening in front of you.
7. **So inquiry is possible, and we should undertake it.** *In words:* the paradox is sophistry that "will make us idle" (81d).

Two cautions about the text. The *Meno* never mentions Forms; the *Phaedo* ties recollection to them ([2.3](02-03-the-soul-its-parts-and-immortality.md)). And at 86b–c Socrates says he is not confident of everything he has said. The only thing he will fight for, "in word and deed", is that we are better off inquiring.

**Knowledge vs true opinion (97a–98a).** Someone with a true opinion about the road to Larisa guides you as well as someone who knows it. Why value knowledge more? Because true opinions are like the statues of Daedalus: they "run away out of the human soul" unless "fastened by the tie of the cause" (Jowett for *aitias logismos*, "working out the reason"). That fastening, Socrates adds, "is recollection".

## The case

![The Divided Line drawn vertically in four segments of lengths one, two, two and four units from the bottom: imagining of shadows and reflections, belief about animals plants and artefacts, thought reasoning from hypotheses in mathematics, and understanding of Forms by dialectic](assets/02-02-fig1.svg)

**The Sun (507b–509c).** The sun makes things visible and the eye able to see. It also makes things grow, though it is not itself growth. Likewise the Good makes the Forms knowable and the mind able to know them, and gives them their being. Yet the Good "is not essence, but far exceeds essence in dignity and power" (509b; Greek *epekeina tes ousias*, "beyond being").

**The Line (509d–511e).** Cut a line into two unequal parts, visible and intelligible, then cut each part again "in the same proportion". Each segment pairs a state of mind with a kind of object, and the states have clarity "in the same degree that their objects have truth" (511e):

| Segment | State | Objects | How it works |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4 (top) | *noesis*, understanding | Forms | treats hypotheses as "steps and points of departure", climbs to the "first principle of the whole", then descends using Forms alone (511b) |
| 3 | *dianoia*, thought | what mathematicians reason about | assumes "the odd and the even and the figures and three kinds of angles" without accounting for them; uses drawn figures as images (510c–d) |
| 2 | *pistis*, belief or trust | animals, plants, artefacts | takes visible things at face value |
| 1 (bottom) | *eikasia*, imagining | shadows, reflections | takes images for what they image |

**The proportion, worked.** Let the lower part of every cut be $k$ times the upper, and call the intelligible part $I$, so the visible part is $V = kI$. The upper visible segment is $V/(1+k) = kI/(1+k)$. The lower intelligible segment is $kI/(1+k)$ too. Try $k = 1/2$ with $I = 6$: then $V = 3$, and the segments from the bottom are $1, 2, 2, 4$. **The two middle segments are always equal**, whatever $k$ is. Plato does not remark on it here. Readers disagree whether it is a deliberate hint (that *pistis* and *dianoia* are in some way on a par) or an unintended by-product. The text also never says which segment of each pair is longer.

**The Cave (514a–517c).** Prisoners chained from childhood see only shadows cast on a wall by objects carried before a fire. One is freed, turned round, dazzled by the fire, dragged up a steep ascent, and adjusts in stages: shadows, reflections, things, the night sky, at last the sun. Back underground he sees badly in the dark, and the others say the ascent ruined his eyes (517a). Education, Socrates concludes, is not putting sight into blind eyes. It is turning the whole soul round (518c–d), which is recollection's thesis restated as an image. How closely the Cave's stages track the Line's four segments is a question the text only partly answers; Module 2's boss problem sends you to check.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean: where does geometry sit?).** A geometer draws a square in the sand and proves that the square on its diagonal is double it. Run the Line. His *object* is not the drawing: he reasons about "the absolute square and the absolute diameter", and the drawn figure is an image he looks *through* (510d). So he is above *pistis*. But he takes "square", "right angle" and "equal" as hypotheses he never accounts for, and he needs the diagram. That is *dianoia*, segment 3. To reach *noesis* he would have to ask what squareness and equality *are*, and trace those answers to a first principle. That is dialectic's job, not geometry's.

Now place the slave boy at the end of his lesson. He has true opinions "stirred up in him, as in a dream" (85c). Asked "frequently… in different forms", he would come to know. The *Meno* calls that the difference between opinion and knowledge. The Line would call even a geometer's finished knowledge *dianoia*, still short of the top. The two dialogues grade on different scales; keep them apart.

**Example 2 (hard: is Plato's *episteme* justified true belief?).** The *Meno* formula looks like the ancestor: knowledge = true opinion + a tie. Modern epistemology's standard analysis, justified true belief ([`philosophical-method` 3.1](../../philosophical-method/lessons/03-01-conceptual-analysis.md)), has the same shape. Three strains show the resemblance is partial.

*The tie is explanatory, not evidential.* *Aitias logismos* is working out *why* the answer is so. The boy has it when he can derive the doubled square from the diagonal construction, not when he has good grounds to trust Socrates. In the *Theaetetus* (201a–c), jurors persuaded by an eloquent speaker to a true verdict have true judgment but lack knowledge of what only an eyewitness could know. So *episteme* is nearer to *understanding* than to justified belief.

*The objects may differ.* In *Republic* V (476d–480a) knowledge and opinion are distinct powers directed at different things: knowledge over what is, opinion over what is and is not. On a "two worlds" reading, no opinion about the visible could ever *become* knowledge, which clashes with the *Meno*'s tied-down opinions. Gail Fine ("Knowledge and Belief in *Republic* V", 1978) argued instead that the contrast concerns the kinds of claims each power can support, which softens the clash. The dispute is live among Plato scholars.

*The unit differs.* Justified true belief analyses knowing a proposition, *that p*. Plato's *episteme* is typically of a *thing*: of virtue, of the equal itself, of the Good.

Verdict for exegesis: the *Meno* supplies the "true opinion plus" template, but what fills the plus is grasp of the cause. Whether knowledge is really more *valuable* than true belief (the Larisa question) is the value problem, argued as a live question in [`epistemology`](../../epistemology/syllabus.md) 1.3.

## Watch out

- **You might think recollection is Descartes's innate ideas.** Plato's latent knowledge was *acquired*, by a soul before birth, and is recovered by questioning; it is not built into the mind's structure. The early-modern debate over innate ideas is [`modern-philosophy`](../../modern-philosophy/syllabus.md)'s. Reading it back into the *Meno* is the anachronism trap here.
- **You might think the slave boy was taught nothing.** Socrates' questions are leading; he draws the diagonal himself (84d–85a). What the episode can and cannot show is Module 2's boss problem, not this lesson's.
- **You might think the labels are fixed.** Jowett calls segment 4 "reason" and segment 3 "understanding"; this lesson follows the other common convention (understanding, thought). Go by the Greek word, not the English.
- **You might think the Good "beyond being" is the God of later theism.** In the *Republic* it is the principle of the Forms' knowability and being, with no will and no creation. Plotinus reads it as the One ([4.4](04-04-plotinus-the-one-intellect-and-soul.md)), and that reading is itself a development.

## One-liner

> You can seek what you don't know because you once knew it; knowing is climbing from shadows to hypotheses to the Good, and it differs from true opinion by being tied down with the reason why.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢) *(Formal.)*** (a) In the *Meno* the boy starts with a square of side 2 feet and is asked for the side of a square of double the area. For each of his two wrong answers, compute the resulting area and state in one sentence the assumption about side and area that the answer shows. (b) Explain why no whole-number side could be right, and give the exact length Socrates' construction yields. (c) Variant: you want a square of **half** the area of the original. Give the exact side, and describe a construction inside the original square, in the spirit of Socrates', that shows the area is right.

**P2 (🟡) *(Exegetical.)*** Close-read the *Source* passage (80d–e). (a) Meno asks three questions; Socrates' restatement answers to only two of them. Which question drops out, and what distinct worry did it raise? (b) Which words in Socrates' restatement make the argument an exhaustive dilemma, and on what assumption about "knows" does its first horn depend? (c) Which part of the recollection reply (see *The argument*) answers the question that dropped out? 150 words or fewer in total.

**P3 (🔴, optional) *(Exegetical.)*** Diagnose this invented op-ed paragraph. (a) Name the two positions from this lesson it borrows. (b) Name one thing it assumes that Plato did not hold, or one thing Plato held that it quietly drops. 150 words or fewer.

> "Stop pouring facts into children. A student who can repeat that the angles of a triangle sum to 180 degrees has learned nothing; tomorrow the fact will be gone. Understanding cannot be transmitted, only drawn out: a good teacher asks, and the pupil finds it in herself. And the finding is what lasts. Once a student can show *why* the angles sum as they do, no one will ever talk her out of it."

<details>
<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Formal.)*

**Must hit (a):** first answer, side 4: area $4 \times 4 = 16$, four times the original, not double. It shows the assumption that doubling the side doubles the area, i.e. that area scales linearly with side. Second answer, side 3 (chosen as between 2 and 4): area $9 \ne 8$. It shows he still expects the answer to be a whole number reached by adjusting the side, not yet grasping that area goes as the square of the side.

**Must hit (b):** the side $s$ must satisfy $s^2 = 8$. No whole number does ($2^2 = 4$, $3^2 = 9$). The exact side is $\sqrt{8} = 2\sqrt{2}$, the diagonal of the original square. Socrates' construction: four copies of the 4-foot square make a 16-foot square; the diagonals of the four inner squares enclose a square made of four half-squares, $4 \times 2 = 8$.

**Must hit (c):** side $\sqrt{2}$, since $(\sqrt 2)^2 = 2$. Construction: join the midpoints of the four sides of the 2-foot square. The four corner triangles each have legs 1 and 1, area $\tfrac12$, together 2, so the inner square has $4 - 2 = 2$. Its side is the diagonal of a 1-foot square, $\sqrt2$.

**Wrong turns:** answering (c) with side 1 (area 1, a quarter); saying $2\sqrt2$ "is about 2.8, so side 3 was nearly right" — the point of the episode is that no adjustment of whole numbers reaches it, and Socrates says "if you would rather not reckon, try and show me the line" (84a).

---

**P2** *(Exegetical — strict.)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** the third question, "if you find what you want, how will you ever know that this is the thing which you did not know?", drops out. It is a problem of *recognition*: even a lucky search couldn't certify its own success. Socrates' version is only about *starting* a search.

**Must hit, strict (b):** "either about that which he knows, or about that which he does not know" makes the dilemma exhaustive; "he does not know the very subject" makes the second horn total ignorance. Both horns treat knowing as all-or-nothing: you have it fully or have nothing at all.

**Must hit, strict (c):** premise 5, recognition by latent knowledge (with 3 as its basis): the found answer is recognised because it was in the soul already.

**Wrong turns:** saying the second question drops out (it is folded into horn 2); calling Socrates' phrase "tiresome dispute" the decisive words — it signals his attitude, not the logical structure.

**Model answer:** (a) The third: how you'd know a found answer is the one you sought — a recognition problem, not a starting problem. (b) "Either about that which he knows, or about that which he does not know" forces two exhaustive options, and "does not know the very subject" makes ignorance total. The dilemma assumes knowing is all-or-nothing. (c) Recollection's middle state: latent knowledge lets you recognise the answer when you meet it.

---

**P3** *(Exegetical — strict.)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** (i) recollection's thesis about teaching: learning is drawn out of the learner by questions, not put in (*Meno* 82b–85d; *Republic* 518c–d, not "sight into blind eyes"). (ii) *Meno* 97–98: true opinion "runs away" unless tied by working out the reason (*aitias logismos*); tied, it is knowledge and "abiding".

**Must hit, strict (b), any one:** it drops the pre-natal soul — Plato's explanation of *why* questioning can draw anything out — leaving a teaching method without the theory; or it treats a mathematical theorem as the pinnacle of understanding, whereas on the Line proof from unexamined hypotheses is *dianoia*, short of *noesis*; or it claims no one could talk her out of it, while for Plato stability comes from the tie to the cause, and knowledge in the full sense concerns Forms.

**Wrong turns:** attributing it to Socratic ignorance or the elenchus alone — the paragraph's claim is that the answer is *in* the pupil, which is recollection, not refutation; identifying the "why" condition as modern justification.

**Model answer:** It borrows recollection's picture of teaching — understanding is drawn out by questions, never poured in — and the *Meno*'s contrast between true opinions that "run away" and knowledge fastened by working out the reason. What it drops is Plato's explanation: the pupil can find the answer in herself because her soul learned it before birth. Without that, "drawn out" is a slogan about method, not a theory of knowledge.

## Flashback

**From Lesson 1.3 (The Sophists and Socrates):** *(Exegetical.)* Asked what piety is, Euthyphro has answered: doing what I am doing, prosecuting my father for murder. Socrates says he did not want examples but wants Euthyphro "to explain the general idea which makes all pious things to be pious", so that he will have "a standard to which I may look, and by which I may measure actions" (*Euthyphro* 6d–e, Jowett). (a) Which Socratic thesis from 1.3 is this demand an instance of, and what is wrong with Euthyphro's answer by its lights? (b) Aristotle reports that Socrates sought universal definitions but did not make them separate. Which words here might tempt a reader to find the Forms of [2.1](02-01-the-forms-and-what-goes-wrong-with-them.md) already in place, and does the passage itself commit Socrates to them? Three sentences or fewer per part.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):** the priority of definition: you cannot tell whether a given act is pious until you know what piety is. Euthyphro offers one case, which is exactly what Socrates excludes: an instance is not the single thing all pious acts share, the same fault as Meno's list of virtues. The decisive words are "general idea" and "makes *all* pious things to be pious".

**Must hit, strict (b):** the tempting words are "makes … to be pious", which gives the idea an explanatory role, and "a standard to which I may look", which treats it as a model to consult. (Jowett's "idea" and "standard" render *eidos*/*idea* and *paradeigma*, words Plato later uses for Forms. Credit, not required.) But the passage says nothing about the idea existing apart from pious acts, being unchanging, or being grasped without the senses. A definition true of every pious act and of nothing else does the job it describes, so the text is consistent with Aristotle's report and does not settle the question.

**Wrong turns:** calling the passage an elenchus. It is the demand that sets one up; nothing has been refuted yet. Reading "idea" in the modern sense of a thought in someone's head. Taking "measure" as Protagorean. It is the reverse: a standard every judge looks to, not each judge as his own measure.

**Model answer:** (a) It is the priority of definition. Euthyphro gives a single example, but Socrates wants "the general idea" that makes *all* pious things pious, so no list of cases can answer him. (b) "Makes … to be pious" and "a standard to which I may look" sound like a Form: a cause of piety and a model to consult. Neither phrase says the standard exists separately, is changeless or is known apart from the senses, and a correct universal definition would meet both descriptions. So the passage fits Aristotle's report and leaves separation to later dialogues.

</details>
