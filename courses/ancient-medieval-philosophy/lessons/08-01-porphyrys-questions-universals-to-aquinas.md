# Ancient & Medieval Philosophy · Lesson 8.1: Porphyry's questions: universals from Boethius to Aquinas

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 8: Universals, Scotus and Ockham · Builds on: [2.1 The Forms](02-01-the-forms-and-what-goes-wrong-with-them.md), [3.4 Substance and essence](03-04-substance-and-essence.md), [3.5 Soul and intellect](03-05-soul-and-intellect.md), [6.2 Avicenna](06-02-avicenna-essence-existence-and-the-necessary-existent.md), [7.2 Aquinas: essence and existence](07-02-aquinas-essence-and-existence.md) · Unlocks: [8.2 Scotus: the univocity of being](08-02-scotus-the-univocity-of-being.md)

## Why this matters

Around 270 AD a Neoplatonist wrote a beginner's guide to Aristotle's *Categories* and, in its first paragraph, listed three questions he was *not* going to answer. For a thousand years every Latin student of logic started with that paragraph. Plato and Aristotle had already disagreed about where form is ([2.1](02-01-the-forms-and-what-goes-wrong-with-them.md), [3.4](03-04-substance-and-essence.md)). Porphyry turned the disagreement into a set exam, and the medieval answers (Boethius's, Abelard's, Aquinas's, and in this module Scotus's and Ockham's) are the positions modern metaphysics still sorts itself by.

## The idea

Socrates is a man and Plato is a man. "Man" is one word and it is true of both. So what is the *one* thing, if there is one thing, that makes it true?

There are three natural places to look:

- **Outside both men**, as a separate pattern they copy. That is Plato.
- **Inside both men**, as something really common to them. The hard question is how one thing can be inside two men who are in two different cities.
- **Nowhere but in the mind or in language.** Out there are just Socrates and Plato; the commonness is a feature of how we think or talk about them.

Each place has a cost. Separate patterns multiply entities and raise the Third Man. A common thing inside both seems to make them one. Commonness only in thought seems to make the sentence "Socrates and Plato are both men" true by our say-so. Along the way the question splits in two: *what, outside the mind, do the two men have?* and *where does universality, being-predicable-of-many, live?* Keeping those apart is most of the skill.

## Source

Porphyry, *Isagoge* ch. 1 (O. F. Owen, 1853). Porphyry says he will leave aside, as too deep for an introduction, questions about genera and species:

> "as to whether they subsist (in the nature of things) or in mere conceptions only; whether also if subsistent, they are bodies or incorporeal, and whether they are separate from, or in, sensibles, and subsist about these"

Three questions, and they are nested: the second and third only arise on one answer to the first. Porphyry, a Platonist, declines to choose. Boethius translated this text into Latin and wrote two commentaries on it, and his translations of Porphyry and of Aristotle's *Categories* and *On Interpretation* became the core of the "old logic" (*logica vetus*) the early Middle Ages learned from.

## The argument

**A. Boethius (c. 480 to c. 525), second commentary on the *Isagoge*** (paraphrased).

1. **Nothing common to many at once is one thing.** A genus wholly present in many individuals at the same time cannot be numerically one. If it is many, a further genus is needed over those, and a regress starts. *In words:* a universal cannot just be one more object alongside Socrates.
2. **But a thought of a genus is not therefore empty.** A thought is false when it *combines* things otherwise than they are. It is not false when it *considers* one thing without another that is really joined to it. *In words:* thinking of "man" without thinking of Socrates' nose misrepresents nothing.
3. **The model is the line.** On the usual reading, his example is the mathematician who thinks of a line apart from any body, though no line exists apart from a body. *In words:* abstraction separates in thought what is not separate in being.
4. **So genera and species subsist in sensible things and are understood apart from them.** They are incorporeal, joined to bodies. *In words:* in things they are singular; in the understanding they are universal.

Boethius adds that he has followed Aristotle here because the book introduces Aristotle's *Categories*, not because he has decided against Plato.

**B. Abelard (1079-1142) against William of Champeaux** (paraphrased; William is known largely through Abelard's reports, so hedge the details).

5. **William's first view (material-essence realism).** One and the same essence, *man*, is wholly present in each man. Individuals differ only by accidents added to it. *In words:* humanity is one stuff, and Socrates is that stuff plus Socratic extras.
6. **Abelard's objections, as usually summarized.** If the same essence is in Socrates in Rome and Plato in Athens, one thing is wholly in two places at once. If one essence, *animal*, is both rational in Socrates and irrational in a donkey, one thing bears contraries at once. And whatever is true of the one essence in Socrates is true of it in Plato, so Socrates and Plato collapse into one. *In words:* a numerically single humanity makes all men the same man.
7. **William's revision.** Abelard's *Story of My Misfortunes* reports that William changed his wording: the essence is in individuals not "essentially" but "indifferently". Each man has his own humanity, and the humanities do not *differ*. *In words:* the men agree without any one thing being shared.
8. **Abelard's dilemma** (a reconstruction). Either "not different" means "the same", and step 6 returns; or it means only that they are alike, and then nothing is shared and the view has left realism. *In words:* non-difference is identity in disguise or likeness in disguise.
9. **Abelard's answer.** Universality belongs to words. "Man" is a word apt to be predicated of many. He first speaks of *voces* (spoken sounds) and later prefers *sermones* (words as meaningful). *In words:* what is universal is a word, never a thing.
10. **But the word is not arbitrary.** It was imposed on Socrates and Plato because of their *status*: *being a man*. The status is a real cause of imposition, and yet it is not a thing (*res*). His well-known comparison: a man may be flogged because he did not want to go to the forum, and "not wanting" is no thing. *In words:* the men really agree, and what they agree in is not an extra object.

**C. Aquinas (1225-1274): one nature, three conditions** (*On Being and Essence* ch. 3; ST I q.85 a.1-2; q.15).

11. **The nature considered absolutely.** Take humanity just as humanity. Neither unity nor plurality belongs to it as such: if plurality did, it could never be one in Socrates, and if unity did, it could never be many. This is Avicenna's "horseness is just horseness" ([6.2](06-02-avicenna-essence-existence-and-the-necessary-existent.md)). *In words:* what it is to be human says nothing about how many humans there are.
12. **The nature in singulars.** Outside the mind, humanity exists only as individuated in this man and that man, many times over. *In words:* in the world there are only particular humanities.
13. **The nature in the intellect.** The agent intellect ([3.5](03-05-soul-and-intellect.md)) abstracts the nature from the conditions of individuality, and only then is it universal. ST I q.85 a.2 ad 2 (English Dominican Province):

    > "humanity understood is only in this or that man; but that humanity be apprehended without conditions of individuality, that is, that it be abstracted and consequently considered as universal, occurs to humanity inasmuch as it is brought under the consideration of the intellect"

    *In words:* what is understood is in things; its being universal is the mind's contribution.
14. **The ideas in God.** Things are made according to exemplars in the divine mind (ST I q.15 a.1-3), so the nature has a third standing, as God's idea. It is not a separate Platonic Form: the ideas are God's own understanding of how his essence can be imitated. *In words:* Plato's patterns survive, relocated into God.
15. **The textbook shorthand.** Later manuals file 11-14 as universals *ante rem* (in God), *in re* (in things) and *post rem* (in our minds). The formula is a later schematization with older roots; Aquinas states the content, not the slogan. *In words:* use the labels, but cite the texts.

## Map of positions

Where each thinker places what is real and what is universal. Placements are rough: the spectrum is a teaching device, not a scale these authors used.

| From realism... | What exists outside any mind | Where universality lives | Lesson |
|---|---|---|---|
| Plato | Separate Forms, plus particulars that participate | In the Forms themselves | [2.1](02-01-the-forms-and-what-goes-wrong-with-them.md) |
| William (first view) | One essence wholly in each individual | In that shared essence | here |
| Scotus | A common nature with a unity less than numerical, individuated by "thisness" | Completed only in the intellect | [8.3](08-03-scotus-common-nature-and-haecceity.md) |
| Aquinas; Boethius nearby | Natures individuated in singulars (and exemplars in God) | Only in the intellect, by abstraction | here |
| Abelard | Individuals, and statuses that are not things | In words | here |
| Ockham | Only singular things | In concepts, as natural signs | [8.4](08-04-ockham-nominalism-and-the-razor.md) |
| **...to nominalism** | | | |

Read the two right-hand columns separately. Aquinas and Ockham agree that universality is a feature of the mind; they disagree about what the mind is tracking.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean): Boethius through Porphyry's questions.**

- *Do genera and species subsist, or are they only in thought?* Both, in different ways: they subsist in sensibles and are thought as universal. Step 2 is what stops "only in thought" from meaning "empty".
- *Bodies or incorporeal?* Incorporeal, but of the kind that cannot exist apart from bodies, like the line.
- *Separate or in sensibles?* In sensibles, as existing; separate, as understood.

Boethius answers the first and third questions by splitting each into *how it exists* and *how it is understood*. Aquinas's step 13 is the same move, sharpened with Aristotle's agent intellect. ST I q.85 a.1 ad 1 makes the old point explicitly: to consider colour without the apple is no falsehood.

**Example 2 (hard): is Aquinas a Platonist after all?**

Step 14 puts natures "before the thing" in God. A critic says that is Plato's first answer with God added: patterns outside the particulars.

Run the three questions for the divine idea of *human*. Does it subsist? Yes, but as God's knowledge, and God is simple, so it is not a *thing* beside God (q.15 a.2 argues that many ideas do not compromise his simplicity). Separate from sensibles? Yes. Does it make Socrates human? Here the parallel breaks. For Plato, Socrates is human by *participating* in the Form. For Aquinas, Socrates is human by his own substantial form, his soul; the idea is the exemplar by which he was made, not what he now is. Aquinas says as much in ST I q.84 a.5: Augustine put Plato's ideas into God's mind, and the intellect does not know things by gazing at them.

Where it strains: Aquinas does call God the first exemplar cause of all things (ST I q.44 a.3). A reader who stresses *participation*, a word Aquinas uses freely, will find more Platonism here than the textbook label "moderate realism" suggests. Neither reading is forced by the texts alone.

## Watch out

- **"Nominalist" is anachronistic for Abelard in its Ockhamist sense.** Twelfth-century schools were labelled by their view of words (the *nominales*), and Abelard's *status* gives predication a real ground in things that Ockham's theory does not need. Place him near nominalism, not on top of it.
- **"Moderate realism" is not Aquinas's term.** It is a modern label, and so is "conceptualism". The labels are useful for filing; they are not what the texts say.
- **You might think Aquinas's absolutely considered nature is a third *thing*, between the individual and the concept.** It exists only in singulars or in minds. The absolute consideration is a way of considering the nature, not a place where it lives. Scotus is the one who gives the common nature a unity of its own ([8.3](08-03-scotus-common-nature-and-haecceity.md)).
- **Porphyry's questions are about genera and species, not "properties".** Redness and charge are the modern test cases ([`metaphysics` 1.3](../../metaphysics/lessons/01-03-universals-the-realists-case.md)). The medievals mostly argued about *kinds*, where the realist stakes (what makes a thing what it is) are higher.

## One-liner

> Porphyry asked where genera and species are and would not answer; Boethius and Aquinas said "in things as singular, in the mind as universal", William put one essence in every man, and Abelard left universality to words grounded in a status that is not a thing.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢) *(Exegetical.)*** Use the Porphyry passage in **Source**. (a) Which words show that the second question depends on one answer to the first, and does the third question depend on it too? (b) Using steps 5 and 9-10, give the answers of William's first view and of Abelard to the first and third questions, one line each. (c) Whose answer to the first question fits neither of Porphyry's two options as worded, and which assumption built into the either/or does it reject? 120 words or fewer in all.

**P2 (🟡) *(Exegetical.)*** William's revised view (step 7) and Abelard's own view (steps 9-10) agree that no numerically single essence is shared by Socrates and Plato, and both say the two men really agree. Name the single premise about what can ground that agreement which one side affirms and the other denies. Then say what argument would move a defender of each side. 150 words or fewer.

**P3 (🔴, optional) *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)*** A student writes in an invented seminar paper:

> "When I call two tomatoes red, the word *red* does not name some redness sitting in both. There is no such object. Both tomatoes are simply red, and that is the whole story. What makes the word general is only our practice of using it, and a different community could have grouped tomatoes with fire hydrants and lemons instead."

(a) Which position on the map is the paper closest to, and which sentence goes beyond what that thinker held? (b) Is Abelard's *status*, a real cause of a word's imposition that is not a thing, a stable middle position, or does it slide toward realism or toward pure convention? Any verdict; 150 words or fewer for (b).

<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical — strict.)*

**Must hit, strict:**
- (a) "whether also if subsistent": the bodies-or-incorporeal question is conditional on answering *subsist* to the first. The third ("separate from, or in, sensibles, and subsist about these") also only arises for things that subsist, since only a subsisting item can be located apart from or in sensibles; the words "subsist about these" show it.
- (b) William's first view: Q1, they subsist, as one real essence; Q3, in sensibles, wholly in each individual. Abelard: Q1, what is universal is a word, not a subsisting thing, yet its imposition rests on a real status; Q3, the ground of the word (the status) is found only in the individuals, but it is not a thing located in them.
- (c) Abelard's. Porphyry's either/or assumes that genera and species are either things that subsist or conceptions in the mind. Abelard puts universality in *words* and the ground of their correct use in a *status* that is neither a thing nor a mere thought.

**Wrong turns:** giving Boethius's or Aquinas's answer for Abelard (universality in the intellect by abstraction), which is not what Abelard's word theory states; making William's essence "separate from sensibles", which is Plato's answer, not his.

**Model answer:** (a) "Whether also if subsistent" makes Q2 conditional; Q3 asks where subsistents are ("subsist about these"), so it depends on Q1 too. (b) William: Q1, subsist, as one essence; Q3, in sensibles, wholly in each. Abelard: Q1, the universal is a word, grounded in a real status; Q3, that status is had only by individuals, and is no thing in them. (c) Abelard's: Porphyry assumes a genus is either a subsisting thing or a conception, and Abelard makes it a word whose ground is a status that is neither.

---

**P2** *(Exegetical — strict on a single premise; any defensible formulation passes.)*

**Must hit, strict:**
- The crux: *whatever grounds a true common predication in the things must itself be some thing (res) in them.* William affirms it: the ground is each man's own humanity, a real item, non-different from the other's. Abelard denies it: the ground is a *status*, being-a-man, which is not a thing.
- What would move William: Abelard's dilemma (step 8). If the two humanities are distinct things, their "non-difference" is either identity or likeness, and likeness in *what* needs a further respect, which invites a regress unless that respect is not a thing.
- What would move Abelard: an argument that a cause which is "not a thing" is no cause at all, so a status either is a real item (William's view) or explains nothing.

**Wrong turns:** naming "whether universals are words" as the crux, which is a consequence, not the premise about grounding; naming "whether one essence is wholly in many", which both already reject.

**Model answer:** The premise is that anything that makes a common predication true of two individuals must be a thing in them. William's revised view keeps it: each man has his own humanity, a real item, and those items do not differ. Abelard rejects it: the men agree in *being a man*, a status that causes the word's imposition without being a thing. William's side would be moved by the dilemma that non-difference between two things is either sameness or bare likeness, and likeness needs a respect that cannot itself be another thing without a regress. Abelard's side would be moved by an argument that only things can be causes, so a status is either a hidden realist item or no explanation.

---

**P3** *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** closest to **Abelard**: no redness-object in both, universality belongs to a word, and "both tomatoes are simply red" echoes agreement *in being* red without a shared thing. The last sentence goes beyond him: for Abelard the word is imposed *because of* a real status, so nothing in his view makes the grouping a matter of community practice alone. (Ockham also accepts no shared object, but grounds generality in a natural concept, not convention, so the last sentence goes beyond him too. Credit that as an alternative placement if the conventionalist sentence is flagged.)

**Must hit, any verdict (b):**
- State what the status is meant to do: ground the correctness of the word without adding an entity.
- Test it from both sides: if the status really explains why "man" fits Socrates and not a stone, the realist asks why it is not a thing; if it is not a thing, the conventionalist asks what it explains beyond "the word fits".
- A verdict with the step that decides it; the flogging comparison (a cause that is no thing) may be used on either side.

**Wrong turns:** answering (b) by asking whether universals really exist, which is [`metaphysics` 1.3-1.4](../../metaphysics/lessons/01-04-nominalism-and-tropes.md)'s question, not Abelard's stability; placing the paper with Aquinas because "the mind contributes universality", which ignores that the paper puts generality in word use.

**Model answer (b), one of several:** The status is built to hold two things at once: that Socrates and Plato really agree, and that no extra entity exists. The realist presses the first: a status explains why "man" fits both and not a stone, and whatever explains that looks like a feature of the men, which is just a humanity by another name. The conventionalist presses the second: if a status is no thing, "they agree in being men" says only that "man" is true of both, which explains nothing. Abelard's flogging comparison is his reply: causes need not be things, since "not wanting to go" really explains the flogging. Verdict: the position is stable if that comparison holds, because it shows explanation without reification. If the comparison fails, it drifts toward William.

## Flashback

**From Lesson 7.2 (Essence and existence):** *(Exegetical, diagnose.)* A student's invented revision notes read:

> "Aquinas's real distinction means every creature is two things glued together: an essence, and an existence added on to it the way varnish is added to a finished table. The essence comes first and the existence arrives afterwards. That is what creation is: God takes essences that are waiting in line and hands each of them its existence."

Name three claims in the notes that Aquinas would reject, and for each give his reason from 7.2. Two sentences or fewer per claim.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict:** any three of these four, each with its reason.
- **"Two things glued together."** Essence and *esse* are two principles of one being, as matter and form are; neither exists on its own. The "real distinction" slogan is a later school label (Giles of Rome against Henry of Ghent), not Aquinas's wording.
- **"Added the way varnish is added to a table."** That is the accident model. Varnish comes to a table that already exists, but before *esse* there is no subject for existence to arrive at, so *esse* is the act by which the essence is actual at all, "the most perfect of all things" (ST I q.4 a.1 ad 3).
- **"The essence comes first."** The essence is prior only as potency to its act, not in time; an essence without *esse* is nothing in reality. The phoenix is understood, not waiting somewhere.
- **"God hands existence to waiting essences."** To create is to give being absolutely, from no presupposed subject (I q.45 a.1). If essences were already there to receive existence, creation would be making out of something.

**Wrong turns:** correcting "two things" by making the distinction merely conceptual. Both readings in 7.2 (Wippel's and Owens's) have Aquinas holding a composition in the creature itself; they disagree about when it is proved. Rescuing "waiting in line" by pointing to the divine ideas (step 14 of this lesson): the ideas are God's own understanding, not created subjects standing ready to be given existence.

**Model answer:** (1) "Two things": no. Essence and *esse* are two principles of one creature, neither of which exists by itself, and "real distinction" is a later label. (2) "Added like varnish": no. Varnish needs a table that already exists, but nothing exists before its *esse*, so existence is the act that makes the essence actual, not an accident laid on it. (3) "Waiting essences": no. Creation gives being from no presupposed subject, and an essence with no *esse* is not a thing in reality at all, only something that can be understood, like the phoenix.

</details>
