# Ancient & Medieval Philosophy · Lesson 6.3: Averroes and Maimonides: philosophy and revealed law

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 6: Anselm, and the Arabic and Jewish philosophers · Builds on: [6.2 Avicenna: essence, existence and the necessary existent](06-02-avicenna-essence-existence-and-the-necessary-existent.md), [3.6 The unmoved mover](03-06-the-unmoved-mover.md) · Unlocks: [7.1 Faith, reason and the Paris crisis](07-01-faith-reason-and-the-paris-crisis.md)

## Why this matters

Aristotle's first mover ([3.6](03-06-the-unmoved-mover.md)) moves a world that never began. Genesis and the Qur'an speak of a world that God made. By the twelfth century, anyone who took both Aristotle and a revealed Law seriously had to say what happens when a proof and a scripture seem to collide. Two men born in Córdoba a dozen years apart gave the answers the Latin universities inherited. Averroes, a Muslim judge, argued that the Law itself commands philosophy and that scripture yields to demonstration. Maimonides, a rabbi, argued that on the eternity of the world there was no demonstration to yield to. Aquinas ([7.1](07-01-faith-reason-and-the-paris-crisis.md)) read both. Whether the world in fact had a beginning, and whether any argument shows it, belongs to [`philosophy-of-religion`](../../philosophy-of-religion/syllabus.md) 2.5. This lesson asks what the two men held, and why.

## The idea

**Averroes: a judge rules on philosophy.** Ibn Rushd (1126–1198) came from a family of jurists and served as *qadi* (judge) in Seville and Córdoba and as a physician at the Almohad court. He wrote commentaries on nearly all of Aristotle, which is why the Latins called him simply "the Commentator". His *Decisive Treatise* (*Fasl al-Maqal*, usually dated c. 1179–80) is framed as a legal question. Is the study of philosophy and logic permitted by the religious Law (*shari'a*), forbidden, or commanded?

His ruling: commanded, for those able to do it. The Qur'an repeatedly tells people to consider the heavens and the earth. Averroes's favorite verse (59:2) can be rendered "reflect, you who have vision", and he reads "reflect" (*i'tibar*) as drawing out the unknown from the known, which is what a syllogism does. Philosophy is nothing more than studying beings insofar as they point to their Maker. So the Law commands philosophy, and the best form of it, demonstration. Jurists already use legal reasoning by analogy, which no one calls a forbidden innovation. The fact that the best logic was worked out by pagan Greeks is no objection: you judge a tool by whether it cuts, not by who made it.

Two further claims do the real work. First, people differ in how they come to assent. Some are moved by **demonstration**, from certain premises; some by **dialectic**, from generally accepted premises; most by **rhetoric**, from persuasion and images. Scripture speaks to all three at once, and Averroes finds the three methods named in Qur'an 16:125. Second, the Law is true and demonstration is true, and truth does not contradict truth; it agrees with it and bears witness to it (a paraphrase of the *Treatise*'s formula). So if a demonstration contradicts the apparent sense (*zahir*) of a verse, the verse must be interpreted (*ta'wil*), following the figurative ways Arabic speaks. Such interpretations belong to the demonstrative class. They are not to be divulged to people who cannot follow them, because a taken-away literal belief with no demonstration to replace it leaves such a person with nothing.

**The quarrel with al-Ghazali.** About eighty years earlier, al-Ghazali (d. 1111) had written *The Incoherence of the Philosophers*. He argued that the philosophers, meaning above all Avicenna ([6.2](06-02-avicenna-essence-existence-and-the-necessary-existent.md)), failed to demonstrate twenty of their theses. On three he went further and charged unbelief (*kufr*): that the world is eternal, that God does not know particulars, and that bodies are not resurrected. Averroes answered point by point in *The Incoherence of the Incoherence* (c. 1180), and in the *Decisive Treatise* he argued that the charge fails. He claimed the eternity dispute is close to verbal. Everyone agrees on things that come to be (made from something, in time) and on the one eternal being (made by nothing). The world as a whole is in between: made by an agent, yet not preceded by time. On the name for that middle case, the theologians and the ancient philosophers largely differ over a word.

**Maimonides: the perplexed student.** Moses Maimonides (1138–1204; some sources give 1135) left Almohad Spain as a young man and settled in Egypt, where he became a court physician and the leading legal authority of his community. The *Guide for the Perplexed* (written in Arabic, c. 1190) addresses a student who has learned philosophy and is troubled by scripture's literal sense.

Two doctrines matter here. The first is **negative theology** (*Guide* I.58–60). No positive attribute can be said of God, because each would add something to his essence and make him composite. The attributes we can properly use are attributes of action ("merciful" names what God's acts are like, not a quality in him) and negations. "God is powerful" says, properly, that God is *not* powerless. Each true negation brings you nearer to knowing God, by ruling out what he is not.

The second is **creation**. *Guide* II.13 lays out three opinions: the Law of Moses (the world made after absolute non-existence, time included), the view Maimonides credits to Plato (God formed an eternal matter), and Aristotle (the world eternal and necessary). Maimonides argues that Aristotle's arguments are not demonstrations and that Aristotle knew it (II.15). He argues creation is not demonstrable either (II.16), though it can be defended as possible and argued to be more probable.

## The argument

**Averroes on interpreting scripture (*Decisive Treatise*).**

1. **The Law commands the capable to study beings by demonstration.** *In words:* philosophy is a religious obligation for those who can do it.
2. **The Law is true, and it calls to a study that leads to truth.** *In words:* God would not command a method that leads away from what he revealed.
3. **Truth does not contradict truth.** *In words:* two true things cannot be in real conflict.
4. **So where demonstration reaches a conclusion that the apparent sense of scripture contradicts, the apparent sense is not what is meant.** *In words:* an apparent conflict is a sign the verse speaks figuratively.
5. **Such verses are to be interpreted according to the figurative ways of Arabic speech.** *In words:* interpretation has rules; it is not whatever you like.
6. **Interpretations belong to the demonstrative class and must not be declared to the dialectical and rhetorical classes.** *In words:* the literal sense stays true for those it was addressed to.

**Maimonides on creation (*Guide* II.13–25).**

1. **There are three opinions on the world's origin: Mosaic, Platonic, Aristotelian** (II.13). *In words:* creation from nothing, formation of eternal matter, or no beginning at all.
2. **Aristotle's arguments for eternity are not demonstrations, and he did not think they were** (II.15). *In words:* the best case for eternity is persuasive, not proven.
3. **You cannot infer how a thing came to be from how it behaves once complete** (II.17). *In words:* a child raised on an island with no women, told how babies grow inside the womb, would call it impossible; he would be arguing from the finished state.
4. **So neither eternity nor creation is demonstrated.** *In words:* the question is open to reason.
5. **Scripture's creation passages could be read figuratively, as the passages making God bodily were** (II.25). *In words:* scripture does not block eternity.
6. **But figurative reading is required only when demonstration forces it. God's incorporeality is demonstrated; eternity is not.** *In words:* no proof, no reason to depart from the text.
7. **Eternity by necessity, as Aristotle held it, would overturn the Law, since nothing could be otherwise and no miracle could happen.** *In words:* the Aristotelian view costs the foundations, not a verse.
8. **∴ Hold creation.** And Maimonides grants that the Platonic view, which leaves room for miracles, could be reconciled with scripture if it were demonstrated (II.25). *In words:* his refusal of eternity is argued, not merely scriptural.

## Map of positions

| | al-Ghazali (*Incoherence of the Philosophers*) | Averroes (*Decisive Treatise*, *Incoherence of the Incoherence*) | Maimonides (*Guide* II) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Are the philosophers' arguments for eternity demonstrative? | No | He answers al-Ghazali's refutations point by point, without conceding that eternity is undemonstrated | No, and Aristotle knew it |
| Does a demonstration license reading scripture figuratively? | Yes, where one exists | Yes: truth does not contradict truth | Yes: incorporeality is the example |
| Is holding eternity unbelief? | Yes | No; the dispute is nearly verbal, and learned error is excused | Aristotelian eternity would overturn the Law; the Platonic view need not |
| Who hears the interpretation? | (not at issue in the *Incoherence*) | The demonstrative class only | The *Guide* is written for the few, with deliberate concealment (Introduction) |

Read across the second row. All three accept that demonstration can govern interpretation. They split on the rows above and below it: whether a demonstration exists, and what denying the apparent sense amounts to.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean: Maimonides's negations).** Take "God exists, is living, is powerful, is wise". Run each through *Guide* I.58. "Exists" means his non-existence is impossible. "Living" means he is not like the lifeless. "Powerful" means he is not powerless, since he brings things about. "Wise" means he is not ignorant. None puts a quality in God, and each excludes something. Maimonides's image (I.60): someone told only that a ship exists learns, negation by negation, that it is not an accident, not a mineral, not a plant, not a solid lump, and so arrives near the idea of a ship with no positive description added. The positive claims have become exclusions, and knowledge grows as the list grows. Where it strains is that the negations are chosen because we already believe God acts. Maimonides accepts that: attributes of action carry that load.

**Example 2 (hard: running Averroes's rule on al-Ghazali's other two charges).** *God's knowledge of particulars.* Al-Ghazali read Avicenna as saying God knows particulars only "in a universal way", and so misses, for instance, that a given man is now sinning. Averroes replies that the philosophers never said God is ignorant of particulars. They said his knowledge is not like ours: ours is caused by the things, and his causes them. So "knows" is said of God and of us in different senses, and the charge attacks a view no philosopher held. The rule works cleanly here, since it redescribes rather than overrides.

*Resurrection.* Here the rule strains. Averroes holds that denying the afterlife altogether is unbelief, while learned disagreement over its *mode*, bodily or spiritual, is a permissible interpretation. The line depends on a judgment of which matters are principles of the Law and which admit interpretation, and the three-classes scheme does not tell you where that line falls. That is exactly where al-Ghazali would press.

## Watch out

- **You might think Averroes taught "double truth", one thing true in philosophy and its opposite true in religion.** His formula says the opposite: truth does not contradict truth. The label came from Latin polemic against thirteenth-century Parisian masters, and whether even they held it is disputed ([7.1](07-01-faith-reason-and-the-paris-crisis.md)).
- **Anachronism trap: "science versus religion".** Averroes's "philosophy" is Aristotelian demonstrative science, and his "Law" is *shari'a*, the whole of revealed guidance, not a code of rules. Casting him as an Enlightenment secularist in a turban, or Maimonides as a fundamentalist, misreads both. Each argues *from inside* a revealed tradition.
- **You might think Maimonides rejects eternity because Genesis says otherwise.** II.25 denies that: the verses could be interpreted. His reason is that eternity is undemonstrated and, in Aristotle's form, would overturn the Law.
- **Don't confuse Averroes with Latin "Averroism".** The unity of the intellect and the Paris condemnations are [7.1](07-01-faith-reason-and-the-paris-crisis.md) and [`church-history`](../../church-history/syllabus.md) 5.2.

## One-liner

> Averroes says the Law commands philosophy and scripture yields to demonstration; Maimonides agrees in principle, then shows that on the eternity of the world there is no demonstration to yield to.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢) *(Exegetical.)*** *Guide for the Perplexed* I.58, opening (Friedländer):

> Know that the negative attributes of God are the true attributes: they do not include any incorrect notions or any deficiency in relation to God, while positive attributes imply polytheism, and are inadequate, as we have already shown.

Two readings. **(R1)** Negations are a second-best: positive attributes are true of God, but too high for us, so we settle for saying what he is not. **(R2)** Negations are the correct way to speak of God, and positive attributes are faulty in themselves, not just beyond our reach. Which does the passage support? Quote the deciding words, and say what "imply polytheism" means given the lesson's account of why positive attributes fail. 100 words or fewer.

**P2 (🟡) *(Exegetical (a) · Exegetical (b))*** (a) Reconstruct, in 4–6 numbered premises and in Averroes's own terms, the *Decisive Treatise*'s argument that the Law makes the study of philosophy obligatory for those capable of it. Flag the premise a jurist hostile to philosophy is most likely to attack.
(b) Diagnose this invented op-ed paragraph:

> Scripture and science must collide. A believer who accepts a scientific result against the plain words of his holy book has already stopped believing, because faith means taking the text at its word. The rest is evasion.

Name the premise of Averroes's position (from The argument) that the op-ed denies, and the assumption about scripture's meaning that it takes for granted. 150 words or fewer for (b).

**P3 (🔴, optional) *(Exegetical.)*** As history: al-Ghazali says philosophers who hold the world eternal are unbelievers; Averroes says they are not. Both allow figurative reading where demonstration requires it. Name the single premise on which they part, and say what kind of evidence or argument, by the standards each accepted, would move the other. 150 words or fewer. (Do not assess whether the world is eternal.)

<details>
<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical — strict.)*

**Must hit, strict:**
- **R2.** The negations are "the true attributes", not the best available approximation. Positive attributes "imply polytheism, and are inadequate": the fault is in what they imply, not in our limits.
- "Imply polytheism": a positive attribute adds something to God's essence, so God plus his attributes would be several things. That is multiplicity where there must be absolute unity.
- "Do not include any... deficiency": a negation adds nothing to the essence, so it cannot introduce composition.

**Wrong turns:** taking "inadequate" to mean "too weak for our minds" (R1). The sentence sets it beside polytheism, a defect in the claim itself. Reading "polytheism" as literal worship of many gods rather than plurality in the divine essence.

**Model answer:** R2. The passage calls negations "the true attributes" and says positive ones "imply polytheism, and are inadequate". Positive attributes are not true-but-too-high. They are wrong in form, since each posits something added to God's essence, making him many. Negations add nothing, so they carry "no deficiency".

---

**P2** *(Exegetical — strict.)*

**Must hit, strict (a):**
- P1: The Law commands reflection on beings (59:2, with other verses on considering the heavens and earth).
- P2: Reflection (*i'tibar*) means inferring the unknown from the known, that is, syllogistic reasoning.
- P3: Philosophy is the study of beings insofar as they point to the Maker.
- P4: The best kind of such reasoning is demonstration, which requires learning logic, as law requires legal reasoning.
- ∴ C: The Law makes philosophical study, including logic, obligatory (or recommended) for those capable of demonstration.
- Flag P2 (reading *i'tibar* as syllogism) or P4 (logic as a forbidden innovation). Either is accepted with a reason; Averroes's own reply is the parallel with legal reasoning.

**Must hit, strict (b):** the op-ed denies premise 3/4 (truth does not contradict truth, so an apparent conflict signals a figurative sense). It assumes scripture's plain sense is its only sense, and it collapses Averroes's classes: for him the literal sense stays binding for the rhetorical class while the demonstrative class interprets.

**Wrong turns:** reconstructing the interpretation argument instead of the obligation argument in (a); in (b), saying the op-ed denies that the Law commands philosophy. It does not mention that.

**Model answer (b):** The op-ed denies that truth cannot contradict truth. It treats a conflict between a proven result and a verse as real, not as a signal that the verse speaks figuratively. It takes for granted that the plain sense is the only sense, so any interpretation counts as "evasion". Averroes denies that. Scripture addresses demonstrative, dialectical and rhetorical audiences at once, and interpretation according to Arabic figurative usage is an obligation for the demonstrative class, not a loss of faith.

---

**P3** *(Exegetical — strict on naming a premise both sides' positions actually turn on.)*

**Must hit, strict:**
- The crux: **whether the world's origin is a matter the Law fixes beyond interpretation**, whether by an apparent sense and a certain consensus, so that denial is unbelief. The alternative is that it is a difficult matter open to learned interpretation, where error is excused. Al-Ghazali affirms; Averroes denies.
- Averroes's supports: the apparent sense of the verses does not itself say creation from nothing in time (he cites verses such as 11:7 on the throne upon the water), and no certain consensus can be established on theoretical questions.
- What would move each: for Averroes, showing a clear apparent sense plus certain consensus. For al-Ghazali, showing the verses are genuinely ambiguous, or that the philosophers' arguments are demonstrations, which his *Incoherence* denies.
- Accept as a secondary crux: whether the philosophers' arguments are demonstrative, if tied to the licence for interpretation.

**Wrong turns:** "whether the world is eternal" (the question is whether holding it is unbelief); "whether reason or faith is supreme" (both accept demonstration can govern interpretation); taking a side.

**Model answer:** They part on whether the world's temporal origin is a point the Law fixes beyond interpretation. Al-Ghazali treats it as a principle, so denying it is *kufr*. Averroes treats it as a difficult question where the learned may interpret and are excused if wrong. He argues that the apparent sense of the verses does not state creation from nothing in time, and that certain consensus cannot exist on such theoretical matters. Al-Ghazali would be moved by showing the verses really are open. Averroes would be moved by a clear apparent sense backed by certain consensus.

## Flashback

**From Lesson 6.1 (Anselm and the Proslogion argument as history):** *(Exegetical.)* Three invented arguments from a philosophy club:

> (i) "We rank paintings, meals and friendships as better and worse. Whatever is ranked is ranked by one and the same goodness, and that goodness must be good through itself."
> (ii) "Just grasp what you mean by 'the greatest thing that can be thought'. Once you see that it can't be a mere idea, you don't need to look at the world at all."
> (iii) "The kettle heats because of the flame, and the flame burns because of the gas. We know God only from effects like these, working back to their cause."

(a) Match each to its closest medieval model: the *Monologion* chain, the *Proslogion* argument, or Aquinas's route in ST I q.2. Name the feature that decides each. One line each. (b) Gaunilo's first objection says the fool has "only the words" and not the thing. Which of the three can it reach, and why can't it reach the others? Three sentences or fewer.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):**
- (i) ***Monologion*:** a one-over-many premise run on goods we experience and compare as more or less good, rising to one thing that is good through itself.
- (ii) ***Proslogion*:** a single argument from a formula grasped in the understanding, needing no premise about the world.
- (iii) **Aquinas:** God's existence is not self-evident to us, so it is demonstrated from known effects back to their cause (a.2, the route of the Five Ways).

**Must hit, strict (b):** only (ii). Gaunilo's objection attacks the step from the fool's *understanding* of the formula to its being "in the understanding" as a thing (premise P2), and only (ii) depends on that step. (i) and (iii) start from things experienced, goods compared and effects observed, so whether the fool has the thing or only the words is not a premise they use.

**Wrong turns:** matching (i) to the *Proslogion* because it ends at something "good through itself". The deciding feature is the start from compared, experienced goods. Matching (iii) to the *Monologion* because both work upward from the world. The *Monologion* rises by degrees of a quality, while (iii) runs back along a chain of causes. In (b), saying the objection reaches everything because every argument uses words. It targets what the understanding holds, not the use of language.

**Model answer:** (a) (i) *Monologion*: it climbs from goods we experience in degrees to one good through itself. (ii) *Proslogion*: one argument from a formula understood, with no look at the world. (iii) Aquinas: not self-evident to us, so argued from effects to their cause. (b) Only (ii), because Gaunilo's objection denies that hearing a formula puts its object in the understanding, and only (ii) moves from understanding to reality. The other two start from things experienced, so what the fool holds when he hears a word is not among their premises.

</details>
