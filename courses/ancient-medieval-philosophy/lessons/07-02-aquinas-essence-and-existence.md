# Ancient & Medieval Philosophy · Lesson 7.2: Essence and existence

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 7: Aquinas: a survey · Builds on: [6.2 Avicenna: essence, existence and the necessary existent](06-02-avicenna-essence-existence-and-the-necessary-existent.md), [3.3 Change: privation, potency and act](03-03-change-privation-potency-and-act.md), [3.6 The unmoved mover](03-06-the-unmoved-mover.md) · Unlocks: [7.3 The Five Ways as text](07-03-the-five-ways-as-text.md)

## Why this matters

Aristotle's act and potency ([3.3](03-03-change-privation-potency-and-act.md)) explained change: bronze is potentially a statue, the sculptor makes it actually one. Avicenna ([6.2](06-02-avicenna-essence-existence-and-the-necessary-existent.md)) noticed that a nature, taken in itself, is indifferent to whether it exists at all. Aquinas puts the two together and gets something neither had: existing is itself an *act*, the deepest one, and every nature except one is a potency to it. That single move is how he tells creatures from God without matter doing the work, what he means by creation, and why the Five Ways ([7.3](07-03-the-five-ways-as-text.md)) end where they do.

## The idea

Start with Avicenna's observation. You can know exactly what a phoenix is (a bird that burns and rises from its ash) and have no idea whether one exists. What a thing is does not tell you that it is.

Avicenna's way of putting the result was that existence *comes to* an essence from outside it, in his vocabulary as something like an accident. Aquinas keeps the observation and changes the picture. An accident, such as a colour, is something a subject that already exists takes on. But existence cannot arrive at a subject that already exists; before existing there is no subject there to arrive at. So existence is not one more feature on the list of what a thing is. It is what makes the whole list *actual*.

A recurring image of his is fire (ST I q.3 a.4, q.44 a.1). Iron in a forge glows, but glowing is not what iron is. It has its heat from something whose nature is to be hot. Likewise, a horse or a man *has* existence without *being* existence, and so has it from something else. Follow that back and you reach one thing that does not have existence but is existence, with nothing received and nothing limiting it. Aquinas calls it *ipsum esse subsistens*, "subsisting being itself" (ST I q.4 a.2), and identifies it with God.

Two terms before we go on. *Esse* is Aquinas's Latin infinitive "to be", used as a noun: the act of existing. *Essence* (or *quiddity*, "whatness") is what a thing is, what its definition states.

## Source

Aquinas, *Summa Theologiae* I q.4 a.1 ad 3 (English Dominican Province translation, public domain):

> Existence is the most perfect of all things, for it is compared to all things as that by which they are made actual; for nothing has actuality except so far as it exists.

"Existence" here translates *esse*. Notice the claim: not that existing things are better than non-existing ones, but that *esse* stands to every form or nature as act to potency. The same reply goes on to say that existence actuates all things, "even their forms". For Aristotle, form was the actuality of matter. Aquinas adds a further actuality above form itself.

## The argument

***De Ente et Essentia* ch. 4 (an early work, from Paris in the mid-1250s), paraphrased.** No public-domain English translation is relied on here; the Latin key phrases are given.

1. **Every essence can be understood without anything being understood about its existence.** Aquinas's examples: one can grasp *quid est homo vel phoenix*, what a man or a phoenix is, and still not know *an esse habeat in rerum natura*, whether it has being in reality. *In words:* knowing the definition leaves the existence question open.
2. **So existence is other than essence, unless there is some thing whose essence is its very existence.** *In words:* for ordinary natures, "what" and "that" come apart; the one possible exception is flagged, not assumed.
3. **There can be at most one such thing.** A nature is multiplied in only three ways: by an added difference (as a genus is divided into species), by being received in different matters (as one species in many individuals), or by being received in something rather than standing alone. Existence *pure and simple* admits none of these without ceasing to be existence alone. *In words:* a being that is nothing but existing has nothing to tell it apart from a second one.
4. **Therefore in everything else, existence is other than essence.** *In words:* every being but (at most) one is composed of what it is and its act of existing.
5. **Whatever a thing has beyond its essence is caused either by its essential principles or by something outside it; nothing is the cause of its own existence.** So whatever has existence other than its essence has existence from another, and the chain ends in a first cause that is existence alone. *In words:* received existence points to a giver whose existence is not received.
6. **What receives something from another is in potency to it, and what it receives is its act.** So in every creature, essence stands to *esse* as potency to act. *In words:* Aristotle's act/potency pair now applies to existing itself, not only to changing.

**The disputed step.** Premise 1 is about *understanding*; premise 4 is about *things*. Does step 1 alone prove a distinction in reality, or only in thought? Many readers say step 1 alone cannot. John Wippel (*The Metaphysical Thought of Thomas Aquinas*, 2000) reads the uniqueness argument of step 3 as completing a proof of distinction in reality *before* God's existence is reached; Joseph Owens, on the usual summary of his view, held that it is secured only once God is reached at step 5. Which reading is right is a question [`thomistic-synthesis`](../../thomistic-synthesis/syllabus.md) takes up.

**In God (ST I q.3 a.4), briefly.** The *Summa* runs the argument from the other end, giving three reasons that God's essence is his existence: existence other than essence must be caused, but God is the first efficient cause; existence is to essence as act to potency, but God has no potency ([3.6](03-06-the-unmoved-mover.md)); what has existence without being it is a being by participation, but God is the first being. From there: all other things are beings by *participation* in being (I q.44 a.1), and to create is to give being absolutely, from no presupposed subject (I q.45 a.1). Being itself is the most universal effect, so it is God's proper effect (I q.45 a.5). Reconstructing q.3 a.4 is Module 7's boss problem.

**Where the "real distinction" label comes from.** Aquinas says essence and *esse* differ, and that *esse* is other than essence. The school slogan "real distinction" hardened later, in the 1270s-80s quarrel between Giles of Rome, who could speak of essence and existence almost as two things, and Henry of Ghent, who denied that. Read Aquinas's texts before the label.

**What changed from Avicenna.** Both hold that a created essence does not include existence. Averroes read Avicenna as making existence an accident and attacked him for it; whether Avicenna meant a categorial accident is disputed by modern readers. Aquinas, in his commentary on *Metaphysics* IV (lect. 2, on the usual reading), says existence is other than essence but not something added the way an accident is. For him *esse* is not a feature received by an already constituted thing but the act by which the thing is at all.

**Analogy, in one paragraph.** If God is existence itself and creatures merely have it, can "being" mean the same in both? Aquinas says names like "being", "good" and "wise" are said of God and creatures neither *univocally* (in exactly the same sense) nor *equivocally* (in unrelated senses) but *analogically*, by a real relation of creature to cause (ST I q.13 a.5). How to read that relation (Cajetan's proportionality or an attribution reading) is [`thomistic-synthesis`](../../thomistic-synthesis/syllabus.md) 4.4, and Scotus's rival univocity is [8.2](08-02-scotus-the-univocity-of-being.md).

## Map of positions

Two compositions, on Aquinas's view. Matter/form is the Aristotelian one from [3.3](03-03-change-privation-potency-and-act.md); essence/*esse* is the new one.

| Being | Matter and form? | Essence and *esse*? | Text |
|---|---|---|---|
| **God** | No | No: essence *is* existence | ST I q.3 a.4 |
| **Angels** (separate intelligences) | No: subsisting forms | **Yes**: the form is potency to its received existence | I q.50 a.2 ad 3 |
| **Humans** | Yes: the soul is the form of the body | Yes: one *esse* of the whole, which the soul communicates to the body and keeps after death | I q.76 a.1 ad 5 |
| **Other bodies** (stones, horses) | Yes | Yes | *De Ente* ch. 4 |

Read down the last column: every row but the first is composite. Matter is no longer what separates creature from Creator; received existence is. That is why angels, with no matter, are still not God, and why (with nothing to multiply them in one species) each angel is its own species (I q.50 a.4).

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean: a horse).** Matter/form: this flesh and bone, informed by the horse's substantial form. Essence/*esse*: the definition of horse says nothing about whether this horse exists (premise 1), so it has its existence from outside its nature (premise 5), and its nature is in potency to that existence (premise 6). Two compositions, nested: matter is potency to form, and the whole matter-form nature is potency to *esse*. When the horse dies, both are undone: the form no longer actualizes this matter, and the horse no longer exists. The matter goes on under other forms.

**Example 2 (hard: doesn't premise 1 apply to God too?).** If *every* essence can be understood without its existence, then God's can, and step 2's exception is empty. Two replies are in the texts.

First, the argument is built to leave room: step 2 says existence differs from essence *unless* something's essence is its existence. Premise 1 is plausible for the essences we actually grasp, horses and phoenixes. Second, Aquinas holds that we do not grasp God's essence in this life: we know that the proposition "God is" is true, from his effects, but understand neither his essence nor his act of existing (q.3 a.4 ad 2). So there is no case in which someone understands the divine essence and still wonders whether it exists. The phoenix test simply cannot be run on God.

This is also why Aquinas rejects Anselm's argument ([6.1](06-01-anselm-and-the-proslogion-as-history.md); ST I q.2 a.1 ad 2). "God's essence is his existence" is, for Aquinas, true in itself but not self-evident to us, because we lack the concept of the essence that would make it so. The identity is reached *from* effects, never read off a definition. Whether any such route succeeds is [`philosophy-of-religion`](../../philosophy-of-religion/syllabus.md)'s question.

## Watch out

- **Anachronism: *esse* is not the existential quantifier.** Since Frege and Russell, "horses exist" is often analysed as "the concept *horse* has instances", a claim about a concept. Aquinas's *esse* is the act of an individual. Reading "existence is not part of essence" as Kant's "existence is not a real predicate" gets both wrong: Kant's target is the inference from concept to existence, which Aquinas also rejects. The live debate is [`metaphysics` 2.5](../../metaphysics/lessons/02-05-essence-and-existence.md).
- **"Act" is not "property".** You might think *esse* is a very general property every existing thing shares. For Aquinas a property belongs to a thing already constituted; *esse* is what constitutes it as actual at all.
- **"Real distinction" does not mean two things.** Essence and *esse* are two principles of one being, as matter and form are. Neither exists by itself, and the label is later than Aquinas.
- **Two compositions, not one.** Essence/*esse* is not a restatement of matter/form. Angels have the first without the second, and that gap is exactly what the new composition was for.

## One-liner

> A creature *has* existence the way hot iron has heat, as an act received into a nature that is potency to it; only one thing *is* existence, and everything else is from it.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢) *(Exegetical.)*** Apply the two compositions. For each, say whether Aquinas's framework finds (a) matter/form composition and (b) essence/*esse* composition, with a reason. One or two lines each.

> (i) A phoenix. (ii) A human soul after death, separated from the body. (iii) One of the several celestial movers Aristotle posits in *Metaphysics* XII.8, placed in Aquinas's framework as a created separate substance.

**P2 (🟡) *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)*** In ST I q.50 a.2 ad 3 (English Dominican Province), Aquinas first says that in material things the matter-form nature is not its own existence, which is its act, so the nature is to existence as potency to act. He then goes on:

> if there be no matter, and supposing that the form itself subsists without matter, there nevertheless still remains the relation of the form to its very existence, as of potentiality to act.

(a) Reconstruct the argument that an angel is composed of potency and act in numbered premises (P1, P2, … ∴ C), and mark the premise the reply does not argue for here. (b) An objector: "In material things, what makes the nature not its own existence might be *matter*, the nature's being received in this matter. Take matter away and the reason is gone." Does the reply have the resources to answer, once the *De Ente* argument is supplied? Any verdict; 150 words or fewer for (b).

**P3 (🔴, optional) *(Exegetical.)*** Avicenna, as Averroes read him, says existence is an accident that comes to an essence; Aquinas says *esse* is other than essence but is its act, not an accident. Both agree that a created essence does not include existence. Name the single premise one side affirms and the other denies, and say what evidence or argument would move a defender of the Avicennian formula. 150 words or fewer.

<details>
<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical, strict.)*

**Must hit, strict:**
- (i) **Phoenix:** neither composition *in reality*, because there is no phoenix. It has an essence we can understand but no *esse* in *rerum natura*. As *thought of*, its nature would be a matter-form one (a bird). This is premise 1's test case, not a being.
- (ii) **Separated soul:** (a) **no** matter/form: the soul *is* a form, not composed of one (q.75 a.5); (b) **yes** essence/*esse*: intellectual substances are composed of form and participated existence (q.75 a.5 ad 4), and the soul keeps the existence it gave the whole human (q.76 a.1 ad 5).
- (iii) **Celestial mover:** (a) **no** matter/form, since it is a separate substance; (b) **yes**, because there are several, and by the uniqueness step at most one being is its own existence. Only God is simple.

**Wrong turns:** calling the phoenix "essence without *esse*" *composition*, since composition belongs to existing things; making the separated soul simple because it is immaterial, which merges the two compositions; treating (iii) as simple because Aristotle calls his movers actualities, which is Aristotle's vocabulary, not Aquinas's second composition.

**Model answer:** (i) Neither in reality: a phoenix is an understood essence with no *esse*. (ii) No matter/form, since it is itself a form; yes essence/*esse*, since it has participated existence it retains. (iii) No matter/form; yes essence/*esse*, since there are many and only one being can be its own existence.

---

**P2** *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)*

**Must hit, strict (a):**
- P1. In a material thing the nature (matter and form) is not its own existence. P2. Existence is the act of that nature, so the nature is to its existence as potency to act. P3. Removing matter does not make a subsisting form its own existence. ∴ C. A subsisting form (an angel) is still to its existence as potency to act, so it is composed of potency and act, though not of matter and form.
- The unargued premise is **P3** (or P1, if the student grounds P3 in it): the reply asserts that the relation "still remains". The argument for it is elsewhere, in *De Ente* ch. 4's uniqueness step and in q.3 a.4 and q.7 a.2 ad 1 (no made thing is its own existence).

**Must hit, any verdict (b):**
- State the objection exactly: in bodies, matter might be the *ground* of the nature's not being its existence, so the ground would not transfer.
- Bring in the *De Ente* resource: the reason a nature is not its existence is not matter but that existence-alone can be only one (step 3), and every other being receives *esse* (step 5).
- Judge whether that answers the objection or moves the weight onto step 3.

**Wrong turns:** reading the passage as proving angels are material; answering (b) with the phoenix premise alone, since that premise is about understanding and meets the objection only if one accepts that it reaches a distinction in things.

**Model answer (b), one of several acceptable:** The reply alone does not answer the objection, since it simply asserts that the relation "still remains". *De Ente* supplies the missing ground: what separates a nature from its existence is not matter but the fact that only one thing can be existence alone, since pure existence has no difference, no matter and no recipient to multiply it. An angel is a determinate nature, one among many, so it cannot be that one. On this basis the objection fails, because matter was never the ground. But the answer is only as strong as step 3. Someone who doubts that "existence alone" is a coherent nature that the three modes of multiplication exhaust will find the objection has been relocated, not refuted. Verdict: answered, if one grants the uniqueness argument.

---

**P3** *(Exegetical, strict on identifying a single premise.)*

**Must hit, strict:**
- The crux: *whatever belongs to a thing but lies outside its essence stands to it as an accident, that is, as a further determination of a subject.* The Avicennian formula (as Averroes reads it) affirms this for existence; Aquinas denies it for *esse*, because an accident presupposes an actually existing subject, while *esse* is what makes the subject actual at all (I q.4 a.1 ad 3; *In Metaph.* IV lect. 2).
- Not the crux: whether essence includes existence, which both deny.
- What would move an Avicennian: textual evidence that "accident" in Avicenna means a non-constitutive concomitant, not a categorial accident. That would make the crux partly verbal. Or the regress argument: if existence inhered in a subject, the subject would have to exist first.

**Wrong turns:** naming "whether existence differs from essence", on which they agree; attributing to Aquinas the view that existence is part of created essence.

**Model answer:** Both deny that a created essence contains existence. They split on whether what lies outside an essence must come to it as an accident, a determination of a subject already there. Avicenna, as Averroes reads him, says yes, so existence is an accident. Aquinas says no: an accident needs an existing subject to inhere in, and before *esse* there is no such subject, so *esse* is the act that makes the essence actual, "the most perfect of all things". A defender of the Avicennian formula could be moved in two ways. Textually, if Avicenna's "accident" means only "not part of the definition", the two may be closer than Averroes thought. Philosophically, by the regress: if existence inheres like a colour, the subject must already exist to receive it.

## Flashback

**From Lesson 6.3 (Averroes and Maimonides: philosophy and revealed law):** *(Exegetical — apply to a hard case.)* An invented lecturer argues: *"In every change anyone has ever observed, something comes from something; matter is never produced from nothing. So the world as a whole cannot have come from nothing, and it has always existed."* (a) Which step of Maimonides's case in *Guide* II answers this, and how does his analogy of the child raised on an island apply to it? (b) Suppose the reply works. Has Maimonides now shown that the world was created? Say what the reply establishes, and what reasons he gives for holding creation all the same. Three sentences or fewer per part. Do not assess whether the world began.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):** premise 3 (II.17): you cannot infer how a thing came to be from how it behaves once it is complete. The lecturer reasons from how things behave inside a world that already exists to how the world itself began. That is the island child's mistake: he knows only fully formed people, so he calls growth inside the womb impossible.

**Must hit, strict (b):** no. The reply shows only that this argument is not a demonstration of eternity. Maimonides holds that creation is not demonstrable either (II.16), so the question stays open to reason. He holds creation because no demonstration forces a figurative reading of the creation passages, as one did for God's incorporeality, and because eternity by necessity, as Aristotle held it, would rule out miracles and overturn the Law (II.25).

**Wrong turns:** answering (a) with negative theology or Averroes's rule that truth does not contradict truth, neither of which touches an argument from how nature behaves; claiming in (b) that II.17 proves creation, or that Maimonides holds creation simply because Genesis says so. II.25 grants that the verses could be interpreted.

**Model answer:** (a) Premise 3: the nature of a completed thing does not reveal how it came to be. The lecturer argues from how things behave inside an existing world to how that world began, like the island child who knows only grown people and calls growth in the womb impossible. (b) No. The reply removes a supposed demonstration of eternity; it does not prove creation, which Maimonides says cannot be demonstrated either. He holds creation because nothing demonstrated forces him to read Genesis figuratively, and because Aristotle's necessary eternity would leave no room for miracles or the Law.
