# Ancient & Medieval Philosophy · Lesson 7.1: Faith, reason and the Paris crisis

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 7: Aquinas: a survey · Builds on: [6.3 Averroes and Maimonides](06-03-averroes-and-maimonides.md), [3.5 Soul and intellect](03-05-soul-and-intellect.md) · Unlocks: [7.2 Essence and existence](07-02-aquinas-essence-and-existence.md)

## Why this matters

By the 1260s the University of Paris taught Aristotle as its core curriculum, and read him through Averroes, "the Commentator." Some of what that Aristotle concluded (a world without beginning, one intellect shared by all humans) contradicted what every master in the building professed as a Christian. What do you do when your best science and your creed collide? Within a decade Paris tried three answers: report the science and yield to the creed, map exactly how far reason reaches, and condemn. Thomas Aquinas (c. 1225–1274) gave the second answer, and it shapes the rest of this module. Whether his harmony of faith and reason is *true* belongs to [`philosophy-of-religion`](../../philosophy-of-religion/syllabus.md). Its standing as Catholic doctrine is [`fundamental-theology` 2.2](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/02-02-credibility-and-the-preambles-of-faith.md). Here the question is what he held and why.

## The idea

Picture a collision: a philosopher's argument concludes *p*, and faith holds not-*p*. Paris produced three responses.

**Divide the labour.** A master of arts expounds Aristotle. His job is to say what follows from the Philosopher's principles. If that clashes with faith, he says so and holds with faith. The science is reported; the creed wins.

**Map the terrain.** Aquinas asks first what reason *can* reach. Some truths about God it can demonstrate; some it cannot even approach. Truth cannot contradict truth. So a genuine collision is impossible, and every apparent one is a sign that something has gone wrong. Either the "philosophical" argument is not a demonstration, or it misreads its own sources, or the believer has confused faith with a bad proof. The work is to find out which, case by case.

**Draw a fence.** The bishop of Paris lists propositions that may not be taught, on pain of excommunication.

The map is not a truce. Aquinas's two test cases came out opposite ways. On the eternity of the world, neither side can demonstrate its view, so the collision dissolves. On the one intellect, philosophy *can* settle the matter, and Aquinas argues it settles it against Averroes, on Aristotle's own terms.

## Source

Aquinas, *Summa contra Gentiles* I.7 (English Dominican Fathers' translation, Book I, 1923). Chapter 7 gives four arguments; this is the first one's conclusion:

> "Seeing then that the false alone is opposed to the true, as evidently appears if we examine their definitions, it is impossible for the aforesaid truth of faith to be contrary to those principles which reason knows naturally."

Notice the object of the comparison. The passage does not say faith cannot contradict *philosophers*. It says faith cannot contradict *principles reason knows naturally*.

## The argument

**Two kinds of truth about God, and why they cannot conflict** (*Summa contra Gentiles* I.3–7, c. 1259–1265).

1. **Our knowledge of a thing is proportioned to our grasp of its essence, and our intellect in this life starts from the senses** (I.3). *In words:* we know what we can work out from what we can perceive.
2. **Sensible things are effects that fall short of the power of their cause, so they show *that* God is but not *what* God is** (I.3). *In words:* from the world you can reason to its source, but not see into it.
3. **So some truths about God are within reason's reach and others wholly exceed it.** Aquinas's examples: that God exists and is one, which he says the philosophers proved, and that God is three and one, which no argument reaches (I.3). *In words:* the Trinity is not hard philosophy. It is not philosophy at all.
4. **Even the reachable truths are fittingly proposed for belief,** because by reason alone few would reach them, late in life, and with errors mixed in (I.4). *In words:* a proof that takes a lifetime of study is no use to the farmer, and believing on God's word is the shortcut. The doctrine of preambles built on this is [`fundamental-theology` 2.2](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/02-02-credibility-and-the-preambles-of-faith.md)'s. No doctrinal weight is assigned here.
5. **Naturally known principles are most true, and what faith holds is confirmed by God; only the false is opposed to the true** (I.7). *In words:* two truths cannot contradict each other, so faith cannot contradict the starting points of reason.
6. **God is the author of our nature and so of those principles, and a teacher who plants contrary knowledge in a pupil deceives him** (I.7). *In words:* the same God cannot teach you one thing through reason and the opposite through revelation.
7. **∴ Any argument against the faith does not proceed rightly from first principles. It is probable or sophistical, not a demonstration, and it can be answered** (I.7). *In words:* when philosophy seems to refute faith, look for the bad step.

Premise 7 has a methodological twin. For truths above reason, the believer's task is to answer objections, not to prove, because weak proofs only persuade opponents that faith rests on weak grounds (I.9, and ST I q.46 a.2 below).

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean): the eternity of the world.** Aristotle held that motion and the world had no beginning ([3.6](03-06-the-unmoved-mover.md)). Christian teaching held that the world began. Run the map.

*Can eternity be demonstrated?* No (ST I q.46 a.1). The world exists because God wills it, and God need not will anything but himself, so the world's existence is not necessary and its always-existing cannot be demonstrated. Aristotle's arguments are demonstrative only relatively, against earlier thinkers who had the world begin in impossible ways. Aristotle cites the ancients' opinions, as one does when arguing for the probable, and the *Topics* lists "whether the world is eternal" as a dialectical problem.

*Can a beginning be demonstrated?* Also no (I q.46 a.2):

> "By faith alone do we hold, and by no demonstration can it be proved, that the world did not always exist."

His reasons are two. A demonstration starts from essences, and essences abstract from here and now, so nothing about *what* a man, a heaven or a stone is shows that none ever existed before. The other route runs through the cause, but God creates by will, and reason cannot read off what God freely wills. Then the methodological twin: offer non-cogent proofs for the faith, and unbelievers will laugh, thinking this is why believers believe (paraphrase).

Result: the collision dissolves. Neither side has a demonstration, so the philosopher who calls eternity proven and the theologian who calls a beginning proven make the same mistake. Maimonides had reached a close cousin of this position ([6.3](06-03-averroes-and-maimonides.md)).

**Example 2 (hard): the unity of the intellect.** In *De Anima* III.4 the intellect is unmixed, has no bodily organ, and is potentially all things ([3.5](03-05-soul-and-intellect.md)). Averroes's Long Commentary concludes that the material (or possible) intellect cannot be the form of any body. It is one separate substance shared by all humans. An individual is joined to it through his own phantasms, the images in his imagination. One motive is that if my intellect were individuated like a body, it would receive forms as individuals and could never grasp universals (compare ST I q.76 a.2 obj. 3).

Here Aquinas does *not* say "undemonstrable either way." In the 1270 treatise he says, as he describes his own method, that he will argue from the philosophers' texts and principles rather than from faith. He adds that Averroes perverted rather than followed Aristotle, who defines the soul, intellect included, as form of the body. (Whether Aquinas or Averroes read III.5 more faithfully is still disputed.) His central argument, also in ST I q.76 a.1, starts from a datum:

> "each one is conscious that it is himself who understands."

This man understands (*hic homo intelligit*). If the intellect is a separate substance touching Socrates only through the images in his imagination, the understanding is not Socrates' act. Colours on a wall have their likenesses in my eye, yet we say the wall is *seen*, not that it sees. By the same logic, the phantasms in Socrates make him what is *understood*, not the one who understands. So the intellect must be a power of the soul that is Socrates' form. The fence came down on the same point: the 1270 list also condemned the claim that "man understands" is false or improper.

This is harder than Example 1 because here the map means combat. When Aquinas thinks reason can decide, he fights the battle on philosophy's ground. Siger, according to modern reconstructions, followed Averroes's reading in his questions on *De Anima* III (c. 1269–1270) and later moved toward Aquinas. In *On the Intellective Soul* (c. 1273), as usually reported, he says he is seeking what the philosophers, and Aristotle above all, meant, rather than the truth, and that where they conflict with faith, faith is to be held. The 1270 treatise ends by targeting a stance like the earlier one. Anyone who says reason necessarily concludes that the intellect is one but faith holds the opposite, Aquinas says, makes faith hold what is false and impossible (paraphrase, *De unitate* ch. 5).

**The 1277 prologue** charges some masters with saying that things are true according to philosophy but not according to the Catholic faith, *quasi sint due contrarie veritates*, "as if there were two contrary truths" (lesson's translation). Note the *as if*: the bishop attributes a consequence. Historians have found no Latin master who asserted a "double truth" as a doctrine. Siger and Boethius of Dacia held faith to be true without qualification and philosophy's conclusions to be relative to natural principles. Whether that is a coherent position or the double truth under a politer name is the question P3 opens.

## Watch out

- **Anachronism: "double truth."** It names a charge, not a school. Treat it as Tempier's gloss on a stance, and ask what the accused actually wrote.
- **"Latin Averroist" does not mean a reader of the *Decisive Treatise*.** The Latins knew Averroes as the commentator on Aristotle. His own view that demonstration and revealed law agree ([6.3](06-03-averroes-and-maimonides.md)) sits closer to Aquinas than to the "double truth." Historians dispute the label itself. Fernand Van Steenberghen preferred "radical" or "heterodox Aristotelianism."
- **This is not modern "science versus religion."** The quarrel is over what follows from Aristotle's principles and how demonstration works. Pierre Duhem's thesis that the 1277 condemnation freed natural philosophy, and so helped birth modern science, is a disputed reading of its consequences, not a description of the event.
- **Harmony is not a promise that reason proves faith.** For truths above reason, I.7 guarantees only that objections can be *answered*. Aquinas calls arguments in their favour "likely" at best (I.9, paraphrase), and says offering them as proofs does harm.

## One-liner

> Truth cannot contradict truth, so every clash between Aristotle and the creed is a bad argument somewhere — and Aquinas's work was to find it, conceding that eternity cannot be disproved and insisting that the one intellect can.

**P3 (🔴, optional) *(Exegetical.)*** In an invented seminar, a student says: "As a cosmologist I conclude the universe had no beginning; as a believer I hold it had one. Both are true, each in its own domain." (a) Is this the stance Tempier's 1277 prologue describes, Siger's stance in *On the Intellective Soul*, or neither? Name the words that decide. (b) Name the single premise that divides Siger's later stance from Aquinas's, and say what argument would move a defender of either side. 150 words or fewer in all.

<details>
<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical — strict.)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** "those principles which reason knows naturally." The passage sets faith against naturally known *principles*, not against philosophers' *conclusions*. What it rules out is faith contradicting the first starting points of reason. The ground is "the false alone is opposed to the true": both are true, so neither can be opposed to the other. Conclusions can still clash with faith, because conclusions can be badly derived.

**Must hit, strict (b):** the argument cannot be a demonstration. It fails to proceed rightly from first principles, so it is probable or sophistical, and it can be answered. That commits Aquinas to finding the failure, which is what ST I q.46 a.1 does: Aristotle's arguments are only relatively demonstrative.

**Wrong turns:** reading I.7 as "reason will prove the faith" (it guarantees answerability, not proof); reading it as "faith overrides reason, so ignore the argument" (the corollary demands the argument be answered).

**Model answer:** (a) "Principles which reason knows naturally": faith cannot contradict reason's starting points, since only the false opposes the true. Philosophers' conclusions are not covered, because they can be misderived. (b) An apparent demonstration of eternity must be probable or sophistical, not a demonstration. So Aquinas owes a diagnosis of where it fails, as q.46 a.1 gives for Aristotle.

---

**P2** *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)*

**Must hit, strict (a):**
- P1. Each one is conscious that it is himself who understands: understanding is the act of this man, Socrates.
- P2. An act belongs to a thing by its whole self, by a part, or accidentally. Understanding is not accidental to Socrates, and Socrates is not a bare intellect, since the same man also senses. So the intellect is a part of Socrates.
- P3. On Averroes's view, the one separate intellect is joined to Socrates only because its intelligible species also exist, as phantasms, in Socrates' imagination.
- P4. A thing whose likenesses are in a power is thereby what that power acts on, not its agent: colours on a wall make the wall *seen*, not seeing.
- P5. So that link makes Socrates, or his phantasms, *understood*, not understanding.
- ∴ C. Averroes's link cannot make understanding Socrates' act; the intellect must be a power of the soul that is his form.

**Must hit, any verdict (b):** name a deniable premise, most naturally P1 as Aquinas reads it (that the self who understands is the individual composite), or P4 (that the wall analogy fits, since phantasms are not passive like colours on a wall but are what the intellect's act is *about* in this man). Then weigh the cost against *De Anima*: the text defines soul as form of the body, and it also says the intellect has no organ and is unmixed. Either verdict passes if both textual pulls are used.

**Wrong turns:** turning P1 into a Cartesian claim of introspective certainty (it is a datum about whose act understanding is); arguing from faith or immortality, which this argument does not use.

**Model answer (b), one of several:** The Averroist should deny P4. Phantasms are not a wall's colours: they are the content Socrates' imagination supplies and the intellect's act depends on. So "Socrates understands" can be true by his *contributing* to the act, as a man is said to see through his eye. The cost is real, though. Aristotle calls the soul the form of the body, and a separate intellect that is no one's form makes the III.4–5 intellect an exception to the definition of II.1. Averroes pays that price to keep the intellect unmixed and universal. Whether it is worth paying is a question about which chapter of *De Anima* governs the other.

---

**P3** *(Exegetical — strict.)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** "Both are true" matches the stance the 1277 prologue *describes*: things true according to philosophy, not according to faith, "as if there were two contrary truths." It is not Siger's stance, which grants truth without qualification only to faith and presents the philosophical result as what the philosophers meant, or what follows from natural principles.

**Must hit, strict (b):** the premise is whether a conclusion that follows *necessarily* from philosophy's principles can be false. Siger allows it, since philosophy reports what follows from the Philosopher's principles and that may differ from the truth. Aquinas denies it (I.7): if the conclusion is false, the argument was not a demonstration. What would move them: for Siger, being shown that the argument fails on philosophy's own principles, which is Aquinas's strategy in *De unitate*, so there is no necessary conclusion to report; for Aquinas, a demonstration from self-evident principles that contradicts a truth of faith.

**Wrong turns:** calling the student a Siger (he claims two truths); making the crux "whether faith outranks reason," which both affirm.

**Model answer:** (a) The prologue's version: "both are true." Siger held only the faith true without qualification. (b) Crux: can a conclusion necessarily derived from philosophy's principles be false? Siger: yes, relative to those principles. Aquinas: no, so such a conclusion was never demonstrated. Siger moves if the argument is shown to fail by philosophy's own standards. Aquinas moves only on a genuine demonstration against faith.

</details>

## Flashback

**From Lesson 6.2 (Avicenna: essence, existence and the necessary existent):** *(Exegetical — diagnose.)* An invented study guide summarizes Avicenna's proof of the truthful this way: *"A possible thing is one that could fail to exist at any moment, even while its causes are in place. Trace any possible thing's causes back one by one, and since an infinite chain of causes is impossible, you reach a first cause. That first cause is the necessary existent: it is necessary because nothing came before it."* Name three claims in the summary that misdescribe Avicenna, and for each give the part of his argument that corrects it. Two sentences or fewer per claim.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict:**
- **"Could fail to exist even while its causes are in place."** Wrong: given its cause, a possible thing is *necessary through another*. "Possible in itself" means only that its essence does not settle whether it exists.
- **"Trace the causes back one by one; an infinite chain is impossible."** Wrong on both counts. Avicenna takes the *totality* of possible existents at once, finite or infinite, and argues that the whole needs a cause outside it. He holds the world has no beginning, so he never needs the chain to stop.
- **"Necessary because nothing came before it."** Wrong: necessity in itself is not being first in time. It means needing no cause, because the thing's essence is not other than its existence.

**Wrong turns:** "correcting" the third claim by saying the necessary existent is necessary because it exists in every possible world, which reads modern modal logic into Avicenna; calling the regress step a harmless summary, when stepping back through a chain is exactly the argument he built this one to avoid.

**Model answer:** (1) The guide says a possible thing could fail to exist with its causes present. For Avicenna, once its cause is present it is necessary through another; "possible in itself" means only that its essence leaves existence open. (2) It traces causes back one by one and bans an infinite chain. Avicenna takes the whole collection of possibles, however infinite, and argues that the collection itself needs a cause outside every member. (3) It makes the first cause necessary by coming first. Avicenna's necessary existent is necessary in itself: uncaused, its essence not other than its existence, whatever the temporal order.
