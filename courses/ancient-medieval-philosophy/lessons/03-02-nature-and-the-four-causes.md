# Ancient & Medieval Philosophy · Lesson 3.2: Nature and the four causes

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 3: Aristotle: logic, nature, substance and the first mover · Builds on: [3.1 Logic: predication, the categories and the syllogism](03-01-predication-categories-and-the-syllogism.md), [1.2 Zeno and the atomists](01-02-zeno-and-the-atomists.md) · Unlocks: [3.3 Change: privation, potency and act](03-03-change-privation-potency-and-act.md)

## Why this matters

Ask "why?" about a bronze statue and you can get four different correct answers: because it is bronze, because it has this shape, because a sculptor made it, because someone wanted a memorial. Aristotle's claim in *Physics* II is that these are not rival answers, one right and three confused, but four kinds of explanation that any full account of a thing needs. The scheme ran Western natural philosophy for nearly two thousand years. The fourth kind, the "that for the sake of which", is the one the early moderns threw out, the one Aquinas's Fifth Way builds on ([7.3](07-03-the-five-ways-as-text.md)), and the one people most often misread as "somebody intended it".

## The idea

Start with the Greek. The word translated "cause" is *aition*, and it means something closer to *the because*: whatever you cite in answer to a why-question. English "cause" has narrowed to the push that starts something off. Aristotle's has not. The bronze *explains* the statue's weight, even though bronze pushes nothing.

So a thing has as many causes as there are kinds of why-question to put to it:

- **Material:** what is it made of? The bronze.
- **Formal:** what is it — what shape, structure or definition makes this bronze a statue rather than a lump?
- **Efficient:** what started the change? The sculptor.
- **Final:** what is it for? The memorial it serves.

Now the other half of Book II. Some things have their principle of change *inside* them, and Aristotle calls that principle their **nature** (*physis*). A foal grows into a horse by itself; a bed never turns itself into a better bed. His definition (II.1, 192b): nature is "a source or cause of being moved and of being at rest in that to which it belongs primarily." An artefact has its source of change outside it, in the craftsman.

Antiphon had argued that a thing's nature is its matter: bury a bed, and if the rotting wood sent up a shoot, what came up would be wood, not a bed (II.1, 193a). Aristotle grants the datum and turns it around: a man is born from a man, but not a bed from a bed. What reproduces itself is the *form*, so form has more claim to be nature than matter does.

That sets up the big claim. Natural things act for ends — teeth are for chewing, the foal grows *toward* being a horse — **without anyone intending it**. The final cause is built into the process, not supplied by a mind watching it.

## Source

Aristotle, *Physics* II.3, 194b (Hardie and Gaye translation, 1930):

> In one sense, then, (1) that out of which a thing comes to be and which persists, is called 'cause', e.g. the bronze of the statue, the silver of the bowl [...]. In another sense (2) the form or the archetype, i.e. the statement of the essence, and its genera, are called 'causes' (e.g. of the octave the relation of 2:1, and generally number), and the parts in the definition. Again (3) the primary source of the change or coming to rest; e.g. the man who gave advice is a cause, the father is cause of the child, and generally what makes of what is made and what causes change of what is changed. Again (4) in the sense of end or 'that for the sake of which' a thing is done, e.g. health is the cause of walking about.

Three things to read off it. The formal cause is "the statement of the essence", a definition, not just a shape: the octave's form is a *ratio*. The efficient cause includes "the man who gave advice", so it need not be a physical push. And the final cause is illustrated by health as the cause of *walking about*: an answer to "why is he walking?", which is to say an explanation, not a force.

## The argument

**Chance and spontaneity (II.4–6).** Before defending final causes, Aristotle has to say what their absence looks like. A man goes to the market for some other reason and happens to meet his debtor, who pays him. He *would* have gone there for the money had he known. The recovery is the kind of thing people do on purpose, but here it came about incidentally: that is **chance** (*tyche*). **Spontaneity** (*automaton*) is the wider class that includes non-choosing things: a horse wanders to safety though it did not go there to be safe. Both are incidental results of processes aimed at something else, and both are *rare*.

**The argument for final causes in nature (II.8, 198b–199b).**

The objector, whose view Aristotle illustrates with Empedocles, says nature needs only necessity. Rain falls because vapour cools, not in order to make the corn grow; if the corn happens to benefit, that is a by-product. Likewise, front teeth came up sharp and molars broad by necessity, and the animals whose parts happened to be fitting survived, while the rest, Empedocles' "man-faced ox-progeny" among them, perished (DK B61).

Aristotle's reply, reconstructed:

1. **Natural things come about in a given way always or for the most part.** Teeth come in sharp at the front and broad at the back in generation after generation. *In words:* nature is regular.
2. **What comes about by chance or spontaneity does not happen always or for the most part.** We call heavy rain in the dog-days of summer chance; heavy rain in winter we do not. *In words:* chance is by definition the exception.
3. **Whatever comes about either comes about by coincidence or for the sake of something.** *In words:* if a fitting result is not an accident, it is the point.
4. **So natural things do not come about by coincidence** (from 1, 2). *In words:* regularity rules out the lucky-accident story.
5. **So natural things come about for the sake of something** (from 3, 4). *In words:* nature has ends.
6. **Purpose does not require deliberation.** "Art does not deliberate" (199b): a skilled shipwright does not stop to reason out each joint, and if the art of shipbuilding were *in the wood*, the wood would produce a ship by nature. *In words:* a process can be aimed at an end without anyone thinking about the end.

Premise 6 blocks the obvious rejoinder — "but nobody is aiming" — and is why a final cause is not an intention.

## The case

The four causes of an artefact and of a natural thing, side by side:

| Cause | Bronze statue | Foal growing into a horse |
|---|---|---|
| **Material** | The bronze | The flesh and bone the body is built from (ultimately, what the mother supplies) |
| **Formal** | The shape and definition of *this* statue, first in the sculptor's mind | The form of horse: what it is to be a horse |
| **Efficient** | The sculptor, strictly the sculptor *in virtue of* the art of sculpture | The sire, a horse already having that form |
| **Final** | The end the statue serves, e.g. to honour someone | The mature horse, able to do what horses do |
| **Where the principle of change sits** | Outside the statue, in the craftsman | Inside the foal: it grows by its own nature |

Look at the right-hand column. In the foal the formal, efficient and final causes nearly coincide: the form of horse is what the sire passes on, what the growth is aimed at, and what the foal is. Aristotle says as much in II.7 — the "what" and the "for the sake of which" are one, and the source of motion is the same in species. In the statue the three stay apart: the bronze has no tendency to become a statue, and the end lives in whoever commissioned it.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean — why is he walking about?).** Aristotle's own case from II.3. A man is out walking.

- *Final:* for the sake of his health. Aristotle says that once we have said "to be healthy", we think we have given the cause.
- *Efficient:* the man himself, moving his legs (and, one step back, his doctor's advice — the "man who gave advice").
- *Also for the sake of health:* everything in between the mover and the end. Aristotle lists slimming, purging, drugs and surgical instruments (II.3, 194b–195a): some are actions, some are tools, and all are explained by the end they serve.

Two lessons fall out. First, one fact can have several causes at once, and they do not compete. Second, things can be **causes of each other** in different senses (II.3, 195a): exercise is the efficient cause of good bodily condition, and good condition is the final cause of exercise. That sounds circular only if every cause must be a push.

**Example 2 (hard — the rain).** Premise 2 said winter rain is *not* chance, because it is regular. Premise 3 then says what is not coincidence is for the sake of something. Put them together and winter rain looks as if it falls for the sake of something — perhaps even for the corn, the very claim the objector used as his absurd example.

Readers split here. On one reading, Aristotle only states the rain case as the objector's and never endorses the view that rain is *for* crops; his teleology is confined to living things and their parts, and the dilemma of premise 3 is meant for outcomes that benefit the thing undergoing them. On another, argued by David Sedley ("Is Aristotle's teleology anthropocentric?", 1991), Aristotle does accept that regular rain serves plant growth, and his teleology reaches the cosmos as a whole. The text of II.8 is brief enough to sustain both; *Politics* I.8, where plants are said to exist for animals and animals for man, is the passage Sedley's side leans on.

The strain matters because it locates premise 3 as the load-bearing step. The dichotomy "coincidence or for an end" leaves no room for a third option: *regular but end-less* necessity, which is what the objector's rain actually is. Whether Aristotle is entitled to exclude that option is what the argument turns on.

## Watch out

- **You might think "cause" means what starts a change.** That is only the efficient cause. An *aition* is any correct answer to "why?"; the bronze and the octave's 2:1 ratio are causes without doing anything.
- **You might think a final cause is an intention.** Aristotle's point in II.8 is the reverse: an acorn or a tooth has an end with no mind anywhere in the process. Whether goal-directedness ultimately requires a directing intelligence is a *later* question — Aquinas's Fifth Way says it does ([7.3](07-03-the-five-ways-as-text.md)) — not one *Physics* II raises.
- **You might think Empedocles anticipated Darwin and Aristotle rejected natural selection.** Anachronism. Empedocles' ox-progeny are one-off monstrous combinations, not variation passed on by heredity across generations, and Aristotle's reply is aimed at *that* story. Whether his "always or for the most part" argument also answers modern selection theory is a question for [`philosophy-of-science`](../../philosophy-of-science/syllabus.md), not something either Greek could have addressed.
- **You might think formal cause means shape.** For a statue it is close. For a horse it is the definition of the kind, and for a ratio it is a number.

## One-liner

> A cause is an answer to "why?" — matter, form, maker, end — and nature is the case where the maker and the end live inside the thing, so it can aim without anyone intending.

## Problems

**P1 (🟡) *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)*** A beaver fells saplings and packs them with mud across a stream; a pond forms behind the dam, in which it builds its lodge. (a) Give all four causes of the dam, one line each. (b) Does the dam have a *nature* in the sense of *Physics* II.1? And is it "by nature" or "by art"? Say where Aristotle's two categories stop fitting. Any verdict, if II.1's definition and II.8's "art does not deliberate" are both used. 150 words or fewer for (b).

**P2 (🟡) *(Exegetical.)*** Read *Physics* II.8, 199a and 199b (Hardie and Gaye, 1930):

> This is most obvious in the animals other than man: they make things neither by art nor after inquiry or deliberation. Wherefore people discuss whether it is by intelligence or by some other faculty that these creatures work,—spiders, ants, and the like. [...] It is absurd to suppose that purpose is not present because we do not observe the agent deliberating. Art does not deliberate. If the ship-building art were in the wood, it would produce the same results by nature. If, therefore, purpose is present in art, it is present also in nature. The best illustration is a doctor doctoring himself: nature is like that.

Two readings. **(R1)** Natural ends need a mind directing them; Aristotle's point is only that the mind need not be the animal's own. **(R2)** End-directedness can be present in a process with no deliberating mind anywhere in it. Which reading does the passage support, and which words decide it? What does the passage *not* settle? 150 words or fewer.

**P3 (🔴, optional) *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)*** (a) Reconstruct Antiphon's argument that a thing's nature is its matter (II.1, 193a) in numbered premises, then Aristotle's reply, and name the crux: the premise about *what persists* or *what reproduces* on which they part. (b) Does Aristotle's reply answer Antiphon on terms Antiphon accepts, or change the question? Any verdict, if the bed example is used by both sides. 150 words or fewer in total.

<details>
<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b))*

**Must hit, strict (a):** material: the saplings, sticks and mud. Formal: the structure of a dam, what makes these sticks a barrier holding back water, not a heap. Efficient: the beaver building it (in the strict sense, the beaver in virtue of its building capacity). Final: the pond, and through it the beaver's safety and food store, i.e. the beaver's life.
**Must hit, any verdict (b):** apply II.1 — the dam has no inner source of motion and rest: it does not grow, reproduce or repair itself, and its principle of change is in the beaver, as an artefact's is in the craftsman. Then II.8 — it is made for an end without art, inquiry or deliberation, which is the mark Aristotle gives natural making (he uses spiders and swallows). So the case sits between his categories: made like an artefact, aimed like nature. A verdict either way passes if both criteria are applied and the tension is named.
**Wrong turns:** giving "the beaver's plan" as the formal cause, which imports the intention II.8 denies; calling the dam a natural thing because a natural animal made it, which confuses "having a nature" with "coming from something that has one".
**Model answer (b), one of several:** No: the dam has no nature of its own. Its source of change lies outside it, in the beaver, and once built it neither grows nor mends itself, which is exactly II.1's mark of an artefact. But it is not made by art either: the beaver neither learns a craft nor deliberates, and II.8 treats that kind of making (the spider's web) as nature at work for an end. The best fit is that the dam is *by nature* in the sense of being the product of the beaver's nature, while not itself a natural thing. Aristotle's pair "nature or art" classifies the source of the making; "has a nature" classifies the product. The beaver pulls them apart.

---

**P2** *(Exegetical — strict)*

**Must hit, strict:** R2. Deciding words: "It is absurd to suppose that purpose is not present because we do not observe the agent deliberating" (purpose is detached from observed deliberation); "Art does not deliberate" (even purposive making needs no reasoning in the moment); "If the ship-building art were in the wood" (the end-directed principle placed *inside* the material, with no maker outside); "a doctor doctoring himself: nature is like that" (the principle of change and its end inside the same thing). The passage leaves open whether a mind is needed somewhere *else* — it never discusses a cosmic or divine intelligence, so it does not refute R1 at that level; it only shows Aristotle's argument does not rely on one.
**Wrong turns:** reading "whether it is by intelligence or by some other faculty" as Aristotle's endorsement of animal intelligence (he reports a discussion); treating the ship-building sentence as a claim that wood literally contains craft (it is a counterfactual illustrating internal principles).
**Model answer:** R2. Aristotle denies that purpose needs a deliberating agent ("It is absurd to suppose that purpose is not present because we do not observe the agent deliberating") and backs it with "Art does not deliberate". The counterfactual "If the ship-building art were in the wood" moves the purposive principle inside the material, and the self-healing doctor is chosen because the source of change and its goal sit in one subject: "nature is like that". What the passage does not settle is whether some higher intelligence stands behind nature as a whole; it simply makes no appeal to one. That question comes later, with Aquinas's Fifth Way.

---

**P3** *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b))*

**Must hit, strict (a):** Antiphon: P1 a thing's nature is what it really is, underneath what is imposed on it. P2 if a buried bed sprouted, what grew would be wood, not bed. P3 so the bed-shape is an incidental arrangement by art, and the wood persists. ∴ the nature is the matter. Aristotle: P1' what a thing generates shows its nature. P2' man comes from man, but bed does not come from bed. ∴ in natural things form is reproduced, so form is nature more than matter is (and the bed's sprouting wood shows only that the *wood* has a nature, the bed not). Crux: whether nature is marked by what *persists* under change (Antiphon) or by what *reproduces itself* (Aristotle).
**Must hit, any verdict (b):** say whether Antiphon would grant that generation, not persistence, is the test; and note that Aristotle keeps Antiphon's datum and reinterprets it.
**Wrong turns:** saying Aristotle denies that matter is ever nature — he allows that matter is called nature in one sense, form in another, and form more properly.
**Model answer (b), one of several:** He answers on shared ground: both accept the sprouting bed, and Aristotle uses it to show that the bed has no nature at all, only its wood. But Antiphon's test was persistence, and Aristotle's is reproduction, so to that extent the question has shifted.

## Flashback

**From Lesson 2.3 (The soul: its parts and its immortality):** *(Exegetical.)* An invented argument from a seminar:

> "Friendship always brings goodwill into whatever relationship it occupies. The opposite of goodwill is ill will. So friendship cannot admit ill will: a spiteful friendship is as impossible as hot snow. Therefore no friendship can ever come to an end."

(a) Match the speaker's sentences to the steps of the *Phaedo*'s final argument, and name the step the speaker skips. (b) Use Socrates' own snow case (106a–c) to show why the conclusion does not follow. Then say what the soul case has, at 106d, that the friendship case lacks. Three sentences or fewer per part.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):** "brings goodwill into whatever relationship it occupies" is steps 1 and 3: a thing that is not itself an opposite always brings one, as soul brings life. "The opposite of goodwill is ill will" is step 4. "Cannot admit ill will … as impossible as hot snow" is steps 2 and 5. "No friendship can ever come to an end" is step 7, reached without step 6: the claim that what cannot admit the opposite is also *imperishable*.

**Must hit, strict (b):** when heat advances, snow does not become hot snow, but it can still melt. It either withdraws or perishes, and only if the not-cold were imperishable would it have to withdraw whole. Likewise, a friendship can never be a spiteful friendship and still simply end when ill will arrives. The soul case has a further premise, agreed at 106d, that "the immortal", like God and the form of life, never perishes. That is what licenses the move from "deathless" to "imperishable". Nothing is offered that makes the un-spiteful imperishable.

**Wrong turns:** attacking step 1 or 4, for instance by arguing that friends sometimes feel ill will. The question asks where the *form* of the argument breaks, and it breaks even if the premises are granted. Saying Socrates' argument has the same gap and stopping there. The text sees the gap and fills it with 106d, however contestable the filling is.

**Model answer:** (a) The first sentence is steps 1 and 3, the second is step 4, the "hot snow" sentence is steps 2 and 5, and the conclusion is step 7, reached without step 6. (b) Snow cannot admit heat, yet it melts: cannot-be-F only means withdraw *or* perish. So a friendship that cannot be spiteful can still perish when spite arrives. The soul argument gets from deathless to imperishable only through the agreement at 106d that the immortal, like God and the form of life, never perishes, and friendship has no such premise.

</details>
