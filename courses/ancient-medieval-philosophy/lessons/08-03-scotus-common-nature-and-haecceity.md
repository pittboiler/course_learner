# Ancient & Medieval Philosophy · Lesson 8.3: Scotus: common nature and haecceity

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 8: Universals, Scotus and Ockham · Builds on: [8.2 Scotus: the univocity of being](08-02-scotus-the-univocity-of-being.md), [6.2 Avicenna: essence, existence and the necessary existent](06-02-avicenna-essence-existence-and-the-necessary-existent.md), [8.1 Porphyry's questions](08-01-porphyrys-questions-universals-to-aquinas.md) · Unlocks: [8.4 Ockham: nominalism and the razor](08-04-ockham-nominalism-and-the-razor.md)

## Why this matters

Two lambs from one ewe share a nature, yet each is *this* lamb and not the other. What makes it this one? Aquinas answered with matter. John Duns Scotus (died 1308) thought that answer could not work. His replacement has three parts: a nature that is really common, an individuating "thisness" later called *haecceitas*, and a new kind of distinction to hold the two together in one thing. The formal distinction is the most argued-over tool of late scholasticism, and it is the exact target Ockham aims at in [8.4](08-04-ockham-nominalism-and-the-razor.md). Whether haecceities or bare particulars really exist is a live question for [`metaphysics` 2.1](../../metaphysics/lessons/02-01-substance-and-accident.md). This lesson asks what Scotus held and why.

## The idea

**Two questions, not one.** "How do I *tell* the lambs apart?" is a question about knowing, and the answer is where each one stands. "What *makes* each one individual?" is a question about being, and it would still need an answer if nobody could ever tell them apart. The medieval problem of individuation is the second question.

**Aquinas's answer: signate matter.** The form "lamb" is the same kind of thing in both lambs. What differs is the stuff it informs. In *De Ente et Essentia* ch. 2, Aquinas says the principle of individuation is designated or signate matter (*materia signata*): matter considered under determinate dimensions, this lump here, not matter in general. Signate matter is not part of the definition of a species, and would be part of the definition of Socrates if Socrates had one. The *Summa* makes the same point: "this particular flesh, these bones, this blackness or whiteness, etc., are not included in the definition of a man" (ST I q.3 a.3). The nature is what the definition captures, and the individual adds matter marked out by quantity. (Whether Aquinas's view shifted from indeterminate to determinate dimensions over his career is debated by historians. Hold that loosely.)

**Scotus's diagnosis.** Start with Avicenna ([6.2](06-02-avicenna-essence-existence-and-the-necessary-existent.md)): horseness is just horseness. Considered in itself, a nature is neither universal nor singular. Scotus takes that literally and asks what the nature is *in the thing*, before any mind considers it. Two claims follow.

1. The nature in the lamb is not of itself *this*. If it were, it could not also be in the other lamb.
2. It is not nothing, either. It has a real unity of its own, less than the unity of an individual but prior to any act of thinking. Scotus calls this a *less-than-numerical unity* (*unitas minor*).

So something else must make the nature this. It cannot be a negation, existence, quantity or matter: Scotus argues against each in turn. It is a positive entity in the category of substance, which he calls in the *Ordinatio* the "ultimate reality" of the being. Some of his other works and his followers name it *haecceitas*, "thisness". Hedge the vocabulary: the *Ordinatio* discussion does not rest on the famous word.

## The argument

The core is *Ordinatio* II d.3 p.1, qq.1–6 (paraphrased, since no public-domain English translation exists).

1. **What belongs to a nature of itself belongs to it wherever it is.** *In words:* if being-this came with stonehood, every stone would carry the same "this".
2. **Suppose the nature of stone were of itself this singular.** *In words:* take the rival view, that stonehood is individual all on its own.
3. **Then being in any other stone would be incompatible with the nature itself.** *In words:* one "this" cannot also be a second "this".
4. **But the intellect grasps stone as universal, true of many, and does not grasp it under a character opposed to its own.** *In words:* when we think "stone", we are not thinking something contradictory.
5. **So the nature is not of itself singular** (from 2–4, by reductio). *In words:* stonehood, taken just as stonehood, is not yet this stone.
6. **Yet the nature has some real unity prior to any act of the intellect.** Scotus supports this with several arguments, for example that real similarity between two white things needs a real foundation that is one. *In words:* two lambs are really alike, not alike because we say so.
7. **So the nature has a real unity that is less than numerical** (5, 6). *In words:* one in kind, really, without being one thing.
8. **This lamb is really and numerically one, and incommunicable: it cannot be divided into many lambs each of which is it.** *In words:* individuality is a fact about the lamb, not about our thinking.
9. **Neither negation, existence, quantity (an accident, posterior to substance) nor matter (itself common, standing in need of individuation) can make a substance this** (qq.2–5). *In words:* each rival either is too late, being an accident, or would need individuating itself.
10. **So there is a positive substantial entity that contracts the nature to this individual:** the individual difference. It is not another nature or quiddity, so it adds no new "what". *In words:* thisness makes the lamb *this* without making it *more of a kind*.
11. **The nature and the individual difference are not two separable things, nor are they distinct only in thought.** *In words:* they are distinguished in the thing, before any mind, yet not even God could pull them apart. That is the **formal distinction** (*distinctio formalis a parte rei*).

The analogy Scotus leans on is genus and specific difference: *rational* contracts *animal* to *human*, and the individual difference contracts *human* to *this human*. It is an imperfect analogy, since a specific difference adds content to a definition and a haecceity adds none. On his view we cannot grasp a haecceity as such in this life, which is why two perfectly alike things would be told apart only by where they are. That last claim is Scotus as usually read; hedge the details.

## Map of positions

The formal distinction sits between the two distinctions everyone already accepted.

| Distinction | Test | Scotist example | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Real** | the items are separable, at least by God's power | Socrates and Plato; a substance and its whiteness; soul and body | between things |
| **Formal** (*a parte rei*) | inseparable even by God, yet one is not included in the definition of the other, and this holds before any intellect acts | the common nature and the individual difference in Socrates; the divine attributes (intellect and will in God) | less than real, more than merely mental |
| **Of reason** (conceptual) | made by the intellect's own act; nothing in the thing answers to two items | "Tully" and "Cicero"; a thing and itself | mental only |

Thomists add a distinction of reason *with a foundation in the thing* (the mind distinguishes, and the thing's richness warrants it). The Scotist question is whether that intermediate is enough. Scotus says it is not, because the common nature's unity and the lamb's incommunicability hold whether or not anyone is thinking.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean: the twin lambs).** Run both views on the two lambs, Dot and Dash.

*Aquinas.* Dot and Dash have the same form of lamb, received in two portions of matter under distinct dimensions. What makes Dot this lamb is this signate matter. Their common nature exists in each as individuated, and as common only when the intellect abstracts it ([8.1](08-01-porphyrys-questions-universals-to-aquinas.md)).

*Scotus.* In each lamb there is the nature *lamb*, with its own less-than-numerical unity, and an individual difference: Dot-ness in Dot, Dash-ness in Dash. The nature and Dot-ness are formally distinct within Dot. Dot and Dash are really distinct from each other. Their likeness is real before anyone notices it, because the nature's unity grounds it. The nature becomes strictly *universal*, predicable of many, only in the intellect. Scotus and Aquinas agree on that last point.

Notice where they part. For Aquinas, what individuates is a principle outside the nature, matter, which the nature needs to be many. For Scotus, it is a further positive entity that is simply *of itself* this.

**Example 2 (hard: two angels of one species).** Angels, on both views, have no matter. Can there be two of the same species?

*Aquinas says no.* In ST I q.50 a.4 he argues that "such things as agree in species but differ in number, agree in form, but are distinguished materially". With no matter, two angels of one species would be like two separate whitenesses with no subjects to be in. So each angel is its own species. Given signate matter, this follows at once, and it shows the theory's reach. Individuation by matter is not a bolt-on. It fixes what immaterial beings can be.

*Scotus says yes, it is possible* (*Ordinatio* II d.3 p.1 q.7). Haecceity does not depend on matter, so nothing in the nature of an immaterial substance blocks two individuals of one kind. The consequence runs the other way too. If Scotus is right that matter, being itself common, cannot individuate even lambs, then Aquinas's conclusion about angels loses its ground. Context sharpens the stakes. Among the propositions condemned at Paris in 1277 was one denying that God could make several intelligences of one species (the exact wording and its target are for [7.1](07-01-faith-reason-and-the-paris-crisis.md) and [`church-history`](../../church-history/syllabus.md) 5.2; do not treat it as aimed at Aquinas by name).

The hard part is saying *which* premise decides the angels case. It is not whether angels are immaterial: both sides agree they are. It is whether being many in one species requires matter, which is just the individuation question again.

## Watch out

- **You might think the common nature is a Platonic Form or a universal existing in things.** It is neither. It exists only in individuals, and it is universal only in the intellect. What it has in the thing is a real unity *less* than that of one individual, and that is exactly why it can be in many.
- **You might think haecceity is a bare particular or a modern "haecceitistic property".** *Anachronism trap.* A bare particular is a propertyless bearer. The modern property of being identical to Socrates, as in Robert Adams's "Primitive Thisness and Primitive Identity" (1979), is a property. Scotus's individual difference is neither: it is a positive substantial entity that contracts a nature. The modern debate in [`metaphysics` 2.1](../../metaphysics/lessons/02-01-substance-and-accident.md) borrowed the name, not the theory.
- **You might think the formal distinction is a distinction of reason with a fancy name.** Its defining feature is that it holds *prior to* any act of intellect. Deny that, and you have the Thomist distinction with a foundation in reality, which is the position Scotus is rejecting.
- **You might think "less-than-numerical unity" means the nature is a bit less than one thing, like a half.** It is a different *kind* of unity, the unity of a kind, which does not come in fractions of individuals.

## One-liner

> For Scotus, a lamb is a real common nature made *this* by a positive thisness, and the two are formally distinct: distinct in the thing, but inseparable even by God.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢) *(Exegetical.)*** Using the Map of positions, say which distinction Scotus draws between each pair (real, formal, or of reason), and give the test that decides it. One line each.

> (i) Dot the lamb and Dot's whiteness.
> (ii) God's wisdom and God's goodness.
> (iii) *Animal* and *rational* in Socrates.
> (iv) The Morning Star and the Evening Star, as names for one planet.
> (v) Dot's lamb-nature and Dot's thisness.

**P2 (🟡) *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)*** Among Scotus's arguments for a real unity less than numerical (*Ordinatio* II d.3 p.1 q.1), paraphrased: *if every real unity were numerical unity, every real diversity would be numerical diversity. But numerical diversity, as such, is equal in every case. Then everything would be equally distinct, and the intellect could no more abstract something common from Socrates and Plato than from Socrates and a line.* (a) Reconstruct the argument in numbered premises in Scotus's terms, ending with his conclusion. (b) Flag the premise a critic would most likely deny, and say briefly why. 150 words or fewer for (b).

**P3 (🔴, optional) *(Exegetical.)*** A Thomist replies to Scotus's premise 9: "Signate matter does not make a substance depend on an accident. Matter, a substantial principle, individuates. Quantity only marks off which matter." Grant the reply. Name the single premise about what a principle of individuation must be that Scotus still affirms and Aquinas denies, and say what consideration might move a reader toward each side. 150 words or fewer.

<details>
<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical — strict.)*

**Must hit, strict:**
- (i) **Real.** A substance and its accident are separable: Dot can lose her whiteness and stay Dot.
- (ii) **Formal.** Inseparable, since God is simple and has no parts to remove, yet wisdom is not included in the definition of goodness, and this holds prior to any intellect. It is Scotus's standard case.
- (iii) **Formal.** Genus and difference are inseparable in Socrates, but *rational* is not in the definition of *animal*. This is the contraction the individual difference is modelled on.
- (iv) **Of reason.** One thing, two ways of naming it, and nothing in the planet answers to two items. Tully and Cicero with a telescope.
- (v) **Formal.** Inseparable even by God, not included in each other's definition, prior to thought.

**Wrong turns:** calling (ii) a distinction of reason because God is simple. That is the Thomist answer, and the Scotist point is that simplicity rules out *real* distinction only. Calling (v) real because Dot and her sister differ really: the pair here is inside Dot.

**Model answer:** (i) real, separable; (ii) formal, inseparable but definitionally distinct before thought; (iii) formal, the same test; (iv) of reason, one planet named twice; (v) formal, as (ii).

---

**P2** *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)*

**Must hit, strict (a):**
- P1: If every real unity is numerical unity, every real diversity is numerical diversity.
- P2: Every numerical diversity, as numerical, is equal.
- P3: So, on the supposition, all real diversity is equal: Socrates differs from Plato exactly as much as from a line.
- P4: If so, the intellect has no more ground in the things to abstract something common from Socrates and Plato than from Socrates and a line.
- P5: But it does have more ground. The concept *human* is drawn from the first pair and not from the second.
- ∴ Not every real unity is numerical. There is a real unity less than numerical.
- The argument must be marked as a **reductio** on the rival supposition, and "real" must mean prior to the act of intellect.

**Must hit, any verdict (b):** pick one premise and say what denying it looks like. Strong candidates: P2 (a critic says things can be numerically diverse *and* more or less similar, with similarity grounded in qualities rather than a shared unity), or P5's "ground in the things" (a critic says resemblance between individuals is enough, and no unity is needed). Then say what the denial costs or whether it begs the question.

**Wrong turns:** concluding that the nature is a universal in things (the argument yields a unity *less* than numerical, not a universal); reading "abstract" as proof that the unity is merely mental, which reverses the argument.

**Model answer (b), one of several:** The weak point is P2 together with P3. The step from "numerically diverse" to "equally diverse in every respect" holds only if numerical diversity is the *only* real diversity or likeness there is. A critic can say that Socrates and Plato are numerically two, like Socrates and the line, but their qualities resemble each other more, and resemblance between individual qualities grounds the concept *human*. That denial keeps the intellect's ground in things without positing a common unity. Whether it succeeds depends on whether resemblance itself needs a foundation that is one, which is Scotus's similarity argument and Ockham's opening in 8.4.

---

**P3** *(Exegetical — strict on naming one premise; any defensible considerations pass.)*

**Must hit, strict:**
- The premise: **a principle of individuation must be something that is of itself this, positively and intrinsically, not something that makes a nature many by receiving it.** Scotus affirms it. Matter, being the same potency in both lambs, is not of itself this, so it would need individuating in turn. Aquinas denies it: a form is multiplied by being received in distinct matter, and matter, as the ultimate subject that is not itself received in anything, can individuate without needing a thisness of its own.
- Granting the reply removes the "accident" objection, so the crux has to be the "matter is itself common" objection, not quantity.
- A consideration each way. Toward Scotus: explaining distinctness by something that is not itself distinct seems to push the question back. Toward Aquinas: a haecceity has no content and, on Scotus's own view, cannot be known in this life, so it may name the problem rather than solve it.

**Wrong turns:** naming "whether angels are immaterial" (both agree); naming the quantity point, which the problem has granted away; naming two premises.

**Model answer:** Scotus holds that whatever individuates must itself be *this* of itself. Matter fails that test, since prime matter is the same potency in every body, so it would need individuating too. Aquinas denies the premise: individuation is a form's being received in this matter, and matter, as the last subject that is received in nothing else, does not need a further thisness. A reader might move toward Scotus on the thought that distinctness cannot come from what is not itself distinct, and toward Aquinas on the thought that a thisness with no content, and unknowable to us, only renames the fact to be explained.

## Flashback

**From Lesson 8.1 (Porphyry's questions: universals from Boethius to Aquinas):** *(Exegetical, sort the cases.)* Take the nature *wolf*, and two wolves, Grey and Scar. For each predicate, say whether Aquinas assigns it to the nature considered absolutely, to the nature as it is in singulars, or to the nature as it is in the intellect, with a one-line reason.

> (i) is an animal. (ii) is a species. (iii) is in the forest near Siena. (iv) is many. (v) is one and the same in Grey and in Scar.

Then (vi): which one of your placements would William of Champeaux's first view change, and to what? One sentence.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict:**
- (i) **Absolutely.** What falls in the definition belongs to the nature as such, wherever it is.
- (ii) **In the intellect.** Being a species is a way of being predicable of many, which the nature has only once abstracted. If it belonged to wolf as such, Grey himself would be a species.
- (iii) **In singulars.** Place belongs to this individuated wolf, not to what a wolf is.
- (iv) **In singulars.** Outside the mind the nature exists many times over. Absolutely considered it is neither one nor many (8.1, step 11), or it could never be one in Grey.
- (v) **In the intellect.** Only the abstracted nature is one notion related to many; in things there are two wolf-natures.
- (vi) William's first view moves (v) into the things: one essence, *wolf*, wholly present in each wolf.

**Wrong turns:** putting (ii) under the absolute consideration because "wolf is a species" sounds true by definition; that is the confusion 8.1's step 11 is built to block. Treating the absolute consideration as a third place where the nature exists, which 8.1's Watch out rules out. Answering (vi) with Scotus's less-than-numerical unity, which is a different view from William's; the question asks about William.

**Model answer:** (i) Absolutely: in the definition. (ii) In the intellect: species-hood is predicability of many, which comes with abstraction. (iii) In singulars: location belongs to Grey or Scar. (iv) In singulars: in reality the nature is multiplied, and as such it is neither one nor many. (v) In the intellect: outside the mind there are two individuated natures, not one. (vi) William's first view puts (v) in the things, as one essence wholly in each wolf.
