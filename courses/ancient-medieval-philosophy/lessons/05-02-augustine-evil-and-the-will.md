# Ancient & Medieval Philosophy · Lesson 5.2: Augustine: evil and the will

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 5: Augustine and Boethius · Builds on: [4.4 Plotinus: the One, Intellect and Soul](04-04-plotinus-the-one-intellect-and-soul.md), [5.1 Augustine: certainty and illumination](05-01-augustine-certainty-and-illumination.md) · Unlocks: [5.3 Time, eternity and foreknowledge](05-03-time-eternity-and-foreknowledge.md)

## Why this matters

For about a thousand years, "what is evil?" got one standard answer in the Latin West: evil is not a thing but a lack in a thing, and wrongdoing starts when a will turns away from a higher good toward a lower one. Aquinas takes that answer over, and so does nearly every medieval theodicy. It came from one man working his way out of a religion that gave the opposite answer. Augustine also left behind a picture of the will as a power that can fight *itself*. Some historians think that picture is the birth of the concept of will. Others think it was just a new way of describing something much older.

## The idea

**The view Augustine left.** The Manichees, followers of the third-century Persian prophet Mani, taught that reality has two co-eternal principles, Light and Darkness. The world is a mixture that came about when Darkness invaded Light, and the evil in us is a portion of that dark substance. Augustine was a Manichaean "hearer" (a lay adherent, below the ascetic "Elect") for about nine years, from age nineteen to twenty-eight (*Confessions* IV.1). What attracted him is plain. Evil is real, it has a source, and the source isn't God. He also names the view's comfort: it seemed to him "that it was not we that sin, but that I know not what other nature sinned in us" (V.10 (18)).

**Two questions, two answers.** Augustine's reply comes in two parts, and they answer different questions.

1. *What is evil?* It is not a substance at all. It is a privation, a lack of good in something that is itself good. He found this through the "books of the Platonists" ([5.1](05-01-augustine-certainty-and-illumination.md)), since Plotinus had already called evil a lack ([4.4](04-04-plotinus-the-one-intellect-and-soul.md)).
2. *Where does moral evil come from?* From a will that turns away from the highest good toward lower goods. This is no second principle. It is a movement of a good created power.

Think of a hole in a sweater. The hole is real: you can point to it, measure it, and put your finger through it. But it isn't made of anything, and nobody knits holes. The Manichee had to find a dark substance to blame for the hole. Augustine says there is only the sweater and where it is missing.

## Source

*City of God* XII.7 (Dods, *Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers*, public domain):

> Let no one, therefore, look for an efficient cause of the evil will; for it is not efficient, but deficient, as the will itself is not an effecting of something, but a defect. For defection from that which supremely is, to that which has less of being,—this is to begin to have an evil will. Now, to seek to discover the causes of these defections,—causes, as I have said, not efficient, but deficient,—is as if some one sought to see darkness, or hear silence.

Notice the word pairs: *efficient/deficient*, *effecting/defect*, *see darkness/hear silence*. Each pair sets a positive against its lack. The whole passage runs the privation account of the argument below on the will itself.

## The argument

**A. Whatever is, is good (*Confessions* VII.12 (18)).**

1. **Whatever is corrupted is harmed, and harm is a loss of good.** *In words:* nothing can be damaged unless there is something good in it to damage.
2. **A supremely good thing cannot be corrupted, and a thing with no good in it has nothing to lose.** *In words:* only things that are good but not perfectly good can be spoiled.
3. **A thing deprived of *all* good would cease to be.** If it went on existing, it would now be beyond corruption, and so better, which is absurd. *In words:* strip away every bit of good and nothing is left over.
4. **So as long as a thing exists, it is good.** In Augustine's words, "whatsoever is, is good." *In words:* existing and being good come together.
5. **So evil is not a substance.** If evil were a substance, it would be good. *In words:* there is no "stuff" of evil for a Principle of Darkness to be made of.

**B. Where moral evil comes from (*Confessions* VII.16 (22); *On Free Choice of the Will*).**

6. **God is the author of all substances, and all of them are good.** *In words:* nothing God made is the bad ingredient.
7. **Evil *done* differs from evil *suffered*.** At the start of *On Free Choice* Book I, Evodius asks whether God is the cause of evil. Augustine separates wrongdoing from the harms that follow it, and traces wrongdoing to inordinate desire: loving things that can be lost against one's will. *In words:* "Where does evil come from?" is really two questions.
8. **The will is a "middle good."** In Book II, great goods such as the virtues cannot be misused, and lesser goods such as bodily beauty are least. The will sits in the middle: a real good, given by God, that can be used well or badly, like a hand. *In words:* a gift that can be misused is still a gift.
9. **Iniquity is "not … a substance, but a perversion of the will"** turned from God "towards these lower things" (VII.16 (22)). The lower things are good. The turning is the evil. *In words:* sin isn't a bad object you choose. It is a good power choosing in the wrong order.
10. **Therefore evil has no author other than the will that turns.** *In words:* no second god is needed, and no one gets to blame some other nature sinning in them.

## Map of positions

| | Manichees | Plotinus ([4.4](04-04-plotinus-the-one-intellect-and-soul.md)) | Augustine |
|---|---|---|---|
| Is evil a substance? | Yes: Darkness, co-eternal with Light | No: a lack | No: a privation of good |
| Where is evil found? | In a hostile nature mixed into us | Matter, the last stage of procession, is evil itself (*Enneads* I.8) | Only in good but changeable natures, and above all in wills |
| Source of wrongdoing | The dark nature in us | The soul's descent toward matter | The will's own turning from higher to lower good |
| Who is responsible? | "Not we": the other nature | The soul, in its weakness | The agent, without remainder |

Boss problem 5 compares Plotinus's and Augustine's columns closely. In this lesson the contrast that matters is the one between Augustine and the Manichees.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean: disease and the healthy body).** *Enchiridion* 11 (Shaw, NPNF) gives the model case: "In the bodies of animals, disease and wounds mean nothing but the absence of health." When a wound heals, Augustine says, it does not "go away from the body and dwell elsewhere." It simply stops existing. Now run steps 1–5. The flesh is a substance, so it is good. The wound is not a second substance lodged in it. It is "a defect in the fleshly substance," an accident of something good. Vices in the soul work the same way: they are "privations of natural good."

Apply this to lying. Speech is good, and so is the ability to say what you think. A lie adds no new ingredient to the world. It is speech missing its proper fit to the mind that produces it. Ask the Manichaean question, "what substance is the lie made of?", and the answer is: the good substance of a speaker, in a disordered condition.

**Example 2 (hard: two men, one temptation).** In *City of God* XII.6, Augustine imagines two men "alike in physical and moral constitution." Both see the same beauty and both face the same temptation. One consents and the other doesn't. What caused the evil will in the first man? It wasn't the beauty, since both saw it. It wasn't the body or the temperament, since those were stipulated to be the same. A tempter's suggestion only pushes the question back to why he consented. Augustine concludes that "we can discern nothing which caused the will of the one to be evil."

Here the theory is under real strain, and Augustine knows it. The Source passage above is his answer. The evil will has a *deficient*, not an *efficient*, cause. Looking for its cause is like trying to see darkness. You detect darkness only by "not seeing," and you can know a defect only as a lack.

Two readings pull apart. On the first, this is the privation account applied consistently. A defect is not a positive thing, so it has no positive producer, and demanding one repeats the Manichee's mistake about the hole. On the second, "deficient cause" just names the absence of an explanation, and the man's choice starts to look like something that *happened* to him. Augustine keeps responsibility with the agent: in XII.8 he says the will "could not become evil, were it unwilling to become so." Whether that line keeps the choice his, or only restates that it was, is a live question. Its modern form is the luck objection in [`metaphysics` 6.4](../../metaphysics/lessons/06-04-libertarianism-and-its-critics.md). This course only asks what Augustine held.

**The divided will.** *Confessions* VIII tells the same story from the inside. Augustine describes "my two wills, one old and the other new, one carnal, the other spiritual," which "contend within me" (VIII.5 (10)). A Manichee reads that as evidence for two souls, one good and one evil, and in VIII.10 (22) Augustine attacks exactly that reading. His own analysis in VIII.9 (21) is P1.

## Watch out

- **"Evil is a privation" does not mean "evil is unreal" or "suffering is an illusion."** Blindness is a privation, and it is entirely real. The claim concerns evil's *ontological status* (a lack in something good), not how serious it is or whether it exists.
- **Anachronism: Augustine's *liberum arbitrium* is not the "libertarian free will" of [`metaphysics` 6.4](../../metaphysics/lessons/06-04-libertarianism-and-its-critics.md).** His question is how a good creature can be the author of evil. It is not whether choices are compatible with causal determinism. His later writings also separate free choice from *libertas*, the freedom to do good, which he thinks fallen humans lack without grace. That development belongs to [`patristics`](../../patristics/syllabus.md) 4.5.
- **"Did Augustine invent the will?" is a disputed claim, not a fact.** Albrecht Dihle (*The Theory of Will in Classical Antiquity*, 1982) argued, roughly, that a notion of will distinct from both reason and desire first appears clearly in Augustine, with biblical roots. Michael Frede (*A Free Will*, 2011) traced the notion back through the Stoics, especially Epictetus, and made Augustine an inheritor rather than an inventor. Report the dispute. Don't settle it by assertion.
- **This lesson doesn't ask whether the privation account solves the problem of evil.** That question, alongside other theodicies, belongs to [`philosophy-of-religion`](../../philosophy-of-religion/syllabus.md) 4.3. Here the account is a piece of history: what it answered and how it works.

## One-liner

> Evil is a hole, not a thing, and moral evil is a good will turning the wrong way; ask what caused the turning and Augustine says you're trying to see darkness.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢) *(Exegetical.)*** Close reading. *Confessions* VIII.9 (21) (Pilkington, NPNF):

> The mind commands the body, and it obeys forthwith; the mind commands itself, and is resisted. … The mind commands the mind to will, and yet, though it be itself, it obeyeth not. … But it willeth not entirely; therefore it commandeth not entirely. For so far forth it commandeth, as it willeth; and so far forth is the thing commanded not done, as it willeth not. … It is, therefore, no monstrous thing partly to will, partly to be unwilling, but an infirmity of the mind, that it doth not wholly rise, sustained by truth, pressed down by custom. And so there are two wills, because one of them is not entire; and the one is supplied with what the other needs.

Reader A says the passage describes two complete wills, a good one and a bad one, in combat. Reader B says it describes one will that wills only in part. (a) Which reading does the text support? Quote the words that decide it. (b) Explain how the final sentence's "two wills" fits your reading, and why the mind–body contrast at the start is there. 150 words or fewer.

**P2 (🟡) *(Exegetical.)*** Diagnose, then find the crux. This is an invented student comment from a seminar:

> "When I lose my temper I'm not really choosing anything. There's a part of me that is just anger, and it isn't the real me, which is calm and wants to be kind. So you can blame the anger, but you shouldn't blame me."

(a) Which position from this lesson is the comment borrowing, and which words show it? (b) The Manichees and Augustine agree that wrongdoing is real and that the soul feels conflict. Name the single premise about the *source* of wrongdoing that one side affirms and the other denies. Say which step of Argument A or B Augustine uses to deny it, and what would move a Manichee to give it up. 150 words or fewer.

**P3 (🔴, optional) *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)*** *City of God* XII.6 (Dods, NPNF), abridged:

> If the further question be asked, What was the efficient cause of their evil will? there is none. … For if anything is the cause, this thing either has or has not a will. If it has, the will is either good or bad. If good, who is so left to himself as to say that a good will makes a will bad? … if this hypothetical thing has a bad will, I wish to know what made it so; and that we may not go on forever, I ask at once, what made the *first* evil will bad? … The only thing that can be suggested in reply is, that something which itself had no will, made the will evil. … But that thing, be it of the lowest and most earthly kind, is certainly itself good, since it is a nature and being … How, then, can a good thing be the efficient cause of an evil will?

(a) Reconstruct the argument in numbered premises and a conclusion, and mark the step that relies on "whatsoever is, is good." (b) A critic says: "If nothing efficiently causes the evil will, the first sin is an uncaused accident, not something the sinner did." Does Augustine's framework have a reply on its own terms? Any verdict. 150 words or fewer for (b).

<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical — strict.)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** Reading B. The deciding words are "it willeth not entirely; therefore it commandeth not entirely," along with "so far forth it commandeth, as it willeth" and "partly to will, partly to be unwilling." Resistance is measured by how incompletely *one* will wills, not by a second agent pushing back. The mind commands "itself" ("though it be itself"), so the one resisted is the one commanding.

**Must hit, strict (b):** "Two wills, because one of them is not entire" makes the duality *derivative*. The one will is split into incomplete parts, and "the one is supplied with what the other needs," so neither part is a full will on its own. That is also why this is not the Manichaean two-natures view that VIII.10 goes on to reject. The mind–body contrast sets up the puzzle. Commanding something *else* works instantly, so the failure of self-command can't come from lack of power over a foreign thing. It has to lie inside the will. "Custom" names the source of the drag: a history of willing, not a second substance.

**Wrong turns:** picking A because of the phrase "two wills" without reading the "because" clause; treating "infirmity" and "custom" as an evil nature, which is exactly the reading Augustine refuses.

**Model answer:** B. The resistance comes because "it willeth not entirely; therefore it commandeth not entirely": a will commands exactly as much as it wills, and wills itself only in part. So it is "no monstrous thing" but "partly to will, partly to be unwilling," an infirmity "pressed down by custom." The final "two wills" is a result, not a starting point: there are two "because one of them is not entire," and each part lacks what the other has. The opening contrast sets the puzzle. The hand obeys at once, so the failure can't be weak control over something external. It is a will not wholly willing what it commands.

---

**P2** *(Exegetical — strict.)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** It borrows the Manichaean position. It splits off a part of the self that is essentially bad ("just anger") from the "real me," which is good, and shifts blame to that part. This matches "it was not we that sin, but … other nature sinned in us" (V.10 (18)).

**Must hit, strict (b):** The crux is *whether wrongdoing requires a positive evil nature or substance as its source*. The Manichee affirms it and Augustine denies it. Augustine denies it using Argument A (whatever is, is good, so evil is not a substance, steps 4–5) together with step 9 (iniquity is a perversion of a good will). What would move a Manichee: accepting steps 1–3, that corruption is only possible in something good, so a wholly evil substance could not exist. Or the introspective point of VIII.9–10 that the "two wills" belong to one self.

**Wrong turns:** naming "whether evil exists" as the crux, when both sides agree it does; naming God's existence, which both sides accept in some form; treating the comment as Plotinian because it mentions a lower part of the soul, when the tell is that the blame is shifted onto a second nature.

**Model answer:** (a) The Manichaean view: an evil portion of the self ("just anger," "not the real me") takes the blame, like Augustine's early excuse that some other nature sinned in him. (b) Crux: *wrongdoing needs a positive evil nature as its source.* The Manichee affirms it, and Augustine denies it with "whatsoever is, is good." Any real nature, anger included, is a good thing that can be disordered, and iniquity is "a perversion of the will," not a substance. A Manichee would be moved by the corruption argument (only a good thing can be damaged, so pure evil could not even exist) or by finding the conflicting "wills" to be one self willing incompletely.

---

**P3** *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)*

**Must hit, strict (a):**
- P1. If anything efficiently causes an evil will, it either has a will or lacks one.
- P2. If it has a good will, a good will would cause a bad will, which is absurd.
- P3. If it has a bad will, we ask what made *that* will bad. To avoid an infinite regress, we come to a first evil will, and the question is what made it bad.
- P4. So the only remaining candidate for the first evil will's cause is something without a will.
- P5. Anything without a will is still a nature and a being, and so good. **(This is the step that relies on "whatsoever is, is good.")**
- P6. A good thing cannot be the efficient cause of evil.
- ∴ C. Nothing is the efficient cause of the (first) evil will.

Credit also for noting that the full text rules out an equal or superior will-less thing separately, and that P2 and P6 are the same principle (good does not efficiently cause evil).

**Must hit, any verdict (b):**
- State the objection precisely. Without an efficient cause, the choice seems to be something that happens *in* the agent, not something done *by* him.
- Name Augustine's resources: the will itself is what "has become so" (the defect is the will's own turning), and XII.8 says it "could not become evil, were it unwilling." The deficient-cause claim concerns the *metaphysics of a lack*, not a denial of agency.
- Test whether the reply meets the objection on shared terms or just restates that the agent was responsible, and give a verdict.

**Wrong turns:** in (a), leaving out the regress step or the will-less branch; in (b), arguing about whether God exists or whether the account solves the problem of evil, which is not asked; grading Augustine against modern libertarian theories without noting that they are asking a different question.

**Model answer (b), one of several acceptable:** Augustine has a reply, though it has limits. The objection assumes that an agent's own act must have a positive efficient cause. For Augustine, the evil will isn't an event the man undergoes. It *is* the man's will turning, and "the will could not become evil, were it unwilling." Nothing *else* makes him sin, which is why the sin is his. A deficient cause is the only kind of cause a lack can have. But the twins case shows the cost. When two identical men choose differently, "their own wills" is the whole explanation, and Augustine says outright that no further account is available. If the critic's demand for an explanation of *why this one* is fair, the reply only restates the responsibility claim. If the demand already assumes that evil is something positive, the reply holds on Augustine's own terms.

## Flashback

**From Lesson 4.4 (Plotinus: the One, Intellect and Soul):** *(Exegetical.)* Diagnose. An invented planetarium show presents itself as "the philosophy of Plotinus":

> "Before time began there was only the One, a vast cosmic mind thinking every idea at once. Wanting company, the One decided to share itself, and in a single burst it poured out the stars. So everything you see *is* the One: you, the rocks, the galaxies. When you meditate, you climb back to where you came from, taking the starlight into yourself until you shine too."

Name four claims Plotinus would reject, and for each give what he holds instead. Then name one thing the script gets right. 150 words or fewer.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (any four of the five):**
- **"Before time began… in a single burst":** procession is not an event and has no date. The One is prior as a cause, not earlier in time (V.1.6).
- **"A vast cosmic mind thinking every idea":** that describes Intellect, the *second* hypostasis. The One is beyond intellect and beyond being, because thinking is already a duality and the Forms are many.
- **"Wanting company… decided":** the One lacks nothing and chooses nothing. It produces the way fire gives heat, out of fullness, without will or need (V.2.1).
- **"Everything you see is the One":** pantheism. The One is "all things and no one of them": everything depends on it, and it is none of them.
- **"Taking the starlight into yourself":** the return works by subtraction and clarification, not by adding. You cut away and straighten until beauty already in the soul shows (I.6.9).

**Also strict:** one genuine match. The soul returns to its source by turning inward. Or: the outflow is like light from a source that loses nothing.

**Wrong turns:** objecting that the script skips matter and evil. That is an omission, not a claim Plotinus rejects. Correcting "the One is a mind" to "the One is Soul." Calling the One a personal creator in the correction, which repeats the "decided" error.

**Model answer:** (1) Procession happens at no moment. The One is prior as cause, not in time. (2) A mind thinking every idea is Intellect, which comes second. The One is beyond thought, since thinking is already a duality. (3) The One neither wants nor decides. It overflows from fullness, as fire gives heat. (4) The One is all things and none of them. Things depend on it without being it. What the script gets right: the way back is inward. It errs again in making the ascent an adding of light, where Plotinus says to cut away.
