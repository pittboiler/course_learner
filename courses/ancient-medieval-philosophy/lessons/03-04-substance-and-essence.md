# Ancient & Medieval Philosophy · Lesson 3.4: Substance and essence

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 3: Aristotle: logic, nature, substance and the first mover · Builds on: [3.1 Logic: predication, the categories and the syllogism](03-01-predication-categories-and-the-syllogism.md), [3.3 Change: privation, potency and act](03-03-change-privation-potency-and-act.md) · Unlocks: [3.5 Soul and intellect](03-05-soul-and-intellect.md)

## Why this matters

Plato said the most real things are Forms, apart from the world. Aristotle's answer, worked out twice, is that the most real things are the ones in front of you: this man, this horse. But the second time he worked it out, in *Metaphysics* VII, "this man" stopped being the whole answer, and a new question took over: what *in* this man makes him a being at all? His reply (form, essence, the what-it-is-to-be) is the vocabulary Avicenna, Aquinas, Scotus and Ockham all inherit and fight over. You cannot read Module 6, 7 or 8 without it.

## The idea

Take everything true of Socrates and sort it. Some of it says **what he is**: a man, an animal. Some of it says **how he is**: pale, five feet tall, in the agora, snub-nosed. Remove Socrates and the paleness has nowhere to be. Remove the paleness and Socrates carries on, tanned. So the individual comes first; everything else is a feature *of* him or a kind he belongs *to*. That is the *Categories*.

Now push. What makes Socrates the individual he is, rather than a heap of flesh that happens to be pale? Not the flesh: the same flesh as a corpse is not Socrates. Not the paleness: he survives losing it. What is left is what he *is*: being a human, a living rational animal. Aristotle calls that the essence, and in the central books of the *Metaphysics* he argues that it, the form, is substance in the primary sense.

So there are two questions, answered in two works:

- **Which things are substances?** (*Categories*): individuals.
- **What is the substance *of* a thing?** (*Metaphysics* VII): its essence, which is its form.

The Greek word for both is *ousia*, a noun built on the verb "to be", roughly "beingness". "Substance" is the Latin tradition's rendering, and it tilts the word toward "what stands under", which is exactly the reading Aristotle rejects below.

## Source

Aristotle, *Categories* 5 (E. M. Edghill, 1928):

> "Substance, in the truest and primary and most definite sense of the word, is that which is neither predicable of a subject nor present in a subject; for instance, the individual man or horse. But in a secondary sense those things are called substances within which, as species, the primary substances are included; also those which, as genera, include the species. For instance, the individual man is included in the species 'man', and the genus to which the species belongs is 'animal'; these, therefore — that is to say, the species 'man' and the genus 'animal' — are termed secondary substances."

The definition is negative: primary substance is marked by what it is *not*. And "secondary" is a rank, not a second kind of thing. Species and genera count as substances because they say what a primary substance is.

## The argument

**A. The *Categories* (chs. 2 and 5).**

1. **Two independent relations.** A thing may be *said of* a subject: "man" is said of Callias, and both the name and the definition carry over. A thing may be *present in* a subject, which ch. 2 defines as not as a part in a whole, but "incapable of existence apart from the said subject." *In words:* one relation tells you what something is, the other tells you what depends on it.
2. **Primary substance is neither.** The individual man or horse is not said of anything and not present in anything. *In words:* it is the thing everything else is about.
3. **Everything else is said of or present in primary substances;** so if they did not exist, nothing else could. *In words:* individuals are the floor of being.
4. **The mark of substance.** Remaining "numerically one and the same", a substance can receive contraries: the same man pale, then dark. *In words:* a substance is what survives the swap of its features.

**B. *Metaphysics* VII (W. D. Ross, 1908).**

5. **The question is reset.** Being is said in many ways, but every other being is a quantity, quality or state *of* a substance. So "what being is" is "just the question, what is substance?" (VII.1). *In words:* to understand being, find what everything else depends on.
6. **Four candidates** for the substance of a thing: the essence, the universal, the genus, and the substratum (VII.3). *In words:* is a thing's beingness what it is, what it shares, its kind, or what underlies it?
7. **Stripping.** If substance is the ultimate subject, strip away every predicate: qualities, then length, breadth and depth. What remains is matter, "that which in itself is neither a particular thing nor of a certain quantity" nor in any other category. *In words:* the subject-test, pushed to the end, crowns featureless stuff.
8. **Matter fails.** That is "impossible", because "both separability and 'thisness' are thought to belong chiefly to substance" (VII.3), and bare matter has neither. So form and the compound are better candidates, and form is prior to both. *In words:* being a subject is necessary for substance but not sufficient; a substance must also be a definite something that can stand on its own.
9. **Essence.** The essence (*to ti en einai*, literally "the what-it-was-to-be") of a thing is what it is said to be in virtue of itself. Socrates is musical, but being Socrates is not being musical. Essence is what a definition states, and in the strict sense only substances have one (VII.4-6). *In words:* the essence is the answer to "what is it?" with everything incidental left out.
10. **No universal is substance** (VII.13). A universal is common to many, while the substance of each thing is peculiar to it. *In words:* whatever makes Socrates a being must be his, not shared with Callias. (You reconstruct this argument in P3.)
11. **Substance as cause** (VII.17). Ask why *these* bricks are a house, or why *this* flesh is a man. The answer is the form, which is why a syllable is more than its letters. *In words:* the substance of a thing is what makes its matter into that thing.

VII.13 onward also aims at anyone who makes a universal, a Platonic Form, into a separate substance ([2.1](02-01-the-forms-and-what-goes-wrong-with-them.md)); naming the exact crux with Plato is Boss problem 3's job.

## Map of positions

The *Categories* grid, from steps 1 and 2:

| | **Said of** a subject | **Not said of** a subject |
|---|---|---|
| **Present in** a subject | universal accidents: *knowledge*, *whiteness* | individual accidents: *this* bit of grammatical knowledge, *this* whiteness |
| **Not present in** a subject | secondary substances: *man*, *animal* | primary substances: *this* man, *this* horse |

One subtlety: ch. 5 adds that the **differentia** (*biped*, *rational*) is said of a subject but not present in one, so it sits with the secondary substances, not the accidents.

*Metaphysics* VII's four candidates, from steps 6 to 10:

| Candidate | Why tempting | Verdict in VII |
|---|---|---|
| Substratum as matter | ultimate subject of predication | not separable, not a "this" (VII.3) |
| Essence / form | what definition states; cause of the thing's being (VII.4-6, 17) | substance in the primary sense |
| Universal | what knowledge is of | not substance (VII.13) |
| Genus | a universal of wider scope | not substance, for the same reason |

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean): Socrates on the VII.3 tests.** Divide him as Aristotle divides a bronze statue into bronze, shape, and statue. *Matter:* flesh and bones. *Form:* the soul, which VII.10-11 treats as the form and substance of an animal. *Compound:* Socrates.

- **Matter.** A subject? Yes, the flesh underlies his features. Separable and a "this"? No: flesh as such is just stuff, and as a corpse it is not even flesh except in name. It fails.
- **Compound.** Separable, yes: Socrates exists on his own. A "this", yes. It qualifies, and it is what the *Categories* called primary substance. But it is posterior: it is explained by its form and matter, not the other way round.
- **Form.** It is what makes the flesh *a man*, and it is what the definition "rational animal" states. It is prior to the compound, as the cause is prior to what it causes.

So the *Categories* answer is not refuted; it is re-ranked. The individual remains a substance, but *the substance of* the individual is its form.

**Example 2 (hard): three texts that will not sit together.** Take three claims, each with textual support:

1. Form is primary substance (VII.7, 17).
2. No universal is substance (VII.13).
3. Form is universal, since "definition is of the universal and of the form" (VII.11), and Socrates and Callias share one definition.

Any two entail the denial of the third. Readers have denied each in turn.

- **Deny 3: particular forms.** Socrates' form is his own, numerically distinct from Callias' though exactly alike. Michael Frede and Günther Patzig's commentary on Book VII (1988) is the standard statement. It saves (1) and (2), but it must explain how a definition, which is general, captures an individual's form.
- **Deny 2, or restrict it.** VII.13 rules out a universal *predicated of many individuals*, as man is said of Socrates, being their substance. The species-form is predicated of *matter*, not of the compound, so it escapes the argument. This saves a shared form, but it has to read a qualification into VII.13 that the chapter does not state.
- **Deny that it is one doctrine.** The central books are a set of problems worked through, not a finished system, and the tension is left standing.

What the example teaches is method, not a winner. The contradiction appears only when you fix what "universal" means in (2) and (3): common to many *individuals*, or predicable of many *matters*. Every serious reading turns on that one word, and 8.1 and 8.3 inherit the problem when Aquinas and Scotus ask what individuates.

## Watch out

- **You might think *ousia* is Locke's "something I know not what".** Step 7 *is* that view: the propertyless underlayer. Aristotle states it in VII.3 and rejects it. Aristotelian substance is the most knowable thing there is, because its essence is what definition captures. The bare substratum's live descendants are in [`metaphysics` 2.1](../../metaphysics/lessons/02-01-substance-and-accident.md).
- **You might think a secondary substance is an abstract object.** The species "man" is not a timeless entity outside space that Socrates is related to. By step 3 it exists only as what individual men are. Reading it as a Fregean or Platonic abstractum imports exactly what Aristotle is denying.
- **You might think "present in" means "located in".** Ch. 2 rules that out: it means dependence. A hand is part of Socrates, not present in him; his pallor is present in him and is nowhere in particular.
- **You might think the *Metaphysics* refutes the *Categories*.** Both call the individual a substance. What changes is the question (which things are substances, versus what their substance is), and whether Aristotle changed his mind as well is disputed.

## One-liner

> The *Categories* says the substances are the individuals; *Metaphysics* VII asks what makes an individual a being at all, and answers: its essence, its form, which is not bare matter and not a universal.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢) *(Exegetical.)*** Place each item in a cell of the *Categories* grid (said of? present in?), and say whether ch. 5 counts it as a primary substance, a secondary substance, or neither. One line each.

> (i) Bucephalus. (ii) Horse. (iii) Four-footed, taken as the differentia of horse. (iv) The particular white of Bucephalus's blaze. (v) Colour.

**P2 (🟡) *(Exegetical.)*** Close-read *Metaphysics* VII.1 (Ross):

> "While 'being' has all these senses, obviously that which 'is' primarily is the 'what', which indicates the substance of the thing. ... And so one might even raise the question whether the words 'to walk', 'to be healthy', 'to sit' imply that each of these things is existent ... for none of them is either self-subsistent or capable of being separated from substance, but rather, if anything, it is that which walks or sits or is healthy that is an existent thing. Now these are seen to be more real because there is something definite which underlies them (i.e. the substance or individual), which is implied in such a predicate."

(a) Which words make walking's claim to being a matter of *dependence* rather than grammar? (b) A reader says this chapter simply restates the *Categories*: the individual is the primary being. Which words support that reading, and which words point past it to the inquiry of VII.4-6? 150 words or fewer in all.

**P3 (🔴, optional) *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)*** *Metaphysics* VII.13 (Ross):

> "For it seems impossible that any universal term should be the name of a substance. For firstly the substance of each thing is that which is peculiar to it, which does not belong to anything else; but the universal is common, since that is called universal which is such as to belong to more than one thing. Of which individual then will this be the substance? Either of all or of none; but it cannot be the substance of all. And if it is to be the substance of one, this one will be the others also; for things whose substance is one and whose essence is one are themselves also one."

(a) Reconstruct the argument in numbered premises and a conclusion, in Aristotle's terms. (b) Flag the premise most open to challenge, and say what a defender of the argument would reply. Any verdict; 150 words or fewer for (b).

<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical — strict.)*

**Must hit, strict:**
- (i) Bucephalus: not said of, not present in → **primary substance**.
- (ii) Horse: said of Bucephalus, not present in him → **secondary substance** (species).
- (iii) Four-footed: said of horse and of Bucephalus, **not** present in a subject → with the secondary substances, not an accident (ch. 5 on the differentia). Accept "neither strictly, but ranked with substance" if the cell is right.
- (iv) This white: not said of anything, present in the body → **neither**; an individual accident.
- (v) Colour: said of white (and so of this white), present in bodies → **neither**; a universal accident.

**Wrong turns:** putting (iii) with the accidents because "four-footed" sounds like a feature. Ch. 5 explicitly denies that a differentia is present in a subject. Putting (iv) in the "said of" column because many things are white: *this* white is said of nothing.

**Model answer:** (i) neither/neither: primary substance. (ii) said of, not in: secondary. (iii) said of, not in: ranked with secondary substance. (iv) in, not said of: individual accident. (v) said of and in: universal accident.

---

**P2** *(Exegetical — strict.)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** "none of them is either self-subsistent or capable of being separated from substance". Walking is denied independent being because it cannot exist apart, not because "walks" is a verb. "Something definite which underlies them" gives the positive side.

**Must hit, strict (b):** *For* the restatement reading: "that which walks or sits or is healthy that is an existent thing" and "(i.e. the substance or individual)". The primary being is the individual walker, as in *Categories* 5. *Past it:* "that which 'is' primarily is the 'what'". The primary sense of being is identified with the *what*, the answer to "what is it?", which is the essence that VII.4-6 investigates. The passage holds both marks, the "this" and the "what", together, and VII.3 will test candidates against both.

**Wrong turns:** treating "the 'what'" as the individual. The "what" is what an individual *is*, not which one it is. Answering (a) with "underlies", which is compatible with the grammatical reading that the stripping argument later exposes.

**Model answer:** (a) "None of them is either self-subsistent or capable of being separated from substance": walking lacks being on its own because it cannot exist apart from a walker. (b) The restatement reading has "it is that which walks … that is an existent thing" and "the substance or individual". But the chapter opens by saying what "is" primarily is "the 'what'". That is the essence, not the particular, and it is where VII.4-6 goes next.

---

**P3** *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)*

**Must hit, strict (a):**
- P1. The substance of each thing is peculiar to it and belongs to nothing else.
- P2. A universal is common: it belongs to more than one thing.
- P3. So if a universal were a substance, it would be the substance of all its instances or of none.
- P4. It cannot be the substance of all: that would violate P1.
- P5. If it is the substance of one, then (since things whose substance and essence are one are themselves one) all the instances would be that one.
- P6. But they are many.
- ∴ C. No universal is the substance of anything.
- (The chapter goes on to a second, independent argument: substance is not predicated of a subject, but a universal always is. Credit for noting it; not required.)

**Must hit, any verdict (b):** name one premise and state the challenge precisely. Most natural target: **P1**. Does "peculiar to it" mean *numerically* unshared (then P1 may beg the question against a shared form), or merely *not an accident borrowed from elsewhere*? Alternatively target the scope of "belong to": belonging to many *individuals* versus being predicated of *matter* (Example 2). Then give the defender's reply in Aristotle's terms: for example, that substance is what makes a thing one and this, and a principle shared by many cannot make each one *this*.

**Wrong turns:** reading the argument as denying that universals exist or can be known. It denies only that they are *substances*, and Aristotle holds that knowledge is of universals. Framing (b) as a verdict on Plato's Forms instead of testing a premise.

**Model answer (b), one of several:** P1 carries the weight. If "the substance of each thing is peculiar to it" means that no two things can share it numerically, then it already assumes what the argument concludes, and a defender of a shared species-form will simply deny it. The defender's reply is that P1 is not arbitrary: substance was introduced (VII.1, VII.3) as what makes a thing a separable "this". What makes Socrates *this* cannot also be what makes Callias *that* without making them one, which is exactly P5. Verdict: the argument is strong against anything that would make individuals one, and weaker against a form that is shared in kind but not in number, which is why the particular-form reading exists.

## Flashback

**From Lesson 3.2 (Nature and the four causes):** *(Diagnose (a) · Exegetical (b).)* A student in a seminar says: "Saying a sunflower turns toward the sun *for the sake of* light is poetry. Botany has found the mechanism: a growth hormone collects on the shaded side of the stem and makes that side lengthen, so the head bends. Either a mind is steering the plant or it is blind mechanism, and we now know it's mechanism, so there is no final cause here." (a) Name the two assumptions about causes the student is relying on that *Physics* II rejects, and say where Aristotle rejects each. (b) Which step of Aristotle's II.8 argument does the student's "blind mechanism" actually put pressure on, and why? Three sentences or fewer per part.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):** (i) that a final cause is an intention, so no mind means no end. II.8 denies this: "Art does not deliberate", and purpose can be present in a process with no deliberating agent (premise 6). (ii) that causes compete, so finding the efficient and material story (the hormone, the cells) pushes out the "for the sake of which". II.3 denies this: one fact has several causes at once, which answer different why-questions, as with the man walking for his health.

**Must hit, strict (b):** premise 3, the dichotomy that whatever is not coincidence is for the sake of something. The student's mechanism is regular, so it is not chance by premises 1 and 2. It is offered as a third option, regular but end-less necessity, which is the option premise 3 excludes and the one the rain case (Example 2) exposes.

**Wrong turns:** picking premise 1 or 2 in (b). The student grants that the bending is regular, so the lucky-accident story is not in play. Calling the hormone the formal cause in (a): it is part of the material and efficient story.

**Model answer:** (a) First, the student assumes a final cause needs a mind steering the plant; II.8 answers that "Art does not deliberate", so end-directedness needs no deliberating agent. Second, the student assumes the mechanism competes with the end; II.3 says one thing has several causes at once, answering different why-questions, so the hormone gives the efficient and material causes and leaves "what is the bending for?" open. (b) Premise 3. The student grants the bending is regular, so it is not chance. But "blind mechanism" is exactly the third option premise 3 leaves out: regular necessity with no end, like the objector's rain. Whether Aristotle may exclude it is where the argument turns.
