## Why this matters

The Five Ways are probably the most quoted and least read page in medieval philosophy. Read in place, they are something narrower and more interesting: one article of a textbook question, five compressed routes from things anyone can observe to a first principle, each built from parts this course has already met in Aristotle, Avicenna and Plato. This lesson reads the article as a text: what it says, where each step comes from, and what it does and does not claim to have shown. Whether any Way succeeds is [`philosophy-of-religion`](../../philosophy-of-religion/syllabus.md) 2.3's question, not ours.

## The idea

Question 2 of the *Summa Theologiae* asks three things in order, and the order is the argument.

**Article 1: is "God exists" self-evident?** Aquinas answers with a distinction: self-evident *in itself*, not *to us*. A proposition is self-evident when its predicate is contained in its subject. In God, he holds, existing is what God is (the thesis of [7.2](07-02-aquinas-essence-and-existence.md), proved at q.3 a.4), so in itself the proposition could not be more evident. But we do not grasp God's essence, so to us it is not. This is where he sets Anselm aside ([6.1](06-01-anselm-and-the-proslogion-as-history.md)).

**Article 2: can it be demonstrated?** Yes, but only one way. A demonstration *propter quid* ("on account of which") reasons from a cause to its effect, and that needs the cause's essence. A demonstration *quia* ("that") reasons from an effect to the existence of its cause. Smoke tells you there is fire, not what fire is. Since effects are better known to us, the existence of God can be shown *quia*. Two replies matter. The middle term is the *meaning of the name* "God", not God's essence (ad 2). And such truths are preambles to the articles of faith, not articles (ad 1), a doctrine [`fundamental-theology` 2.2](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/02-02-credibility-and-the-preambles-of-faith.md) treats as teaching.

**Article 3: does God exist?** Now come the Five Ways. Each starts from a different effect: motion, efficient causing, things that come to be and pass away, degrees of perfection, and regular action for an end. Each adds a principle of dependence. Each blocks a regress, or reaches a maximum, or reaches a director. And each ends with a naming step, roughly *and this everyone understands to be God*.

So the Ways are not five definitions of God tested against the world. They are five effects, each run backward to a cause that the word "God" is then attached to. The attributes that make it God in any rich sense come after.

## Source

Aquinas, *Summa Theologiae* I q.2 a.3, the Third Way (English Dominican Province translation, public domain):

> But it is impossible for these always to exist, for that which is possible not to be at some time is not. Therefore, if everything is possible not to be, then at one time there could have been nothing in existence.

"These" are things we find generated and corrupted. Note the translator's "could have been". The Latin is flatter: *aliquando nihil fuit in rebus*, "at some time nothing was". A sentence later the translation itself switches back to *was*. Hold on to that wobble for Example 2.

## The argument

**The method of q.2 a.1-2, in Aquinas's terms.**

1. **A proposition is self-evident when its predicate is included in the essence of its subject.** *In words:* you need only understand the terms to see that it is true.
2. **In "God exists" the predicate is the same as the subject, since God is his own existence.** *In words:* for God, to be God is to be. So the proposition is self-evident in itself.
3. **We do not know God's essence.** *In words:* the terms that would make it obvious are exactly the ones we do not grasp. So it is not self-evident to us.
4. **Demonstration is either through the cause (*propter quid*) or through the effect (*quia*).** *In words:* you can argue down from what explains, or up from what is explained.
5. **From any effect better known to us, the existence of its proper cause can be demonstrated, because the effect depends on the cause.** *In words:* if the effect is there, whatever it needs must be there too.
6. **In a demonstration *quia*, the meaning of the name serves as the middle term, not the essence.** *In words:* you prove that there is *something answering to the name* without knowing what it is.
7. **So God's existence can be demonstrated from effects, and article 3 does so five times.**

**The common shape of a Way.** (i) An observed fact. (ii) A dependence principle: what is so-and-so is so from another. (iii) A stopping principle: no infinite regress in *this kind* of series, or a maximum that causes the rest, or a knower that directs. (iv) A first, a maximum or a director. (v) The naming step, which connects (iv) to the meaning of the name fixed in a.2 ad 2.

**Two kinds of series (I q.46 a.2 ad 7).** Step (iii) of the first three Ways denies an infinite regress. Elsewhere Aquinas says which regress is impossible. Causes *per se* ordered are ones where each intermediate causes only as it is caused at that moment: a stone moved by a stick moved by a hand. Such a series, he says, cannot go to infinity. Causes *per accidens* ordered are different. Their multiplication is incidental to the causing, as when "an artificer acts by means of many hammers accidentally, because one after the other may be broken". Such a series, he says, can go to infinity. A man begets a son as a man, not as someone's son, so an infinite line of begetters is not impossible. Whether the distinction marks something real is [`metaphysics` 4.4](../../metaphysics/lessons/04-04-ordered-causal-series.md)'s question.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean): the Fifth Way.** Run the common shape.

(i) *Fact.* Natural bodies without knowledge act for an end. The evidence Aquinas gives is regularity: they act always or nearly always in the same way, reaching what is best. That is Aristotle's datum in *Physics* II.8 ([3.2](03-02-nature-and-the-four-causes.md)): what happens always or for the most part is not the work of chance. (ii) *So not by chance but by intention.* (iii) *Dependence principle.* What lacks knowledge cannot move toward an end unless something with knowledge and intelligence directs it, as an archer directs an arrow. (iv) *So some intelligent being directs all natural things to their end.* (v) The naming step.

The text rules out two readings. It is not a design argument from intricate contrivance, like William Paley's watch (1802). The datum is ordinary regularity: fire heats and acorns become oaks. It is not biological complexity. And step (iii) is exactly where Aquinas goes past Aristotle, for whom a final cause needs no mind at all. Whether Darwinian explanation touches a Way built on regularity is contested, and belongs to [`philosophy-of-religion`](../../philosophy-of-religion/syllabus.md). John Damascene's argument from the world's order (*On the Orthodox Faith* I.3) is often named as a likely influence.

**Example 2 (hard): the Third Way's disputed step.** Set out the first half:

1. We find things that are generated and corrupted, so they are possible to be and not to be.
2. What is possible not to be at some time is not.
3. So if everything is possible not to be, at some time nothing was.
4. If at some time nothing was, nothing would exist now, since what does not exist begins to exist only through something already existing.
5. Something exists now. So not everything is possible not to be: something is necessary.

The second half then asks whether that necessary thing has its necessity from another. It blocks that regress by appeal to what was shown for efficient causes and concludes to a being necessary of itself. "Necessary" here means *without potency to not-be*, which is incorruptible. It is not modern logical necessity. Aquinas allows *caused* necessary beings, which is why the second half exists at all. The pairing of possible and necessary, and necessity from another versus of itself, is Avicenna's ([6.2](06-02-avicenna-essence-existence-and-the-necessary-existent.md)).

Now look at step 3. Read as logic, it moves from *each* thing's failing to exist at some time to a time at which *all* things fail to exist. That is the quantifier shift. Everyone is asleep at some time, but it does not follow that at some time everyone is asleep. Anthony Kenny's *The Five Ways* (1969) presses this and related difficulties hard. The step is standardly called the weakest in the article.

Readers who want to be charitable have several routes, and none is forced on the text:

- **Infinite time.** On the Aristotelian principle that a genuine possibility is realized in infinite time, a past without beginning would have realized the possibility of total non-being. Maimonides's version of this argument in *Guide* II.1 is generally read as taking that route explicitly. On this reading the Third Way *uses* the hypothesis of an eternal world rather than denying it.
- **Restricted domain.** "Everything" ranges only over things generated from other corruptible things. Thomist readers such as John Knasas have defended readings in this broad family.
- **The Latin, not the translation.** The Dominican "could have been" makes the step a claim about possibility, which is weaker and would not support step 4. The Latin *fuit* ("was") is the reading step 4 needs.

The exegetical point: the step's validity depends on a premise the article does not state, and each charitable reading supplies a different one.

## Watch out

- **You might think *motus* means locomotion, so that inertia refutes the First Way.** Aquinas defines motion as the reduction of something from potency to act, which covers heating, growing and learning. Whether Newtonian inertia bears on that account is a real dispute, but it is not settled by the word ([`mechanics-refresher` 1.2](../../mechanics-refresher/lessons/01-02-newtons-laws.md); soundness in [`philosophy-of-religion`](../../philosophy-of-religion/syllabus.md)).
- **You might think the naming step claims to have proved the God of Christian faith.** It connects a first mover, first cause and so on to the *meaning of the name* (a.2 ad 2). Questions 3 onward argue for simplicity, perfection, infinity, immutability, eternity and unity, and so for the claim that the five conclusions pick out one being. Treating a.3 as the finished case is reading a first step as the whole argument.
- **You might think the no-regress premises deny an infinite past.** Read with q.46 a.2 ad 7, the regresses Aquinas rejects are *per se* series. He holds elsewhere that a beginning of the world cannot be demonstrated.
- **You might think this is Aquinas's full statement.** The *Summa* is a textbook for beginners; the argument from motion runs for pages in *Summa contra Gentiles* I.13.

## One-liner

> Five observed effects, five dependence principles, five stopping points and one borrowed name: the Five Ways prove *that* something answers to "God" from what is better known to us, and leave *what* it is to the questions that follow.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢) *(Exegetical.)* Close reading.** The Fourth Way first says that more and less are said of things as they resemble, in different ways, something that is the maximum. It then adds:

> Now the maximum in any genus is the cause of all in that genus; as fire, which is the maximum heat, is the cause of all hot things.

Two readings of the maximum the Way reaches. **(R1)** It is a *standard of comparison*: "more" and "less" need a top of the scale to be measured against. **(R2)** It is a *cause* of the perfection in whatever has it to a lesser degree. (a) Which reading does the quoted sentence require, and which words decide it? (b) Which reading does the earlier "resemble" sentence, taken alone, fit better? (c) What does the fire example contribute, and what anachronism should a modern reader avoid in weighing it? 150 words or fewer.

**P2 (🟡) *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)* Reconstruct.** Using the First Way in ST I q.2 a.3 (English Dominican Province, about 250 words): (a) reconstruct it in numbered premises in Aquinas's terms, marking which premise comes from the act/potency analysis of [3.3](03-03-change-privation-potency-and-act.md) and which is the no-regress premise. (b) Flag the premise you judge weakest. State the strongest objection a medieval or early modern reader pressed against it, and the reply Aquinas's own text or the *per se* distinction makes available. You are graded on the moves, not on whether the premise survives. 150 words or fewer for (b).

**P3 (🔴, optional) *(Exegetical.)* Find the crux.** Some thirteenth-century masters, Bonaventure the best known, held that reason *can* demonstrate that the world began. One of their arguments runs: if the world had no beginning, an infinite number of days would have passed before today, and an infinite cannot be passed through. Aquinas states this argument as objection 6 of I q.46 a.2 and denies it is a demonstration. Both sides agree that a *per se* series cannot regress infinitely. Name the single premise one side affirms and the other denies, say how Aquinas's reply to objection 6 handles it, and say what consideration could move a reader toward either side. 150 words or fewer.

<details>
<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical — strict.)*

**Must hit, strict:**
- (a) **R2.** The decisive words are "is the cause of all in that genus". A yardstick measures but does not cause. The Way's conclusion, a cause of being, goodness and every perfection in all beings, confirms that the maximum is reached as a cause.
- (b) The "resemble" sentence alone fits **R1**, or a participation reading short of efficient causation. Resembling a maximum is a relation of comparison, in the style of Plato's things that fall short of the Form ([2.1](02-01-the-forms-and-what-goes-wrong-with-them.md)). So the Way has two steps: gradation → a maximum, then maximum → cause. The second step comes from Aristotle, *Metaphysics* II.1, where the same fire example appears.
- (c) Fire illustrates the causal principle from Aristotelian physics, where fire is the hot element par excellence and the source of heat in other things. It is an illustration, not a premise. Neither "physics has no hottest thing, so the Way fails" nor "fire proves the principle" reads it correctly.

**Wrong turns:** answering R1 because the sentence begins with "maximum", which misses the verb "is the cause"; treating the fire example as the argument's evidence. (Extra credit, not required: being and goodness are not genera for Aristotle, so "in any genus" is being stretched to the transcendentals.)

**Model answer:** (a) R2: "is the cause of all in that genus" makes the maximum a cause, not a measure, and the conclusion names it the cause of every perfection. (b) Taken alone, "resemble … the maximum" is comparative, a Platonic falling-short that fits R1. The Way needs a second, Aristotelian step (*Metaphysics* II.1) from maximum to cause. (c) Fire illustrates that step from Aristotle's physics of the elements. Rejecting or accepting the Way because of modern thermodynamics mistakes the example for the premise.

---

**P2** *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)*

**Must hit, strict (a):**
- P1. Some things are in motion (evident to sense).
- P2. Motion is the reduction of something from potency to act. **[3.3]**
- P3. Nothing is reduced from potency to act except by something in act (fire and wood). **[3.3]**
- P4. Nothing is in act and potency in the same respect at once.
- C1. So nothing moves itself in the same respect: whatever is moved is moved by another.
- P5. If the mover is itself moved, it is moved by another, and so on.
- P6. This cannot go to infinity, because then there would be no first mover and so no other mover, since later movers move only as moved by the first (staff and hand). **[no-regress]**
- C. So there is a first mover, moved by no other, and this is understood to be God.

**Must hit, any verdict (b):**
- One premise, named by number.
- An objection actually pressed historically, stated fairly. Examples: Scotus is generally reported to have denied that "whatever is moved is moved by another" holds universally, allowing self-motion (e.g. the will). Or: after Newton, uniform motion needs no mover. Or: why can't the series of movers be infinite?
- The reply the text makes available. Self-movers move one part by another part, so not in the same respect. *Motus* is any passage from potency to act. The staff-and-hand illustration marks P6's series as *per se* (q.46 a.2 ad 7).
- A one-line verdict, either way.

**Wrong turns:** reconstructing the Second Way's efficient causes instead of movers; putting "the world began" among the premises; in (b), asserting that the First Way is sound or unsound without engaging the premise.

**Model answer (b), one of several:** The weakest is C1, drawn from P4. Scotus is reported to have held that some things, the will above all, move themselves, so "moved by another" is not universal. The reply the text allows is that P4 forbids only act and potency *in the same respect*. An animal moves one part by another, which fits the principle. The will is harder: Aquinas's own account elsewhere has the will moved by its object and ultimately by God, which a critic may say assumes what is at issue. Verdict: the reply covers bodies cleanly, and whether it covers the will depends on a theory of the will the article does not give.

---

**P3** *(Exegetical — strict on the single premise.)*

**Must hit, strict:**
- The crux: *whether a past succession with no first member would amount to having passed through an infinite*. The Bonaventurean side affirms it and so concludes a beginning is demonstrable. Aquinas denies it.
- Aquinas's reply to obj. 6: passing through is always from one term to another. From any past day you choose, only finitely many days lie between it and today, so no infinite is ever passed through. The objection pictures an infinite span between two end points.
- Not the crux: whether *per se* regresses are impossible (both agree), or whether the world in fact began (both hold it did, Aquinas by faith).
- What could move a reader: toward Aquinas, the observation that every traversed segment is finite and there is no first term to start from. Toward the other side, the observation that the *whole* past has in fact elapsed, which looks like a completed infinite. Aquinas's own unease about an actual infinity of souls (obj. 8) shows the point is not idle. The modern kalam debate is [`philosophy-of-religion`](../../philosophy-of-religion/syllabus.md) 2.5.

**Wrong turns:** naming "whether infinite regress is possible" without the *per se*/*per accidens* qualification; attributing to Aquinas the view that the world is eternal.

**Model answer:** The disputed premise is that a past without a first day would be an infinite already passed through. The Bonaventurean affirms it and infers that reason can show the world began. Aquinas denies it (obj. 6): any passage runs between two terms, and from any past day to today the days are finite. Nothing infinite is traversed, because there is no first term for a traversal to start from. Both agree an essentially ordered series cannot be infinite, so that is not what divides them. A reader drawn by the thought that the whole past has been completed will side with Bonaventure. A reader who thinks a whole with no first member need never have been "crossed" will side with Aquinas.

</details>

## Flashback

**From Lesson 7.1 (Faith, reason and the Paris crisis):** *(Exegetical — sort the cases.)* (a) Place each claim in one of Aquinas's four bins, with a one-line reason: **A** true and demonstrable by natural reason; **B** true, held by faith, and not demonstrable; **C** false, and refutable by philosophical argument; **D** false, but not demonstrably false. (i) God exists. (ii) God is three persons in one nature. (iii) The world had a beginning in time. (iv) The world has always existed. (v) One intellect is shared by all human beings. (b) If (i) can be demonstrated, why does Aquinas think it fitting that it also be proposed for belief? Two sentences or fewer.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):**
- (i) **A.** Sensible effects show *that* God is, though not *what* God is (*Summa contra Gentiles* I.3).
- (ii) **B.** Aquinas's own example of a truth wholly beyond reason's reach (I.3).
- (iii) **B.** "By faith alone do we hold" it, and no demonstration proves it (ST I q.46 a.2).
- (iv) **D.** False for Aquinas, but since a beginning cannot be demonstrated, neither can the world's always having existed be refuted; Aristotle's arguments for it are only relatively demonstrative (q.46 a.1).
- (v) **C.** Here reason can decide, and in *De unitate* Aquinas argues against it on Aristotle's own terms: *hic homo intelligit*.

**Must hit, strict (b):** I.4: by reason alone few would reach even the demonstrable truths, and they would reach them late in life and with errors mixed in, so faith gives everyone certainty at once.

**Wrong turns:** putting (iii) in A because it is Christian teaching, or (iv) in C because Aquinas rejects it. The whole point of q.46 is that neither side of the eternity question is demonstrated. Putting (v) in D by treating both of Aquinas's test cases alike, when they came out opposite ways.

**Model answer:** (a) (i) A: reason reaches it from effects. (ii) B: beyond reason altogether. (iii) B: held by faith alone, not demonstrable. (iv) D: denied on faith, but no demonstration refutes it. (v) C: Aquinas refutes it from Aristotle's own principles. (b) Reason alone would bring few people to it, late in life and mixed with error. Proposed for belief, it is held with certainty by everyone at once.

</details>
