# Ancient & Medieval Philosophy — Syllabus

> Philosophy · Tier 1 · ~29 lessons · Prereqs: [`philosophical-method`](../philosophical-method/syllabus.md) · Roadmap id: `ancient-medieval-philosophy`

## Goal

This course is the history of Western philosophy from Parmenides to Ockham, read as one long argument about being, change, knowledge and God. Its thread runs from Parmenides' denial of change, through Plato's Forms and Aristotle's substance, act and potency, into the late-antique schools and Augustine, then through the Arabic and Jewish philosophers who reshaped Aristotle for the Latin West, and on to Aquinas, Scotus and Ockham, whose quarrel over universals ends the medieval synthesis. You will finish able to close-read a passage from a public-domain translation and say which words decide between two readings, reconstruct a thinker's central argument in their own terms, and name the premise on which two schools part ways, above all realism against nominalism. It also lays the textual groundwork `thomistic-synthesis` builds on: the four causes, act and potency, substance, and essence and existence, read in Aristotle, Avicenna and Aquinas, while [`metaphysics`](../metaphysics/syllabus.md) tests the same positions against their contemporary rivals. The course deliberately skips the ethics of Plato, Aristotle and Aquinas (owned by [`ethics`](../ethics/syllabus.md)), their political thought (owned by [`history-of-political-thought`](../history-of-political-thought/syllabus.md)), and the Fathers as theologians (owned by [`patristics`](../patristics/syllabus.md)). It also skips any verdict on whether the ontological argument or the Five Ways succeed (owned by [`philosophy-of-religion`](../philosophy-of-religion/syllabus.md)), Aristotle's biology, rhetoric and poetics, Middle Platonism, Proclus and Pseudo-Dionysius, Eriugena, Bonaventure, Albert the Great, Buridan, Byzantine philosophy, and any philosophy outside the line that fed the Latin West.

## How practice works in this course

Problems follow [HUMANITIES-BUILD-BRIEF.md](../../HUMANITIES-BUILD-BRIEF.md), verdict-neutral. Parmenides and Democritus, Plato and Aristotle, the Stoa and the Garden, Scotus and Ockham are each presented at the strength their best readers would recognize, and no rubric rewards siding with any of them. Three archetypes carry the course:

- **Close reading** is the workhorse. It uses a public-domain passage of 150 words or fewer and asks which interpretation its words support. It is graded strictly as exegesis.
- **Reconstruct** asks for a thinker's argument in numbered premises, in *their* terms, with the weakest premise flagged. For example, Aristotle's final cause is not a conscious purpose, and Avicenna's "necessary existent" is not a modern modal-logic necessary being.
- **Find the crux** takes two schools answering one question (Parmenides and the atomists on what-is-not, Plato and Aristotle on where form is, Averroes and Maimonides on the eternity of the world, Scotus and Ockham on common natures) and asks for the single premise one of them must deny.

**Apply to a hard case** runs Aristotle's causes or act and potency on a change written for the problem. **Diagnose** gives an invented op-ed or classroom argument and asks which ancient or medieval position it is borrowing. Invented material only; no invented words are ever attributed to a real person. Syllogism-validity checks in 3.1 are the course's only **formal** parts. **Evaluative** parts appear in every boss problem and many lessons. They are graded on the moves, never the verdict, and they stay historical-dialectical: whether a reply answers an objection on terms both sides accepted, or whether a later thinker read an earlier one faithfully. Whether a theistic argument is sound is left to [`philosophy-of-religion`](../philosophy-of-religion/syllabus.md). A recurring exegetical discipline is **anachronism**: *ousia* is not "substance" in Locke's sense, Plato's *episteme* is not modern justified true belief, and Scotus's univocity is a thesis about concepts, not a claim that God and creatures share a nature.

**Texts.** Close-reading passages come only from public-domain translations: the Presocratic fragments in Burnet's *Early Greek Philosophy*; Plato (Jowett); Aristotle in Ross's *Metaphysics* (1908), Edghill's *Categories* (1928), Hardie and Gaye's *Physics* (1930) and Hicks's *De Anima* (1907); Epicurus via Hicks's Diogenes Laertius (1925); Lucretius (Munro); Cicero's *Academica* and *On Fate* (Yonge); Plotinus (MacKenna); Augustine's *Confessions* and *City of God* in the *Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers*; Boethius's *Consolation of Philosophy* (James); Anselm's *Proslogion*, Gaunilo's reply and Anselm's answer (Deane); Maimonides's *Guide for the Perplexed* (Friedländer); and Aquinas's *Summa Theologiae* and *Summa contra Gentiles* (English Dominican Province). Sextus Empiricus, Avicenna, Averroes, Scotus and Ockham have no English translation this course can rely on being public domain. Lessons paraphrase and cite them, never use them for close reading, and practise them through reconstruct, find the crux and diagnose instead. Modern scholarship (Barnes, Vlastos, Kenny, Adamson) is paraphrased and cited.

## Dangerous Checklist

When you finish, you can:

- [ ] Reconstruct Parmenides' argument against coming-to-be and say which premise Zeno defends, the atomists deny, and Aristotle dissolves with potency
- [ ] Explain why Plato posited Forms, map the Divided Line onto the Cave, and reconstruct the Third Man regress with the premises it needs
- [ ] Apply Aristotle's four causes and the act/potency distinction to any change, and tell substantial from accidental change
- [ ] Say what substance is in Aristotle's *Categories* and in *Metaphysics* VII, and why he denies that universals are substances
- [ ] Reconstruct Aristotle's argument from eternal motion to a mover whose essence is actuality, and state his definition of the soul
- [ ] Say how Epicureans, Stoics and Skeptics each tied their physics or epistemology to a tranquil life, and name the crux between the Epicurean swerve and Stoic compatibilism
- [ ] Trace a Neoplatonic idea (evil as privation, the Forms in a divine mind, the ascent of the soul) from Plotinus into Augustine and say what Augustine changed
- [ ] State Augustine's account of time and Boethius's solution to foreknowledge and freedom, including the two kinds of necessity
- [ ] Reconstruct Anselm's *Proslogion* argument and the exchange with Gaunilo as history, and tell Anselm's route to God from Avicenna's and Aquinas's
- [ ] Explain Avicenna's distinction of essence and existence, what Averroes and Maimonides each held about philosophy, scripture and the eternity of the world, and how Aquinas turned existence into the act of being
- [ ] Read the Five Ways as a text: reconstruct each, name its source in earlier philosophy, and say what the article does and does not claim
- [ ] Place any position on universals (Boethius, Abelard, Aquinas, Scotus, Ockham) on the realism-nominalism map and name the premise its nearest rival denies

## Modules

### Module 1: The Presocratics and the Socratic turn

Parmenides argues that change is impossible, and nearly everything after him is a reply. Then Socrates turns philosophy from nature to definitions and the good life.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1.1 | Heraclitus and Parmenides | Reconstruct the first great argument in Western philosophy and the view it opposed | the Milesians' *arche* in brief; fragments and doxography (Aristotle, Simplicius, Diels-Kranz numbers) as sources; Heraclitus's flux, unity of opposites and *logos*; Parmenides' two ways, what-is cannot not be, the denial of coming-to-be, change and plurality |
| 1.2 | Zeno and the atomists: two answers to Parmenides | Say how Zeno defends Parmenides by reductio and how the atomists escape him | Zeno's dichotomy, Achilles and arrow; whether the convergent-series reply ([`calc-refresher`](../calc-refresher/syllabus.md) 3.1) answers Zeno's question; Empedocles and Anaxagoras in brief; Leucippus and Democritus: atoms and void, what-is-not exists no less than what-is, qualities "by convention" |
| 1.3 | The Sophists and Socrates | Reconstruct Protagoras's relativism and its refutation, and describe the Socratic method | Protagoras's man-the-measure and the self-refutation reply in the *Theaetetus*; *nomos* vs *physis*; the elenchus and the priority of definition; Socratic ignorance (*Apology*); virtue as knowledge and the denial of *akrasia*; the Socratic problem (Plato, Xenophon, Aristophanes as sources) |

**Boss problem 1:** Take the long fragment of Parmenides on what-is (DK B8, Burnet's translation) and Aristotle's report of the atomists in *Metaphysics* I.4 (Ross), where Leucippus and Democritus call the full and the empty being and non-being. (a) Reconstruct Parmenides' argument that what-is is ungenerated in numbered premises, and close-read the fragment to show which words carry the premise that what-is-not cannot be thought or spoken of. (b) Close-read the Aristotle passage and name the crux: the premise of (a) that the atomists deny, and what they gain and lose by denying it. (c) Diagnose an invented classroom argument that "there is no truth, only what is true for you" as Protagorean, and state the self-refutation reply from 1.3 as Plato frames it. (d) In 300-500 words, assess whether the atomist reply answers Parmenides or changes the subject, any verdict, if (a)'s premises are used. Parts (a)-(c) are exegetical; (d) is evaluative.

### Module 2: Plato

Plato's answer to Heraclitus and Parmenides together: a changing world of appearances that shares in unchanging Forms, known by a soul that is itself akin to them.

|---|---|---|---|

| 2.2 | Knowledge: recollection, the Line and the Cave | Explain how Plato thinks the Forms are known, and map the Line onto the Cave | Meno's paradox of inquiry; the slave-boy and recollection; the Sun, the Divided Line (*eikasia*, *pistis*, *dianoia*, *noesis*); the Cave; the Form of the Good; *episteme* vs modern justified true belief ([`philosophical-method`](../philosophical-method/syllabus.md) 3.1) |
| 2.3 | The soul: its parts and its immortality | Reconstruct the *Phaedo*'s arguments for immortality and the *Republic*'s argument for a divided soul | the cyclical, recollection and affinity arguments; Simmias's harmony objection; the final argument from the Form of Life; the principle of opposites and the three parts (*Republic* IV), with the city-soul analogy cited from [`history-of-political-thought`](../history-of-political-thought/syllabus.md) 1.1 |

**Boss problem 2:** Take the Divided Line at the end of *Republic* VI, the opening of the Cave in Book VII, and the Third Man passage of the *Parmenides* (all Jowett). (a) Map the four segments of the Line and their states of mind onto the stages of the Cave, and close-read the Book VII passage where Socrates tells Glaucon how to apply the image: which correspondences does the text itself draw, and which are readers' additions? (c) Name the crux: which premise a defender of the Forms should deny, and what denying it costs the reasons for positing Forms in 2.1. (d) In 300-500 words, assess whether the slave-boy episode in the *Meno* supports the existence of Forms or only the claim that some knowledge is not learned from experience, any verdict, if the text of the episode is used. Parts (a)-(c) are exegetical; (d) is evaluative.

### Module 3: Aristotle: logic, nature, substance and the first mover

The system the Middle Ages inherited. It gives an answer to Parmenides that keeps change real, puts form back inside things, and runs from the logic of predication to a first mover that is pure actuality. This is the module `thomistic-synthesis` depends on most.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3.1 | Logic: predication, the categories and the syllogism | Sort predicates into categories and test a syllogism for validity | categorical propositions and the square of opposition; the ten categories as highest kinds of predicate; first-figure syllogisms (Barbara, Celarent), **formal**; demonstration from first principles (*Posterior Analytics*); the principle of non-contradiction and its elenctic defence (*Metaphysics* IV) |
| 3.2 | Nature and the four causes | Give all four causes of a natural thing and an artefact, and reconstruct the argument for final causes | nature as an inner principle of motion and rest (*Physics* II.1); material, formal, efficient and final causes (II.3); chance and spontaneity; the rain example and the reply to Empedocles (II.8); a final cause is not a conscious purpose |
| 3.3 | Change: privation, potency and act | Explain how Aristotle makes change possible against Parmenides | the three principles of change (matter, form, privation; *Physics* I.7-8); coming-to-be from what is not *per accidens*; potency and actuality; motion as the actuality of the potential as such (III.1); substantial vs accidental change; prime matter and whether Aristotle held it; the live debate over real potency cited to [`metaphysics`](../metaphysics/syllabus.md) 2.2 |
| 3.4 | Substance and essence | Say what substance is in the *Categories* and in *Metaphysics* VII, and why universals are not substances | primary and secondary substance, said-of and present-in; "what is being?" becomes "what is substance?" (*Metaphysics* VII.1); essence (*to ti en einai*) and definition; form as primary substance and the matter-form compound; universals are not substances (VII.13) and the critique of separate Forms; substance and hylomorphism as live theories cited to [`metaphysics`](../metaphysics/syllabus.md) 2.1 and 2.3 |
| 3.5 | Soul and intellect | State Aristotle's definition of soul and locate the chapter the Middle Ages fought over | soul as the first actuality of a natural body that has life potentially (*De Anima* II.1); nutritive, sensitive and rational powers; perception as receiving form without matter; the intellect that becomes all things and the intellect that makes all things (III.5), and why that chapter became the most disputed in the tradition |
| 3.6 | The unmoved mover | Reconstruct the argument from eternal motion to a substance whose essence is actuality | the priority of actuality over potency (*Metaphysics* IX.8); the argument of XII.6; moving as an object of love (XII.7); thought thinking itself (XII.9); what the first mover does not do (create, exercise providence) and the dispute over whether it knows the world |

**Boss problem 3:** Take *Metaphysics* XII.6-7 (Ross), where Aristotle argues from the eternity of motion to a principle whose very essence is actuality and which moves by being loved. (a) Reconstruct the argument in numbered premises, and mark where it relies on the four causes (3.2), the act/potency distinction (3.3) and the doctrine of substance (3.4). (b) Close-read the passage on how the first mover moves: which words make it a final rather than an efficient cause, and why does Aristotle need that? (c) Apply the four causes and the act/potency distinction to a change written for the problem, such as a cutting taken from a vine that grows into a new plant. Say whether the change is substantial or accidental, and what is in potency to what. (d) Name the crux between Aristotle and Plato on where form exists, using *Metaphysics* VII.13 and the Third Man (2.1). (e) In 300-500 words, assess whether the argument of (a) still reaches a first mover if the premise that motion is eternal is dropped, any verdict, if the role of that premise in (a) is used. Parts (a)-(d) are exegetical; (e) is evaluative.

### Module 4: The Hellenistic schools and Plotinus

After Aristotle, philosophy becomes a way of life. Three schools compete to offer tranquility through physics or through suspending judgment, until Plotinus rebuilds Plato as a single hierarchy flowing from the One.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4.1 | Epicurus: atoms, the swerve, and death | Reconstruct the Epicurean case that physics frees us from fear | atomism as Democritus revised; the swerve and freedom (Lucretius II); all perceptions are true; "death is nothing to us" (*Letter to Menoeceus*); Lucretius's symmetry argument; tranquility (*ataraxia*) and static pleasure, with hedonism cited from [`ethics`](../ethics/syllabus.md) 1.2 |
| 4.2 | The Stoics: *logos*, fate and virtue | Explain how Stoic physics, fate and ethics fit into one system | corporealism and the active and passive principles; *pneuma* and the rational cosmos; fate and Chrysippus's cylinder (compatibilism); propositional logic in brief ([`mathematical-logic`](../mathematical-logic/syllabus.md) for the modern system); virtue as the only good and the preferred indifferents; the passions as false judgments |
| 4.3 | The Skeptics | Reconstruct the Academic attack on the Stoic criterion and the Pyrrhonian way of suspending judgment | the Stoic cognitive (*kataleptic*) impression; Arcesilaus and Carneades, the persuasive impression; Pyrrhonism as Sextus reports it: equipollence, suspension (*epoche*), Agrippa's five modes; the objection that the skeptic cannot act, and the reply; skepticism as a live problem cited to [`epistemology`](../epistemology/syllabus.md) |
| 4.4 | Plotinus: the One, Intellect and Soul | Explain the Neoplatonic hierarchy and what it did to Plato's Forms and to evil | the One beyond being; procession and return; Intellect as the Forms thinking themselves; Soul and nature; matter as privation and as evil (*Enneads* I.8); the ascent of the soul |

**Boss problem 4:** Take the passage on death in Epicurus's *Letter to Menoeceus* (Hicks's Diogenes Laertius) and Lucretius's symmetry argument in *On the Nature of Things* III (Munro). (a) Reconstruct Epicurus's argument that death is nothing to us, and close-read Lucretius: what premise does the symmetry argument add that Epicurus's argument lacks? (b) Name the crux between the Epicurean swerve and Chrysippus's cylinder analogy (as Cicero reports it in *On Fate*): the single premise about necessity and responsibility one side must deny. (c) A Stoic claims a cognitive impression that it is day. Run the Academic reply from 4.3 and then the Pyrrhonian one, and say where they differ in what they conclude. (d) In 300-500 words, assess whether Epicurean ethics can stand without Epicurean atomism, any verdict, if the role the physics plays in (a) is used. Parts (a)-(c) are exegetical; (d) is evaluative.

### Module 5: Augustine and Boethius

Two Latin Christians read the Platonists and reshape them. Augustine answers the skeptics, makes evil a privation and the will a power of its own, and asks what time is. Boethius, awaiting execution, gives the Middle Ages its account of eternity.

|---|---|---|---|
| 5.1 | Augustine: certainty and illumination | Reconstruct Augustine's answer to the skeptics and his account of how the mind knows unchanging truth | the "books of the Platonists" (*Confessions* VII) as the bridge from Plotinus; "if I am mistaken, I exist" (*City of God* XI.26) and its later echo in Descartes ([`modern-philosophy`](../modern-philosophy/syllabus.md)); interior truth; divine illumination and the inner teacher (*On the Teacher*); what illumination does and does not replace |
| 5.2 | Augustine: evil and the will | Explain how Augustine makes evil a privation and moral evil a work of the will | Manichaean dualism; evil as privation of good (*Confessions* VII); the free-will account of moral evil (*On Free Choice of the Will*); the divided will (*Confessions* VIII); whether Augustine invented the will as a faculty. Grace and Pelagius are cited to [`patristics`](../patristics/syllabus.md), and the problem of evil as a live problem to [`philosophy-of-religion`](../philosophy-of-religion/syllabus.md) |
| 5.3 | Time, eternity and foreknowledge: Augustine and Boethius | State Augustine's account of time and Boethius's solution to foreknowledge and freedom in their own terms | memory (*Confessions* X) and "what then is time?" (XI); past, present and future as memory, attention and expectation; time as a stretching of the soul; eternity as not a very long time; Boethius's definition of eternity (*Consolation* V.6); simple vs conditional necessity as Boethius draws it, with the modern modal analysis, Ockhamism and Molinism cited to [`philosophy-of-religion`](../philosophy-of-religion/syllabus.md) 1.3 |

**Boss problem 5:** Take *Enneads* I.8 on matter and evil (MacKenna), *Confessions* VII.12 on evil as no substance (*Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers*), *Confessions* XI, and the definition of eternity in *Consolation of Philosophy* V.6 (James). (a) Close-read Plotinus and Augustine: where does each locate evil, and which words show that Augustine refuses a step Plotinus takes about matter? (b) Reconstruct Augustine's argument in *Confessions* XI that the past is no longer, the future is not yet and the present has no length, and close-read the passage where he concludes that time is a stretching of the mind itself. (c) Explain how Boethius's eternity differs from endless duration, why V.6 denies that even a world without beginning or end would be eternal in that sense, and which passage of *Confessions* XI anticipates the contrast. (d) In 300-500 words, assess whether Augustine's privation account of evil is Plotinus's theory with matter redeemed or a different theory, any verdict, if (a) and the Manichaean position Augustine was answering (5.2) are both used. Parts (a)-(c) are exegetical; (d) is evaluative.

### Module 6: Anselm, and the Arabic and Jewish philosophers

In the Latin West a monk seeks proof by reason alone. Meanwhile philosophers writing in Arabic rebuild Aristotle around essence and existence, necessity and the eternity of the world, and set the agenda the Latin universities will inherit.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 6.1 | Anselm and the *Proslogion* argument as history | Say what Anselm set out to do, how his argument runs, and how it was received from Gaunilo to Aquinas | *Monologion* vs *Proslogion* method; the preface's search for a single argument; "I believe in order to understand" (*Proslogion* 1); the argument of chapters 2-3 in outline, with the full reconstruction and the classic replies assessed in [`philosophy-of-religion`](../philosophy-of-religion/syllabus.md) 2.1; Gaunilo's lost island and Anselm's answer as an exchange between monks; Aquinas's rejection (ST I q.2 a.1 ad 2) |
| 6.2 | Avicenna: essence, existence and the necessary existent | Explain Avicenna's distinction and his route from contingent things to a necessary existent | the Arabic Aristotle (al-Kindi, al-Farabi) in brief; essence considered in itself ("horseness is just horseness"); existence as something that comes to an essence, and the dispute over how to read that; possible vs necessary existent; the proof from contingency; the flying man |
| 6.3 | Averroes and Maimonides: philosophy and revealed law | Say what each held about philosophy, scripture and whether the eternity of the world can be demonstrated | Averroes as "the Commentator"; the *Decisive Treatise*: philosophy commanded by the Law, demonstrative, dialectical and rhetorical audiences, interpreting scripture when it conflicts with demonstration; Maimonides's *Guide for the Perplexed*: negative attributes, creation and eternity both undemonstrated, why he holds creation, and his statement that a demonstration of eternity would change how he reads Genesis |

**Boss problem 6:** Take the preface and chapter 1 of the *Proslogion* (Deane) and ST I q.2 a.1 (English Dominican Province). (a) Close-read the preface and the end of chapter 1: what does Anselm say he is looking for, and for whom? Which words tell against reading the argument as addressed to an unbeliever who must be argued into faith, and which words tell for it? (b) Close-read Aquinas's objection 2 and his reply to it: what does Aquinas grant about the meaning of the name "God", and what does he deny follows from it? (c) Name the crux between Anselm's route to God and Avicenna's: the premise about how existence relates to what a thing is that separates reasoning from a concept from reasoning from contingent existents. (d) Using the *Guide* (Friedländer) on creation and the paraphrased *Decisive Treatise*, state what Maimonides and Averroes each held about whether the eternity of the world is demonstrated, and what each would do with scripture if it were. (e) In 300-500 words, assess whether Maimonides's or Averroes's way of relating demonstration to scripture costs its own tradition less, any verdict, if both are stated as their authors state them. Parts (a)-(d) are exegetical; (e) is evaluative.

### Module 7: Aquinas: a survey

Aquinas takes Aristotle, Avicenna and Augustine into one system. This course reads three of its load-bearing parts; `thomistic-synthesis` reads the whole in depth.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 7.1 | Faith, reason and the Paris crisis | Explain Aquinas's account of what reason can reach and how he fought the radical Aristotelians | two kinds of truth about God (*Summa contra Gentiles* I.3) that cannot conflict (I.7), with the doctrine of preambles cited from [`fundamental-theology`](../fundamental-theology/syllabus.md) 2.2; Averroes's unity of the intellect and Aquinas's reply (*On the Unity of the Intellect*, 1270); the eternity of the world as neither demonstrable nor refutable (ST I q.46 a.2); Siger of Brabant, the condemnations of 1270 and 1277 (events in [`church-history`](../church-history/syllabus.md) 5.2), and the disputed "double truth" label |
| 7.2 | Essence and existence | Explain how Aquinas turns Avicenna's distinction into existence as the act of being | *esse* as act and essence as potency to it; the real distinction and its Avicennian roots; God's essence is his existence (ST I q.3 a.4), subsistent being itself; creation as the giving of existence; analogy of being in one paragraph, cited forward to `thomistic-synthesis` |
| 7.3 | The Five Ways as text | Reconstruct each of the Five Ways and name its source, without deciding whether it succeeds | the structure of ST I q.2 (self-evident? demonstrable? the Ways); each way's source (Aristotle's first mover, efficient causes, Avicenna's contingency, Platonic degrees, governance); the Third Way's disputed step from each thing to everything; accidentally vs essentially ordered series (I q.46 a.2 ad 7), with the distinction as a live one in [`metaphysics`](../metaphysics/syllabus.md) 4.4; what the Ways reach and what q.3 onward adds. Soundness is left to [`philosophy-of-religion`](../philosophy-of-religion/syllabus.md) 2.3 |

**Boss problem 7:** Take ST I q.2 a.3 and I q.3 a.4 (English Dominican Province). (a) Map each of the Five Ways onto its source in this course (3.2, 3.3, 3.6, 6.2, 2.1), and say what Aquinas's version changes. (b) Reconstruct the Second Way in numbered premises. Then close-read it and say whether the article itself distinguishes essentially from accidentally ordered causes, or whether that distinction must be imported from I q.46 a.2 ad 7. (c) Explain from I q.46 a.2 why none of the Ways assumes that the world had a beginning, and why that matters after Averroes and Maimonides (6.3). (d) Reconstruct the argument in I q.3 a.4 that God's existence is not other than his essence, and mark where it depends on Avicenna's distinction (6.2) and on the priority of act (3.6). (e) In 300-500 words, assess whether Aquinas's *esse* as act is a development of Aristotle's actuality or a transformation of it, any verdict, if 3.3, 3.6 and 7.2 are each used. Parts (a)-(d) are exegetical; (e) is evaluative.

### Module 8: Universals, Scotus and Ockham

The question Porphyry left open, whether genera and species are real, becomes the fault line of late medieval thought. Moderate realism, Scotus's common natures and Ockham's nominalism are three answers, and the third ends the synthesis.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 8.1 | Porphyry's questions: universals from Boethius to Aquinas | State the problem of universals and place Boethius, Abelard and Aquinas on it | Porphyry's three questions (do genera and species exist or only in thought; bodily or incorporeal; separate or in sensible things); Boethius as translator of Aristotle's logic and his abstractionist answer; Abelard against William of Champeaux's realism, universal words and the *status*; Aquinas's moderate realism: the nature in things, in the intellect, in God's mind; abstraction by the agent intellect (3.5); realism and nominalism as live positions cited to [`metaphysics`](../metaphysics/syllabus.md) 1.3-1.4 |
| 8.2 | Scotus: the univocity of being | Reconstruct Scotus's argument for a univocal concept of being and say what it does and does not claim | Henry of Ghent's analogy as the target; the argument from a concept that stays certain while another is doubted; univocity as a thesis about concepts, not a shared nature; infinite being and natural knowledge of God; intrinsic modes (finite, infinite); the Thomist reply in outline |
| 8.3 | Scotus: common nature and haecceity | Explain what makes an individual this individual on Scotus's view, and why he needs a formal distinction | the problem of individuation and Aquinas's answer (matter under dimensions); the common nature and its less-than-numerical unity, from Avicenna's essence in itself (6.2); haecceity ("thisness") as the individuating difference; the formal distinction, which is less than real and more than merely mental |
| 8.4 | Ockham: nominalism and the razor | Reconstruct Ockham's case against common natures and say what the razor is and does | the argument that contradictory predicates require really distinct things, turned against the formal distinction in creatures; universals as concepts that are natural signs (mental language); the razor in Ockham's own wording ("plurality is not to be posited without necessity") vs the later slogan; reducing the categories to substance and quality; God's absolute vs ordained power; the *via moderna*; Ockham's clash with John XXII as an event in [`church-history`](../church-history/syllabus.md) 5.2 |

**Boss problem 8:** Socrates and Plato are both human. Is there anything in the world, outside any mind, that they share? (a) State the answers of Aquinas (8.1), Scotus (8.3) and Ockham (8.4), each in the thinker's own vocabulary (nature considered absolutely, common nature with less-than-numerical unity, concept as natural sign), graded strictly for fidelity. (b) Reconstruct Ockham's objection to Scotus's formal distinction in numbered premises, and name the crux: the single premise Scotus must deny. (c) Diagnose an invented op-ed by a biologist arguing that species are "just labels we find useful". Say which medieval position it is closest to, and what it assumes that Ockham himself did not. (d) In 300-500 words, assess whether Ockham's razor settles the dispute with Scotus or already presupposes nominalism, any verdict, if the razor is stated in Ockham's own form and applied to the formal distinction. Parts (a)-(c) are exegetical; (d) is evaluative.

## Notes

- **Lesson count.** The roadmap estimated ~24; the syllabus runs 29 (+21 percent, inside the band). The steering lists some twenty thinkers, and two constraints add lessons rather than squeeze them. First, `thomistic-synthesis` lists this course as a prerequisite for act and potency, substance, essence and existence, and the four causes. Aristotle therefore gets six lessons, with four causes (3.2), change and potency (3.3), substance (3.4) and the priority of act (3.6) each taught on its own and not compressed into one "Aristotle's metaphysics" lesson. Second, the realism-nominalism crux needs its own module (8.1-8.4) to be practised rather than named. Merges already made to hold the line: the Milesians are folded into 1.1; Zeno and the atomists share 1.2 as the two answers to Parmenides; Socrates and the Sophists share 1.3; Plato's self-criticism (the Third Man) joins the Forms in 2.1; Augustine on time joins Boethius on eternity in 5.3; Averroes and Maimonides share 6.3 around the eternity of the world, and Averroes's unity of the intellect moves to 7.1, where Aquinas answers it. If a lesson overflows when built, split it and trim elsewhere rather than grow past ~30.
- **What `thomistic-synthesis` can assume.** From this course, the texts and their history: the four causes (3.2), matter, form, privation, act and potency (3.3), substance and essence (3.4), soul as form and the agent intellect (3.5, 8.1), the priority of act (3.6), Avicenna's essence and existence (6.2), *esse* as act (7.2) and the Five Ways as text (7.3). From [`metaphysics`](../metaphysics/syllabus.md) (2.1-2.5, 4.4), the same positions defended against contemporary rivals. The division follows [`metaphysics`](../metaphysics/syllabus.md)'s own Notes: this course asks what Aristotle and Aquinas held and why, and that course asks whether it is true now. `thomistic-synthesis` should cite both and go straight to depth: analogy, participation, pure act, the structure of the *Summa*.
- **Ownership collisions resolved.** No built lessons in the library teach this course's topics. The only mentions are [`mechanics-refresher`](../mechanics-refresher/syllabus.md) 1.2, which uses Aristotle's view of motion as a foil for inertia (a bridge to 3.3, not a collision), and "hypothetical syllogism" as a rule name in [`mathematical-logic`](../mathematical-logic/syllabus.md) Module 1. Among the syllabi, [`history-of-political-thought`](../history-of-political-thought/syllabus.md) cedes Plato's and Aristotle's metaphysics, epistemology and ethics here, with the ethics going on to [`ethics`](../ethics/syllabus.md). [`ethics`](../ethics/syllabus.md) cedes "Aristotle, Aquinas, Kant as historical figures and systems" here and to `thomistic-synthesis`, but keeps their ethics. [`church-history`](../church-history/syllabus.md) cedes "scholastic philosophy taught in the new universities" here and to `thomistic-synthesis`, and keeps universities and friars as institutions. [`fundamental-theology`](../fundamental-theology/syllabus.md) reads ST I q.1 and II-II qq.1-2 doctrinally, so 7.1 uses *Summa contra Gentiles* I and the Paris controversy instead. Hellenistic ethics had no owner; it is taken here (4.1-4.2) because the schools cannot be taught without it. Four sibling syllabi landed while this one was being written, and each overlap is now resolved on their terms. [`metaphysics`](../metaphysics/syllabus.md) keeps the live debates over universals, substance, act and potency, hylomorphism, essence and existence and ordered series, and its Notes ask this course to keep the historical exegesis and cite 1.3 and 2.1-2.3; the lessons named above do so. [`philosophy-of-religion`](../philosophy-of-religion/syllabus.md) reconstructs *Proslogion* 2-3 (2.1) and uses *Consolation* V.3 and V.6 for its Boss 1, so 6.1 and 5.3 here treat Anselm and Boethius as history and reception, and Bosses 5 and 6 were rewritten to avoid those passages' reconstructions. [`patristics`](../patristics/syllabus.md) cedes Augustine's philosophy (time, memory, illumination, his Platonism), Plotinus and Boethius here, and keeps *Confessions* as theology (4.3). [`epistemology`](../epistemology/syllabus.md) cedes Plato's epistemology as history and keeps the *Meno*'s value problem as a live one.
- **Bridges.** Zeno's paradoxes (1.2) meet convergent series in [`calc-refresher`](../calc-refresher/syllabus.md) 3.1 and [`real-analysis`](../real-analysis/syllabus.md) Module 3. Aristotle's account of motion (3.3) is the view [`mechanics-refresher`](../mechanics-refresher/syllabus.md) 1.2 overturns. Stoic propositional logic (4.2) is an ancestor of the propositional calculus in [`mathematical-logic`](../mathematical-logic/syllabus.md). Augustine's "if I am mistaken, I exist" (5.1) and Avicenna's flying man (6.2) are forerunners of Descartes in [`modern-philosophy`](../modern-philosophy/syllabus.md). The Stoic cylinder (4.2) returns in the free-will lessons of [`metaphysics`](../metaphysics/syllabus.md) (Module 6), and Boethius's two necessities (5.3) in the foreknowledge lesson of [`philosophy-of-religion`](../philosophy-of-religion/syllabus.md) (1.3). Aristotle's soul (3.5) is the hylomorphism `philosophy-of-mind` treats as a live option. Ockham's nominalism (8.4) opens onto the natural-kinds debate in [`metaphysics`](../metaphysics/syllabus.md) and `philosophy-of-science`.
