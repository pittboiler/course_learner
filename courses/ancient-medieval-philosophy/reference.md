# Ancient & Medieval Philosophy · Reference Card

> Open book. This card is meant to be open while you work — including during
> quizzes. Nothing here is something you're expected to recall cold.

The course reads Parmenides to Ockham as one long argument about being, change, knowledge and God. Parmenides denies that what-is can change or be many; the atomists, Plato and Aristotle each pay a different price to get change back (void, separate Forms, potency). The Hellenistic schools tie physics and epistemology to a tranquil life, Plotinus and Augustine put the Forms in a divine mind and make evil a lack, and Avicenna, Averroes, Maimonides and Aquinas reshape Aristotle around essence, existence and revealed law, until Scotus and Ockham split over whether anything common exists outside the mind. The standing discipline is **anachronism**: read each thinker in his own vocabulary (*ousia* is not Locke's substratum, *episteme* is not justified true belief, *psyche* is not consciousness, univocity is a thesis about concepts), and treat later labels (the "ontological argument", "double truth", "moderate realism", "compatibilism", "Neoplatonist", "pure act") as labels. Use the card mid-problem to look up a term, a position, an argument's premises, who said it where, and how to read a citation. Problems ask you to *use* these, not recite them.

## Positions and distinctions

### The Milesian search for an arche

Behind the variety we see, one thing persists and changes its appearance.

Thales: water. Anaximander: the *apeiron*, out of which opposites separate. Anaximenes: air, rarefied to fire and condensed to wind, water, earth, stone. The shared question matters more than the answers.

*Introduced:* [1.1](lessons/01-01-heraclitus-and-parmenides.md)

### Heraclitus on flux, opposites and logos

Everything changes, but in fixed measures.

**Flux:** "Upon those who step into the same rivers, different and again different waters flow" (B12); "cannot step twice" (B91) is Plutarch's wording. **Unity of opposites:** the road up and down is one (B60); sea water purest and foulest (B61); opposites succeed each other (B88, B126). **Logos:** an order that holds always (B1, B50); the world is fire "kindled in measures and quenched in measures" (B30). **Two readings:** Plato's and Aristotle's universal flux (nothing stable, nothing knowable), or the measure reading (constant exchange in fixed proportion). Both live.

*Introduced:* [1.1](lessons/01-01-heraclitus-and-parmenides.md)

### Parmenides two ways

You cannot think what is not, so "is not" is no road, and change, which needs a "was not", goes with it.

The goddess offers two ways, "is" and "is not"; the second is "wholly unlearnable" (B2); "the same thing is for thinking and for being" (B3); mortals take a mixed road (B6); judge by *logos*, not the senses (B7). B8 draws the signs of what-is: ungenerated, imperishable, whole, unmoved, complete.

*Introduced:* [1.1](lessons/01-01-heraclitus-and-parmenides.md)

### Three readings of the Parmenidean is

Which job "is" does changes which premise looks weak.

**Existential** ("exists"), **predicative** ("is F"), **veridical** ("is the case"). Owen (1960), Kahn (veridical, 1969), Mourelatos (predicative, 1970). Not the existential quantifier.

*Introduced:* [1.1](lessons/01-01-heraclitus-and-parmenides.md)

### The Zenonian reductio

Zeno does not prove motion unreal; he shows the pluralist cannot make sense of it.

Strategy stated at Plato *Parmenides* 128c–d. Paradoxes of motion: dichotomy, Achilles, arrow, stadium (known mainly from *Physics* VI.9). Argument against plurality (DK 29 B1–B3): parts with no size add to nothing, sized parts divided without end add to an infinite size.

*Introduced:* [1.2](lessons/01-02-zeno-and-the-atomists.md)

### Pluralism after Parmenides

Keep "nothing really comes to be"; make birth and death rearrangements.

**Empedocles:** four roots mixed by Love, separated by Strife. **Anaxagoras:** a share of everything in everything (59 B11); no smallest amount (B3); cosmic Mind started the mixture. **Atomists:** below.

*Introduced:* [1.2](lessons/01-02-zeno-and-the-atomists.md)

### Atomism

Unchanging uncuttables moving in a void that is what-is-not and yet exists.

Leucippus and Democritus accept Parmenides' unchanging being (atoms) and deny that what-is-not cannot be (the void), which buys back plurality and motion. They also deny that bodies divide without limit, which stops Zeno's division. Sensory qualities are "by convention"; atoms and void are real. Epicurus's revisions: weight, limited shapes, true perceptions, the swerve.

*Introduced:* [1.2](lessons/01-02-zeno-and-the-atomists.md), developed in [4.1](lessons/04-01-epicurus-atoms-the-swerve-and-death.md)

### Three positions on unlimited divisibility

One premise, three verdicts.

**Zeno** uses unlimited division against the pluralist. **Aristotle** allows it, but only potentially: a continuous run crosses no infinity of actual stages (*Physics* VIII.8). **Atomists** deny it for bodies. Anaxagoras asserts it of stuffs. Ask *which* divisibility, of *what*.

*Introduced:* [1.2](lessons/01-02-zeno-and-the-atomists.md), developed in [3.3](lessons/03-03-change-privation-potency-and-act.md)

### Protagorean relativism

No standpoint above the perceiver can correct how things seem to him.

"Of all things the measure is man: of the things that are, that they are, and of the things that are not, that they are not" (DK 80 B1, via *Theaetetus* 152a); glossed: things are to you as they appear to you. Aristotle (*Metaphysics* IV.5) reads it as making contradictory appearances all true. "Relativism" is our label.

*Introduced:* [1.3](lessons/01-03-the-sophists-and-socrates.md)

### Nomos and physis

Is justice nature or convention?

**Protagoras:** justice is what each city thinks and enacts; convention makes shared life possible. **Antiphon** (*On Truth*): law's demands are agreed, nature's necessary, so keep the laws when witnessed. **Callicles** (*Gorgias* 482e–484c): by nature the stronger should have more; convention is the weak many's device.

*Introduced:* [1.3](lessons/01-03-the-sophists-and-socrates.md)

### The Socratic elenchus

Your own beliefs, taken together, refute your definition.

Thesis; agreed further premises; they entail the thesis's negation; the thesis goes (Vlastos 1983). It proves inconsistency, not a positive account: the "problem of the elenchus".

*Introduced:* [1.3](lessons/01-03-the-sophists-and-socrates.md)

### Priority of definition

You cannot know what virtue is like until you know what it is.

"When I do not know the 'quid' of anything how can I know the 'quale'?" (*Meno* 71b). Its puzzle form is Meno's paradox.

*Introduced:* [1.3](lessons/01-03-the-sophists-and-socrates.md), developed in [2.2](lessons/02-02-recollection-the-line-and-the-cave.md)

### Virtue is knowledge

Whoever knows what is best does it, so wrongdoing is ignorance.

"No man voluntarily pursues evil, or that which he thinks to be evil" (*Protagoras* 358c–d). The argument runs on the many's hedonism, and scholars dispute whether Socrates holds it. Plato's divided soul gives the thesis up.

*Introduced:* [1.3](lessons/01-03-the-sophists-and-socrates.md)

### The theory of Forms

What we know when we know "equal" is something no stick is: an unchanging F itself, in which F things participate.

Four routes in: **one over many** (*Republic* X 596a); **compresence of opposites** (*Phaedo* 74; *Republic* V 479); **knowledge versus opinion** (*Republic* V 477); **flux of sensibles** (Aristotle, *Metaphysics* I.6). Three commitments out: **participation** ("the presence and participation of beauty", *Phaedo* 100d), **separation**, **self-predication**. Compresence needs predicates with opposites that apply relatively; one over many needs only a shared name.

*Introduced:* [2.1](lessons/02-01-the-forms-and-what-goes-wrong-with-them.md)

### Self-predication and non-identity

The Form is a perfect instance of itself, and what shares in a Form is not that Form.

Self-predication (the F itself is F) makes the Form a standard things fall short of. Non-identity is Vlastos's unstated premise of the Third Man; he held the premises jointly inconsistent (1954). Forms are also paradigms, not only Aristotelian universals.

*Introduced:* [2.1](lessons/02-01-the-forms-and-what-goes-wrong-with-them.md)

### Recollection

You can seek what you don't know because you once knew it.

"All enquiry and all learning is but recollection" (*Meno* 81d): the soul, immortal and born many times, has seen all things; knowledge is latent and recovered by questioning; the slave boy (82b–85b) is the demonstration. The *Meno* never mentions Forms; the *Phaedo* ties recollection to them.

*Introduced:* [2.2](lessons/02-02-recollection-the-line-and-the-cave.md)

### Knowledge and true opinion in the Meno

True opinion guides as well as knowledge but runs away unless tied down.

Like the statues of Daedalus, true opinions must be "fastened by the tie of the cause" (*aitias logismos*, 97a–98a), and the fastening "is recollection". The tie is explanatory, not evidential; the unit is often a thing, not a proposition. On a two-worlds reading of *Republic* V no opinion becomes knowledge; Gail Fine (1978) softens this.

*Introduced:* [2.2](lessons/02-02-recollection-the-line-and-the-cave.md)

### The Sun and the Good

The Good does for knowledge what the sun does for sight.

The Good makes the Forms knowable and gives them being, yet "is not essence, but far exceeds essence in dignity and power" (509b). No will, no creation; Plotinus reads it as the One.

*Introduced:* [2.2](lessons/02-02-recollection-the-line-and-the-cave.md)

### The Divided Line

Four grades of cognition, each with its own objects.

A line cut into visible and intelligible, each part cut again "in the same proportion" (509d–511e). Bottom to top: *eikasia* (shadows, reflections); *pistis* (animals, plants, artefacts); *dianoia* (mathematicians' objects, from unexamined hypotheses, using images); *noesis* (Forms, by dialectic, up to the "first principle of the whole"). With segment ratio k, the two middle segments are always equal (k = 1/2, intelligible part 6: segments 1, 2, 2, 4). The text never says which part of each cut is longer. Jowett labels segment 4 "reason" and 3 "understanding".

*Introduced:* [2.2](lessons/02-02-recollection-the-line-and-the-cave.md)

### The Cave

Education turns the whole soul round.

Prisoners see shadows cast by objects carried before a fire; the freed one climbs in stages (shadows, reflections, things, night sky, sun) and on return sees badly in the dark (514a–517c). "Not putting sight into blind eyes" (518c–d). How closely its stages track the Line is only partly stated in the text (Boss 2).

*Introduced:* [2.2](lessons/02-02-recollection-the-line-and-the-cave.md)

### The four arguments of the Phaedo

Each argument patches the hole the last one left.

**Cyclical** (70c–72e): opposites come from opposites, so the living from the dead. **Recollection** (72e–77a): we knew the Equal before birth; proves pre-existence only (77b–c). **Affinity** (78b–84b): the soul is more like the invisible, unchanging kind. **Final argument** (102a–107a): soul brings life and cannot admit death. Objections: Simmias's attunement (85e–86d), Cebes's weaver (87b–88b).

*Introduced:* [2.3](lessons/02-03-the-soul-its-parts-and-immortality.md)

### The tripartite soul

The soul is caught opposing itself, so it has more than one source of motivation.

*Republic* IV 436b–441c: **reason** (*logistikon*), **spirit** (*thumoeides*), **appetite** (*epithumetikon*). Read here as psychology; the city-soul analogy belongs to `history-of-political-thought`. Parts or "kinds": agent-like, or sources of desire with their own objects, is disputed.

*Introduced:* [2.3](lessons/02-03-the-soul-its-parts-and-immortality.md)

### The principle of opposites

Genuine opposition in one subject means two subjects.

"The same thing clearly cannot act or be acted upon in the same part or in relation to the same thing at the same time, in contrary ways" (*Republic* IV 436b). Three qualifiers; the spinning top shows how to dissolve false oppositions. A near relative of Aristotle's non-contradiction.

*Introduced:* [2.3](lessons/02-03-the-soul-its-parts-and-immortality.md)

### Divided soul and immortal soul

The affinity argument needs an incomposite soul; *Republic* IV divides it.

Three handlings: the conflict belongs to embodiment (*Republic* X 611b–612a, the sea-god Glaucus); only the rational kind is immortal (*Timaeus*); Plato changed his mind. The thirsty non-drinker proves soul against body in the *Phaedo* (94b–e) and a divided soul in the *Republic*.

*Introduced:* [2.3](lessons/02-03-the-soul-its-parts-and-immortality.md)

### The ten categories

Every simple thing said of a subject falls under one highest kind, and substance comes first.

*Categories* 4: **substance** (man), **quantity** (two cubits), **quality** (white), **relation** (double), **place** (in the Lyceum), **time** (yesterday), **position** (sitting), **having** (shod), **acting** (cutting), **being acted on** (being cut). They classify what is signified, not words. Being is not a genus (*Metaphysics* III.3); being "is said in many ways" (IV.2). Ockham cuts the things down to substance and quality.

*Introduced:* [3.1](lessons/03-01-predication-categories-and-the-syllogism.md)

### The square of opposition

Four propositions and how their truth values constrain each other.

**Contradictories** (A–O, E–I): exactly one true. **Contraries** (A–E): never both true. **Subcontraries** (I–O): never both false. **Subalterns** (A→I, E→O): universal implies particular. The last three need non-empty S; in modern logic (A as "for all x, Sx implies Px") they fail for empty terms. Aristotle's terms all denote.

*Introduced:* [3.1](lessons/03-01-predication-categories-and-the-syllogism.md)

### The first-figure syllogism

The middle term carries the conclusion across.

| Mood | Major | Minor | Conclusion |
|---|---|---|---|
| Barbara | Every M is P | Every S is M | Every S is P |
| Celarent | No M is P | Every S is M | No S is P |
| Darii | Every M is P | Some S is M | Some S is P |
| Ferio | No M is P | Some S is M | Some S is not P |

Validity is a property of the form; show invalidity by a substitution with true premises and false conclusion. Mood names and "illicit major" are medieval.

*Introduced:* [3.1](lessons/03-01-predication-categories-and-the-syllogism.md)

### Demonstration and first principles

Scientific knowledge is knowing the cause; proof must bottom out in what is grasped, not proved.

*Posterior Analytics* I.2–3: premises true, primary, immediate, better known, prior, causes. Not all knowledge is demonstrative; first principles are known by *nous* through induction (II.19). Two valid syllogisms about the planets: only "whatever is near does not twinkle" demonstrates, because nearness is the cause (I.13).

*Introduced:* [3.1](lessons/03-01-predication-categories-and-the-syllogism.md)

### The principle of non-contradiction

The firmest principle, defensible only elenctically.

"The same attribute cannot at the same time belong and not belong to the same subject and in the same respect" (*Metaphysics* IV.3 1005b19–20). Stated of things, of belief (IV.3) and of assertions (IV.6). It cannot be demonstrated without circularity.

*Introduced:* [3.1](lessons/03-01-predication-categories-and-the-syllogism.md)

### The four causes

Four kinds of answer to "why?", which do not compete.

**Material** (the bronze), **formal** ("the statement of the essence": the octave's 2:1), **efficient** ("the man who gave advice", the father), **final** ("that for the sake of which": health, of walking) (*Physics* II.3 194b). Things can be causes of each other in different senses (exercise and good condition). In natural things formal, efficient and final nearly coincide (II.7).

*Introduced:* [3.2](lessons/03-02-nature-and-the-four-causes.md)

### Nature as an inner principle

A natural thing has its source of change inside it; an artefact has it in the craftsman.

Nature is "a source or cause of being moved and of being at rest in that to which it belongs primarily" (II.1 192b). Against Antiphon's buried bed: a man is born from a man, not a bed from a bed, so form has more claim to be nature than matter.

*Introduced:* [3.2](lessons/03-02-nature-and-the-four-causes.md)

### Chance and spontaneity

Incidental results of processes aimed at something else, and rare.

Chance (*tyche*): the man who meets his debtor at market. Spontaneity (*automaton*), the wider class: the horse that wanders to safety (II.4–6). Winter rain is not chance; dog-day rain is.

*Introduced:* [3.2](lessons/03-02-nature-and-the-four-causes.md)

### Final causes without intention

Nature aims at ends without anyone intending them.

"Art does not deliberate" (II.8 199b): if shipbuilding were in the wood, the wood would make a ship by nature. Whether II.8 extends teleology to rain and the cosmos is disputed (Sedley 1991, leaning on *Politics* I.8). Aquinas's Fifth Way adds the directing mind Aristotle does not need.

*Introduced:* [3.2](lessons/03-02-nature-and-the-four-causes.md)

### The three principles of change

Every change has a subject that persists, a privation that goes, and a form that arrives.

"The man became musical"; "the unmusical became musical"; but not "the unmusical is musical" (*Physics* I.7). Subject and privation are one in number, two in account. Privation is not any absence but a lack in a subject apt for the form (*Metaphysics* V.22).

*Introduced:* [3.3](lessons/03-03-change-privation-potency-and-act.md)

### Potency and act

A potency belongs to a subject and is bounded by what it actually is; change is the potential being actualized while still potential.

"The fulfilment of what exists potentially, in so far as it exists potentially, is motion" (*Physics* III.1 201a10–11). Not the lump, not the finished statue, but the being-shaped. Whether *entelecheia* here is "actualization" (a process) or "actuality" (Kosman 1969) is disputed. Not modern logical possibility.

*Introduced:* [3.3](lessons/03-03-change-privation-potency-and-act.md)

### Accidental and substantial change

A substance persists and gains a feature, or a new substance arises.

**Accidental:** the man becomes musical, the sailor tans. **Substantial** (*haplos*): seed to plant, water to air. What underlies substantial change? Traditionally **prime matter**, pure potency (the commentators, Aquinas). Dissent: King (1956), Charlton (1970): a proximate subject with some character suffices.

*Introduced:* [3.3](lessons/03-03-change-privation-potency-and-act.md)

### Substance in the Categories

The individual is the floor of being.

"Substance, in the truest and primary and most definite sense of the word, is that which is neither predicable of a subject nor present in a subject; for instance, the individual man or horse" (*Categories* 5). Species and genus are secondary substances. Mark: remaining numerically one, a substance receives contraries.

| | Said of a subject | Not said of a subject |
|---|---|---|
| Present in a subject | universal accidents (knowledge, whiteness) | individual accidents (this whiteness) |
| Not present in a subject | secondary substances (man, animal); differentiae | primary substances (this man, this horse) |

*Introduced:* [3.4](lessons/03-04-substance-and-essence.md)

### Substance in Metaphysics VII

The substance *of* a thing is its essence, its form.

"What being is" is "just the question, what is substance?" (VII.1). Candidates (VII.3): substratum, essence, universal, genus. Matter fails (not separable, not a "this"); form is prior to matter and compound; essence is what definition states (VII.4–6); no universal is substance (VII.13); substance is the cause that makes matter into this thing (VII.17). The *Categories* answer is re-ranked, not refuted.

*Introduced:* [3.4](lessons/03-04-substance-and-essence.md)

### Form, universal and substance

Three texts that will not sit together.

(1) Form is primary substance (VII.7, 17). (2) No universal is substance (VII.13). (3) Form is universal, since "definition is of the universal and of the form" (VII.11). Readings: deny 3, particular forms (Frede and Patzig 1988); restrict 2 to universals predicated of individuals, not of matter; or deny that it is one doctrine. Everything turns on "universal".

*Introduced:* [3.4](lessons/03-04-substance-and-essence.md)

### Soul as first actuality

The soul is to the living body what sight is to the eye.

"Soul is the first actuality of a natural body having in it the capacity of life" (*De Anima* II.1, Hicks), of a body "furnished with organs". No more question whether soul and body are one than wax and imprint. A corpse is a body only equivocally. Possible exception: parts that "are not the actualities of any body whatever" (413a).

*Introduced:* [3.5](lessons/03-05-soul-and-intellect.md)

### The powers of soul

Souls differ by powers, and the powers nest.

Nutritive (all living things), perceptive (animals, touch first), appetitive and locomotive (most animals), thinking (humans). "The earlier form exists potentially in the later, as ... the triangle potentially in the quadrilateral" (414b).

*Introduced:* [3.5](lessons/03-05-soul-and-intellect.md)

### Agent and possible intellect readings

Fifteen lines on an intellect that "makes all things", and four places to put it.

*De Anima* III.5: an intellect that "becomes all things" and one that "makes all things", like light; the second "separable and impassive and unmixed", "alone ... immortal and eternal"; "we do not remember". **Alexander** (c. 200): the maker is God. **Avicenna**: a separate Agent Intellect, lowest celestial mind; each human has a receiving intellect. **Averroes**: the receiving intellect too is one and separate for all. **Aquinas**: each soul has its own agent and possible intellect, powers of the form of a body. Read as a scale of how much lies outside the individual.

*Introduced:* [3.5](lessons/03-05-soul-and-intellect.md), developed in [7.1](lessons/07-01-faith-reason-and-the-paris-crisis.md)

### Priority of actuality

The actual is prior to the potential in definition, in time (in the kind), and in substance.

*Metaphysics* IX.8: "able to build" is defined by building; every seed came from an actual oak; the potency is for the sake of the act. Stricter sense: what can be can also not be, so nothing eternal is merely potential.

*Introduced:* [3.6](lessons/03-06-the-unmoved-mover.md)

### The unmoved mover

An eternal, immaterial substance whose essence is actuality moves the heavens as the object of their love.

It "produces motion by being loved" (XII.7 1072b3): a final cause. Its life is thought thinking itself (XII.9). It does **not** create, choose, plan, or love; whether it knows the world is disputed (Aquinas, Brentano: yes, through itself; many moderns: no; Norman 1969: "thinking on thinking" names pure thought). "On such a principle, then, depend the heavens and the world of nature" (1072b13–14). "Depend" is not "created by".

*Introduced:* [3.6](lessons/03-06-the-unmoved-mover.md)

### One mover or many

XII.6 reaches *a* pure actuality; *one* god takes more work.

XII.8 counts fifty-five spheres (forty-seven on a variant), each with an unmoved mover, yet argues the first mover is one because many-in-number requires matter. The usual repair ranks the movers under the first (XII.10's army and general). Aquinas's one-angel-one-species is his repair, not Aristotle's.

*Introduced:* [3.6](lessons/03-06-the-unmoved-mover.md)

### Epicurean therapy

Physics is medicine: it removes fear of the gods and of death, and pleasure's low ceiling does the rest.

Three sources of misery: fear of the gods, fear of death, unlimited desire. Without those troubles "we would have no need to study nature" (PD 11, paraphrased). Gods exist and are blessed, so they do nothing: "A blessed and eternal being has no trouble himself and brings no trouble upon any other being" (PD 1). The *tetrapharmakos* summarizes.

*Introduced:* [4.1](lessons/04-01-epicurus-atoms-the-swerve-and-death.md)

### Epicurean pleasure

Freedom from pain is itself the highest pleasure, and it cannot be increased, only varied.

The end is pleasure, its limit the removal of all pain (PD 3). *Ataraxia* and *aponia* are katastematic pleasures, not numbness (DL X.136). Unlimited time holds no greater pleasure than limited (PD 18–20), which is the resource against the deprivation account of death.

*Introduced:* [4.1](lessons/04-01-epicurus-atoms-the-swerve-and-death.md)

### All perceptions are true

A perception faithfully registers what arrived; error is the opinion we add.

The *Canon* (DL X.31–32, 50–51): thin films of atoms strike the organ. The square tower looks round from afar because its corners wore off in transit; the mistake is judging it round (Lucretius IV).

*Introduced:* [4.1](lessons/04-01-epicurus-atoms-the-swerve-and-death.md)

### The Epicurean swerve

Atoms veer "only just", at no fixed time or place, which makes worlds and keeps choices ours.

Known from Lucretius II.216–293 and Cicero, not Epicurus's letters. Jobs: **cosmogony** (otherwise parallel fall forever) and **freedom** (breaks "the decrees of fate"). Better the myths of the gods than the "destiny" of the natural philosophers (*Menoeceus* 134). Cicero: an uncaused swerve gives chance, not freedom. Furley (1967): a swerve need not occur in each free act.

*Introduced:* [4.1](lessons/04-01-epicurus-atoms-the-swerve-and-death.md)

### Stoic physics

One bodily, rational, living cosmos.

Only bodies exist, because only bodies act or are acted on; void, place, time and *lekta* subsist. Two bodily principles: passive matter and the active *logos*-god-*pneuma*, whose *tonos* gives cohesion, nature, soul, reason by degree. Periodic conflagration and rebirth in the same order. Philosophy as a field: logic the wall, physics the soil, ethics the fruit (DL VII.40).

*Introduced:* [4.2](lessons/04-02-the-stoics-logos-fate-and-virtue.md)

### Stoic fate and the cylinder

Everything is fated, but what happens through you depends on what kind of thing you are.

Fate (*heimarmene*) is one unbroken causal chain; seen as rational, providence. Chrysippus (Cicero, *On Fate* 41–43): external causes are auxiliary and proximate; your character is the perfect and principal cause of assent, as a cylinder rolls by its own shape when pushed. Fate is not fatalism: the outcome comes *through* what you do. "Compatibilism" is a modern label; the question is what is *eph' hemin*.

*Introduced:* [4.2](lessons/04-02-the-stoics-logos-fate-and-virtue.md)

### What is up to us

Only judgment and choice are yours; the criterion is what can be hindered.

"Under our control are conception, choice, desire, aversion ... not under our control are our body, our property, reputation, office" (Epictetus, *Enchiridion* 1). The body is on the wrong side although you move your arm.

*Introduced:* [4.2](lessons/04-02-the-stoics-logos-fate-and-virtue.md)

### Virtue the only good and preferred indifferents

Only what cannot be misused is good; health and wealth still have selective value.

Virtue alone good, vice alone bad; health, wealth, life are indifferents, preferred or dispreferred (DL VII.102–103). From *oikeiosis*, the adult comes to value the rational order of selection above anything selected (*On Ends* III.20–21). Telos: living in agreement with nature. Indifferent to outcome, not to selection.

*Introduced:* [4.2](lessons/04-02-the-stoics-logos-fate-and-virtue.md)

### Stoic passions

A passion is a false judgment that an indifferent is good or bad, plus a judgment that reacting is right.

Zeno: excessive impulse disobedient to reason; Chrysippus: a judgment. Four genera: **distress** (present bad), **pleasure** (present good), **fear** (future bad), **appetite** (future good). The sage's *eupatheiai*: joy, caution, wish; none for distress. "First movements" (flinching) are not yet assent (Seneca). Posidonius: passions come from non-rational parts.

*Introduced:* [4.2](lessons/04-02-the-stoics-logos-fate-and-virtue.md)

### Stoic propositional logic

A logic of whole propositions joined by connectives.

The five indemonstrables: (1) if the first, the second; the first; so the second. (2) If the first, the second; not the second; so not the first. (3) Not both; the first; so not the second. (4) Either (exclusive); the first; so not the second. (5) Either; not the first; so the second. Contrast Aristotle's term logic.

*Introduced:* [4.2](lessons/04-02-the-stoics-logos-fate-and-virtue.md)

### The cognitive impression

An appearance that guarantees its own truth, the Stoic criterion.

(i) From what is, (ii) stamped in exact accordance with it, (iii) such as could not come from what is not (Sextus, *M* VII.248); clause (iii) added under Arcesilaus's pressure. Zeno's hand: open fingers impression, fingers drawn in assent, fist grasp, other hand over the fist knowledge. The wise man never opines.

*Introduced:* [4.3](lessons/04-03-the-skeptics.md)

### Academic and Pyrrhonian skepticism

Academics break the Stoic criterion; Pyrrhonists balance every argument and suspend.

**Academics** (Arcesilaus, Carneades): no impression is cognitive, so the sage assents to nothing; Carneades offers the persuasive impression for action. **Pyrrhonists** (Sextus): skepticism is "an ability" to oppose appearances and thoughts (*PH* I.8); equipollence brings suspension; tranquility follows "as a shadow follows a body" (I.29), like Apelles' sponge (I.28). Sextus calls the New Academy negative dogmatists who follow persuasion "with a strong inclination" (I.226–230).

*Introduced:* [4.3](lessons/04-03-the-skeptics.md)

### The persuasive impression

A guide to action that never certifies truth.

Three grades: persuasive; persuasive and undiverted; persuasive, undiverted and tested (*M* VII.166–189), matched to the stakes (the coiled rope). Whether Carneades endorsed it or argued it against the Stoics was disputed in antiquity (Clitomachus, *Academica* II.139).

*Introduced:* [4.3](lessons/04-03-the-skeptics.md)

### The Agrippan modes

Five standard patterns for producing equipollence.

**Disagreement**, **infinite regress**, **relativity**, **hypothesis**, **reciprocity** (*PH* I.164–177; credited to Agrippa by DL IX.88). The earlier **Ten Modes** (animals, people, senses, conditions, positions, customs) are more concrete. "Agrippa's trilemma" (regress, hypothesis, reciprocity) is a modern distillation (Albert's "Münchhausen trilemma", 1968).

*Introduced:* [4.3](lessons/04-03-the-skeptics.md)

### Rustic and urbane Pyrrhonism

Does suspension reach every belief or only dogmatic belief about the non-evident?

The skeptic lives by appearances: nature, feelings, laws and customs, arts (*PH* I.21–24). **Rustic** (Burnyeat 1980): all belief, so the life is unlivable or smuggles belief. **Urbane** (Frede 1979): only dogma, assent to the non-evident (I.13). Labels are Barnes's (1982).

*Introduced:* [4.3](lessons/04-03-the-skeptics.md)

### The Plotinian hypostases

Everything real is one, so the first thing is perfectly one, and the rest proceeds from it.

**The One** (the Good): simple, beyond being and intellect, indescribable; "we can and do state what it is not" (V.3.14). **Intellect** (*nous*): the Forms as its own thoughts, thinker and thought identical (V.5, V.9). **Soul**: World-Soul and individual souls; part of ours never descends (IV.8.8). Below: **nature**, soul's lowest phase, making by contemplation (III.8.3); **matter**, privation. "The One is all things and no one of them" (V.2.1): not pantheism, not a personal creator.

*Introduced:* [4.4](lessons/04-04-plotinus-the-one-intellect-and-soul.md)

### Procession and return

Things come from the One as light from the sun and are formed by turning back to it.

The perfect produces without diminution, like fire warming (V.4.1); "circumradiation" (V.1.6); "we dare not talk of generation in time". Procession gives existence, *epistrophe* gives determinate form. The ascent practised: "never cease chiselling your statue" (I.6.9).

*Introduced:* [4.4](lessons/04-04-plotinus-the-one-intellect-and-soul.md)

### Matter as evil in Plotinus

Evil is not a rival power but where form runs out.

Matter is formless privation and "primal evil" (I.8); "as necessarily as there is Something after the First, so necessarily there is a Last" (I.8.7). Tension: generated and yet absolute evil (O'Brien: consistent; others: dualist residue). Augustine keeps privation and drops matter as evil.

*Introduced:* [4.4](lessons/04-04-plotinus-the-one-intellect-and-soul.md)

### Augustine against the Academics

Some beliefs confirm themselves when doubted, and none of them is known through a sense impression.

"If I am deceived, I am" (*City of God* XI.26). Also: I know that I know and I love; disjunctions; mathematical truths; how things appear (*Against the Academics*); the *verisimile* standard is incoherent; whoever doubts lives, remembers, understands, wills (*On the Trinity* X.10). A counterexample to a universal negative, and a step toward an image of the Trinity, not a foundation for all knowledge.

*Introduced:* [5.1](lessons/05-01-augustine-certainty-and-illumination.md)

### Augustinian illumination

The mind sees unchangeable truth in a light it does not make, as the eye sees bodies in sunlight.

Truths common to all minds and unchangeable; we judge by them, not about them; so they are above the mind; the light is God, the inner teacher (*On Free Choice* II; *On the Teacher*; *On the Trinity* XII.15). Limit: sensibles are learned by sense or testimony (*saraballae*). Three readings: **concept-supplying**; **judgment-guaranteeing** (Gilson); **ontologist** (Malebranche), which most reject.

*Introduced:* [5.1](lessons/05-01-augustine-certainty-and-illumination.md)

### Evil as privation

Evil is a hole, not a thing: a lack of good in something good.

"Whatsoever is, is good" (*Confessions* VII.12 (18)); disease is "nothing but the absence of health" (*Enchiridion* 11). Against the Manichees' two co-eternal principles, Light and Darkness, and their comfort that "it was not we that sin". Privation is not unreality: blindness is real.

*Introduced:* [5.2](lessons/05-02-augustine-evil-and-the-will.md)

### Manichees, Plotinus and Augustine on evil

Three answers to where evil is and who is responsible.

| | Manichees | Plotinus | Augustine |
|---|---|---|---|
| Evil a substance? | Yes: Darkness | No: a lack | No: privation of good |
| Where found? | A hostile nature mixed into us | Matter, evil itself (I.8) | Good but changeable natures, above all wills |
| Source of wrongdoing | The dark nature in us | Soul's descent toward matter | The will's turning from higher to lower good |
| Who is responsible? | "Not we" | The soul, in its weakness | The agent, without remainder |

*Introduced:* [5.2](lessons/05-02-augustine-evil-and-the-will.md)

### The will as a middle good

Sin is a good power choosing in the wrong order.

Evil done versus evil suffered; wrongdoing is inordinate desire for what can be lost unwillingly (*On Free Choice* I). The will, like a hand, can be used well or badly (II). Iniquity is "a perversion of the will" turned "towards these lower things" (*Confessions* VII.16 (22)). The divided will: "my two wills, one old and the other new" (VIII.5 (10)), not two souls (VIII.10 (22)). Whether Augustine invented the will: Dihle (1982) yes, roughly; Frede (2011) no.

*Introduced:* [5.2](lessons/05-02-augustine-evil-and-the-will.md)

### Distentio animi

Time, as we measure it, is a stretching of the soul across memory, attention and expectation.

No time before creation: God made time with the world and precedes it by eternity (*Confessions* XI.13). "If no one asks me, I know; if I want to explain it to someone who asks, I do not know" (XI.14). The psalm recited from memory (XI.28). Time is not simply heavenly motion: the potter's wheel (XI.23). Whether *distentio* is time as such or measured time only, and whose soul, is disputed.

*Introduced:* [5.3](lessons/05-03-time-eternity-and-foreknowledge.md)

### Boethian eternity

Eternity is not a very long time; it is having the whole of life at once.

"The whole, simultaneous and perfect possession of unending life" (*Consolation* V.6). A beginningless, endless world would be **perpetual**, not eternal. God sees all times as present: *providentia*, not *praevidentia*.

*Introduced:* [5.3](lessons/05-03-time-eternity-and-foreknowledge.md)

### Simple and conditional necessity

Necessity from a thing's nature is not the necessity that follows from its being known.

"One is simple, as that it is necessary that all men are mortal. The other is of condition, as that if you know someone is walking, it is necessary that he is walking ... this condition by no means drags that simple necessity along with it" (V.6, lesson's translation). The sun rises by simple necessity; the walker walks necessary only given the seeing. The labels *necessitas consequentiae* / *consequentis* and the modal-scope reading are later.

*Introduced:* [5.3](lessons/05-03-time-eternity-and-foreknowledge.md)

### Anselm method

A chain up from experienced goods, then one argument from a believed formula, both as faith seeking understanding.

**Monologion** (1075–76): argue without Scripture's authority; many goods are good through one thing, supremely good. **Proslogion** (1077–78): a single argument needing nothing else; addressed to God; "I do not seek to understand that I may believe, but I believe in order to understand" (ch. 1). Readings: a proof for anyone who understands the words, or theology inside faith (Barth 1931). Boss 6 decides from the text.

*Introduced:* [6.1](lessons/06-01-anselm-and-the-proslogion-as-history.md)

### Gaunilo and Anselm

A quarrel among believers over what the formula can do.

Gaunilo, *On Behalf of the Fool*: the fool has only the words, without genus or species to conceive the thing; the **lost island** most excellent of all lands. Anselm's *Reply* answers "the Catholic": Gaunilo misquotes ("greater than all" is not the formula); the reasoning fits only that than which a greater cannot be conceived, or he will hand over the island "not to be lost again". Aquinas later files the argument under self-evidence (ST I q.2 a.1 ad 2).

*Introduced:* [6.1](lessons/06-01-anselm-and-the-proslogion-as-history.md)

### Self-evident in itself and to us

"God exists" is self-evident in itself, not to us.

A proposition is self-evident when the predicate is included in the essence of the subject. In God existence is essence, so in itself; we do not know God's essence, so not to us (ST I q.2 a.1). Even granting Anselm's meaning of "God", what follows is existence in the mind, not in reality (ad 2).

*Introduced:* [6.1](lessons/06-01-anselm-and-the-proslogion-as-history.md), developed in [7.3](lessons/07-03-the-five-ways-as-text.md)

### Essence and existence in Avicenna

What a thing is never tells you that it is.

"Horseness is just horseness" (*Metaphysics* of *The Healing* V.1): in itself neither one nor many, in things nor in minds. Existence comes to or accompanies an essence rather than belonging to it. The Latins rendered it an *accident*; Averroes attacked that (regress). Many moderns (Wisnovsky 2003): a concomitant, not a categorial accident.

*Introduced:* [6.2](lessons/06-02-avicenna-essence-existence-and-the-necessary-existent.md)

### Necessary and possible existents

Considered in itself, a thing's existence is guaranteed, left open, or ruled out.

The possible in itself is indifferent to existing, so if it exists a cause tips the balance; given its cause it is **necessary through another**, without it **impossible through another**. The necessary in itself has no cause, and its essence is not other than its existence. Not S5 necessity; not modern contingency: whatever exists is necessary through its cause.

*Introduced:* [6.2](lessons/06-02-avicenna-essence-existence-and-the-necessary-existent.md)

### Three routes to a first principle

Aristotle from motion, Avicenna from existence, Anselm from a name.

**Aristotle:** eternal motion to a final cause (physics and theology). **Avicenna:** metaphysics proves its own principle from existence as such; the giver of existence, with emanation through intellects (closer to Plotinus). **Anselm:** from what is understood by a formula. Avicenna's proof needs no beginning of the world; the *kalam* (al-Ghazali) argues from a temporal first moment.

*Introduced:* [6.2](lessons/06-02-avicenna-essence-existence-and-the-necessary-existent.md)

### Averroes on philosophy and the Law

The Law commands philosophy for those able, and scripture yields to demonstration.

*Decisive Treatise* (c. 1179–80): a legal ruling. "Reflect, you who have vision" (Qur'an 59:2) commands inference; judge a tool by whether it cuts, not who made it. Truth does not contradict truth. Where demonstration contradicts the *zahir*, apply *ta'wil* by the figurative ways of Arabic; never divulge it to those who cannot follow. Not "double truth".

*Introduced:* [6.3](lessons/06-03-averroes-and-maimonides.md)

### Three classes of assent

People come to assent by demonstration, dialectic or rhetoric, and scripture speaks to all three.

Demonstration from certain premises; dialectic from generally accepted ones; rhetoric from persuasion and images (Qur'an 16:125). Interpretations belong to the demonstrative class.

*Introduced:* [6.3](lessons/06-03-averroes-and-maimonides.md)

### Al-Ghazali and the philosophers

Twenty theses undemonstrated; three unbelief.

*Incoherence of the Philosophers*: *kufr* on the world's eternity, God's not knowing particulars, no bodily resurrection. Averroes (*Incoherence of the Incoherence*, c. 1180): eternity is nearly a verbal dispute (the world is made by an agent yet not preceded by time); God's knowledge causes things rather than being caused, so "knows" is said in different senses; denying the afterlife is unbelief, but its mode admits interpretation.

*Introduced:* [6.3](lessons/06-03-averroes-and-maimonides.md)

### Maimonides on negative attributes

Say what God is not; each true negation brings you nearer.

No positive attribute, since each would make God composite (*Guide* I.58–60). Proper terms are attributes of action and negations: "powerful" means not powerless. The ship known by negation (I.60). God's existence is not added to his essence (I.57).

*Introduced:* [6.3](lessons/06-03-averroes-and-maimonides.md)

### Maimonides on creation

Neither eternity nor creation is demonstrated, so hold creation.

Three opinions (II.13): Mosaic (after absolute non-existence), Platonic (eternal matter formed), Aristotelian (eternal and necessary). Aristotle's arguments are not demonstrations and he knew it (II.15); creation is not demonstrable either (II.16); the island child who argues from the finished state (II.17). Scripture could be read figuratively (II.25), but only demonstration forces that, and Aristotelian necessity would overturn the Law; the Platonic view could be reconciled if demonstrated.

*Introduced:* [6.3](lessons/06-03-averroes-and-maimonides.md)

### Two kinds of truth about God

Some truths about God reason reaches, some it cannot approach, and none conflict.

*Summa contra Gentiles* I.3–7: that God exists and is one, reachable; that God is three and one, not philosophy at all. Reachable truths are still fittingly believed (few, late, with errors). "It is impossible for the aforesaid truth of faith to be contrary to those principles which reason knows naturally" (I.7). For truths above reason, answer objections; do not offer weak proofs (I.9).

*Introduced:* [7.1](lessons/07-01-faith-reason-and-the-paris-crisis.md)

### Three Paris responses

Divide the labour, map the terrain, or draw a fence.

**Arts masters:** expound what follows from Aristotle, and hold with faith where it clashes (1272 statute). **Aquinas:** a genuine collision is impossible, so find the bad step, case by case. **Tempier:** condemn (13 propositions in 1270; 219 in 1277).

*Introduced:* [7.1](lessons/07-01-faith-reason-and-the-paris-crisis.md)

### The eternity of the world undemonstrable

Neither the world's eternity nor its beginning can be demonstrated.

Eternity not (ST I q.46 a.1): the world exists because God wills it, not necessarily; Aristotle's arguments are relative, dialectical. Beginning not (a.2): "By faith alone do we hold, and by no demonstration can it be proved, that the world did not always exist"; essences abstract from here and now, and God creates by will. Close to Maimonides. Frees the Five Ways from assuming a beginning.

*Introduced:* [7.1](lessons/07-01-faith-reason-and-the-paris-crisis.md)

### Unity of the intellect

Averroes: one separate possible intellect for all humans; Aquinas: then this man does not think.

Averroes's Long Commentary: the possible intellect cannot be the form of a body, is one for all, joined to individuals through their phantasms. Aquinas, *On the Unity of the Intellect* (1270), argues from Aristotle's principles, not faith, and says Averroes perverted Aristotle. Siger followed Averroes (c. 1269–70) and later moved toward Aquinas. Condemned in 1270.

*Introduced:* [7.1](lessons/07-01-faith-reason-and-the-paris-crisis.md), background in [3.5](lessons/03-05-soul-and-intellect.md)

### The double truth label

A charge, not a school.

1277 prologue: things true according to philosophy but not the Catholic faith, "as if there were two contrary truths". Historians have found no master who asserted it; Siger and Boethius of Dacia held faith true without qualification and philosophy's conclusions relative to natural principles. Averroes's own formula says the opposite.

*Introduced:* [7.1](lessons/07-01-faith-reason-and-the-paris-crisis.md), background in [6.3](lessons/06-03-averroes-and-maimonides.md)

### The real distinction

In every creature, essence stands to existence as potency to act.

"Existence is the most perfect of all things, for it is compared to all things as that by which they are made actual" (ST I q.4 a.1 ad 3); it actuates "even their forms". *Esse* is not an accident received by an existing subject but what makes the whole essence actual (commentary on *Metaphysics* IV). The label hardened in Giles of Rome against Henry of Ghent (1270s–80s). Two principles of one being, not two things. Whether *De Ente* proves the distinction before reaching God (Wippel 2000) or only after (Owens) is disputed.

*Introduced:* [7.2](lessons/07-02-aquinas-essence-and-existence.md)

### Subsisting being itself

One thing is existence; everything else has it, as hot iron has heat.

*Ipsum esse subsistens* (ST I q.4 a.2); God's essence is his existence (q.3 a.4). Creatures are beings by participation (q.44 a.1); to create is to give being from no presupposed subject (q.45 a.1); being is God's proper effect (q.45 a.5).

*Introduced:* [7.2](lessons/07-02-aquinas-essence-and-existence.md)

### Two compositions

Matter/form and essence/*esse* are different compositions; received existence, not matter, separates creature from Creator.

| Being | Matter and form? | Essence and *esse*? | Text |
|---|---|---|---|
| God | No | No: essence is existence | ST I q.3 a.4 |
| Angels | No: subsisting forms | Yes | I q.50 a.2 ad 3 |
| Humans | Yes: soul is form of body | Yes: one *esse*, kept by the soul after death | I q.76 a.1 ad 5 |
| Other bodies | Yes | Yes | *De Ente* ch. 4 |

With nothing to multiply them in one species, each angel is its own species (I q.50 a.4).

*Introduced:* [7.2](lessons/07-02-aquinas-essence-and-existence.md)

### Analogy

Names said of God and creatures neither univocally nor equivocally, but by a real relation of creature to cause.

"No name is predicated univocally of God and of creatures"; equivocal predication would expose reasoning to equivocation (ST I q.13 a.5). Cajetan's proportionality (1498) versus attribution readings belong to `thomistic-synthesis` 4.4.

*Introduced:* [7.2](lessons/07-02-aquinas-essence-and-existence.md), developed in [8.2](lessons/08-02-scotus-the-univocity-of-being.md)

### Demonstration quia and propter quid

From effects better known to us you can prove *that* a cause exists without knowing *what* it is.

*Propter quid*: from cause to effect, needing the cause's essence. *Quia*: from effect to the cause's existence (smoke to fire). The middle term is the meaning of the name "God", not the essence (ST I q.2 a.2 ad 2); such truths are preambles, not articles (ad 1).

*Introduced:* [7.3](lessons/07-03-the-five-ways-as-text.md)

### Accidentally vs essentially ordered series

Which regress Aquinas says is impossible.

**Per se:** each intermediate causes only as it is caused at that moment (stone, stick, hand); cannot go to infinity. **Per accidens:** the multiplication is incidental (a craftsman's many hammers; a man begets as a man, not as someone's son); can go to infinity (ST I q.46 a.2 ad 7). Whether the article of the Second Way itself draws this distinction is Boss 7.

*Introduced:* [7.3](lessons/07-03-the-five-ways-as-text.md)

### Porphyry questions

Do genera and species subsist, and if so, are they bodies, and are they separate from sensibles?

"As to whether they subsist (in the nature of things) or in mere conceptions only; whether also if subsistent, they are bodies or incorporeal, and whether they are separate from, or in, sensibles" (*Isagoge* ch. 1). Nested: the second and third arise only on one answer to the first. Porphyry declines to choose. About kinds, not "properties".

*Introduced:* [8.1](lessons/08-01-porphyrys-questions-universals-to-aquinas.md)

### Boethius on universals

In things as singular, in the mind as universal.

Nothing common to many at once is one thing; but considering one thing without what is joined to it is not false (combining is); the mathematician's line. So genera and species subsist in sensibles, incorporeal, and are understood apart. He follows Aristotle because the book introduces the *Categories*, not having decided against Plato.

*Introduced:* [8.1](lessons/08-01-porphyrys-questions-universals-to-aquinas.md)

### Material-essence realism and indifference

William of Champeaux: one essence wholly in each man; then, each man's humanity "indifferently" the same.

First view: individuals differ only by accidents added to one essence. Abelard: one thing in two places, bearing contraries, all men one man. Revision (reported in *Story of My Misfortunes*): not essentially but indifferently the same. William is known largely through Abelard; hedge.

*Introduced:* [8.1](lessons/08-01-porphyrys-questions-universals-to-aquinas.md)

### Abelard status

Universality belongs to words, but words are imposed because of a real status that is not a thing.

"Man" is a word apt to be predicated of many (*voces*, later *sermones*). Its imposition is caused by the *status* of being a man, which is no *res*: a man flogged because he did not want to go to the forum, and "not wanting" is no thing. Near nominalism, not Ockhamist nominalism.

*Introduced:* [8.1](lessons/08-01-porphyrys-questions-universals-to-aquinas.md)

### Moderate realism

One nature, three conditions: in itself neither one nor many, individual in things, universal only in the intellect.

Aquinas (*On Being and Essence* ch. 3; ST I q.85 a.1–2): the **nature considered absolutely** (Avicenna's horseness); the **nature in singulars**; the **nature in the intellect**, abstracted by the agent intellect: "that humanity be apprehended without conditions of individuality ... occurs to humanity inasmuch as it is brought under the consideration of the intellect" (q.85 a.2 ad 2). The absolute consideration is a way of considering, not a third place. "Moderate realism" is a modern label.

*Introduced:* [8.1](lessons/08-01-porphyrys-questions-universals-to-aquinas.md)

### The divine ideas

Plato's patterns survive, relocated into God.

Things are made according to exemplars in the divine mind (ST I q.15 a.1–3), which are God's understanding of how his essence can be imitated, not separate Forms; many ideas do not compromise simplicity (a.2). Socrates is human by his own form, not by participating in the idea; Augustine put Plato's ideas in God's mind (q.84 a.5). Those who stress participation (q.44 a.3) find more Platonism.

*Introduced:* [8.1](lessons/08-01-porphyrys-questions-universals-to-aquinas.md)

### Realism to nominalism

Where each thinker puts what is real and what is universal (a teaching spectrum, not their scale).

| Thinker | What exists outside any mind | Where universality lives |
|---|---|---|
| Plato | Separate Forms, and participating particulars | In the Forms |
| William (first view) | One essence wholly in each individual | In that shared essence |
| Scotus | A common nature with less-than-numerical unity, individuated by thisness | Completed only in the intellect |
| Aquinas (Boethius nearby) | Natures individuated in singulars; exemplars in God | Only in the intellect, by abstraction |
| Abelard | Individuals, and statuses that are not things | In words |
| Ockham | Only singular things | In concepts, as natural signs |

*From* [8.1](lessons/08-01-porphyrys-questions-universals-to-aquinas.md), [8.3](lessons/08-03-scotus-common-nature-and-haecceity.md), [8.4](lessons/08-04-ockham-nominalism-and-the-razor.md)

### Univocity of being

One concept of being applies to God and creatures in the same sense; in reality they are primarily diverse.

Scotus, *Ordinatio* I d.3 p.1 qq.1–2, against Henry of Ghent (two concepts so close they seem one). A concept is univocal when its unity suffices (i) for a **contradiction** when affirmed and denied of one thing, and (ii) to serve as the **middle term** without equivocation. Not a shared nature or genus; being is not a genus. Infinite being is the most perfect natural concept of God, still imperfect. Aquinas died when Scotus was a boy.

*Introduced:* [8.2](lessons/08-02-scotus-the-univocity-of-being.md)

### Intrinsic modes

Infinity is how God is, not an ingredient added to being.

Finite and infinite are degrees of intensity, not differentiae (*Ord.* I d.8), like whiteness at a degree. Only pure perfections pass to God with the mode removed. The Thomist denies any mode-free *ratio* is left; the Scotist appeals to the whiteness case.

*Introduced:* [8.2](lessons/08-02-scotus-the-univocity-of-being.md)

### Individuation by signate matter

Aquinas: what makes this lamb this is its matter under determinate dimensions.

*De Ente* ch. 2: *materia signata*, not in the species definition. "This particular flesh, these bones ... are not included in the definition of a man" (ST I q.3 a.3). Consequence: two angels of one species are impossible (I q.50 a.4). Whether his view moved from indeterminate to determinate dimensions is debated.

*Introduced:* [8.3](lessons/08-03-scotus-common-nature-and-haecceity.md)

### Common nature

A nature in the thing, not of itself this, with a real unity less than numerical.

Scotus (*Ordinatio* II d.3 p.1): the nature is not of itself singular, yet has real unity prior to any intellect (real likeness needs a real foundation). Exists only in individuals, universal only in the intellect. Not a Platonic Form; "less than numerical" is a different kind of unity, not a fraction.

*Introduced:* [8.3](lessons/08-03-scotus-common-nature-and-haecceity.md)

### Haecceity

A positive substantial entity that contracts the nature to this individual and adds no new "what".

Not negation, existence, quantity or matter (qq.2–5). The *Ordinatio* calls it the "ultimate reality" of the being; *haecceitas* is used elsewhere and by followers. Analogy: as *rational* contracts *animal*. Not grasped as such in this life. Two angels of one species are possible (q.7). Not a bare particular and not Adams's haecceitistic property (1979).

*Introduced:* [8.3](lessons/08-03-scotus-common-nature-and-haecceity.md)

### Formal distinction

Distinct in the thing before any mind, yet inseparable even by God.

| Distinction | Test | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Real | Separable, at least by God | Socrates and Plato; substance and its whiteness |
| Formal *a parte rei* | Inseparable, one not in the definition of the other, prior to intellect | Common nature and individual difference; intellect and will in God |
| Of reason | Made by the intellect's act | "Tully" and "Cicero" |

Thomists' distinction of reason with a foundation in the thing is what Scotus rejects as not enough. Ockham admits the formal distinction only for the divine essence and persons.

*Introduced:* [8.3](lessons/08-03-scotus-common-nature-and-haecceity.md), attacked in [8.4](lessons/08-04-ockham-nominalism-and-the-razor.md)

### Ockham nominalism

Everything that exists is singular; a universal is a sign that signifies many.

Primary universals are **concepts**, natural signs, each a singular item in a singular mind (*Summa Logicae* I.14–17). Spoken and written words signify by convention, subordinated to concepts. Socrates and Plato really agree more than Socrates and a donkey, but agreement is not a thing. Sometimes called conceptualism; "nominalism" is a later label.

*Introduced:* [8.4](lessons/08-04-ockham-nominalism-and-the-razor.md)

### Mental language and supposition

Concepts form mental propositions with their own grammar.

*Oratio mentalis*, without Latin's synonyms and ornaments. Early *fictum* theory (concepts have objective being); mature view (most readings): the concept is the act of understanding. Supposition: personal (singulars), simple (the concept), material (the word); never a common nature.

*Introduced:* [8.4](lessons/08-04-ockham-nominalism-and-the-razor.md)

### Ockham razor

Plurality is never to be posited without necessity, and necessity comes from reason, experience or authority.

*Pluralitas numquam est ponenda sine necessitate*; *frustra fit per plura quod potest fieri per pauciora*. Bounds: self-evident, known by experience, or proved by the authority of Scripture (*Ordinatio* I d.30 q.1). Not original to him (ST I q.2 a.3 obj. 2); *entia non sunt multiplicanda praeter necessitatem* traces to John Punch (1639). Not "the simplest theory is true". Applied: no relation-things among creatures; only substances and qualities are absolute things.

*Introduced:* [8.4](lessons/08-04-ockham-nominalism-and-the-razor.md)

### Absolute and ordained power

What God can do without contradiction, versus what he has decreed; a tool for testing which connections are necessary.

Whatever God does through secondary causes he can do without them, so he could preserve an intuitive cognition of a star that does not exist. Ockham (*Quodlibet* VI q.6, common reading): such a cognition would ground the judgment that it does not exist; and God does not work so in the ordained order. Not skepticism, not a deceiving demon.

*Introduced:* [8.4](lessons/08-04-ockham-nominalism-and-the-razor.md)

## Arguments

Skeletons as the lessons state them. "In words" glosses are in the lessons. Where a lesson leaves a reconstruction to a boss problem or a lesson problem, the entry stays at outline level.

### The two ways of inquiry

1. There are only two ways of inquiry to be thought of: "is" and "is not".
2. What is not cannot be known or pointed out.
3. What is there to be spoken of and thought of must be (B3, B6).
4. So "is not" is a path along which nothing can be learned.
5. Mortals take a mixed road, holding to be and not to be "the same and not the same" (B6).
6. The mixed road depends on "is not", so it inherits 4's defect; judge by *logos*, not habit and the senses (B7).
7. ∴ Only "is" remains; B8: what-is is ungenerated, imperishable, whole, unmoved, complete.

**Attacked at:** 2 (atomists: what-is-not exists as void); 1 (Aristotle: "is" and "is not" have more than one sense).

*From* [1.1](lessons/01-01-heraclitus-and-parmenides.md)

### Parmenides against coming-to-be

*(outline; full reconstruction from B8 is Boss 1)* What comes to be must come either from what is or from what is not. What is cannot come to be, because it already is. From what is not nothing could come to be. So nothing comes to be (as Aristotle reports it, *Physics* I.8 191a). The load falls on the ban on what-is-not from the two ways.

*From* [1.1](lessons/01-01-heraclitus-and-parmenides.md), [3.3](lessons/03-03-change-privation-potency-and-act.md)

### Zeno dichotomy

1. To move from start to goal, the runner must first reach the halfway point.
2. The same holds for every remaining stretch, without limit.
3. So the run consists of infinitely many stages, each completed before the goal.
4. Infinitely many things cannot be traversed or completed in a finite time.
5. ∴ Nothing moves from start to goal, as the pluralist understands motion.

**Replies:** the series (stages 1/2, 1/4, ... sum to 1, times to a finite total) denies 4, though the supertask objector says a finite sum is not a completed act; Aristotle VI.2 matches infinite parts of time to parts of distance, and VIII.8 says the halves exist only potentially; atomists deny unlimited division (2).

*From* [1.2](lessons/01-02-zeno-and-the-atomists.md)

### Zeno arrow

*(outline; full reconstruction is 1.2 P3)* At every instant the arrow occupies a space equal to itself; what occupies a space equal to itself is at rest; the flight is composed of instants; so the arrow is at rest throughout. Aristotle denies composition: "time is not composed of indivisible moments" (*Physics* VI.9 239b). The at-at theory keeps instants and denies that motion is a state had at one instant.

*From* [1.2](lessons/01-02-zeno-and-the-atomists.md)

### The peritrope

1. Man-measure: whatever anyone judges is true for the one who judges it.
2. Most people judge that man-measure is false.
3. So it is false for them, and "more untrue than true" (171a).
4. Protagoras, holding all judgments true, must grant his opponents' judgment is true, including that he is wrong.
5. The opponents grant no such thing about him.
6. ∴ The doctrine "will be true neither to himself" nor to anyone else (171c).

**Attacked at:** the step from 4 to 6, where "for them" drops out. Rejoinder (Burnyeat 1976, on one summary): in asserting and teaching the thesis, Protagoras claims more than "true for me".

*From* [1.3](lessons/01-03-the-sophists-and-socrates.md)

### The elenctic pattern

1. The interlocutor asserts a thesis, usually a definition.
2. Socrates secures agreement to further premises that seem independent.
3. The premises entail the negation of the thesis.
4. The interlocutor concludes, or is left to conclude, that the thesis is false.

**Limit:** it shows inconsistency; the thesis goes only because the other premises are held more firmly. *Meno* 73c–d: "the power of governing mankind", repaired with "justly", uses a virtue to define virtue.

*From* [1.3](lessons/01-03-the-sophists-and-socrates.md)

### One over many

1. "Whenever a number of individuals have a common name, we assume them to have also a corresponding idea or form" (*Republic* X 596a).
2. Each such group of things is many.
3. ∴ For each shared name there is one Form, distinct from the many things that bear it.

*From* [2.1](lessons/02-01-the-forms-and-what-goes-wrong-with-them.md)

### The equal itself

1. There is such a thing as the equal itself, and we know it.
2. Sensible equals appear equal and also unequal.
3. The equal itself is never unequal.
4. Things that differ in what can be true of them are not the same thing.
5. ∴ The equal itself is not any sensible equal (*Phaedo* 74a–c).

*From* [2.1](lessons/02-01-the-forms-and-what-goes-wrong-with-them.md)

### The Third Man

*(outline; full reconstruction is Boss 2)* Greatness is posited because many great things share one character; if Greatness is itself great, the same reasoning gives a further Greatness over Greatness and the great things, and so on, so "each idea instead of being one will be infinitely multiplied" (*Parmenides* 132a–b). Vlastos (1954): it needs **one over many**, **self-predication** and **non-identity**, jointly inconsistent. The escape "Forms are thoughts" is blocked: a thought must be of something (132b–c).

*From* [2.1](lessons/02-01-the-forms-and-what-goes-wrong-with-them.md)

### Meno paradox

1. For any subject, a person either knows it or does not.
2. If he knows it, he has no need to inquire.
3. If he does not, he cannot inquire.
4. ∴ Inquiry is either needless or impossible (*Meno* 80e).

Meno's own form asks three questions, including how you would recognize the answer; Socrates hands back a two-horned dilemma.

*From* [2.2](lessons/02-02-recollection-the-line-and-the-cave.md)

### The recollection reply

1. The soul is immortal, born many times, and has seen all things (81c; credited to priests and poets).
2. "All nature is akin", so recovering one thing lets the persistent inquirer recover the rest (81d).
3. So a person can hold knowledge he is not now aware of (premise 1 of the paradox hides a latent and an actual sense).
4. "All enquiry and all learning is but recollection" (81d).
5. The found answer is recognized as the one that was in you.
6. The slave boy produces untaught true opinions, so they were in him (82b–85d).
7. ∴ Inquiry is possible, and we should undertake it.

Socrates will fight only for 7 (86b–c). The boy's questions are leading, and Socrates draws the diagonal.

*From* [2.2](lessons/02-02-recollection-the-line-and-the-cave.md)

### The final argument of the Phaedo

1. Some things that are not themselves opposites always bring an opposite into what they occupy (fire heat, snow cold, three oddness).
2. Such a thing cannot admit the opposite of what it brings (no hot snow).
3. Soul always brings life to what it occupies.
4. Death is the opposite of life.
5. So soul does not admit death: it is deathless.
6. What is deathless is also imperishable (agreed from God and "the form of life itself").
7. ∴ At death the soul withdraws unharmed and does not perish (102a–107a).

**Attacked at:** 6; the snow parallel (106a–c) shows "cannot be dead while it exists" is weaker than "cannot stop existing". Premise 3 needs *psyche* as life-principle.

*From* [2.3](lessons/02-03-the-soul-its-parts-and-immortality.md)

### The affinity argument

*(outline; full reconstruction is 2.3 P2)* There are two kinds of thing, the visible and changing and the invisible and unchanging; composite things are the ones that come apart (78c); the soul is more like the invisible, unchanging kind; so it is likely to be indissoluble. Objections: Simmias's attunement, Cebes's weaver. Tension with *Republic* IV's divided soul.

*From* [2.3](lessons/02-03-the-soul-its-parts-and-immortality.md)

### The division of the soul

1. The same thing cannot do or undergo opposites in the same part, in relation to the same thing, at the same time (436b).
2. Going for something and rejecting it are opposites.
3. Thirst, as thirst, is for drink simply, not good drink.
4. Some thirsty people refuse to drink.
5. So what urges and what forbids are distinct: appetite and reason.
6. Anger fights appetite (Leontius), so spirit is not appetite.
7. Spirit appears in children and animals before reason, and reason rebukes it (Odysseus), so spirit is not reason.
8. ∴ There are three kinds in the soul.

**Attacked at:** 3 blocks the one-desire-for-the-good reading; 1 has slack wherever redescription (other respect, other time) dissolves the opposition.

*From* [2.3](lessons/02-03-the-soul-its-parts-and-immortality.md)

### The elenctic defence of non-contradiction

1. Non-contradiction cannot be demonstrated without begging the question.
2. Do not demand that the opponent say something is or is not; demand only that he "say something which is significant both for himself and for another" (IV.4 1006a).
3. If he signifies something (let "man" signify two-footed animal), signifying one thing already excludes its opposite.
4. So in speaking he relies on the principle he denies.
5. If he says nothing, he cannot give an account of anything and is "no better than a vegetable".

It shows reliance, not proof; the silent opponent is silenced, not refuted.

*From* [3.1](lessons/03-01-predication-categories-and-the-syllogism.md)

### The regress of demonstration

1. If every item of knowledge must be demonstrated, the chain is infinite (nothing known) or circular (a thing prior to itself).
2. Both are unacceptable.
3. ∴ Not all knowledge is demonstrative; immediate first principles are known indemonstrably, by *nous* through induction (*Posterior Analytics* I.3, II.19).

*From* [3.1](lessons/03-01-predication-categories-and-the-syllogism.md)

### Final causes in nature

1. Natural things come about in a given way always or for the most part.
2. What comes about by chance or spontaneity does not.
3. Whatever comes about is either by coincidence or for the sake of something.
4. So natural things do not come about by coincidence (1, 2).
5. So natural things come about for the sake of something (3, 4).
6. Purpose does not require deliberation: "art does not deliberate" (199b).

**Attacked at:** 3, which leaves no room for regular but end-less necessity, the objector's rain (Empedocles' ox-progeny, DK B61). Premise 6 blocks "nobody is aiming".

*From* [3.2](lessons/03-02-nature-and-the-four-causes.md)

### Aristotle reply to Parmenides

1. Every change is from a contrary, or intermediate, to a contrary.
2. Contraries cannot act on each other directly, so something must underlie them.
3. So change needs subject (*hypokeimenon*), form and privation (*steresis*).
4. The subject persists; the privation does not.
5. "X comes to be from Y" is said *per se* or *per accidens* (the doctor builds *qua* builder).
6. What comes to be comes *per se* from the subject, *per accidens* from not-being, namely the privation.
7. So nothing comes to be *without qualification* from what is not, and change is still real.
8. Second route: what comes to be comes from what is potentially it (I.8; *Metaphysics* IX).

He grants Parmenides' principle and denies that the dilemma is exhaustive.

*From* [3.3](lessons/03-03-change-privation-potency-and-act.md)

### The stripping argument

1. If substance is the ultimate subject, strip away every predicate: qualities, then length, breadth and depth.
2. What remains is matter, "neither a particular thing nor of a certain quantity" nor in any category.
3. But "separability and 'thisness' are thought to belong chiefly to substance", and matter has neither.
4. ∴ Matter is not substance; form and compound are better candidates, and form is prior (VII.3).

Being a subject is necessary for substance, not sufficient.

*From* [3.4](lessons/03-04-substance-and-essence.md)

### No universal is substance

*(outline; full reconstruction is 3.4 P3)* A universal is common to many, while the substance of each thing is peculiar to it; so no universal is the substance of anything (VII.13). Aimed at making a universal, a Platonic Form, a separate substance; Boethius's first step and the Plato crux in Boss 3 use it.

*From* [3.4](lessons/03-04-substance-and-essence.md)

### The definition of soul

1. Substance is said of matter, form and compound; matter is potentiality, form actuality.
2. Natural bodies are substances above all, and some are alive.
3. A living body is not itself the soul, since body is subject, not what is had.
4. So soul is substance as form of a natural body that has life potentially, and such form is actuality.
5. Actuality is twofold, like knowledge had and used; soul is present in sleep, so it is the first kind.
6. ∴ Soul is the first actuality of a natural body that has life potentially, a body furnished with organs.
7. Corollary: no question whether soul and body are one, any more than wax and imprint.

*From* [3.5](lessons/03-05-soul-and-intellect.md)

### The two intellects

1. Thinking is like perceiving: the thinker receives the form and is potentially like it.
2. Mind thinks all things, so it must be "unmixed".
3. So it is "nothing at all actually before it thinks", and has no organ.
4. Everywhere in nature there is what is potentially all things of a kind and a cause that makes them.
5. So in the soul there is an intellect that "becomes all things" and one that "makes all things", like light.
6. The second is "separable and impassive and unmixed", and "alone is immortal and eternal" (430a17–23).

*From* [3.5](lessons/03-05-soul-and-intellect.md)

### The argument of Metaphysics XII.6

*(outline; full reconstruction is Boss 3)* Substances are first, so if all were perishable everything would be; but motion and time cannot come to be or perish; only circular locomotion is continuous and eternal; an eternal motion needs a mover that actually moves, and whose essence includes no potency; so there is a principle whose essence is actuality, without matter; it moves as an object of desire and thought (XII.7). Load-bearing: eternal motion, and IX.8's priority of act. Uses substance (3.4), potency and act (3.3), final cause (3.2).

*From* [3.6](lessons/03-06-the-unmoved-mover.md)

### Death is nothing to us

*(the whole Epicurean system as 4.1 outlines it; the death argument alone in standard form is Boss 4(a))*

1. The end of life is pleasure, and its limit the removal of all pain (PD 3).
2. The greatest turmoils come from false beliefs about gods, death and the limits of desire.
3. Everything is bodies and void; bodies are compounds of atoms.
4. The heavens run by atoms, and a blessed being neither suffers nor causes trouble.
5. All good and evil lie in sensation, and death is the privation of sensation, since the soul's atoms disperse.
6. So death is nothing to us: "when we are, death is not come, and, when death is come, we are not" (*Menoeceus* 125).
7. What causes no pain when present causes only empty pain in anticipation.
8. Not everything happens by necessity, because atoms swerve.
9. ∴ Study of nature with prudent reckoning of desires removes the chief causes of turmoil.

**Attacked at:** 5 (Nagel's deprivation account, 1970). Epicurean resource: pleasure cannot be increased by more time (PD 18–20).

*From* [4.1](lessons/04-01-epicurus-atoms-the-swerve-and-death.md)

### The symmetry argument

Before birth, through the wars of Carthage and Rome, we felt nothing and nothing troubled us; the time after death is the mirror nature holds up of that time; so nothing in it is fearful (Lucretius III, around 830–842, 972–977). Nagel's reply: you could not have been born much earlier and still be you, but you could have died later.

*From* [4.1](lessons/04-01-epicurus-atoms-the-swerve-and-death.md)

### Virtue is the only good

1. The good benefits and does not harm, as heat heats and does not cool.
2. Health and wealth can harm as well as benefit.
3. So they are not goods but indifferents.
4. Virtue, knowledge of how to live and use things, always benefits and cannot be misused.
5. ∴ Virtue alone is good, vice alone bad.
6. Some indifferents accord with nature and have selective value: preferred and dispreferred.

*From* [4.2](lessons/04-02-the-stoics-logos-fate-and-virtue.md)

### The cylinder

1. Everything happens by fate, through antecedent causes.
2. Causes are perfect and principal, or auxiliary and proximate.
3. "By antecedent causes" means by auxiliary and proximate ones.
4. Assent needs an impression to set it off, but comes from the mind's own character.
5. A pushed cylinder rolls because of its own shape; a cube would not.
6. ∴ Assent is "in our power", though fated, and praise and blame are fitting (*On Fate* 41–43).

*From* [4.2](lessons/04-02-the-stoics-logos-fate-and-virtue.md)

### The Lazy Argument and the co-fated reply

1. If it is fated that you recover, you will recover whether or not you call a doctor.
2. If it is fated that you do not, you will not, whether or not you call one.
3. One or the other is fated.
4. ∴ Calling a doctor is pointless (*On Fate* 28–30).

**Reply** (Chrysippus): some events are *confatalia*. "Laius will have a son" is fated together with his lying with a woman, so "whether or not you call a doctor" misdescribes a conjoined event. Pressure: calling the doctor is fated too; the Stoic returns to the cylinder, deliberation as principal cause.

*From* [4.2](lessons/04-02-the-stoics-logos-fate-and-virtue.md)

### Arcesilaus against the criterion

1. The wise man never opines.
2. An impression is cognitive only if no false impression could be exactly like it.
3. For any true impression, an indistinguishable false one can occur (twins, Delian eggs, dreams, madness).
4. So there are no cognitive impressions.
5. ∴ The wise man assents to nothing: *epoche* on everything.

**Stoic reply:** deny 3; no two things are wholly alike. Crux: indistinguishable to us versus in principle.

*From* [4.3](lessons/04-03-the-skeptics.md)

### The Agrippan trilemma

1. A claim about the non-evident is disputed (disagreement), so a reason must be given.
2. That reason needs support in turn.
3. Either another reason, without end (infinite regress); or nothing, it is laid down (hypothesis, and the opposite may be laid down too); or the original claim or something resting on it (reciprocity).
4. Relativity blocks appeal to how things are in themselves.
5. ∴ Equipollence, then suspension of judgment.

The three-horned core is a modern distillation of the Five Modes.

*From* [4.3](lessons/04-03-the-skeptics.md)

### The simplicity of the One

1. Whatever is not first depends on something earlier; whatever is composite depends on its components; the First depends on nothing, so has no components (V.4.1).
2. So the One is beyond being (many Forms) and beyond intellect (thinker and thought) (V.6.1, V.3.13).
3. The perfect produces without changing, as fire gives heat; the One "has overflowed" (V.2.1).
4. What first proceeds turns back and becomes Intellect, and at once the whole realm of Being.
5. Intellect's objects are its own content; knower and known identical (V.5, V.9).
6. Intellect produces Soul; part of our soul never descends (IV.8.8).
7. Soul's lowest phase is nature, which makes by contemplating (III.8.3).
8. At the limit is matter: privation, evil itself (I.8).

*From* [4.4](lessons/04-04-plotinus-the-one-intellect-and-soul.md)

### If I am deceived, I am

*Si fallor, sum.*

1. The Academic challenge is "what if you are deceived?", pressed against every assent.
2. Whoever is deceived exists: "he who is not, cannot be deceived".
3. So if I am deceived about whether I exist, I exist.
4. So my belief that I exist cannot be false.
5. This knowledge is not received through a bodily sense or sense image.
6. ∴ At least one thing is known, and "nothing can be known" is false (*City of God* XI.26).

*From* [5.1](lessons/05-01-augustine-certainty-and-illumination.md)

### Interior truth and illumination

1. Some truths are grasped by every reasoning mind alike and cannot change (number; the eternal is to be preferred to the temporal).
2. The mind is changeable.
3. We judge by these truths, not about them.
4. So they are not produced by the mind and not inferior to it.
5. A changeable mind sees unchangeable truth only in a light it receives.
6. ∴ That light is God, the inner teacher.

*From* [5.1](lessons/05-01-augustine-certainty-and-illumination.md)

### Whatever is, is good

1. Whatever is corrupted is harmed, and harm is a loss of good.
2. The supremely good cannot be corrupted; what has no good has nothing to lose.
3. A thing deprived of all good would cease to be (else it would be incorruptible, so better).
4. So as long as a thing exists, it is good.
5. ∴ Evil is not a substance (*Confessions* VII.12 (18)).

*From* [5.2](lessons/05-02-augustine-evil-and-the-will.md)

### The deficient cause of the evil will

1. God authors all substances, and all are good.
2. Evil done differs from evil suffered; wrongdoing is inordinate desire.
3. The will is a middle good, usable well or badly.
4. Iniquity is not a substance but a perversion of the will turned toward lower things.
5. So evil has no author but the will that turns.
6. Two men alike in constitution face one temptation; one consents; nothing is found that caused the evil will (*City of God* XII.6).
7. ∴ Seek no efficient cause: "it is not efficient, but deficient", like trying "to see darkness, or hear silence" (XII.7).

**Two readings:** privation applied consistently, or a name for the absence of explanation (ancestor of the luck objection). The will "could not become evil, were it unwilling" (XII.8).

*From* [5.2](lessons/05-02-augustine-evil-and-the-will.md)

### The three presents

1. The past is no longer and the future is not yet.
2. The present, if it never passed, would be eternity, and any stretch of present divides into past and future, so the present has no length.
3. Yet we measure times, long and short syllables.
4. What we measure must be present when we measure it.
5. What is present is the impression (*affectio*) passing things leave in the mind.
6. ∴ Time is a stretching of the soul across memory, attention and expectation (*Confessions* XI.14–28).

Boss 5 asks for the close reading of XI.26.

*From* [5.3](lessons/05-03-time-eternity-and-foreknowledge.md)

### Boethius on foreknowledge

1. Everything is known according to the nature of the knower (V.4).
2. God's nature is eternal: unending life possessed all at once.
3. So God's knowledge embraces all times as present.
4. Seeing something as present imposes no necessity on it.
5. What God sees must happen, but with conditional necessity only.
6. Conditional necessity does not bring simple necessity with it.
7. ∴ A free act seen by God is necessary given the seeing and free in itself.

The V.3 dilemma it answers is reconstructed in `philosophy-of-religion` Boss 1, not here.

*From* [5.3](lessons/05-03-time-eternity-and-foreknowledge.md)

### The Monologion chain

1. Whatever things are more, less or equally F are F through something that is the same in each.
2. Goods are compared as more and less good.
3. So all goods are good through one thing (horse: strength and speed are not it, since a strong swift robber is bad; usefulness and *honestas* trace back to the one).
4. That thing is good through itself, and what alone is good through itself is supremely good.

A one-over-many engine, climbing from experienced goods.

*From* [6.1](lessons/06-01-anselm-and-the-proslogion-as-history.md)

### Proslogion 2-3 in outline

*(outline only; the full reconstruction and its assessment are `philosophy-of-religion` 2.1)*

1. We believe God is "a being than which nothing greater can be conceived".
2. The fool understands this when he hears it, and what is understood is in the understanding.
3. To be in the understanding is not yet to be understood to exist (the painter).
4. It cannot be in the understanding alone, since it could be conceived to exist in reality, which is greater.
5. So it exists in understanding and reality.
6. (Ch. 3) What cannot be conceived not to exist is greater, so God cannot be conceived not to exist.

**Pressed at:** 2 (Gaunilo: only the words), 4 to 5 (the island; Aquinas: the name gives existence in the mind only).

*From* [6.1](lessons/06-01-anselm-and-the-proslogion-as-history.md)

### The modal division

1. Whatever is considered in itself is necessary, possible or impossible in itself.
2. The possible in itself is indifferent to existing, so if it exists a cause tips the balance.
3. Given its cause it is necessary through another; without it, impossible through another.
4. The necessary in itself has no cause, and its essence is not other than its existence.

*From* [6.2](lessons/06-02-avicenna-essence-existence-and-the-necessary-existent.md)

### The proof of the truthful

1. There is something existent.
2. Every existent is necessary in itself or possible in itself.
3. If necessary in itself, a necessary existent exists.
4. If possible in itself, take the totality of all possible existents, finitely or infinitely many.
5. That totality exists only through its possible members, so it is possible in itself and needs a cause.
6. The cause is not the totality (nothing causes itself) and not a member (it would cause itself with the rest).
7. ∴ The cause lies outside every possible existent and is necessary in itself.

Premise 4 makes it indifferent to an eternal world. **Pressed at:** 5 (does a collection need a cause beyond its members' causes?) and 6.

*From* [6.2](lessons/06-02-avicenna-essence-existence-and-the-necessary-existent.md)

### The flying man

A man created mature, suspended in air, eyes covered, limbs apart, feeling nothing, without memory or sensation, would affirm that he exists without affirming any body or bodily part. What is affirmed differs from what is not affirmed, so the self is not the body (*De Anima* of *The Healing* I.1, V.7). Offered as an aid to noticing, not a demonstration.

*From* [6.2](lessons/06-02-avicenna-essence-existence-and-the-necessary-existent.md)

### The Law commands philosophy

1. The Law commands the capable to study beings by demonstration.
2. The Law is true and calls to a study that leads to truth.
3. Truth does not contradict truth.
4. So where demonstration contradicts scripture's apparent sense, the apparent sense is not what is meant.
5. Such verses are interpreted by the figurative ways of Arabic.
6. Interpretations belong to the demonstrative class and are not declared to the dialectical and rhetorical.

*From* [6.3](lessons/06-03-averroes-and-maimonides.md)

### Maimonides for creation

1. Three opinions: Mosaic, Platonic, Aristotelian (II.13).
2. Aristotle's arguments for eternity are not demonstrations, and he knew it (II.15).
3. You cannot infer how a thing came to be from how it behaves once complete (II.17).
4. So neither eternity nor creation is demonstrated.
5. Creation passages could be read figuratively (II.25).
6. But figurative reading is required only where demonstration forces it; incorporeality is demonstrated, eternity is not.
7. Aristotelian eternity by necessity would overturn the Law (no miracle).
8. ∴ Hold creation; the Platonic view could be reconciled if demonstrated.

*From* [6.3](lessons/06-03-averroes-and-maimonides.md)

### Faith cannot contradict reason

1. Our knowledge is proportioned to our grasp of essence and starts from the senses (SCG I.3).
2. Sensible effects show that God is, not what God is.
3. So some truths about God are within reason's reach and others exceed it.
4. Even the reachable ones are fittingly proposed for belief (I.4).
5. Naturally known principles are most true, what faith holds is confirmed by God, and only the false is opposed to the true (I.7).
6. God authors our nature and its principles; a teacher who plants contrary knowledge deceives.
7. ∴ Any argument against the faith is probable or sophistical, not a demonstration, and can be answered.

*From* [7.1](lessons/07-01-faith-reason-and-the-paris-crisis.md)

### This man understands

1. Each one is conscious that it is himself who understands (*hic homo intelligit*; ST I q.76 a.1).
2. If the intellect were a separate substance joined to Socrates only through his phantasms, the understanding would not be his act.
3. Likenesses of colours in my eye make the wall *seen*, not seeing; so phantasms in Socrates make him *understood*, not understanding.
4. ∴ The intellect is a power of the soul that is Socrates' form.

Argued from Aristotle's principles, not faith (*On the Unity of the Intellect*, 1270). Against the "reason concludes one intellect, faith the opposite" stance: that makes faith hold the false and impossible (ch. 5).

*From* [7.1](lessons/07-01-faith-reason-and-the-paris-crisis.md)

### The intellectus essentiae argument

*De Ente et Essentia* ch. 4, paraphrased.

1. Every essence can be understood without anything understood about its existence (*quid est homo vel phoenix*).
2. So existence is other than essence, unless some thing's essence is its existence.
3. There can be at most one such thing: a nature multiplies only by an added difference, by reception in different matters, or by being received rather than standing alone, and pure existence admits none.
4. So in everything else existence is other than essence.
5. What a thing has beyond its essence is caused by its principles or from outside; nothing causes its own existence; so received existence comes from a first cause that is existence alone.
6. What receives from another is in potency to what it receives; so essence stands to *esse* as potency to act.

**Disputed step:** 1 is about understanding, 4 about things (Wippel: 3 completes the real distinction before God; Owens: only at 5). God's case: we do not grasp his essence, so the phoenix test cannot be run on him (q.3 a.4 ad 2).

*From* [7.2](lessons/07-02-aquinas-essence-and-existence.md)

### God is his own existence

*(outline; full reconstruction is Boss 7)* ST I q.3 a.4 gives three reasons: existence other than essence must be caused, but God is the first efficient cause; existence is to essence as act to potency, but God has no potency; what has existence without being it is a being by participation, but God is the first being. Depends on Avicenna's distinction (6.2) and the priority of act (3.6).

*From* [7.2](lessons/07-02-aquinas-essence-and-existence.md)

### The method of ST I q.2 a.1-2

1. A proposition is self-evident when its predicate is included in the essence of its subject.
2. In "God exists" predicate and subject are the same, so it is self-evident in itself.
3. We do not know God's essence, so it is not self-evident to us.
4. Demonstration is through the cause (*propter quid*) or through the effect (*quia*).
5. From an effect better known to us, its proper cause's existence can be demonstrated.
6. In a demonstration *quia*, the meaning of the name is the middle term.
7. ∴ God's existence can be demonstrated from effects, and a.3 does so five times.

*From* [7.3](lessons/07-03-the-five-ways-as-text.md)

### The Five Ways

**Common shape:** (i) an observed fact; (ii) a dependence principle; (iii) a stopping principle (no infinite regress in *this kind* of series, a maximum that causes the rest, or a knower that directs); (iv) a first, a maximum or a director; (v) the naming step, "and this everyone understands to be God", tied to the meaning of the name (a.2 ad 2).

| Way | (i) Fact | (ii)-(iii) Principles | (iv) Reaches | Ingredients from |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Motion | Some things are in motion | Nothing goes from potency to act except by something in act; no infinite regress of movers | A first mover, moved by no other | [3.3](lessons/03-03-change-privation-potency-and-act.md), [3.6](lessons/03-06-the-unmoved-mover.md) |
| 2. Efficient cause | There is an order of efficient causes | Nothing causes itself; no infinite regress of efficient causes | A first efficient cause | [3.2](lessons/03-02-nature-and-the-four-causes.md) |
| 3. Possibility and necessity | Some things come to be and pass away | What can not-be at some time is not; no regress of caused necessary beings | A being necessary of itself | [6.2](lessons/06-02-avicenna-essence-existence-and-the-necessary-existent.md) |
| 4. Gradation | Things are more and less good, true, noble | More and less by approach to a maximum; the maximum in a kind causes the rest | Cause of being, goodness, every perfection | [2.1](lessons/02-01-the-forms-and-what-goes-wrong-with-them.md) |
| 5. Governance | Things without knowledge act for an end | What lacks knowledge tends to an end only if directed by a knower (the archer) | An intelligent director of natural things | [3.2](lessons/03-02-nature-and-the-four-causes.md) |

**Second Way:** table level only; the numbered reconstruction, and whether the article itself distinguishes per se from per accidens series, is Boss 7.

**Third Way, first half:**
1. We find things generated and corrupted, so possible to be and not to be.
2. What is possible not to be at some time is not.
3. So if everything is possible not to be, at some time nothing was.
4. If at some time nothing was, nothing would exist now, since what does not exist begins only through something existing.
5. Something exists now, so something is necessary.

Second half: that necessary thing has its necessity from another or of itself; no regress, so a being necessary of itself. "Necessary" means without potency to not-be. **Weak step:** 3, a quantifier shift (Kenny 1969). Charitable routes: infinite time realizes every possibility (Maimonides, *Guide* II.1); a restricted domain (Knasas); the Latin *fuit* ("was") rather than the translation's "could have been".

**Fifth Way:** regularity ("always or nearly always") shows action for an end, not by chance but by intention; what lacks knowledge moves to an end only if directed; so an intelligent being directs natural things. Not Paley's contrivance; step (iii) goes past Aristotle.

*From* [7.3](lessons/07-03-the-five-ways-as-text.md)

### Boethius against a universal as one thing

1. Nothing common to many at once is one thing; a genus wholly in many at once cannot be numerically one, and a further genus over the many starts a regress.
2. A thought is false when it combines things otherwise than they are, not when it considers one thing without another joined to it.
3. The mathematician thinks a line apart from body, though no line exists apart from body.
4. ∴ Genera and species subsist in sensibles and are understood apart from them.

*From* [8.1](lessons/08-01-porphyrys-questions-universals-to-aquinas.md)

### Abelard against indifference

1. William: each man's humanity is his own, and the humanities do not *differ*.
2. Either "not different" means "the same", or it means only "alike".
3. If the same, one essence is in two places, bears contraries, and makes all men one man.
4. If only alike, nothing is shared and the view has left realism.
5. ∴ Non-difference is identity in disguise or likeness in disguise.

A reconstruction; William is known largely through Abelard.

*From* [8.1](lessons/08-01-porphyrys-questions-universals-to-aquinas.md)

### Certain and doubtful concepts

1. An intellect certain of one concept and doubtful of others has a certain concept distinct from the doubtful ones.
2. A wayfarer can be certain God is a being while doubting whether God is finite or infinite, created or uncreated.
3. So the concept of being he is certain of is distinct from *finite being* and *infinite being*.
4. It is neither of the two but included in each.
5. A concept included in both, and one by the contradiction and middle-term test, is univocal.
6. ∴ Being is univocal to God and creatures.

**Pressed at:** 1 (Henry: certainty may attach to a confused pair). Further arguments: perfections purified of creaturely limits need something of the concept to stay the same; without univocity no natural concept of God could be formed.

*From* [8.2](lessons/08-02-scotus-the-univocity-of-being.md)

### Less-than-numerical unity

1. What belongs to a nature of itself belongs to it wherever it is.
2. Suppose the nature of stone were of itself this singular.
3. Then being in any other stone would be incompatible with the nature.
4. But the intellect grasps stone as universal without grasping it under a character opposed to its own.
5. So the nature is not of itself singular (reductio).
6. Yet it has real unity prior to any act of intellect (real likeness needs a real foundation).
7. So the nature has a real unity less than numerical.
8. This lamb is really and numerically one and incommunicable.
9. Negation, existence, quantity and matter cannot make a substance this.
10. So a positive substantial entity, the individual difference, contracts the nature, adding no new "what".
11. Nature and individual difference are neither separable things nor distinct only in thought: formally distinct.

*From* [8.3](lessons/08-03-scotus-common-nature-and-haecceity.md)

### Ockham against the formal distinction

1. On Scotus's view, the nature in Socrates is common and the haecceity is not.
2. Where a predicate and its contradictory are truly affirmed and denied, there must be distinct items.
3. Outside the mind, items are either really distinct things or one and the same thing.
4. If nature and haecceity are really distinct things, the nature is itself singular, so not common.
5. If they are one thing, contradictory predicates are true of one thing.
6. ∴ No common nature distinct from the individual, and no formal distinction in creatures.

Naming the premise Scotus must deny is Boss 8. Ockham allows the formal distinction for the divine essence and persons only.

*From* [8.4](lessons/08-04-ockham-nominalism-and-the-razor.md)

### What a universal is for Ockham

1. Everything outside the soul is singular.
2. A universal is what is predicable of many.
3. Only signs are predicated.
4. So a universal is a sign that signifies many.
5. Spoken and written terms signify by convention, subordinated to concepts, which signify by nature.
6. ∴ Universals in the primary sense are concepts, each singular, universal only in what it signifies.

*From* [8.4](lessons/08-04-ockham-nominalism-and-the-razor.md)

**How to read the loci.**

- **DK 28 B8**: Diels–Kranz. The first number is the thinker (22 Heraclitus, 28 Parmenides, 29 Zeno, 59 Anaxagoras, 68 Democritus, 80 Protagoras); A is testimony about him, B his own words; then the fragment number. "B8.22–25" adds lines.
- **Stephanus pages (Plato):** *Phaedo* 74a–c, *Republic* VI 509b = page number of the 1578 edition plus section letter a–e; the book number is added for the *Republic*.
- **Bekker numbers (Aristotle):** *Metaphysics* IV.3 1005b19–20 = page 1005, column b, lines 19–20. Book and chapter come first; *Metaphysics* books are given as Roman numerals (VII = Zeta, XII = Lambda).
- **Enneads:** I.6.9 = Ennead I, treatise 6, chapter 9.
- **Diogenes Laertius:** DL X.125 = book X, section 125. **PD 11** = *Principal Doctrines* 11 (in DL X.139–154). *Menoeceus* 125 uses DL's section numbers.
- **Sextus:** *PH* I.8 = *Outlines of Pyrrhonism* book I, section 8; *M* VII.248 = *Against the Mathematicians* VII, section 248.
- **Cicero:** *On Fate* 41 and *Academica* II.77 = (book and) section. **Lucretius** III.830 = book III, line 830.
- **Augustine:** *Confessions* VII.12 (18) = book VII, chapter 12, paragraph 18. *City of God* XI.26 = book, chapter.
- **Boethius:** *Consolation* V.6 = book V, prose 6.
- **Aquinas:** ST I q.2 a.3 = *Summa Theologiae*, First Part, question 2, article 3; "obj. 2" = the second objection; "ad 2" = the reply to it; the unmarked body is the *respondeo*. I-II and II-II are the two halves of the Second Part. SCG I.7 = *Summa contra Gentiles* book I, chapter 7. *De Ente* ch. 4 = chapter.
- **Maimonides:** *Guide* II.13 = part II, chapter 13.
- **Scotus and Ockham:** *Ordinatio* I d.3 p.1 qq.1–2 = book I, distinction 3, part 1, questions 1–2. *Summa Logicae* I.16 = part I, chapter 16. *Quodlibet* VI q.6 = quodlibet VI, question 6.
- **Averroes and al-Ghazali** are cited by work and topic; the Qur'an by sura:verse (59:2).

## Pitfalls

### Module 1: The Presocratics and the Socratic turn

- *Logos* in Heraclitus is an order or proportion, and in Parmenides B7 reasoning; reading it as formal logic is anachronism. *([1.1](lessons/01-01-heraclitus-and-parmenides.md))*
- Parmenides' "is" is not the existential quantifier; say whether you read it existentially, predicatively or veridically. *([1.1](lessons/01-01-heraclitus-and-parmenides.md))*
- Fragments are quotations chosen for later authors' purposes, not miniature books. *([1.1](lessons/01-01-heraclitus-and-parmenides.md))*
- Parmenides does not ignore experience; step 6 argues about which authority wins, and the poem goes on to a cosmology. *([1.1](lessons/01-01-heraclitus-and-parmenides.md))*
- Zeno's arguments draw consequences from the pluralist's premises; they do not report that he believed motion never happens. *([1.2](lessons/01-02-zeno-and-the-atomists.md))*
- "Infinite divisibility" differs for Zeno, Aristotle, the atomists and Anaxagoras; say which kind, of what. *([1.2](lessons/01-02-zeno-and-the-atomists.md))*
- "Zeno didn't know about limits" is true and beside the point; whether the arithmetic answers him is the dispute. *([1.2](lessons/01-02-zeno-and-the-atomists.md))*
- The atomists' void is what-is-not, a thesis about being, not just empty space. *([1.2](lessons/01-02-zeno-and-the-atomists.md))*
- Say "Plato's Socrates": Aristophanes, Xenophon, Plato and Aristotle give different figures. *([1.3](lessons/01-03-the-sophists-and-socrates.md))*
- "I know that I know nothing" is not in the *Apology*; Socrates disclaims the conceit of knowledge (21d). *([1.3](lessons/01-03-the-sophists-and-socrates.md))*
- *Arete* is excellence, closer to a craft than to Victorian moral virtue. *([1.3](lessons/01-03-the-sophists-and-socrates.md))*
- "Sophist" is not yet an insult, and "relativist" is our label for a Protagoras known mainly through Plato. *([1.3](lessons/01-03-the-sophists-and-socrates.md))*

### Module 2: Plato

- A Platonic *idea* is not a mental item; Plato has Socrates try that reading and drop it (*Parmenides* 132b–c). *([2.1](lessons/02-01-the-forms-and-what-goes-wrong-with-them.md))*
- Forms are not just Aristotelian universals; they are also paradigms, which is where self-predication lives. *([2.1](lessons/02-01-the-forms-and-what-goes-wrong-with-them.md))*
- *Doxa* is not weakly justified belief about the same objects as knowledge; *Republic* V distinguishes the powers by their objects. *([2.1](lessons/02-01-the-forms-and-what-goes-wrong-with-them.md))*
- The *Parmenides* objections do not show Plato abandoned the Forms; later dialogues still use them. *([2.1](lessons/02-01-the-forms-and-what-goes-wrong-with-them.md))*
- Recollection is not Descartes's innate ideas: the knowledge was acquired before birth and recovered by questioning. *([2.2](lessons/02-02-recollection-the-line-and-the-cave.md))*
- The slave boy was led: Socrates' questions steer, and he draws the diagonal himself. *([2.2](lessons/02-02-recollection-the-line-and-the-cave.md))*
- Line labels vary by translator (Jowett's "reason" and "understanding"); go by the Greek. *([2.2](lessons/02-02-recollection-the-line-and-the-cave.md))*
- The Good "beyond being" has no will and creates nothing; it is not the God of later theism. *([2.2](lessons/02-02-recollection-the-line-and-the-cave.md))*
- *Psyche* is the life-principle, not consciousness; read it as mind and the final argument's premise 3 stops being true by definition. *([2.3](lessons/02-03-the-soul-its-parts-and-immortality.md))*
- A *harmonia* is an attunement, not a tune, and not functionalism. *([2.3](lessons/02-03-the-soul-its-parts-and-immortality.md))*
- The three parts are not three little people; they are also called "kinds", and the agent-like reading is disputed. *([2.3](lessons/02-03-the-soul-its-parts-and-immortality.md))*
- Recollection alone proves only pre-existence, by the text's own admission (77b–c). *([2.3](lessons/02-03-the-soul-its-parts-and-immortality.md))*

### Module 3: Aristotle

- Aristotle's "syllogism" is any argument whose conclusion follows of necessity; "distributed", "illicit major" and the mood names are medieval. *([3.1](lessons/03-01-predication-categories-and-the-syllogism.md))*
- Subalternation is not "a fallacy"; the two systems read A differently, and Aristotle's terms all denote. *([3.1](lessons/03-01-predication-categories-and-the-syllogism.md))*
- The categories classify what is signified, not words. *([3.1](lessons/03-01-predication-categories-and-the-syllogism.md))*
- The elenctic defence is not a proof of non-contradiction; a silent opponent is silenced, not refuted. *([3.1](lessons/03-01-predication-categories-and-the-syllogism.md))*
- "Cause" is not only what starts a change; the bronze and the 2:1 ratio are causes. *([3.2](lessons/03-02-nature-and-the-four-causes.md))*
- A final cause is not an intention; the need for a directing mind is Aquinas's addition. *([3.2](lessons/03-02-nature-and-the-four-causes.md))*
- Empedocles did not anticipate Darwin: his ox-progeny are one-off combinations, not heredity. *([3.2](lessons/03-02-nature-and-the-four-causes.md))*
- Formal cause is not shape: for a horse it is the definition of the kind. *([3.2](lessons/03-02-nature-and-the-four-causes.md))*
- *Kinesis* covers alteration and growth, not only locomotion. *([3.3](lessons/03-03-change-privation-potency-and-act.md))*
- Potency is not logical possibility; it belongs to a subject and is bounded by what it is. *([3.3](lessons/03-03-change-privation-potency-and-act.md))*
- Privation is not a fourth thing; it is not-being in a subject. *([3.3](lessons/03-03-change-privation-potency-and-act.md))*
- Aristotle does not refute Parmenides' principle; he grants it and denies that the dilemma is exhaustive. *([3.3](lessons/03-03-change-privation-potency-and-act.md))*
- *Ousia* is not Locke's "something I know not what"; VII.3 states that view and rejects it. *([3.4](lessons/03-04-substance-and-essence.md))*
- A secondary substance is not an abstract object outside space. *([3.4](lessons/03-04-substance-and-essence.md))*
- "Present in" means dependence, not location; a hand is a part, not present in Socrates. *([3.4](lessons/03-04-substance-and-essence.md))*
- The *Metaphysics* does not refute the *Categories*; it asks a different question. *([3.4](lessons/03-04-substance-and-essence.md))*
- Soul is not mind: plants have one. *([3.5](lessons/03-05-soul-and-intellect.md))*
- Form of the body is not shape or arrangement; a corpse keeps its shape. *([3.5](lessons/03-05-soul-and-intellect.md))*
- "Agent intellect" is the commentators' label, and III.5's "immortal" is not obviously personal immortality ("we do not remember"). *([3.5](lessons/03-05-soul-and-intellect.md))*
- "Pure act" and "unmoved mover" are later labels carrying Thomist meanings Book XII lacks. *([3.6](lessons/03-06-the-unmoved-mover.md))*
- The mover is loved; it does not love, will or attend to anything. *([3.6](lessons/03-06-the-unmoved-mover.md))*
- "Depend" is not "created by"; nothing in XII gives the world existence. *([3.6](lessons/03-06-the-unmoved-mover.md))*
- Thought thinking itself is not self-consciousness, and not Hegel's Spirit. *([3.6](lessons/03-06-the-unmoved-mover.md))*

### Module 4: The Hellenistic schools and Plotinus

- "Epicurean" does not mean gourmet. *([4.1](lessons/04-01-epicurus-atoms-the-swerve-and-death.md))*
- *Ataraxia* is a katastematic pleasure, not numbness; "mere absence" is Cicero's reading. *([4.1](lessons/04-01-epicurus-atoms-the-swerve-and-death.md))*
- The swerve is not quantum indeterminacy. *([4.1](lessons/04-01-epicurus-atoms-the-swerve-and-death.md))*
- "All perceptions are true" does not make the tower round; the judgment is opinion. *([4.1](lessons/04-01-epicurus-atoms-the-swerve-and-death.md))*
- Stoic is not "stoic": passions are false judgments to be corrected, and first movements are allowed. *([4.2](lessons/04-02-the-stoics-logos-fate-and-virtue.md))*
- Fate is not fatalism: the outcome comes through what you do. *([4.2](lessons/04-02-the-stoics-logos-fate-and-virtue.md))*
- The Stoic god is a body immanent in the world, not Aquinas's transcendent being. *([4.2](lessons/04-02-the-stoics-logos-fate-and-virtue.md))*
- "Indifferent" does not mean "don't care"; preferred indifferents have selective value. *([4.2](lessons/04-02-the-stoics-logos-fate-and-virtue.md))*
- The skeptic concedes that honey appears sweet; he suspends on whether it is sweet in nature, and his target is dogma, not the external world. *([4.3](lessons/04-03-the-skeptics.md))*
- Academics and Pyrrhonists differ: Sextus calls the New Academy negative dogmatists. *([4.3](lessons/04-03-the-skeptics.md))*
- "Agrippa's trilemma" is a modern distillation of five modes. *([4.3](lessons/04-03-the-skeptics.md))*
- Suspension is not a technique chosen to buy calm; the calm came after giving up. *([4.3](lessons/04-03-the-skeptics.md))*
- *Pithanon* is "persuasive", not a probability. *([4.3](lessons/04-03-the-skeptics.md))*
- Procession is not an event in time and not a Big Bang. *([4.4](lessons/04-04-plotinus-the-one-intellect-and-soul.md))*
- "Neoplatonist" is a modern label; Plotinus claimed only to make Plato explicit. *([4.4](lessons/04-04-plotinus-the-one-intellect-and-soul.md))*
- The One is neither pantheism nor a personal creator: "all things and no one of them". *([4.4](lessons/04-04-plotinus-the-one-intellect-and-soul.md))*
- "Beyond being" means prior to multiplicity, not unreal. *([4.4](lessons/04-04-plotinus-the-one-intellect-and-soul.md))*

### Module 5: Augustine and Boethius

- Augustine's "if I am deceived, I am" is not Descartes's foundation; it is one counterexample among several. *([5.1](lessons/05-01-augustine-certainty-and-illumination.md))*
- "Interior" does not mean private; the truths found within are common to all minds. *([5.1](lessons/05-01-augustine-certainty-and-illumination.md))*
- Illumination does not replace the senses; sensibles are learned by sense and testimony. *([5.1](lessons/05-01-augustine-certainty-and-illumination.md))*
- The light is not Kant's a priori; it is expressly not the mind's own. *([5.1](lessons/05-01-augustine-certainty-and-illumination.md))*
- Evil as privation does not make evil unreal; blindness is real. *([5.2](lessons/05-02-augustine-evil-and-the-will.md))*
- *Liberum arbitrium* is not libertarian free will; the question is how a good creature authors evil. *([5.2](lessons/05-02-augustine-evil-and-the-will.md))*
- "Augustine invented the will" is disputed (Dihle against Frede). *([5.2](lessons/05-02-augustine-evil-and-the-will.md))*
- The privation account is not assessed here as a theodicy. *([5.2](lessons/05-02-augustine-evil-and-the-will.md))*
- Eternity is not time that never ends; that is perpetuity. *([5.3](lessons/05-03-time-eternity-and-foreknowledge.md))*
- Boethius's two necessities are not, as exegesis, the modern scope distinction. *([5.3](lessons/05-03-time-eternity-and-foreknowledge.md))*
- Augustine does not make time an illusion, a Kantian form, or a presentist ontology. *([5.3](lessons/05-03-time-eternity-and-foreknowledge.md))*
- God does not "foresee": *providentia*, not *praevidentia*. *([5.3](lessons/05-03-time-eternity-and-foreknowledge.md))*

### Module 6: Anselm, and the Arabic and Jewish philosophers

- "The ontological argument" is Kant's label, not Anselm's. *([6.1](lessons/06-01-anselm-and-the-proslogion-as-history.md))*
- Gaunilo was a monk writing on behalf of the fool, not a skeptic or atheist. *([6.1](lessons/06-01-anselm-and-the-proslogion-as-history.md))*
- The *Proslogion* is not the *Monologion* shortened; one is a chain from experienced goods, the other one argument from a formula. *([6.1](lessons/06-01-anselm-and-the-proslogion-as-history.md))*
- "Faith seeking understanding" means neither faith replacing reason nor the reverse. *([6.1](lessons/06-01-anselm-and-the-proslogion-as-history.md))*
- Avicenna's necessary existent is not the S5 necessary being. *([6.2](lessons/06-02-avicenna-essence-existence-and-the-necessary-existent.md))*
- "Possible in itself" is not modern contingency: whatever exists is necessary through its cause. *([6.2](lessons/06-02-avicenna-essence-existence-and-the-necessary-existent.md))*
- Existence as "accident" need not mean a categorial accident; but the Latins did read it so. *([6.2](lessons/06-02-avicenna-essence-existence-and-the-necessary-existent.md))*
- The flying man is not the *cogito*: no radical doubt, and the soul remains form of a body. *([6.2](lessons/06-02-avicenna-essence-existence-and-the-necessary-existent.md))*
- Averroes did not teach "double truth"; his formula says truth does not contradict truth. *([6.3](lessons/06-03-averroes-and-maimonides.md))*
- Not "science versus religion": philosophy is demonstrative science, *shari'a* the whole of revealed guidance, and both men argue from inside a tradition. *([6.3](lessons/06-03-averroes-and-maimonides.md))*
- Maimonides does not reject eternity because Genesis says so; he denies there is a demonstration. *([6.3](lessons/06-03-averroes-and-maimonides.md))*
- Averroes is not Latin "Averroism". *([6.3](lessons/06-03-averroes-and-maimonides.md))*

### Module 7: Aquinas

- "Double truth" names a charge, not a school. *([7.1](lessons/07-01-faith-reason-and-the-paris-crisis.md))*
- "Latin Averroist" does not mean a reader of the *Decisive Treatise*; the label itself is disputed. *([7.1](lessons/07-01-faith-reason-and-the-paris-crisis.md))*
- The Paris crisis is not modern "science versus religion"; Duhem's thesis about 1277 is a disputed reading of consequences. *([7.1](lessons/07-01-faith-reason-and-the-paris-crisis.md))*
- Harmony does not promise that reason proves faith; for truths above reason, objections can only be answered. *([7.1](lessons/07-01-faith-reason-and-the-paris-crisis.md))*
- *Esse* is not the existential quantifier, and not Kant's "existence is not a real predicate". *([7.2](lessons/07-02-aquinas-essence-and-existence.md))*
- *Esse* is an act, not a very general property. *([7.2](lessons/07-02-aquinas-essence-and-existence.md))*
- "Real distinction" does not mean two things, and the label postdates Aquinas. *([7.2](lessons/07-02-aquinas-essence-and-existence.md))*
- Essence/*esse* is not matter/form again: angels have the first without the second. *([7.2](lessons/07-02-aquinas-essence-and-existence.md))*
- *Motus* is reduction from potency to act, so inertia does not settle the First Way by the word. *([7.3](lessons/07-03-the-five-ways-as-text.md))*
- The naming step does not claim the God of Christian faith is proved; qq.3 onward do further work. *([7.3](lessons/07-03-the-five-ways-as-text.md))*
- The no-regress premises concern per se series, not an infinite past. *([7.3](lessons/07-03-the-five-ways-as-text.md))*
- The *Summa* is a textbook; the motion argument runs for pages in SCG I.13. *([7.3](lessons/07-03-the-five-ways-as-text.md))*

### Module 8: Universals, Scotus and Ockham

- Abelard is not a nominalist in the Ockhamist sense; his *status* gives predication a real ground. *([8.1](lessons/08-01-porphyrys-questions-universals-to-aquinas.md))*
- "Moderate realism" and "conceptualism" are modern labels. *([8.1](lessons/08-01-porphyrys-questions-universals-to-aquinas.md))*
- Aquinas's absolutely considered nature is a way of considering, not a third thing. *([8.1](lessons/08-01-porphyrys-questions-universals-to-aquinas.md))*
- Porphyry's questions concern genera and species, not "properties". *([8.1](lessons/08-01-porphyrys-questions-universals-to-aquinas.md))*
- There was no "Aquinas's reply to Scotus": Aquinas died in 1274; Scotus attacks Henry, and the Thomist reply is Cajetan's. *([8.2](lessons/08-02-scotus-the-univocity-of-being.md))*
- Univocity is a thesis about concepts, not a shared nature or genus. *([8.2](lessons/08-02-scotus-the-univocity-of-being.md))*
- Modes are not differentiae; otherwise God would be composed of genus and difference. *([8.2](lessons/08-02-scotus-the-univocity-of-being.md))*
- The onto-theology charge is a reading (Milbank, Pickstock), contested (Cross 2001), not a report. *([8.2](lessons/08-02-scotus-the-univocity-of-being.md))*
- The common nature is not a Platonic Form or a universal in things. *([8.3](lessons/08-03-scotus-common-nature-and-haecceity.md))*
- Haecceity is not a bare particular or a haecceitistic property; the modern debate borrowed the name. *([8.3](lessons/08-03-scotus-common-nature-and-haecceity.md))*
- The formal distinction is not a distinction of reason with a fancy name: it holds prior to any intellect. *([8.3](lessons/08-03-scotus-common-nature-and-haecceity.md))*
- Less-than-numerical unity is a different kind of unity, not a fraction. *([8.3](lessons/08-03-scotus-common-nature-and-haecceity.md))*
- Ockham's razor does not say the simplest theory is true; it bars positing things without reason, experience or authority. *([8.4](lessons/08-04-ockham-nominalism-and-the-razor.md))*
- Nominalism is not "just words": primary universals are concepts, natural signs. *([8.4](lessons/08-04-ockham-nominalism-and-the-razor.md))*
- Ockham does not deny that Socrates and Plato really are alike; he denies agreement is a thing. *([8.4](lessons/08-04-ockham-nominalism-and-the-razor.md))*
- Absolute power is not skepticism or a deceiving demon; it concerns what involves no contradiction. *([8.4](lessons/08-04-ockham-nominalism-and-the-razor.md))*
