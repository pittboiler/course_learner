# The Thomistic Synthesis · Reference Card

> Open book. This card is meant to be open while you work — including during
> quizzes. Nothing here is something you're expected to recall cold.

This course reads Aquinas as the Church's common doctor: one working system in which theology does its thinking, and the vocabulary the dogmatic sequence will use without stopping to explain it. Use the card mid-problem to look up a scholastic term, the precise statement of a doctrine or a distinction, a canonical argument as a numbered skeleton, where a citation lands in the plan of the *Summa*, who a commentator or a twentieth-century Thomist was and what divides him from the next man — and, above all, **what level a claim sits at and which act fixes it there**. Levels are marked only by citing [`fundamental-theology` 4.4](../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-04-the-ladder-of-doctrinal-authority.md); where that course and this one differ, it wins. Disputes here are maps, not verdicts. Where an entry touches a boss problem it stops at outline level: what the text is and what the positions are called, never the worked reading.

## Positions and distinctions

One `###` per doctrine or distinction: the plain-English line first, then the precise statement, then the lesson that introduced it.

### Sed contra

The one-line counter-move that answers the objections — almost always an authority, and **not always Aquinas's own answer**.

It marks where the weight of the tradition falls and licenses the enquiry. Usually Aquinas lands with it; "usually" is not "always". The standing counter-case is ST I q.13 a.5, whose *sed contra* argues for **equivocity** by two compact arguments from reason, and whose closing paragraph refuses it in writing: those arguments prove the names are not univocal, but do not prove they are equivocal. Before quoting a *sed contra* as Aquinas's teaching, read to the end of the article.

*Introduced:* [1.2](lessons/01-02-reading-a-whole-question.md); used in [2.3](lessons/02-03-participation.md), [3.1](lessons/03-01-demonstrating-that-god-exists.md), [4.4](lessons/04-04-analogy.md)

### Distinguo

The *respondeo* states one distinction; each reply is that distinction laid against a single objection, which is why replies are often one line.

Skip the *respondeo* and the replies read as a brush-off; read the question as a unit and they read as receipts. The model case is ST I q.13 a.6, where the distinction is *what the name signifies* against *the imposition of the name*, and reply 1 in full is "This objection refers to the imposition of the name."

*Introduced:* [1.2](lessons/01-02-reading-a-whole-question.md)

### Reverent interpretation

When a recognized authority has said something he cannot accept flatly, Aquinas keeps the words and supplies a sense in which they are true, rather than saying the man erred.

Usually done in a reply: ST I q.13 a.1 ad 1 keeps Dionysius's "of Him there is neither name" by giving the *reason why* he is said to be above being named; ST I q.75 a.4 ad 1 saves 2 Corinthians' "inward man" by the rule that a thing is named from what is principal in it, as a state is named from its governor. The cost is exegetical and real: **Aquinas's Damascene is not a report of Damascene.** Note the asymmetry — an authority inside the tradition is rescued in a reply, while one outside it (Rabbi Moses at q.13 a.2) is named in the *respondeo* and refuted.

*Introduced:* [1.2](lessons/01-02-reading-a-whole-question.md); used in [5.3](lessons/05-03-the-human-soul-in-the-system.md)

### Formal object

The aspect under which a power or a science takes its material; what makes a science one.

Not a list of subjects but a light. Man, ass and stone are one object for sight because each is coloured, and colour is sight's formal object (ST I q.1 a.3). The unity of a faculty or habit is gauged by its object "not in its material aspect, but as regards the precise formality under which it is an object". Two consequences: the same sentence can belong to two sciences (the astronomer and the natural philosopher both prove the earth round, a.1 ad 2), and a science of God can treat creatures without changing its subject.

*Introduced:* [1.3](lessons/01-03-sacred-doctrine-one-science-about-god.md)

### Sacred doctrine

Revealed teaching as **one** science, whose subject is God and which takes everything it treats under one formality: as divinely revealed.

ST I q.1 a.3 with a.7: God is the subject, and creatures enter only so far as they are referable to him as their beginning or end (a.3 ad 1). Hence the rest of q.1 — speculative and practical, speculative rather (a.4); noblest, by the certitude of its light and the worth of its matter (a.5); wisdom, since it judges by the highest cause (a.6). Its principles are the articles of faith, held from a higher knowledge, which is [subalternation](#subalternation), owned elsewhere. The English Dominican translation renders *subiectum* as "object" in a.7; read it as subject matter.

*Introduced:* [1.3](lessons/01-03-sacred-doctrine-one-science-about-god.md)

### Subalternation

A science that takes its first principles on authority from a higher science.

Sacred doctrine's higher science is God's own knowledge and the blessed's (ST I q.1 a.2). **Assumed, not taught here** — [`fundamental-theology` 5.4](../fundamental-theology/lessons/05-04-sacred-doctrine-as-a-science.md) owns it, along with a.8 on arguing from those principles and the ranking of authorities.

*Cited in:* [1.3](lessons/01-03-sacred-doctrine-one-science-about-god.md)

### Philosophy as handmaiden

Theology does not lean on philosophy as on a higher science; it uses it as a lesser thing, and only because our intelligence is defective.

ST I q.1 a.5 ad 2: theology accepts its principles "not from other sciences, but immediately from God, by revelation", and uses the philosophical sciences "as of the lesser, and as handmaidens" — not from its own insufficiency but from the defect of our intelligence, which is led more easily from what natural reason knows to what is above reason. a.6 ad 2 adds that it does not supply another science's principles but judges its conclusions. The norm is therefore negative, not productive: theology's approval adds nothing to an argument, and a bad demonstration stays bad. *Aeterni Patris* n. 6 restates the same office.

*Introduced:* [1.3](lessons/01-03-sacred-doctrine-one-science-about-god.md); restated in [6.2](lessons/06-02-leo-xiii-and-the-thomist-revival.md)

### Grace does not destroy nature but perfects it

The one-sentence warrant for every borrowing of metaphysics in Modules 2 to 5.

ST I q.1 a.8 ad 2: "Since therefore grace does not destroy nature but perfects it, natural reason should minister to faith as the natural bent of the will ministers to charity."

*Introduced:* [1.3](lessons/01-03-sacred-doctrine-one-science-about-god.md)

### Act is limited by potency

An act is finite only through the potency that receives it — so an act received into nothing is unlimited.

ST I q.7 a.1 states it for matter and form running both ways: matter is terminated by the form it receives, and a form common to many is determined to this one by the matter receiving it. The hinge is the clause "being is the most formal of all things": if existing behaves as form does, the rule reaches where there is no matter left, and the divine being, "not a being received in anything", is unlimited. Two cautions. The general form of the axiom is the school's generalisation of what q.7 a.1 states for matter and form, and **whether Aquinas demonstrates or presupposes it is disputed among Thomists**. And "infinite" here is a negative word reached by denying a receiver, not a measurement: the infinity of quantity is the infinity of matter, and q.7 a.1 ad 2 denies it of God.

*Introduced:* [2.1](lessons/02-01-act-and-potency-extended.md); paid off at [3.4](lessons/03-04-divine-simplicity-and-the-attributes.md)

### The four compositions

Four receiver-and-received pairs, each naming a limit; which ones a thing has is its rung on the ladder.

**Matter and form** (bodies only; [`metaphysics` 2.3](../metaphysics/lessons/02-03-hylomorphism-and-composition.md)). **Substance and accident** ([`metaphysics` 2.1](../metaphysics/lessons/02-01-substance-and-accident.md)). **[Power and operation](#power-and-operation)**. **Essence and *esse***, the deepest and the last. The diagnostic question is the same every time: is there in this something that receives, and something received?

*Introduced:* [2.1](lessons/02-01-act-and-potency-extended.md)

### Power and operation

No creature is its own activity, which is why every creature can be without acting.

ST I q.77 a.1 for the soul, with an argument that is decisive and homely: if the soul's essence just were its power, whatever has a soul would always be actually performing its vital acts — and you are sometimes asleep. The same holds of angels: an angel's act of understanding is not its substance (q.54 a.1) and its power of understanding is not its essence (q.54 a.3). Only in God are substance, existence and action the same.

*Introduced:* [2.1](lessons/02-01-act-and-potency-extended.md)

### Pure act

God as *actus purus*: no potency of any kind, and therefore infinite, simple and immutable.

ST I q.3 a.1: "the first being must of necessity be in act, and in no way in potentiality" — the lever by which q.3 removes every composition in turn. q.50 a.2 ad 3: "God alone is pure act." It does **not** mean maximal busyness; it means nothing in God is waiting to be actualised, which is why immutability follows from it rather than fighting it. A God who never rested would be a God with somewhere to get to. Note the level: pure act is the Thomist *analysis*, not itself the defined doctrine of simplicity — see [Simplicity at three levels](#simplicity-at-three-levels).

*Introduced:* [2.1](lessons/02-01-act-and-potency-extended.md); used in [3.2](lessons/03-02-the-first-and-second-ways-inside-the-system.md), [3.4](lessons/03-04-divine-simplicity-and-the-attributes.md), [6.3](lessons/06-03-strict-observance-and-existential-thomism.md)

### The ladder of beings

Each rung has one composition fewer than the one below it.

Bodies (all four compositions) — the human soul, a subsistent form that still forms a body — angels, with no matter at all but still essence and *esse* (ST I q.50 a.2) — God, with none. Only the last step is a step into a different kind of thing: the first two subtract ways of being a creature, the third subtracts creaturehood. The human soul is less a rung than the **seam**: read down, the highest form of matter; read up, the lowest intellect, and not an angel of a lesser grade, since two subsisting forms cannot differ in number without differing in species (q.75 a.7).

*Introduced:* [2.1](lessons/02-01-act-and-potency-extended.md); reopened at [5.3](lessons/05-03-the-human-soul-in-the-system.md)

### The act of existing

*Esse* is not one more item on a thing's list of features; it is the actuality of the whole list.

*De Potentia* q.7 a.2 ad 9, paraphrased: *esse* is the most perfect of all things, because act is always more perfect than potency. Take any determinate form — humanity, fieriness — and it can be considered as a potency in matter, as within an agent's power, or as a concept in a mind; what makes it actually existing is nothing but having *esse*. Hence *actualitas omnium actuum*, the actuality of all acts, and the perfection of all perfections. The same reply adds the move that matters: nothing can be added to *esse* from outside, since outside being there is only non-being, so *esse* is narrowed only by the essence that receives it. A creature is finite not because it is made of stuff but because its existing is received into *this* nature.

*Introduced:* [2.2](lessons/02-02-esse-and-essence-the-real-distinction.md); the centre of the quarrels in [6.1](lessons/06-01-the-commentators.md), [6.2](lessons/06-02-leo-xiii-and-the-thomist-revival.md), [6.3](lessons/06-03-strict-observance-and-existential-thomism.md)

### Real distinction

In every creature, essence and *esse* are two principles of one thing, the essence standing to the *esse* as potency to act.

Not two *things*: no party holds that, and a statement that lets the essence sit there waiting to be switched on is a caricature. ST I q.3 a.4 establishes it by denying it of God, and q.61 a.1 applies it to angels. Its four theological loads: **creation** (to give *esse* absolutely, with no subject presupposed); **the identification of God** as [subsisting being itself](#subsisting-being-itself), in no genus (q.3 a.5) and so indefinable and demonstrable only from effects; **angelic finitude** (no matter, still not God, because the existing is received); and **the Incarnation** (whether the assumed human nature has its own *esse* becomes askable at all). Level: a school thesis, rung 5, freely disputed — see [School thesis](#school-thesis).

*Introduced:* [2.2](lessons/02-02-esse-and-essence-the-real-distinction.md); used in [3.3](lessons/03-03-the-third-fourth-and-fifth-ways.md), [5.1](lessons/05-01-creation-and-conservation.md), [5.3](lessons/05-03-the-human-soul-in-the-system.md), [6.5](lessons/06-05-what-the-church-has-said-about-thomism.md)

### Subsisting being itself

*Ipsum esse subsistens*: the one being whose essence is its existence.

ST I q.3 aa.4–5. Because members of a genus share an essence and differ in existence, and in God they do not differ, God falls into no genus — hence no definition of him, and no demonstration of him except from effects. It is also why in God "wise" signifies nothing other than the divine essence, which is what blocks univocity in [4.4](lessons/04-04-analogy.md), and it is the reason "He Who Is" is the most proper name.

*Introduced:* [2.2](lessons/02-02-esse-and-essence-the-real-distinction.md); used in [3.4](lessons/03-04-divine-simplicity-and-the-attributes.md), [4.3](lessons/04-03-the-names-of-god.md), [4.4](lessons/04-04-analogy.md), [5.1](lessons/05-01-creation-and-conservation.md)

### Formal distinction

Scotus's rival: essence and existence are inseparable formalities of one reality, distinct in the thing but less than really distinct.

Neither two things nor merely two of our words. The Thomist reply is that no intermediate distinction is available — it collapses into a real distinction or into a distinction of reason; the Scotist reply is that this begs the question. The same machinery reappears among the divine attributes: see [The formal distinction among the attributes](#the-formal-distinction-among-the-attributes).

*Introduced:* [2.2](lessons/02-02-esse-and-essence-the-real-distinction.md); returns in [4.4](lessons/04-04-analogy.md)

### Distinction of reason with a foundation in the thing

Suárez's rival: one reality in the existing creature, which the mind distinguishes into essence and existence on the real foundation of its contingency.

All three parties agree the creature need not have existed. They disagree about whether that gap is a seam in the thing (Thomist), a formality in it (Scotist), or a distinction the mind draws on a real foundation (Suárezian). None of the three denies creation, divine simplicity or contingency; what the rivals deny is that *this* premise is what gets you there.

*Introduced:* [2.2](lessons/02-02-esse-and-essence-the-real-distinction.md)

### Participation

What a thing has partially, it has from what has it essentially — and what it receives becomes formally its own.

ST I q.44 a.1: "whatever is found in anything by participation, must be caused in it by that to which it belongs essentially, as iron becomes ignited by fire." The fire does not *get* hot; the iron does, from the fire, to the degree iron can take. q.6 a.4 closes with the formula that saves the doctrine from pantheism: "of all things there is one goodness, and yet many goodnesses." The limit is set by the essence receiving, per [act is limited by potency](#act-is-limited-by-potency). *Esse* is not a quantity divided out: there is no stock of being from which portions are issued.

*Owned by:* [2.3](lessons/02-03-participation.md); used in [3.3](lessons/03-03-the-third-fourth-and-fifth-ways.md) (the Fourth Way), [5.1](lessons/05-01-creation-and-conservation.md) (creation), [4.2](lessons/04-02-knowing-god-from-creatures-the-three-ways.md) and [4.4](lessons/04-04-analogy.md) (the reach of the divine names)

### The two conditions of participation

Both must hold, and each failure has its own name.

**(i) Caused**: what is had by participation is caused by what has it essentially — the exemplary, efficient and final principle. **(ii) Received**: and therefore formally the receiver's own form, not the giver's perfection lodged inside it ("which is formally its own goodness", ST I q.6 a.4). Drop (i) and creaturely goods are underived, which q.44 a.1 exists to exclude. Drop (ii) and you have pantheism. Pantheism is therefore not an exaggeration of participation but the denial of one of its conditions.

*Introduced:* [2.3](lessons/02-03-participation.md)

### Being by essence

The contrast term: a thing that **is** the perfection rather than having it — no receiver, nothing received, nothing falling short.

Of being, true of God alone ([subsisting being itself](#subsisting-being-itself), ST I q.3 a.4); of any perfection whatever, true only of God. Note what Aquinas does with Plato here: he drops the separate Ideas by name, on Aristotle's objections, and keeps the top of the ladder, relocated — not a Form we posit but a being we demonstrate.

*Introduced:* [2.3](lessons/02-03-participation.md)

### Extrinsic denomination

The third option, to be kept clear of both: the thing does not have the perfection at all and is named from something else that does.

A medicine is called healthy from the health it causes in an animal, not because health is in the bottle; ST I q.6 a.4's "placed" from place and "measured" from measure. Half the errors about participation are really the claim that creaturely goodness is nothing but this — and reducing the **divine names** to it is the causality-only reading that [4.3](lessons/04-03-the-names-of-god.md) refuses.

*Introduced:* [2.3](lessons/02-03-participation.md)

### Liber de causis

A short Arabic-Latin book of propositions on the first cause, read in the schools as Aristotle's, and one of the two channels carrying Neoplatonic participation to Aquinas.

The other is the Dionysian corpus, owned by [`patristics`](../patristics/syllabus.md). Commenting on the *Liber* in 1272 with William of Moerbeke's Latin Proclus in front of him, Aquinas said the book was excerpted from Proclus's *Elements of Theology* — a working theologian correcting an attribution by reading.

*Introduced:* [2.3](lessons/02-03-participation.md)

### Transcendentals

Properties said of absolutely everything there is; each adds only a mode of being made explicit, never new content.

Nothing lies outside being to be brought in — anything you could fetch would itself be something. So the addition can only be a **negation** or a **relation**. The classic list is *De Veritate* q.1 a.1, paraphrased: **being**; **thing**, being named from the side of essence; **one**, undivided in itself; **something** (*aliquid*), divided off from everything else; **true**, answerable to an intellect; **good**, answerable to an appetite. Two negations and two relations. They are convertible with being: the same things fall under each, and the difference is of idea, not of inventory. They therefore admit of more and less by how much *esse* a thing holds, which is what restricts the Fourth Way's gradation premise.

*Owned by:* [2.4](lessons/02-04-the-transcendentals.md); used in [3.3](lessons/03-03-the-third-fourth-and-fifth-ways.md)

### Good and being are convertible

Goodness and being are really the same and differ only in idea.

ST I q.5 a.1: good adds desirability; a thing is desirable so far as it is perfect, perfect so far as it is in act, in act so far as it exists. The ladder of act and potency climbed in reverse — goodness tracks actuality, and actuality bottoms out in *esse*. That good adds an *idea* and not a reality does not make "good" empty: it expresses a relation to appetite that "being" by itself does not.

*Introduced:* [2.4](lessons/02-04-the-transcendentals.md)

### One as undivided

"One" adds no reality to being, only the negation of division — but it does add an idea.

ST I q.11 a.1: "one" means undivided being, which is why it is the same as being; what is simple is undivided actually and potentially, and what is compound has no being while its parts are divided. Hence "everything guards its unity as it guards its being". Ad 3 keeps the idea from being empty; ad 1 distinguishes the *one* convertible with being from the *one* that is the principle of number, against Avicenna.

*Introduced:* [2.4](lessons/02-04-the-transcendentals.md)

### Truth in the intellect and in things

Truth is primarily in the intellect, and derivatively in things, which are true so far as they answer to the intellect they depend on.

ST I q.16 a.1: a house is true by matching the architect's idea, a natural thing by matching the divine one. q.16 a.3 lines the pair up — as good adds to being a relation to appetite, true adds a relation to intellect.

*Introduced:* [2.4](lessons/02-04-the-transcendentals.md)

### Evil as privation

Evil is no nature, form or being: it is the absence of a good — and specifically of a good that is *due*.

ST I q.48 a.1 spends q.5 a.1's result rather than re-arguing it: every nature desires its own being and perfection, so the being of any nature is good, so "evil" cannot signify a being, form or nature; therefore it signifies the absence of good. q.48 a.3 adds the refinement that makes the doctrine usable: a **privative** absence, not a merely negative one — a man who lacks a roe's swiftness is not thereby evil, because that good was never due to him. Two clauses stop the doctrine from shrinking the horror. q.48 a.1 ad 4: evil corrupts *formally*, as a privation, and effects nothing except by the good annexed to it. q.48 a.1 ad 2: moral evil is a real good joined to the privation of another good — the intemperate man pursues a pleasure, outside reason's order, and the act is real while the due order is missing. A privation is not a nothing-at-all; blindness is a privation and entirely real.

*Introduced:* [2.4](lessons/02-04-the-transcendentals.md)

### Whether beauty is a transcendental

Disputed, and this course settles nothing.

Aquinas never enumerates beauty among the transcendentals. But ST I q.5 a.4 ad 1 makes beauty and goodness identical in the thing and distinct only in idea, both resting on form, with goodness relating to appetite and beauty to a cognitive power — "beautiful things are those which please when seen". Maritain (*Art and Scholasticism*) and much of the Neoscholastic aesthetic tradition read that as a transcendental's exact profile. Critics answer that the same text makes beauty the good under a relation to knowing, a species of the good rather than a seventh item, and that no list Aquinas wrote includes it.

*Introduced:* [2.4](lessons/02-04-the-transcendentals.md)

### Self-evident in itself and to us

"God exists" is the first and not the second, which is why it has to be demonstrated.

ST I q.2 a.1: a proposition is self-evident when the predicate is contained in the essence of the subject; it is self-evident *to us* only if we know that essence. In God the predicate is the subject, since God is his own existence — but that is known only to one who sees the essence, and we do not. So the proposition "needs to be demonstrated by things that are more known to us, though less known in their nature — namely, by effects." Note the order: that God's essence is his existence is proved later, at q.3 a.4, so it cannot be a premise in q.2. The grant is not a concession to the *Proslogion*.

*Introduced:* [3.1](lessons/03-01-demonstrating-that-god-exists.md)

### Demonstration quia and propter quid

Two directions of inference, and only one of them is open to us about God.

*Propter quid* argues from cause to effect and needs the cause's **essence** as its middle term; *quia* argues from effect to cause. No essence, no *propter quid*. ST I q.2 a.2: "from every effect the existence of its proper cause can be demonstrated, so long as its effects are better known to us." The English Dominican translation renders the pair "a priori" and "a posteriori" — read them as Latin, not as Kant; both of Aquinas's kinds start from experience, and they classify a demonstration by the direction of the inference.

*Introduced:* [3.1](lessons/03-01-demonstrating-that-god-exists.md); cited in [6.4](lessons/06-04-transcendental-thomism-and-the-ressourcement-quarrel.md)

### Nominal definition as middle term

In a *quia* demonstration the middle term is the meaning of the word, not the essence of the thing.

ST I q.2 a.2 ad 2: "in order to prove the existence of anything, it is necessary to accept as a middle term the meaning of the word, and not its essence… Now the names given to God are derived from His effects." You prove that *something answering to this name* exists and postpone what it is — the right order, since "what is it?" only arises once "is it?" is settled.

*Introduced:* [3.1](lessons/03-01-demonstrating-that-god-exists.md)

### Nominal definition of God

What "and this all call God" does at the end of each Way: it attaches a name fixed from the effects, and claims nothing more.

The definition is deliberately **thin** — first mover, first efficient cause, necessary of itself, cause of every perfection, intelligent director — and the thickening is done afterwards, one attribute at a time, from simplicity to unity. Read q.2 a.3 as the finished case and you have mistaken the first step for the whole argument; and notice the cost, which the course does not hide: if the later articles fail, the Ways survive as proofs of five thin somethings.

*Introduced:* [3.1](lessons/03-01-demonstrating-that-god-exists.md); used in [3.3](lessons/03-03-the-third-fourth-and-fifth-ways.md)

### Preambles of faith

Truths about God that natural reason can reach are not articles of faith but preambles to them.

ST I q.2 a.2 ad 1, with the rider that matters pastorally: one who cannot follow a proof may still hold the same truth by faith. **Owned by** [`fundamental-theology` 2.2](../fundamental-theology/lessons/02-02-credibility-and-the-preambles-of-faith.md); used, not re-derived, here.

*Cited in:* [3.1](lessons/03-01-demonstrating-that-god-exists.md)

### Motion as reduction from potency to act

*Motus* is not locomotion. It is any passage from potency to act.

ST I q.2 a.3 defines it on the spot: motion is "nothing else than the reduction of something from potentiality to actuality". So heating, growth and a learner coming to understand are all *motus*; the stone and the staff are illustrations, not the subject matter. **The disputed question, which this course does not settle:** how wide the term runs. On the narrow reading Aquinas means Aristotle's strict sense, the act of what is in potency precisely as in potency, belonging only to divisible bodies, and the *Summa contra Gentiles* I.13's proofs about bodies moved simultaneously pull that way. On the broad reading the article's own definition is unrestricted, the engine is act and potency rather than any particular physics, and q.3 a.1 immediately restates the conclusion as one about being in act. The later articles need the broad conclusion whichever way the word is read.

*Introduced:* [3.2](lessons/03-02-the-first-and-second-ways-inside-the-system.md)

### Cause of becoming and cause of being

"First" is not "earliest".

ST I q.104 a.1: the builder causes the house's *becoming*, and it stands after he dies; the sun causes the air's *being lit*, and the light goes when the sun does. The Ways reach a first in an order of **being**, in force now — which is why nothing in them is about the start of the universe and nothing in them is threatened if the world always existed. **Owned by** [`metaphysics` 4.4](../metaphysics/lessons/04-04-ordered-causal-series.md).

*Used in:* [3.2](lessons/03-02-the-first-and-second-ways-inside-the-system.md), [5.1](lessons/05-01-creation-and-conservation.md), [5.2](lessons/05-02-providence-and-secondary-causes.md)

### Necessary per se and necessary from another

In the Third Way "necessary" means *incorruptible* — so Aquinas's world is full of necessary creatures.

"Possible" is glossed by the text itself as generated and corrupted: having matter in potency to losing its form. "Necessary" is the contradictory: no potency to not-be. Hence angels, which are subsisting forms with nothing to separate (q.50 a.5), the human soul (q.75 a.6), and on the physics he inherited the heavenly bodies (q.9 a.2), all count. That is exactly why the Way does not stop at the first necessary thing it meets: some necessary things have a **cause of their necessity** (q.44 a.1 ad 2; q.50 a.5 ad 3, that it is not repugnant to an incorruptible being to depend on another for its existence). Do **not** import the modern sense — what could not have failed to exist under any description — which belongs to [`metaphysics` 3.1](../metaphysics/lessons/03-01-kinds-of-necessity.md) and makes the article's second half unintelligible.

*Introduced:* [3.3](lessons/03-03-the-third-fourth-and-fifth-ways.md)

### Natural finality

Things without knowledge tend to determinate ends, evidenced by acting always or nearly always the same way — and so are aimed by an intellect.

ST I q.2 a.3, the Fifth Way; run again at I-II q.1 a.2, where determination to an end comes either by rational appetite (will) or by natural inclination, "as an arrow tends to a determinate end through being moved by the archer", and where a.2 ad 3 names the archer as the divine will. The aim is stored in the exemplar ideas (q.15 a.1, q.44 a.3). The datum is **regularity, not intricacy**: "designedly" translates *ex intentione*, and the very next sentence says whose intention, the archer's and not the arrow's. This is not Paley's argument from contrivance ([`philosophy-of-religion`](../philosophy-of-religion/syllabus.md) 3.1), which is what evolutionary biology answers.

*Introduced:* [3.3](lessons/03-03-the-third-fourth-and-fifth-ways.md)

### Divine simplicity

God has no composition of any kind, so there is nothing in God that is not God.

In plain words: God is not a thing that *has* properties. God does not have goodness or power or existence; God is them. ST I q.3 removes one composition per article: **a.1** not a body; **a.2** not matter and form; **a.3** nature not distinct from *suppositum*; **a.4** essence is existence; **a.5** not in a genus; **a.6** no accidents; **a.7** altogether simple — with **a.8** adding the guard that God is not a component of anything else. Article 7 collects the six denials and adds four free-standing arguments: a composite is posterior to and dependent on its parts; every composite has a cause, since things that differ do not unite unless something unites them; every composite involves potency and act; and nothing composite is wholly predicated of any of its parts. "Simple" does not mean thin — on this account it is the only being with nothing missing, which is why perfection comes next. Levels: see [Simplicity at three levels](#simplicity-at-three-levels).

*Introduced:* [3.4](lessons/03-04-divine-simplicity-and-the-attributes.md)

### How he is not

The method of ST I qq.3–11, announced in the q.3 prologue — and not agnosticism.

"Because we cannot know what God is, but rather what He is not, we have no means for considering how God is, but rather how He is not… by denying Him whatever is opposed to the idea of Him, viz. composition, motion, and the like." It is a remark about our intellect, not about God, and the same sentence immediately books the repair: **(2) how He is known by us** (q.12) and **(3) how He is named** (q.13). Aquinas denies quidditative knowledge, not that anything true can be said.

*Introduced:* [3.4](lessons/03-04-divine-simplicity-and-the-attributes.md)

### The formal distinction among the attributes

Scotus's alternative: wisdom and goodness in God are neither two things nor merely two of our words, but distinct formalities on the side of the thing, prior to any act of our minds, within one wholly indivisible reality.

He **affirms** the doctrine of simplicity and rejects the Thomist analysis of it. Thomists reply that no intermediate distinction is available; Scotists reply that this begs the question. A live intramural argument among Catholics, not a denial of Lateran IV — and it is inserted at exactly the joint ST I q.13 a.4 opens, where the plurality of divine names is put in our conceptions.

*Introduced:* [3.4](lessons/03-04-divine-simplicity-and-the-attributes.md)

### Phantasm

The image of an individual left in the imagination by sense: always particular, never universal.

The intellect cannot understand, even from a species it already has, without turning back to one (ST I q.84 a.7). That article is read as a live theory of mind by [`philosophy-of-mind`](../philosophy-of-mind/syllabus.md) 5.2–5.3; here it is taken for what it costs theology. There is no phantasm of God, so the machinery of abstraction has nothing to work on.

*Introduced:* [4.1](lessons/04-01-how-a-human-intellect-knows.md)

### Agent intellect

The soul's active power that makes the content of a phantasm actually intelligible, as light makes colour visible.

ST I q.79 a.3, in three steps: forms of natural things do not exist apart from matter; forms in matter are not actually intelligible, since a thing is intelligible in proportion as it is separate from matter; nothing goes from potency to act except by something in act. Therefore a power on the intellect's side. Abstraction is the price of putting the natures in the things: on Plato's supposition no agent intellect would be needed. It is a power **of each soul**, not one separate substance for all (aa.4–5) — a participated light in the air is not the sun.

*Introduced:* [4.1](lessons/04-01-how-a-human-intellect-knows.md)

### Intelligible species

The likeness of a nature received in the possible intellect — and it is *that by which* we understand, not *what* we understand.

ST I q.85 a.2 kills the other reading with two reductios: if the species were what is understood, every science would be about the intelligible species in the soul rather than about things outside it; and a faculty that knows only its own impression can judge only that impression, so "whatever seems, is true" and contradictories stand together. Aquinas is not proving realism from a first principle; he is showing that its denial destroys the possibility of being wrong. The concession: by reflecting on itself the intellect does understand its own act and species — *secondarily*. Get this backwards and Module 4 becomes an account of our God-language rather than of God.

*Introduced:* [4.1](lessons/04-01-how-a-human-intellect-knows.md)

### Abstraction

Taking the specific nature from the phantasm while disregarding the conditions of individuality.

ST I q.85 a.1 and ad 2: **individual** matter is dropped, **common** matter kept — the species of man is abstracted from "this flesh and these bones", not from flesh and bones. The universality belongs to our consideration, not to a universal thing: the nature is in this oak and that one, and being apprehended without conditions of individuality happens to it in the intellect (a.2 ad 2).

*Introduced:* [4.1](lessons/04-01-how-a-human-intellect-knows.md)

### Proper object of the human intellect

The quiddity or nature existing in corporeal matter — and "proper" does not mean "only possible".

ST I q.84 a.7 and q.85 a.6. "Proper" means what a power is *per se* directed to and therefore cannot fail about; intellectual knowledge extends further than sense, since sense is not its entire cause (q.84 a.6 ad 3). So the constraint on knowing God is not that we cannot think past what we have seen; it is that God is outside the genus the proper object names, and so is reached only from his effects.

*Introduced:* [4.1](lessons/04-01-how-a-human-intellect-knows.md)

### The ladder of intellects

Three grades of cognitive power, sorted by one variable: distance from matter.

ST I q.85 a.1, whose principle is that the object of knowledge is proportionate to the power. **Sense** is the act of a corporeal organ, so its object is a form as existing in corporeal matter, and it knows only individuals. The **human intellect** holds a middle place: not an organ's act, yet a power of the soul that is the form of a body, so its object is the quiddity of a material thing, taken by abstraction — and it is discursive, advancing from one thing understood to another, which is why we are called rational where angels are called intellectual (q.79 a.8). The **angelic intellect** is unconnected with matter, so its object is a form existing apart from matter, without abstraction or reasoning. At the top, **God's** act of understanding is his substance (q.14 a.4).

*Introduced:* [4.1](lessons/04-01-how-a-human-intellect-knows.md)

### What natural reason reaches

Four things, and not the essence.

ST I q.12 a.12: that God is; that he is the cause of all things; that creatures differ from him; and that the difference is by his **excess**, not by any defect in him. Not the essence — ad 1, reason cannot reach up to simple form so as to know "what it is", though it can know "whether it is"; ad 2, the knowing runs through images of his effects. The obstacle is metaphysical, not moral: God's sensible effects "do not equal the power of God as their cause", and ad 3 says the knowledge is open to good and bad alike.

*Introduced:* [4.2](lessons/04-02-knowing-god-from-creatures-the-three-ways.md)

### The light of glory

A created light, added by grace, so that a created intellect can bear the divine essence as its intelligible form.

ST I q.12 a.4: no created intellect sees the essence by its natural power, since a knower knows according to its own mode of being and only God's mode is subsistent being. a.5: a supernatural disposition is required, and the blessed are made deiform. a.7: even so, they see God without **comprehending** him, since a finite light cannot know an infinite object infinitely. The boundary that matters here: the three ways are the *natural* route and never cross into the essence. The vision as doctrine is ceded to [`creation-grace-last-things`](../creation-grace-last-things/syllabus.md).

*Introduced:* [4.2](lessons/04-02-knowing-god-from-creatures-the-three-ways.md)

### Apophatic theology and its limits

How far negation goes in Aquinas, and where he stops.

What he shares with the Greeks: no created intellect comprehends God (q.12 a.7), and none sees his essence while living this mortal life (q.12 a.11). Gregory of Nyssa's *Life of Moses* makes the ascent an endless ascent into darkness ([`patristics` 3.3](../patristics/lessons/03-03-the-two-gregories.md)). So the split is **not** degree of apophaticism. It is whether affirmative names are true of God himself — Aquinas says yes (q.13 a.2, against both the negation-only and the causality-only readings), and that is what leaves analogy something to do.

*Introduced:* [4.2](lessons/04-02-knowing-god-from-creatures-the-three-ways.md)

### The thing signified and the mode of signifying

*Res significata* against *modus significandi*: what a divine name signifies belongs to God properly; the creature-shaped way the word means it does not.

ST I q.13 a.3: "As regards what is signified by these names, they belong properly to God, and more properly than they belong to creatures… But as regards their mode of signification, they do not properly and strictly apply to God." The packaging is unavoidable — a concrete noun signifies a subsisting composite, an abstract noun a form that is not a subject (a.1 ad 2), and God is neither. So "God is good" is **literally true and permanently inadequate**. A failing mode makes the sentence *inadequate*, not false; collapse the line and everything said of God becomes a picturesque lie, which is the reading a.3 exists to refuse.

*Introduced:* [4.3](lessons/04-03-the-names-of-god.md)

### Signifying the divine substance

Absolute affirmative names — good, wise, living — signify God's substance, truly and imperfectly at once.

ST I q.13 a.2 names and refutes both reductions in three arguments. **Remotion only** (attributed to Rabbi Moses, Maimonides): "God lives" means only that God is not like a stone. **Causality only**: "God is good" means only that God causes goodness. Against them: a rule that lets everything through is no rule, since God causes bodies exactly as he causes good things, so "God is a body" would be equally licensed; every divine name would be secondary, as "healthy" is of medicine; and it is against the intention of those who speak of God. The load-bearing premise is divine perfection (q.4 aa.2–3), with q.4 a.3 on every creature representing God as an excelling principle. Aquinas states the meaning and reverses the causal order of explanation: not "God is the cause of goodness" nor "God is not evil", but "whatever good we attribute to creatures pre-exists in God", more excellently — and he causes goodness *because* he is good.

*Introduced:* [4.3](lessons/04-03-the-names-of-god.md)

### Why the divine names are not synonyms

One utterly simple reality, many conceptions — and the plurality is never in God.

ST I q.13 a.4: our intellect, knowing God from creatures, forms conceptions proportioned to the many perfections of creatures, "which perfections pre-exist in God unitedly and simply, whereas in creatures they are received and divided and multiplied". So the multiplicity is in our conceptions and in the creatures grounding them; simplicity is untouched. "Not synonyms" does **not** mean the attributes are really distinct in God — and this is exactly the joint at which Scotus inserts his [formal distinction](#the-formal-distinction-among-the-attributes).

*Introduced:* [4.3](lessons/04-03-the-names-of-god.md)

### Metaphor and proper predication

The test is not vividness. It is whether the creature's *way* of having the perfection is part of what the word signifies.

If yes — rock, lion, a father's anger — the name is metaphorical, true by likeness, and said primarily of creatures in **both** orders (ST I q.13 a.3 ad 1, a.6). If no — being, good, living, wise — it is said of God literally and primarily as to what is signified, and only the mode of meaning is creaturely.

*Introduced:* [4.3](lessons/04-03-the-names-of-god.md)

### Names said of God in time

Names importing a relation to creatures — Lord, Creator, Saviour — are predicated of God temporally, because the relation is real in the creature and of reason in God.

ST I q.13 a.7: a relation can be real at both ends (father and son), at neither, or at one only — as a column becomes "on the right" of an animal when the animal walks round it, with nothing happening to the column. God is outside the whole order of creation; creatures are ordered to him and not he to them. Two replies keep it from being paradox: ad 5, because the relation of subjection is real *in the creature*, God is Lord in reality and not merely in idea; ad 6, God was not Lord until there was a creature subject to him. Nothing new in God; a new creature, really related. **Do not carry this into the Trinity**: the relations there are real *in God*, which is what makes them persons. This is groundwork for `trinity-and-god`, not a preview of it.

*Introduced:* [4.3](lessons/04-03-the-names-of-god.md); used in [5.1](lessons/05-01-creation-and-conservation.md)

### He Who Is

The most proper name of God, for three reasons.

ST I q.13 a.11: it signifies no form but existence itself, and God's existence is his essence ([subsisting being itself](#subsisting-being-itself)); it is the least determinate name there is, so adds no mode; and it consignifies present existence. Ad 1 concedes the other order: as regards what a name is *imposed* to signify, "God" is more proper, and the Tetragrammaton more proper still.

*Introduced:* [4.3](lessons/04-03-the-names-of-god.md)

### Analogical predication

Not one idea repeated, and not two idioms sharing a noise: many meanings ordered to a first.

ST I q.13 aa.5–6. "Healthy" is said of a body, a medicine, a diet, urine — health belongs to the body, and every other use is fixed by a stated relation to it, which the medicine causes and the urine signals. a.6's structural claim: in analogical names "all are predicated because they have reference to some one thing; and this one thing must be placed in the definition of them all." Analogy is **not** a blurred univocity or a meaning halfway between the other two; naming the relation is what blocks the fallacy of equivocation and lets an argument run.

*Introduced:* [4.4](lessons/04-04-analogy.md)

### The order of naming

Two orders that come apart, which is the whole of ST I q.13 a.6.

As regards **what the name signifies**, perfection-names belong primarily to God, since the perfections flow from him to creatures. As regards **the imposition of the name**, primarily to creatures, which we know first. We learn "wise" from wise men; the wisdom is in God first. Drop the first half and you get Maimonides; drop the second and you have claimed to know *how* God is wise.

*Introduced:* [4.4](lessons/04-04-analogy.md)

### Analogy of attribution

Reading 1 of ST I q.13 a.5's two modes: one analogate is primary, and every other use is defined by its relation to it — the relation being creature to God as principle and cause.

This is what a.5's own next sentences and a.6's first sentence say on their face, and *SCG* I.34 rules out the many-to-one mode, which would require something prior to God. Defended in the twentieth century by Ralph McInerny (*The Logic of Analogy*, 1961; *Aquinas and Analogy*, 1996) and Bernard Montagnes (*La doctrine de l'analogie de l'être*, 1963), who argue that analogy in Aquinas is a doctrine about *names* and their ordered meanings, not a separate metaphysical machine.

*Introduced:* [4.4](lessons/04-04-analogy.md)

### Analogy of proper proportionality

Reading 2: the likeness is between two *relations* — as a creature's wisdom is to the creature, so God's is to God.

Cajetan's *De Nominum Analogia* (1498) sorts analogy into inequality, attribution and proportionality, and argues that only proportionality is analogy properly so called and the only kind yielding knowledge of God — attribution risks leaving the perfection formally in one analogate only, so that "God is good" would say no more than that God causes goodness, which a.6 rejects. The manual tradition made it *the* Thomist account for four centuries. Cajetan had a text: *De Veritate* q.2 a.11 distinguishes agreement of proportion from agreement of proportionality and rules the first out for God and creature. The counter-reading notes that the rejected category there covers **both** modes a.5 later distinguishes, and that a.5's chosen mode is precisely such a determinate relation; Montagnes reads this as development, a Cajetanian as two complementary descriptions. Freely disputed, rung 5 — the course's cleanest [school thesis](#school-thesis).

*Introduced:* [4.4](lessons/04-04-analogy.md); the disputed passage of *De Veritate* is one the course flags as unverified

### Univocity of being

Scotus's rival to analogy: one concept of being said univocally of God and creatures.

It has its own guardrail — the univocal concept is thin, and thick predicates stay analogical. **Owned by** [`ancient-medieval-philosophy` 8.2](../ancient-medieval-philosophy/lessons/08-02-scotus-the-univocity-of-being.md), where the argument is the concept that stays certain while another is doubted. A school position of a Catholic doctor of the Church, never condemned: demoting it is graded as strictly as promoting Cajetan.

*Cited in:* [4.4](lessons/04-04-analogy.md)

### Creation

The emanation of all being from the universal cause, presupposing nothing — and so "from nothing".

ST I q.45 a.1. Not one making among others but the giving of *esse* as such: where a particular agent makes *this* out of *that*, the universal cause of being has no *that* to work from, because there is no being outside what it causes. God is efficient, exemplar and final cause of all things (q.44 aa.1, 3–4).

*Introduced:* [5.1](lessons/05-01-creation-and-conservation.md)

### Creation as a real relation in the creature

What creation puts *in* the creature is a relation to the Creator as to the principle of its being.

ST I q.45 a.3: remove movement from action and passion and only relation remains. Two riders. The relation is **real in the creature and of reason in God** (a.3 ad 1, resting on q.13 a.7) — the creature is genuinely ordered to God, while God acquires nothing by creating. And the name "creation" carries a note of newness, so a creature is not *being created* throughout its life (a.3 ad 3) — which is the fence on the far side of which conservation stands.

*Introduced:* [5.1](lessons/05-01-creation-and-conservation.md)

### Conservation

Keeping in being is not a second act. It is the continuation of the one action by which God gives existence.

ST I q.104 a.1 ad 4: "The preservation of things by God is a continuation of that action whereby He gives existence, which action is without either motion or time." The article's image is the sun and the air: the builder causes the house's becoming and may die while it stands, but air has no light of its own and is not lit a moment past the sun. Hence God is present in all things **as an agent** is present to what it acts on, and innermostly, since being is innermost in anything (q.8 a.1) — divine presence is a corollary of causing *esse*, not a separate doctrine about where God is. Not continuous re-creation: re-creation posits a series of acts, the doctrine posits one.

*Introduced:* [5.1](lessons/05-01-creation-and-conservation.md)

### A beginning in time is held by faith

That the world did not always exist is held by faith and cannot be demonstrated.

ST I q.46 a.2: not from the world, since a thing's species is indifferent to "when"; not from the cause, since God creates by will, which reason cannot read off. Aquinas adds a pastoral reason — parading a bad proof of what is in fact believed invites unbelievers to laugh at the faith. Keep the two claims apart: that the world **did** begin in time is *de fide* (Lateran IV ch. 1; *Dei Filius* ch. 1); that it is **indemonstrable** is Aquinas's own freely disputed thesis, denied by Bonaventure. The consequence for modern cosmology: neither a beginningless universe nor an absolute first moment settles creation, since creation is not a change and so not the earliest member of a series of changes.

*Introduced:* [5.1](lessons/05-01-creation-and-conservation.md)

### Providence as plan and government

Providence is two things, and the pair does all the work.

ST I q.22 a.3: the **type of the order** of things foreordained towards their end, existing in God's intellect; and the **execution** of that order, called government. Immediate as to the plan, reaching this individual and not merely the species (a.2), because God has in his intellect the types of everything, even the smallest, and gives assigned causes the power to produce their effects. Mediated as to the execution — he governs things inferior by superior, "not on account of any defect in His power, but by reason of the abundance of His goodness; so that the dignity of causality is imparted even to creatures." Two questions answered in opposite directions: *who plans?* God, immediately, all of it. *Who does it?* God and the creature, both, wholly.

*Introduced:* [5.2](lessons/05-02-providence-and-secondary-causes.md)

### Primary and secondary causes

A difference of **level**, not of **share**.

ST I q.105 a.5's image is a craftsman and an axe, and it is not a division of labour: the whole cut is the axe's, by its sharpness, and the whole cut is the craftsman's, who applies it. The article first refuses the extreme view that no created power has any effect — that it is not fire that gives heat but God in the fire — for two reasons: it takes the order of cause and effect away from creatures, which implies a lack of power in the Creator, since bestowing active power on an effect belongs to a cause's power; and the powers we see would have been given for nothing, so creatures would be purposeless. Then the positive account, in three: God works in every agent **as the end**, since every operation is for some good; **as the first agent**, since where agents are ordered the second acts in virtue of the first; and as **giver, preserver and applier** of the very forms and powers by which agents act. "Primary" means first in the order of dependence, not in time — the axe depends on the craftsman while it is cutting, not before.

*Introduced:* [5.2](lessons/05-02-providence-and-secondary-causes.md)

### Contingent causes and contingent effects

Contingency is a feature of the plan, not leakage from it.

ST I q.19 a.8 rejects the obvious defence — that God's plan is firm while the causes he works through are floppy — on two grounds: a defective second cause can block a first cause's effect elsewhere, but no creaturely defect blocks God's will; and it would put the division of necessary from contingent outside God's intention. The answer is the efficacy of the divine will: an efficacious cause gets the *manner* of the doing as well as the doing, so "it is not because the proximate causes are contingent that the effects willed by God happen contingently, but because God prepared contingent causes for them, it being His will that they should happen contingently." Compare q.22 a.4's "according to the nature of their proximate causes", which out of context reads like the rejected explanation and is not. A **contingent** cause is one that *can fail*; it is fully a cause, and "contingent" does not mean uncaused or random.

*Introduced:* [5.2](lessons/05-02-providence-and-secondary-causes.md)

### The dispute over efficacious grace

How God's causality reaches a free act. Named here, ceded, and never reported as settled.

**Physical premotion**: God moves the will from within (Báñez and the Dominicans). **Middle knowledge**: God knows, prior to any decree, what each free creature would freely do in any circumstances, and arranges them accordingly (Molina). Rome examined it under Clement VIII and Paul V and closed the Congregation *de auxiliis* in 1607 without deciding, leaving both schools free. Rung 5, freely disputed; **owned by** [`creation-grace-last-things`](../creation-grace-last-things/syllabus.md). Telling anyone it is settled either way is the [school thesis](#school-thesis) error, graded strictly.

*Named in:* [5.2](lessons/05-02-providence-and-secondary-causes.md), [6.1](lessons/06-01-the-commentators.md), [6.5](lessons/06-05-what-the-church-has-said-about-thomism.md)

### One esse in the composite

The soul does not lend the body a second existence. It lets the body into its own.

ST I q.76 a.1 ad 5: "The soul communicates that existence in which it subsists to the corporeal matter, … so that the existence of the whole composite is also the existence of the soul. … For this reason the human soul retains its own existence after the dissolution of the body; whereas it is not so with other forms." Not two acts of existing, hence not dualism with Aristotelian manners: the dualist has two things that must be related, Aquinas has one act of existing with two principles, one of which can hold it alone. Survival is then not a new fact needing a new argument. What it does **not** make the soul: its essence is still not its existence, so the [real distinction](#real-distinction) holds in it, and its *esse* is created, conserved and in principle annihilable by the withdrawal of that act.

*Introduced:* [5.3](lessons/05-03-the-human-soul-in-the-system.md)

### Unicity of substantial form

There is no substantial form in a human being besides the intellectual soul.

ST I q.76 a.4: a substantial form makes a thing *be simply*, an accidental form only makes it be *such*; if some other substantial form were already in the matter, the soul would arrive at an actual being and so would make it only *such* — which would make it accidental, and a human being's coming to be an alteration rather than a generation. So the rational soul **virtually contains** the sensitive and nutritive souls and "itself alone does whatever the imperfect forms do in other things"; the forms of the elements remain in a mixed body "not actually but virtually" (a.4 ad 4). Consequences Aquinas accepts: with the form gone, a corpse is called a man or a hand only equivocally, "as we speak of a painted animal or a stone animal" (q.76 a.8), and what continuity there is runs through matter and accidents. A **school thesis**, not a doctrine: denied by Franciscan and Augustinian masters (the [form of corporeity](#terms)) and by Scotists, and not settled by Vienne.

*Introduced:* [5.3](lessons/05-03-the-human-soul-in-the-system.md)

### The natural desire to see God

Aquinas's claim that every intellectual creature naturally desires the vision of the divine essence.

ST I q.12 a.1, whose *respondeo* is blunt — "if the intellect of the rational creature could not reach so far as to the first cause of things, the natural desire would remain void" — with I-II q.3 a.8. **The disputed point is not whether the texts say it but whether the desire is innate or elicited**, and that turns on what "nature" means: for Garrigou-Lagrange and the commentators a nature is a complete essence specified by an end proportioned to its own powers, so the desire must be conditional or elicited and pure nature is at least possible; for de Lubac "nature" means the concrete spiritual creature as actually made, the kind of being defined by a capacity that outruns its powers. *Humani Generis* left that crux where it found it — see the [Authority and levels](#authority-and-levels) table.

*Introduced:* [6.4](lessons/06-04-transcendental-thomism-and-the-ressourcement-quarrel.md)

## Arguments

The canonical arguments as numbered-premise skeletons, in the form the lessons give them. Each premise's source is marked, because in this course marking the source is half the exegesis.

### The First Way

From motion to a first mover, moved by no other (ST I q.2 a.3).

1. Some things are in motion.
2. Motion is the reduction of something from potentiality to actuality — the article's own definition; see [Motion as reduction from potency to act](#motion-as-reduction-from-potency-to-act), and note the live dispute over how wide the term runs.
3. Nothing is so reduced except by something already in act: only what already has a perfection can hand it on.
4. Nothing can be mover and moved in the same respect, so whatever is moved is moved by another.
5. The series cannot run to infinity, because in an essentially ordered series intermediates move only as moved ([`metaphysics` 4.4](../metaphysics/lessons/04-04-ordered-causal-series.md), assumed).
6. ∴ A first mover, moved by no other, "and this everyone understands to be God" — a [nominal definition](#nominal-definition-of-god), not a divine nature.

The *Summa contra Gentiles* I.13 gives the same argument room: it treats premises 4 and 5 as two propositions each needing proof, supplies three Aristotelian arguments for each, and raises two objections against its own chapter — that the arguments presuppose the eternity of motion, and that they presuppose the first moved body to be self-moving. Aquinas's replies are honest rather than triumphant: the demonstration is in fact *more* efficacious granted an eternal world, since a world with a beginning obviously needs a cause; and if the first mover is not taken to be self-moving, an unmoved separate mover follows at once. The strain is not that the argument fails but that the tidy *Summa* version reads as free-standing while the fuller treatment leans on a cosmology nobody holds. Whether it survives detaching is [`philosophy-of-religion`](../philosophy-of-religion/syllabus.md) 2.3's.

*From* [3.2](lessons/03-02-the-first-and-second-ways-inside-the-system.md), with the text and its sources at [`ancient-medieval-philosophy` 7.3](../ancient-medieval-philosophy/lessons/07-03-the-five-ways-as-text.md)

### The Second Way

From an order of efficient causes to a first efficient cause (ST I q.2 a.3).

1. In the world of sense there is an order of efficient causes.
2. Nothing is the efficient cause of itself, for so it would be prior to itself.
3. In efficient causes following in order, the first is the cause of the intermediate and the intermediate of the ultimate, whether the intermediates be many or one.
4. To take away the cause is to take away the effect. So with no first there is no ultimate and no intermediate.
5. ∴ A first efficient cause, "to which everyone gives the name of God."

Read it for **tense and role**: every verb is present, and "first", "intermediate", "ultimate" are positions in a structure fixed by what causes what, not by when anything happened. And note what the passage never says — not that everything has a cause, but that nothing causes *itself* and that intermediates cause only as caused.

*From* [3.2](lessons/03-02-the-first-and-second-ways-inside-the-system.md)

### Why a first mover must be pure act

ST I q.3 a.1, second argument of the *respondeo*: the hinge of the whole treatise on God.

1. Motion is the reduction of something from potency to act, and nothing is so reduced except by something already in act. **[q.2 a.3; act and potency from 2.1]**
2. In any single thing that passes from potency to act, its potency is prior **in time** to its act — the wood is cold before it is hot. **[q.3 a.1, conceded]**
3. Absolutely speaking, act is nonetheless prior to potency, since a potency is reduced to act only by something in act. **[q.3 a.1, granting 2]**
4. The first mover and the first efficient cause are moved by nothing and caused by nothing. **[q.2 a.3]**
5. Whatever has potency of any kind depends on something else in act to reduce it — a potency is a debt, paid from outside. **[1, 3]**
6. ∴ In the first being there is no potentiality whatever: it is [pure act](#pure-act).

Two things to notice: no premise comes from authority, so this is a philosophical argument inside a theological book and can be assessed on its own terms; and premise 2 is a concession, not an oversight.

*From* [3.2](lessons/03-02-the-first-and-second-ways-inside-the-system.md)

### The argument for the real distinction

The *De Ente et Essentia* argument, paraphrased — and the dispute over where it stands in the order of proof.

1. You can grasp *what* a thing is without knowing *that* it is (the phoenix observation), so the concept of an essence does not contain its existing.
2. At most one thing can be its own *esse*: a nature that is nothing but existing has no difference, no matter and no recipient by which a second one could be told from it.
3. So in everything else *esse* is received from another, and essence stands to it as potency to act.
4. Whatever has a perfection without being it has it by participation, and what is by participation is caused by what is such essentially — "as everything ignited is caused by fire".
5. ∴ Every creature is caused in its very existing. ST I q.61 a.1 runs exactly this on the angels, *citing* q.3 a.4 rather than arguing it.

**The disputed order, which Thomists have not settled.** On the earlier route, step 2 does real work on its own, so the distinction can be demonstrated before God's existence is reached. On the later route, step 2 yields only a conditional; what you actually have beforehand is step 1, which is about *understanding*, and a distinction in *things* arrives only once a being whose essence is its *esse* has been shown to exist. The stake is structural: on the later reading *De Ente* ch. 4 analyses creatures rather than opening an independent road to God, and the Five Ways carry the whole evidential weight. The *Summa*'s own order is a datum both sides claim and settles nothing, since the order of teaching and the order of proof are not the same. (The chapter of *De Ente* holding the argument is cited as ch. 4; numbering differs between editions, and this course does not resolve it.)

*From* [2.2](lessons/02-02-esse-and-essence-the-real-distinction.md); the pattern to carry forward is that the real distinction is rarely a Thomist conclusion and almost always a premise

### The derivation of the attributes

How ST I qq.4–11 hang on q.3 — and why teaching order is not proof order.

**The prologue's plan has five items, not eight:** simplicity, perfection, infinity, immutability, unity. Goodness, presence in all things and eternity are each attached later by the prologue of their own question. The stated reason for taking perfection second is an *objection*, not a premise: simple things in the material world are the imperfect bits out of which bigger things are made, so "simple" sounds like *meagre* and the charge has to be cleared at once.

| Question | Attribute | What the article actually argues from |
|---|---|---|
| q.4 | Perfection | Actuality: an agent as such is in act, so the first **active** principle must be most actual and therefore most perfect. q.3 is *not* among its premises |
| qq.5–6 | Goodness | Perfection ("whatever is perfect is called good"); q.6 a.3, God alone good by his essence, cites q.3 a.6 |
| q.7 | Infinity | [Act is limited by potency](#act-is-limited-by-potency), with q.3 a.4 cited by number: being not received in anything is unlimited |
| q.8 | Presence in all things | Filed by its prologue under infinity, but argued from **causality**: God is being by his essence, so created being is his proper effect, as igniting is fire's, and he causes it as long as the thing is kept in being — so he is in each thing innermostly. The only back-reference is to q.7 a.1, for being's innermost character |
| q.9 | Immutability | Three arguments: from pure act (q.2 a.3), from simplicity (a.7, cited by number), and from infinite perfection |
| q.10 | Eternity | Follows on immutability; a.2, God is his own eternity |
| q.11 | Unity | Three sources, one of them simplicity |

The treatise is a **web, not a chain**, and the prologues alone will not show you the web. Do not read the five-item plan as a proof chain.

*From* [3.4](lessons/03-04-divine-simplicity-and-the-attributes.md)

### The three ways

Causality, negation and eminence: **three phases of one motion** from a creature to a word said of God, not three arguments with three conclusions.

Start from a perfection you actually meet — this wise man, this good act.

1. **Causality.** Whatever perfection is here came from God, the cause of everything that is; so something in God answers to it.
2. **Negation** (*remotion*). Strip the creaturely way of having it: received, limited, held as an accident by a subject, had partly and by degrees. This is not a new activity — it is what qq.3–11 have been doing all along.
3. **Eminence.** Reaffirm the perfection of God, not in the thinned sense left by step 2 but as his by excess: the creature falls short of God, and God does not fall short of the creature.

Aquinas performs the circuit at ST I q.12 a.12, whose closing sentence *is* the three ways in order — cause of them all; creatures differ from him; not by any defect on his part but because he superexceeds them all — and names it as a method at q.13 a.1 ("as their principle, and also by way of excellence and remotion") and q.13 a.8 ad 2. The triad comes from Dionysius; what Aquinas adds is the causal metaphysics underneath, since the circuit works because creatures *are* effects. Drop step 1 and there is no warrant for the word; drop step 2 and God is a very clever man; drop step 3 and "God is wise" means only "God is not stupid".

*From* [4.2](lessons/04-02-knowing-god-from-creatures-the-three-ways.md)

### Why analogy and not univocity or equivocity

ST I q.13 a.5's *respondeo*, in the premises lesson [4.4](lessons/04-04-analogy.md) marks.

1. An effect that does not match the full power of its cause receives its likeness in a lesser measure, so what is divided and multiplied in the effects is one in the cause. **[q.13 a.4; behind it q.4 a.2]**
2. So a perfection-word said of a creature signifies something *distinct* from that creature's essence, power and existence; said of God, nothing distinct from these. **[simplicity, 3.4]**
3. And the word, said of a creature, circumscribes what it signifies; said of God it leaves the thing signified uncomprehended and exceeding the name.
4. ∴ No name is predicated univocally of God and creatures.
5. Nor purely equivocally, "as some have said": then nothing could be known or demonstrated of God from creatures, and every argument would be exposed to the fallacy of equivocation. **[the philosophers who proved things about God; Romans 1:20]**
6. So they are said analogically, "according to proportion", which happens in two ways: "either according as many things are proportionate to one … or according as one thing is proportionate to another."
7. Which applies? We can name God only from creatures **[a.1]**, so whatever is said of God and creatures is said according to the relation of a creature to God as its principle and cause.
8. ∴ A mean between pure equivocation and simple univocation: the idea is not one, as in univocals, nor wholly diverse, as in equivocals.

Premise 6 is genuinely ambiguous on the page, which is why [attribution](#analogy-of-attribution) and [proper proportionality](#analogy-of-proper-proportionality) are both live readings of it. Premise 5 is worth a pause: the argument for analogy is that theology must be *possible*, so the doctrine is defended by its consequences as much as by its metaphysics.

*From* [4.4](lessons/04-04-analogy.md)

### Creation is not a change

Change needs a subject that persists. In creation there is none, so there is nothing to be first this and then that.

1. Change means that the same something should be different now from what it was before. **[the definition, from [`ancient-medieval-philosophy` 3.3](../ancient-medieval-philosophy/lessons/03-03-change-privation-potency-and-act.md)]**
2. Sometimes that same thing is the same actually (motion in quantity, quality, place), sometimes the same only potentially (substantial change, whose subject is matter).
3. In creation the whole substance of a thing is produced, so nothing pre-exists. **[q.44 a.1, reached through participation]**
4. ∴ The "first not existing at all, afterwards existing" belongs only to **our way of understanding**, not to a structure in the thing (ST I q.45 a.2 ad 2).
5. And, movement removed from action and passion, only relation remains — so what creation puts in the creature is [a relation to the Creator](#creation-as-a-real-relation-in-the-creature) (q.45 a.3).

What Aquinas concedes is exactly and only the grammar: "except according to a mode of understanding". Corollaries worth having: **"before creation" names no time**, since time is measured by change, creation is not a change, and time is itself created (q.46 a.3); and creation is not an event in the world's history, so it is not the earliest member of a series of changes. Hence the sentence you can use anywhere: *creation is not a claim about the first moment; it is a claim about every moment.* Note also that this is the **stronger** claim, not a hedge — saying God changed nothing into something would make him one cause among causes, working on a subject.

*From* [5.1](lessons/05-01-creation-and-conservation.md)

### The whole effect from both causes

One effect, wholly from God and wholly from the creature, because "cause" is not said in the same sense twice.

The rule is ST I q.105 a.5 ad 2: **"One action does not proceed from two agents of the same order. But nothing hinders the same action from proceeding from a primary and a secondary agent."** *Summa contra Gentiles* III.69–70 argues it at length against those who take proper actions away from natural things, and gives the formula the tradition has used since: the effect is not partly God's and partly nature's but wholly both, in different ways, as one effect is wholly the instrument's and wholly the principal agent's. Any sentence beginning "how much of it did God do" has already gone wrong: a 50/50 split and a 100/0 split are the same error, treating "cause" as one quantity to be divided.

The two collapse positions this is meant to exclude: **occasionalism**, which denies that creatures have real active powers, and **deism**, which denies that God gives, preserves and applies those powers here and now. Where the account strains is evil — "permits" carries the whole weight and has to carry it asymmetrically: God causes the *being* of every act, and evil is a privation, so it is not caused the way an act is caused. Dual causation by itself does not make God the author of the defect, because the defect is not an act; whether the asymmetry survives the worst cases is [`philosophy-of-religion`](../philosophy-of-religion/syllabus.md)'s problem of evil.

*From* [5.2](lessons/05-02-providence-and-secondary-causes.md), with [2.4](lessons/02-04-the-transcendentals.md) on privation

## The map of the Summa

### Citing the Summa

Read a reference right to left.

> *ST* I-II q.94 a.2 ad 1

**ad 1** is the reply to the first objection; **a.2** is article 2; **q.94** is question 94; **I-II** is the part. The five parts are **I** (*Prima Pars*), **I-II** (*Prima Secundae*), **II-II** (*Secunda Secundae*), **III** (*Tertia Pars*) and the **Supplement**. There is no "Part II" to cite: the second part comes in two halves and you must say which — and the two repairs are not always equally available, since I-II q.3 a.8 exists (happiness as the vision of the divine essence) while II-II q.3 has only two articles. A reference with no *ad* points to the body of the article, the *respondeo*.

*From* [1.1](lessons/01-01-the-plan-of-the-summa.md)

### Aquinas by genre

Each genre takes its order from somewhere, and only one takes it from the subject.

| Genre | When | What fixes the order |
|---|---|---|
| Scriptural commentaries | throughout; his actual job as a *magister in sacra pagina* | the biblical book's |
| Commentary on the *Sentences* (the *Scriptum*) | 1250s, as a bachelor | Peter Lombard's |
| Disputed questions (*De Veritate*, *De Potentia*, *De Malo*) | the university's public debates, written up | the occasions on which they were held |
| *Summa contra Gentiles* | early 1260s; four books of continuous argument in short chapters, not articles | a purpose: what unaided reason reaches against what only revelation gives |
| *Summa Theologiae* | begun in Italy about 1265; the Second Part finished at Paris, Part III written at Naples and broken off in 1273 | nothing but its own subject matter and the reader it names |

The *Summa contra Gentiles* is **not** a first draft of the *Summa Theologiae*: different genre, audience and order, so where the two differ that is a fact about the works, not a development.

*From* [1.1](lessons/01-01-the-plan-of-the-summa.md)

### The order of the subject matter

The general prologue's one positive standard, set against three complaints.

Aquinas complains that beginners have been hampered by "the multiplication of useless questions, articles, and arguments" (the disputed questions), by teaching ordered "according as the plan of the book might require, or the occasion of the argument offer" (commentary and disputation), and by "frequent repetition". Against all three he sets the sequence the material itself has, rather than the sequence in which it came up — and one audience, **beginners**: young Dominicans who have done the arts course, so that milk rather than meat is about order and proportion, not dilution.

*From* [1.1](lessons/01-01-the-plan-of-the-summa.md)

### The three parts of the Summa

From the prologue to I q.2: one subject, God, under three descriptions.

**(1) God.** Part I: the divine essence — whether God is (q.2), how he is *not* (qq.3–11), his operations, knowledge, will, power (to q.26); the distinction of persons (qq.27–43); the procession of creatures — creation, angels, the six days, man, government (qq.44–119).
**(2) The rational creature's movement towards God.** I-II and II-II.
**(3) Christ, who as man is our way to God.** Part III, breaking off at q.90 inside the treatise on penance, with the **Supplement** compiled afterwards by another hand.

Note the asymmetry in what generates the parts: the first two are read off the descriptions of the subject itself, while the third is added because the way to the end was **given in the Incarnation**, a datum of revelation rather than another aspect under which God is considered. The plan is recursive — every prologue is a table of contents for what follows — and the phrase "as is clear from what has been already said" points back to q.1, so the division is argued, not asserted. Aquinas laid down his pen on 6 December 1273 after an experience at Mass he would not describe beyond saying that what he had written now seemed to him of little worth; he died the following March.

*From* [1.1](lessons/01-01-the-plan-of-the-summa.md)

### Exitus and reditus

M.-D. Chenu's reading of the plan (*Introduction à l'étude de saint Thomas d'Aquin*, 1950): a Neoplatonic circle inherited through Dionysius, all things going out from God and returning to him.

**What it explains:** Part I's third section is announced in Aquinas's own words as "the procession of creatures" from God; the Second Part opens with the human being as the principle of his own acts, moving towards the end; and it explains at a stroke why a book about God contains a treatise on the passions. **Where it strains:** Part III. Christ, who as man is our way, arrives *after* the return has been laid out, as the means by which it is actually made; on a strict circle he should be the form of the return, not an appendix to it. Aquinas never names the scheme, and the general prologue names only the order of the subject matter and the needs of beginners. Defenders answer that the *ordo disciplinae* is not an order of importance, and that a historical scheme can organize a text whose author never labels it. **An influential reading, not settled**, and this course holds both side by side.

### Ordo disciplinae

The order of teaching is not an order of importance.

The standing reply to the charge that the *Summa* makes Christ an afterthought, or that its arrangement commits Aquinas to a ranking. Aquinas is arranging a course for beginners by the order the subject matter has: you must know what an act is before you can say what grace does to acts, and what the last end is before you can say who the way to it is. The Part III prologue's own word for what it adds is *completion of the work of theology*, not supplement. What the reply concedes is real — the order does let a reader take the Second Part as a free-standing philosophical ethics, and readers have. What it denies is that Aquinas invited them to. Both halves are live among Catholic theologians.

### The question as the unit

A question of the *Summa* is one argument spread over ordered articles; an article read alone is an argument with its premises removed.

The protocol, in the order you should work: **(1)** read the prologue first and treat its order as a dependency claim — q.13's sixth item is written as conditional on an answer the fifth has not yet given, so the list is a route and not an index; **(2)** for each article find the back-references ("as shown above", "in the preceding article") and fetch what they supply; **(3)** for each objection ask whose position it is — it is never a straw man, and if it looks silly you have misread it; **(4)** treat the [*sed contra*](#sed-contra) as a premise with a source, not as the answer; **(5)** find the [distinction](#distinguo) in the *respondeo*, then watch it do piecework in the replies. The four parts of an article themselves are assumed from [`philosophical-method` 4.4](../philosophical-method/lessons/04-04-arguing-within-a-tradition-the-disputed-question.md).

### The authorities

### Man as the principle of his own acts

The phrase in the I-II prologue that generates the whole Second Part.

Three expressions carry the plan. *Exemplar* names what Part I did; *image* names the new object — Damascene's "intelligent being endowed with free-will and self-movement"; and **principle of his actions** names the aspect under which the image is studied. Not the human being's nature, which was Part I's business at qq.75–89, but the human being as a **source of acts**. Everything in the next two parts is an act, a principle of acts, or the end acts are for. The Second Part is therefore not ethics bolted onto theology, and it could not begin at all without [5.2](lessons/05-02-providence-and-secondary-causes.md)'s result that creatures are true causes. Note: "image" here is doing exactly one job; the richer doctrine of the image is I q.93's.

### The last end as the vision of God

### The plan of the Third Part

Christ showed us in his own Person the way of truth; the consideration of the Saviour follows "in order to complete the work of theology".

What is printed after q.90 is the [Supplement](#terms), compiled from the *Scriptum* by another hand.

## Schools and thinkers

### The commentators

Aquinas was canonized by John XXII on 18 July 1323 and proclaimed a Doctor of the universal Church by Pius V in 1567, who had the first complete edition of his works printed at Rome in 1570 — with Cajetan's commentary running alongside the *Summa*. Between those dates a genre grew up around him.

| Who | When | Key work | One line |
|---|---|---|---|
| John Capreolus, OP | d. 1444 | *Defensiones theologiae divi Thomae Aquinatis*, four books on the order of the *Sentences* | the first sustained defence of Aquinas against Scotus, Durandus, Aureol and Henry of Ghent; called in the school "prince of Thomists" — an attribution this course flags as unverified |
| Thomas de Vio Cajetan, OP | 1469–1534 | commentary on the whole *Summa* (1507–1522); *De Nominum Analogia* (1498); a commentary on *De Ente et Essentia* | the first commentary on the entire *Summa*, and primarily a defence against Scotus; his readings became the school's default, on [analogy](#analogy-of-proper-proportionality) above all |
| Sylvester of Ferrara, OP | d. 1528 | commentary on the *Summa contra Gentiles* | the companion volume to Cajetan's; the two were later printed with the Leonine text |
| Domingo Báñez, OP | 1528–1604 | Salamanca commentaries on the *Summa*; the Dominican side against Molina's *Concordia* (1588) | [physical premotion](#the-dispute-over-efficacious-grace), and the Roman congregations on the aids of grace |
| John of St Thomas, OP | 1589–1644 | *Cursus philosophicus*; *Cursus theologicus* | the school as a complete course of studies — logic, natural philosophy, metaphysics, then theology: the shape the manuals inherit |
| Francisco Suárez, SJ | 1548–1617 | *Disputationes Metaphysicae* (1597), fifty-four disputations | **not a commentary at all**: metaphysics ordered by topic, standing free of any master's text; taught directly in Protestant as well as Catholic universities, which is how scholastic vocabulary reached Descartes, Leibniz and Wolff |

**The genre's three features**, which explain everything the tradition later fought about: a commentary follows the master's order and can only expound, extend and defend where the text raises the matter; it answers the **commentator's** opponents, not the master's; and it teaches a school with an order of study. The consequence is that a commentator **systematizes** — a position Aquinas takes once, in passing, acquires a name, a canonical argument, a standard list of objections and a slot in the syllabus — and that the school's vocabulary absorbs its opponents', since you cannot answer Scotus while refusing his distinctions.

*From* [6.1](lessons/06-01-the-commentators.md)

### The essentialism charge and the genre defence

Whether the commentators handed Aquinas on or quietly replaced him. **Both sides are school theses; no act of the Church touches either.**

**The charge** (Gilson, *Being and Some Philosophers*, 1949; de Lubac, *Surnaturel*, 1946). **C1** For Aquinas *esse* is [the act of the existing thing](#the-act-of-existing), not one more item on its list of features. **C2** The commentators had to defend that against Scotus, inside a taxonomy of distinctions Scotus set, so the question became how many distinct somethings a creature contains — "real, formal, or of reason?" in place of "what is it for a creature to be?". **C3** A metaphysics organized round essence can still reach the right conclusions while the reason Aquinas cared never surfaces: the thesis survives, the centre of gravity moves. **C4** They also read later problems back into the text — de Lubac's case is pure nature. **∴** What the nineteenth century received is an essentialist system under Aquinas's name.

**The defence.** **R1** A commentary is a weapon in a live fight, and you cannot answer an opponent while refusing his vocabulary. **R2** New questions are not distortions: Baius, Molina and Luther asked things Aquinas was never asked, and a tradition that declined to answer would have been dead, not faithful. **R3** "Back to the text itself" is itself a modern, philological instinct, about a century old when Gilson used it — the charge grades the commentators by a standard invented after them. **R4** And the charge is a reading too: Gilson's Aquinas, discoverer of *esse* against Greek essentialism, is a twentieth-century portrait.

**Where they actually part:** both sides grant the history — of course the commentators answered their own century. They disagree about whether the result may still be called Aquinas's teaching, which no further history will settle.

### Leo XIII's programme and its instruments

*Aeterni Patris* (4 August 1879) has three parts, and people routinely remember two.

**An argument**: philosophy prepares the road to faith (n. 4), demonstrates the preambles (n. 5, restating Vatican I), is what theology needs to be a science at all (n. 6, [philosophy as handmaiden](#philosophy-as-handmaiden)), and is the faith's defence (n. 7) — with a diagnosis that the public crisis has a philosophical cause (n. 2). **A person**: not scholasticism in general but Aquinas, drawn "from his own fountains", not from streams said to flow from them (nn. 17–18, 31). **A limit**: what the scholastic doctors said too subtly, too carelessly, or in a way that "ill agrees with the discoveries of a later age" is not proposed for imitation (n. 31). Reading that clause is the discipline of the whole module; note that the verb governs **proposing**, not condemning, that the excluded material is attributed to the *doctors* in the plural rather than to Aquinas, and that the clause names **no judge** of what is "improbable in whatever way". The exact section of the encyclical holding the limiting clause is something this course flags as to be verified rather than relied on.

| Instrument | Date | What it is |
|---|---|---|
| Leonine Commission | 1880 | the critical edition: fixes wording from manuscripts, separates authentic from spurious works, and **dates** them — which makes a chronology, and so a *developing* Aquinas, possible |
| *Doctoris Angelici* | 29 June 1914 | Pius X's motu proprio, headed *pro Italia et insulis adiacentibus*; makes the *Summa* the textbook of lectures in degree-granting faculties. Its reach beyond Italy comes through the 1917 Code, not through itself |
| The Twenty-Four Theses | 27 July 1914 | approved by the Congregation of Studies as "plainly containing the principles and major pronouncements" of St Thomas |
| The 1916 *responsum* | 7 March 1916 | the Congregation of Seminaries and Universities, confirmed by Benedict XV, on whether the theses must be imposed: they express Aquinas's genuine doctrine and are proposed *veluti tutae normae directivae*, safe directive norms. Neither a definition nor an imposition. (It cites the theses decree as of 29 July 1914 while the decree is dated 27 July.) |
| 1917 Code, can. 1366 §2 | 1917 | professors are to handle rational philosophy and theology, and students' training in them, entirely according to the **method, doctrine and principles** of the Angelic Doctor, and hold to these reverently — a law about teaching. The canon number is believed correct and is flagged as to be verified |
| The manuals | after 1879 | Latin textbooks organized thesis, proof, adversaries, objections answered. What they do well: the whole field mapped, vocabulary exact, every position locatable. What they do badly: a thesis stated for proof is a thesis already decided, the question form disappears, and the author's development is invisible |

**The irony that shapes the module:** the same pope who wanted Aquinas taught ordered a critical edition, and a critical edition produces a *historical* author who can be used against the school the programme built up. Leo's revival carried its own solvent.

| Who | When | Key work | One-line thesis |
|---|---|---|---|
| Réginald Garrigou-Lagrange, OP | 1877–1964; Angelicum 1909–1959 | *Le sens commun*; *La synthèse thomiste* (1946); *The Three Ages of the Interior Life* (1938) | strict observance: the Twenty-Four Theses as the backbone, act and potency first, the whole system as one deductive order — because if dogmatic formulas are to hold, the metaphysics they are stated in must hold always |

### What divides them

Not conclusions. Two premises, one in each quarrel.

**Quarrel 1: can Thomism be set out as ordered theses?** The perennialist argument runs: a philosophical claim is made true by what things are, not by what anyone thought **(1)**; what things are does not change **(2)**; Aquinas's principles are true **(3)**; so they are perennially true, a *philosophia perennis* of which Aquinas is the fullest statement rather than the owner **(4)**; a body of truths independent of its date can be extracted and set out as ordered propositions **(5)**; so the right teaching form is a numbered, ordered set of theses, and the commentators were transmitters **(6)**. **Gilson grants 1–3 and denies 5**, and for a reason about *this* content: every apparatus philosophy has for stating things — definitions, theses, *what* a thing is — is built to state essences, so the one doctrine most likely to evaporate in a restatement is the act of existing. If that is right, "is this still Thomism?" is a **historical** question, and reading Aquinas historically is not a preliminary to being a Thomist but what being one requires. **Maritain** splits 5: the principles are perennial, but a list of conclusions is a summary of a knowledge and never the way into one — you reach the metaphysical order by the intuition of being, so he needs neither the thesis list nor the archive, and goes on using John of St Thomas.

| | Garrigou-Lagrange | Maritain | Gilson |
|---|---|---|---|
| The core of Thomism | act and potency, first in an ordered set of theses | the intuition of being, and the distinction of the orders of knowing | the primacy of *esse*, Aquinas's own discovery |
| What he fears losing | the stability of dogmatic formulas, hence of the metaphysics they use | the peace between the orders, each swallowing the others | the discovery itself, silently, while the vocabulary survives |
| The commentators | transmitters who drew out what was there | a resource, used freely and critically | essentialists who kept the words and lost the thing |
| Is Thomism perennial? | yes, and that is why it can be taught as theses | perennial in its nature; always dated in its exercise | its truth is not dated; the achievement is, and can be lost |
| How you check a Thomism | against the theses, deductively | against the intuition, and by keeping the orders distinct | against the texts, historically |

Read the rows, not the columns: three answers to premise 5, not three parties with fixed membership.

**Quarrel 2: what does "nature" mean?** The whole natural-desire dispute turns on one word — see [The natural desire to see God](#the-natural-desire-to-see-god). Garrigou-Lagrange must deny that the desire is innate; de Lubac must deny that a nature is fixed by an end proportioned to its powers.

**The 1930s "Christian philosophy" debate** sits across the first quarrel. The objection (Bréhier, Brunschvicg): if a doctrine's warrant is revelation it is theology, and if its warrant is reason, calling it Christian adds nothing. **Gilson**: the category is historical — creation from nothing, God as He Who Is, the person entered philosophy because faith proposed them, and once there are defensible on rational grounds; "Christian philosophy" names philosophies whose *genesis* required faith and whose *structure* is rational. **Maritain**: distinguish a philosophy's **nature**, fixed by its object, from its **state**, the concrete condition under which someone philosophizes. Allies on the verdict, opposed on premise 5 — Gilson's answer makes history unskippable, Maritain's keeps a nature standing clear of it. Other Thomists held that a philosophy is simply true or false and that "Christian" can only describe the philosopher.

**The transcendental objection and its reply.** Gilson and Garrigou-Lagrange: starting from the subject concedes the modern turn; Aquinas starts from what is and reaches God by [demonstration *quia*](#demonstration-quia-and-propter-quid) from effects, so if God is instead whatever every act of knowing implicitly reaches, he becomes the **horizon of consciousness**, always co-affirmed and never concluded to. The reply: the *Vorgriff* is no experience of God and yields no concept of him; it is a condition reached by reflecting on an act, and the step to a real term is an inference, not an intuition. Still disputed. Rahner's further guard is the **supernatural existential** — the natural dynamism is a condition of finite knowing as such, while the graced orientation is God's free offer, owed to no one; where it strains is that no phenomenological mark separates the two, so a gratuity you cannot locate may be a gratuity in name, and the distinction is itself drawn to protect a doctrinal requirement, which is the move de Lubac accused Cajetan of.

## Authority and levels

The course's signature discipline. The ladder is [`fundamental-theology` 4.4](../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-04-the-ladder-of-doctrinal-authority.md), used unchanged, with [4.5](../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-05-reading-a-documents-weight.md) for reading a document's genre; where that course and this one differ, **it wins**. Its cardinal rule is the one to import: **it grades acts, not topics.** Never ask how important Thomism is. Ask which act proposed *this particular sentence*, and what that act proposed it **as**. A level is not a verdict on truth: "rung 5" means no act binds you, not that the thesis is weak.

**Three things, never to be collapsed.** (1) **A doctrine that happens to be stated in scholastic words** — the vocabulary is the dress, and the level belongs to the act underneath. (2) **The Church's recommendation of Aquinas** — real acts with real force, whose object is a *master* and a *curriculum*, not a list of propositions. (3) **A [school thesis](#school-thesis)** — no magisterial act proposes it, and nobody is bound.

### The two errors

Mirror images, graded equally hard, in every module.

**Error A, promotion.** *Aquinas held it, and the Church recommends Aquinas, therefore it binds.* This runs the recommendation through a school thesis and comes out with a dogma. It fails at the first step: recommending a teacher is not proposing his conclusions.

**Error B, demotion.** *That is just the Thomists' Aristotelian apparatus, and the Church canonizes no philosophy, so I may take it or leave it.* This runs a genuine conciliar definition backwards through the same vocabulary and comes out with an opinion. It fails because the sentence's level was fixed by the council, not by the idiom it was phrased in.

A third form runs alongside: **treating a disciplinary norm as a doctrine**, or a doctrine as mere discipline. Can. 252 §3 is law — it can be dispensed, amended or repealed, which no dogma can. And the question "is Thomism binding?" is not well formed; only a particular sentence is answerable.

*Stated outright in* [6.5](lessons/06-05-what-the-church-has-said-about-thomism.md); drilled from [1.2](lessons/01-02-reading-a-whole-question.md) onward

### What Vatican I defined about natural knowledge of God

A **capacity**, not a **performance** — and the distinction is the model for every level assignment in this course.

*Dei Filius* (1870), in its first canon on revelation, defines that the one true God can be certainly known by the natural light of human reason through created things. It canonizes no argument and names no philosopher. On the ladder, rung 1, *de fide*. So: that reason **can** reach God is defined; that the First Way is a sound demonstration is not defined, not taught at any level, and argued about by Catholics in good standing. Upward error: treating "the First Way is sound" as something a Catholic must hold. Downward error: treating the canon itself as one school's philosophy, so that natural knowledge of God is optional. **Owned by** [`fundamental-theology` 1.2](../fundamental-theology/lessons/01-02-natural-knowledge-of-god.md), which reads the canon word by word.

*Used as the model case in* [3.1](lessons/03-01-demonstrating-that-god-exists.md)

### Simplicity at three levels

The cleanest test of the whole discipline, because all three levels are in play over one word.

**The doctrine.** That God is altogether simple is taught by Lateran IV (1215), whose opening confession professes one true God, three persons in one altogether simple essence, substance or nature; and by Vatican I, *Dei Filius* ch. 1. Both are solemn conciliar acts proposing it as the Catholic faith — **rung 1**. Note the date: Lateran IV is a decade before Aquinas was born. **The analysis.** What simplicity excludes, item by item — above all q.3 a.4's identification of essence with *esse*, which leans on the real distinction in creatures — is Thomist theology, defined by no council. **Rung 5.** **The alternative.** Scotus's [formal distinction among the attributes](#the-formal-distinction-among-the-attributes) affirms the doctrine and rejects the analysis: a Catholic option, not a denial of Lateran IV. Both errors graded strictly — calling Scotus's position a denial of simplicity promotes a school thesis to a council's level; calling simplicity "Aquinas's theory of God" demotes a conciliar doctrine to a school thesis.

*From* [3.4](lessons/03-04-divine-simplicity-and-the-attributes.md); generalised in [6.5](lessons/06-05-what-the-church-has-said-about-thomism.md)

### The greater unlikeness

Lateran IV (1215), canon 2, stated while condemning Joachim of Fiore's error about the Trinity: between Creator and creature no likeness can be noted without a greater unlikeness having to be noted between them.

Conciliar teaching, and the **guardrail binding every theory of analogy** — the Thomist one included, which must show its mean leaning to the unlikeness side rather than putting God and creatures on one scale. It is also the target of the twentieth century's argument about the *analogia entis*: Erich Przywara's *Analogia Entis* (1932) built a metaphysics out of the Lateran formula, making ever-greater dissimilarity the rhythm of the creature's relation to God; Karl Barth in the same years called the analogy of being the "invention of Antichrist" and the reason he could not become a Catholic, since any common concept of being spanning God and creature gives the creature a foothold to reach God without grace. The Thomist reply is already in place — analogy is not a common concept, and the idea is denied to be one. Barth later developed analogies of his own, of faith and of relation. Note that the objection is to the **enterprise**, so both readings of q.13 a.5 answer it alike. And note the level trap on the other side: the canon is not "only a claim about Trinitarian language" because it appears inside a condemnation of Joachim.

### The sequence of acts about Thomism

Seven centuries of increasingly warm recommendation, and its object never changes.

| Act | What it does | What it binds |
|---|---|---|
| Canonization (1323); Doctor of the Church (Pius V, 1567) | declares sanctity; names him a Doctor, with a general commendation | nothing propositional |
| *Aeterni Patris* (Leo XIII, 1879) | restores scholastic and above all Thomist philosophy in Catholic schools, **with its limiting clause** | rung 3, and a programme of study |
| *Doctoris Angelici* (Pius X, 1914); the Twenty-Four Theses, same year | sets capital theses as the foundation of instruction | contested from the start; **do not report a settled level** |
| 1917 Code can. 1366 §2; *Studiorum Ducem* (1923) | law: teach by his method, doctrine and principles | a curriculum |
| *Humani Generis* (Pius XII, 1950) | warns against abandoning the scholastic vocabulary the Church has tested; notes that a papal pronouncement on a disputed matter closes it for theologians | rung 3 |
| *Optatam Totius* 16 (Vatican II, 1965) | seminarians are to penetrate the mysteries of salvation by speculation, **with St Thomas as teacher** (*S. Thoma magistro*) | a curriculum, by conciliar decree |
| *Lumen Ecclesiae* (Paul VI, 1974) | apostolic letter for the seventh centenary; restates the recommendation and its reasons | rung 3 |
| 1983 Code can. 252 §3 | law: dogmatic theology especially with St Thomas as teacher | a curriculum |
| *Fides et Ratio* (John Paul II, 1998) | proposes Aquinas as a master of thought and a model of how to do theology; states that the Church has no philosophy of her own | rung 3 |

The right-hand column never contains a proposition of Aquinas's that a Catholic must now hold. **A stronger act does not make a broader object:** a conciliar decree outranks an encyclical in dignity, and the object is still a curriculum.

**Two sentences that look like a flat contradiction.** *Studiorum Ducem* (1923) says the Church "has adopted his philosophy for her own"; *Fides et Ratio* says she has no philosophy of her own. Both are encyclical statements at rung 3, so neither outranks the other by its act. Read each for what it is doing: Pius XI claims **adoption in use** — the Church thinks in these terms — and the same encyclical, thirty sections later, quotes the Code's teaching norm *and* reserves liberty, "in questions, which in Catholic schools are matter of controversy between the most reputable authorities, let none be prevented from adhering to whatever opinion seems to him the more probable." John Paul II denies that any **system is canonized** as the faith's own, such that the faith would stand or fall with it, while proposing Aquinas as the man in whom the right *relation* between faith and reason is visible. The formula: **the Church prescribes a teacher, not a set of theses.** Where genuine tension remains, a reader owes religious submission to both and a report of the difficulty, not a choice of favourites.

**Discipline is not doctrine, in either direction.** What happened to de Lubac in 1950 — removal from teaching and editing, three books withdrawn from Jesuit houses — was disciplinary, and discipline has no rung on the doctrinal ladder; he was made a cardinal in 1983. *Humani Generis* names no theologian, does not use the term "pure nature", and does not choose between the readings of the natural desire.

## Sources and hedges

**Translations.** Every passage of the *Summa Theologiae* and the *Summa contra Gentiles* quoted in this course is the English Dominican Province translation (public domain); *Aeterni Patris* and other pre-1930 papal documents are quoted in public-domain translations. Cajetan, John of St Thomas, Suárez and all twentieth-century authors are **paraphrased and cited by work**, never used for close reading. Where a modern translation is the only English (*Summa contra Gentiles* I.13 in [3.2](lessons/03-02-the-first-and-second-ways-inside-the-system.md)), the lessons describe rather than quote.

**Translators' quirks to know.** "A priori" and "a posteriori" in ST I q.2 a.2 render *propter quid* and *quia* — read as Latin, not as Kant. *Subiectum* is rendered "object" at q.1 a.7; read it as subject matter. The Latin of q.13 a.3 says two things must be considered, the perfection signified and the mode of signifying; the English is broken there. "The form the body" at q.85 a.1 is a typographical slip in the online text for "the form of the body".

**Flagged, not resolved.** These are carried with the lessons' hedging rather than settled: the exact section of *Aeterni Patris* holding the limiting clause; the Trent-altar story, which *Aeterni Patris* n. 22 and *Studiorum Ducem* n. 11 tell in different forms and which historians have not documented; the binding force of the Twenty-Four Theses and the wording of the later clarification; the 1917 Code's canon number on teaching by Aquinas's method (can. 1366 §2 is believed correct); the section numbers of *Fides et Ratio* on Aquinas and on "no philosophy of its own"; the chapter of *De Ente et Essentia* holding the real-distinction argument (numbering differs between editions); the passage in *De Veritate* on proportion against proportionality; and the attribution to Capreolus of "prince of Thomists". No DH numbers are used anywhere. Lateran IV on dissimilarity and on simplicity, and *Dei Filius* ch. 1 on simplicity and providence, are cited by council and chapter only. Chenu's *exitus*-*reditus* thesis is one influential reading, not a settled result.

## Pitfalls

Every lesson's "Watch out", deduped and grouped by theme.

### Reading the Summa

- "Summa" does not mean "summary" — it is a complete ordered treatment, and "for beginners" describes the order, not the difficulty. *([1.1](lessons/01-01-the-plan-of-the-summa.md))*
- "*ST* II q.94" is not a citation: the second part is always I-II or II-II, and the two repairs are not always equally available. *([1.1](lessons/01-01-the-plan-of-the-summa.md))*
- A Supplement reference is not the *Summa*'s plan; it is early Aquinas re-sorted posthumously, so say so when you cite it. *([1.1](lessons/01-01-the-plan-of-the-summa.md), [5.4](lessons/05-04-a-map-of-the-second-and-third-parts.md))*
- The *Summa contra Gentiles* is not a first draft of the *Summa Theologiae*; where the two differ, that is a fact about the works, not a development. *([1.1](lessons/01-01-the-plan-of-the-summa.md))*
- Do not read the *Summa* as a system of theses. It is a course: a claim can appear thin in Part I and be filled out later, and an objection or a *sed contra* is not Aquinas talking. *([1.1](lessons/01-01-the-plan-of-the-summa.md), [1.2](lessons/01-02-reading-a-whole-question.md))*
- The *sed contra* is *usually* Aquinas's answer, and "usually" is not "always" — read to the end of the article before quoting one. *([1.2](lessons/01-02-reading-a-whole-question.md))*
- An objection that looks like a straw man is an objection you have misread; in this genre a weak objection would be a defect. *([1.2](lessons/01-02-reading-a-whole-question.md))*
- Reverent interpretation is a method, not politeness, and it is exegetically expensive: when Aquinas tells you what Damascene meant, you have learned what Aquinas holds. *([1.2](lessons/01-02-reading-a-whole-question.md))*
- Quoting an *ad 1* as a free-standing thesis is the commonest way to misquote him: a reply is calibrated to its objection. *([1.1](lessons/01-01-the-plan-of-the-summa.md))*
- The map of the Second and Third Parts is a map: question numbers tell you where a text sits and what it presupposes, never what it argues. *([5.4](lessons/05-04-a-map-of-the-second-and-third-parts.md))*
- "One science" does not mean one subject matter — a class of subjects is not what unifies a science, which is exactly what q.1 a.3's objections assume and Aquinas denies. *([1.3](lessons/01-03-sacred-doctrine-one-science-about-god.md))*
- The handmaiden clause does not make philosophy dispensable: the stated reason for using it is the defect of *our* intelligence, a permanent feature of the learner. *([1.3](lessons/01-03-sacred-doctrine-one-science-about-god.md))*
- Do not import the modern word "science": *scientia* is demonstrated knowledge from principles, and whether theology is one strictly is freely disputed. *([1.3](lessons/01-03-sacred-doctrine-one-science-about-god.md))*

### Act, potency and the ladder

- Potency is not a small amount of act, and God's infinity is not a large amount: the infinite of quantity is the infinite of matter, and it is denied of God. *([2.1](lessons/02-01-act-and-potency-extended.md))*
- "Pure act" does not mean maximally busy; it means no potency, which is why immutability follows from it rather than fighting it. *([2.1](lessons/02-01-act-and-potency-extended.md))*
- A "real" distinction does not mean two things: if your statement lets the essence sit there waiting to be switched on, you have stated a caricature. *([2.2](lessons/02-02-esse-and-essence-the-real-distinction.md))*
- ST I q.61 a.1 does not *prove* the real distinction from the angels' case; it cites q.3 a.4 and applies it. Getting this backwards produces the circular version of the argument. *([2.2](lessons/02-02-esse-and-essence-the-real-distinction.md))*
- The one-*esse* thesis does not make the soul its own existence; it makes it the holder of an existence it also gives away. *([5.3](lessons/05-03-the-human-soul-in-the-system.md))*
- "Subsistent" does not make the soul a complete substance: it is not a hypostasis, lacking the complete nature of its species as a hand does, and a dualist's soul would need no natural inclination back to a body. *([5.3](lessons/05-03-the-human-soul-in-the-system.md))*

### Participation and the transcendentals

- Participation is not getting a piece of God — *esse* is not a quantity, and the guard is the word *formally*: the goodness denominating a creature good is the creature's own form. *([2.3](lessons/02-03-participation.md))*
- Aquinas is not a Platonist with better manners: he rejects the separate Forms by name, and what replaces them is the divine ideas, God knowing his own essence as imitable. *([2.3](lessons/02-03-participation.md))*
- That good adds no *reality* to being does not make calling a thing good empty: it adds an idea, a relation to appetite. *([2.4](lessons/02-04-the-transcendentals.md))*
- Evil as privation does not make evil unreal or minor: a privation is not a nothing-at-all, and moral evil is a real good joined to the privation of another. *([2.4](lessons/02-04-the-transcendentals.md))*

### The Five Ways and simplicity

- The Ways do not argue to a starter: Aquinas holds that reason cannot demonstrate that the world began, so he could hardly build his proofs on it. *([3.2](lessons/03-02-the-first-and-second-ways-inside-the-system.md))*
- The premise is not "everything has a cause", so "then what caused God?" misses: the text says nothing is the efficient cause of *itself*, and that intermediates cause only as caused. *([3.2](lessons/03-02-the-first-and-second-ways-inside-the-system.md))*
- "Necessary" in the Third Way means incorruptible, and Aquinas's universe is full of incorruptible creatures — importing the modern sense makes the article's second half unintelligible. *([3.3](lessons/03-03-the-third-fourth-and-fifth-ways.md))*
- Evolutionary biology answers Paley's argument from contrivance, not the Fifth Way, whose datum is that natures act always or nearly always the same way. *([3.3](lessons/03-03-the-third-fourth-and-fifth-ways.md))*
- "And this all call God" does not claim the five conclusions are one God; it attaches a name fixed by a nominal definition, and the identification is qq.3–11's work. *([3.3](lessons/03-03-the-third-fourth-and-fifth-ways.md))*
- "Self-evident in itself" is not a concession to the *Proslogion*: it is granted because God is his own existence, which is proved later at q.3 a.4, and a conclusion of q.3 cannot be a premise in q.2. *([3.1](lessons/03-01-demonstrating-that-god-exists.md))*
- A nominal definition is not an argument from a gap: the premise is that a sensible thing is an *effect*, which holds whether or not physics has explained how it came to be. *([3.1](lessons/03-01-demonstrating-that-god-exists.md), [4.2](lessons/04-02-knowing-god-from-creatures-the-three-ways.md))*
- "Simple" does not mean easy or thin: it means undivided, and on this account the only being with nothing missing. *([3.4](lessons/03-04-divine-simplicity-and-the-attributes.md))*
- God is not at the top of a scale — then he is *on* the scale, and q.3 a.5 has just denied any genus containing him. *([3.4](lessons/03-04-divine-simplicity-and-the-attributes.md))*
- The five-item plan of the q.3 prologue is an order of teaching, not a proof chain; only some edges in the derivation are ones the articles actually make. *([3.4](lessons/03-04-divine-simplicity-and-the-attributes.md))*

### Knowing and naming God

- The intelligible species is not what we think about: it is *that by which*, and the misreading turns Aquinas into an ancestor of the veil of ideas. *([4.1](lessons/04-01-how-a-human-intellect-knows.md))*
- Abstraction does not free the intellect from the body: it has no organ, and still cannot understand without turning to phantasms. *([4.1](lessons/04-01-how-a-human-intellect-knows.md))*
- "Proper object" does not mean "only possible object"; the constraint on knowing God is that he is outside the genus it names, not that we cannot think past what we have seen. *([4.1](lessons/04-01-how-a-human-intellect-knows.md))*
- "We cannot know what God is" is not agnosticism: the same prologue books how he is known and how he is named as qq.12 and 13, and negation is knowledge, not a confession of ignorance. *([3.4](lessons/03-04-divine-simplicity-and-the-attributes.md), [4.2](lessons/04-02-knowing-god-from-creatures-the-three-ways.md))*
- A failing mode of signification makes a sentence inadequate, not false; collapse the line and everything said of God becomes a picturesque lie. *([4.3](lessons/04-03-the-names-of-god.md))*
- "Not synonyms" does not mean the attributes are really distinct in God: the multiplicity is in our conceptions and in the creatures grounding them. *([4.3](lessons/04-03-the-names-of-god.md))*
- Metaphor is not a weaker grade of proper predication but a different mechanism, and the test is not vividness but whether the creature's *way* of having the perfection is part of what the word signifies. *([4.3](lessons/04-03-the-names-of-god.md))*
- Do not carry q.13 a.7's relations of reason into the Trinity: the relations there are real in God, which is what makes them persons. *([4.3](lessons/04-03-the-names-of-god.md))*
- Analogy is not a hedge ("sort of wise") and not metaphor: it is several strictly ordered meanings, one of them primary. *([4.4](lessons/04-04-analogy.md))*

### Creation, providence and the soul

- "Creation is not a change" is the stronger claim, not a hedge: no subject makes the dependence total. *([5.1](lessons/05-01-creation-and-conservation.md))*
- Conservation is not re-creation each instant: re-creation posits a series of acts, and Aquinas says twice that preservation is the continuation of one. *([5.1](lessons/05-01-creation-and-conservation.md))*
- "Before creation" names no time: time is measured by change, creation is not a change, and time is itself created. *([5.1](lessons/05-01-creation-and-conservation.md))*
- The two levels of causing are not a split, 50/50 or 100/0 — both treat "cause" as one quantity to be divided. *([5.2](lessons/05-02-providence-and-secondary-causes.md))*
- "Primary" is not "earliest": that is deism, and it is the mistake objection 3 of q.105 a.5 makes. *([5.2](lessons/05-02-providence-and-secondary-causes.md))*
- "Contingent" does not mean uncaused or random: a contingent cause is one that can fail, and it is fully a cause. *([5.2](lessons/05-02-providence-and-secondary-causes.md))*

### The tradition and its authority

- Printing is not endorsement: the Leonine edition carries Cajetan's and Sylvester's commentaries, which is an editorial decision and fixes no level. *([6.1](lessons/06-01-the-commentators.md))*
- "Commentator Thomism" is a polemical label, not a position — ask which of its several meanings is meant before agreeing or objecting. *([6.1](lessons/06-01-the-commentators.md))*
- Suárez is not a commentator who drifted: the *Disputationes* are a rival architecture, and treating them as bad Thomism concedes the point at issue. *([6.1](lessons/06-01-the-commentators.md))*
- If the commentators misread Aquinas, the doctrine does not fall with them: arguing from a reading's bad pedigree to the doctrine's falsity is a genetic move, as bad from a Thomist against Suárez as from a critic against Cajetan. *([6.1](lessons/06-01-the-commentators.md))*
- *Aeterni Patris* did not make Thomism binding — and do not overcorrect into "just a preference": four popes taught it and it was written into the Code, as law about teaching. *([6.2](lessons/06-02-leo-xiii-and-the-thomist-revival.md))*
- The Twenty-Four Theses were approved, not imposed; *Doctoris Angelici* was addressed to Italy and the adjacent islands, and its reach beyond came through the 1917 Code. *([6.2](lessons/06-02-leo-xiii-and-the-thomist-revival.md))*
- "Perennial philosophy" is not a level of authority, and "existential Thomism" has nothing to do with existentialism. *([6.3](lessons/06-03-strict-observance-and-existential-thomism.md))*
- "The commentators lost *esse*" is a thesis, not a finding — as is the claim that Aquinas has anything answering to Maritain's intuition of being. *([6.3](lessons/06-03-strict-observance-and-existential-thomism.md))*
- *Humani Generis* did not condemn de Lubac: it names no one, and what happened to him in 1950 was disciplinary, which has no rung on the doctrinal ladder. *([6.4](lessons/06-04-transcendental-thomism-and-the-ressourcement-quarrel.md))*
- "Transcendental Thomism" is not Kantian idealism in a cassock: it is the transcendental *method* applied to judgement, with a realist conclusion. *([6.4](lessons/06-04-transcendental-thomism-and-the-ressourcement-quarrel.md))*
- A stronger act does not make a broader object, and "is Thomism binding?" is not a well-formed question. *([6.5](lessons/06-05-what-the-church-has-said-about-thomism.md))*
- Never read a level off the vocabulary instead of off the act — the mistake this course grades hardest, in both directions. *([1.3](lessons/01-03-sacred-doctrine-one-science-about-god.md), [6.5](lessons/06-05-what-the-church-has-said-about-thomism.md))*

## Conventions

- **One card per course**, covering every lesson. The linter checks that every
  lesson file is cited somewhere on this card.
- **Intuition first**, same as lessons: a plain-English line before any formal
  statement.
- **Headings are anchors.** Renaming a `###` breaks inbound lesson links; the
  linter catches it, but prefer not to rename.
- **Levels only by citing** [`fundamental-theology` 4.4](../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-04-the-ladder-of-doctrinal-authority.md).
  Where that course and this one differ, it wins. Overstating a level and
  understating one are the same error, and both are graded strictly.
- **Boss problems are reserved.** Entries touching boss material stay at outline
  level: what the text is and what the positions are called, never the worked
  reading.
- **No prose dollar signs** — write "ten dollars", not the symbol (see CLAUDE.md).
- Length is not capped the way a lesson's is: this is a lookup surface, not a
  read-through. But every line still has to earn its place.
