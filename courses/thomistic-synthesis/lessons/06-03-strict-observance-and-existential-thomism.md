# The Thomistic Synthesis · Lesson 6.3: Strict observance and existential Thomism

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 6: The Thomist tradition and its authority · Builds on: [6.1 The commentators](06-01-the-commentators.md), [6.2 Leo XIII and the Thomist revival](06-02-leo-xiii-and-the-thomist-revival.md) · Unlocks: [6.4 Transcendental Thomism and the ressourcement quarrel](06-04-transcendental-thomism-and-the-ressourcement-quarrel.md), [6.5 What the Church has said about Thomism, and at what level](06-05-what-the-church-has-said-about-thomism.md)

## Why this matters

Leo XIII told the schools to teach Aquinas ([6.2](06-02-leo-xiii-and-the-thomist-revival.md)). He did not say how to tell whether you are teaching Aquinas or only something written in his vocabulary. Within two generations three men who all believed they were obeying gave answers that cannot all be right, and the split still runs under every Thomist argument you will read — including the ones in this course. It is also the easiest place to forget what Module 6 exists to teach: none of this is dogma, and the Church has not settled it.

## The idea

There are two ways a body of thought can be true, and they ask different things of you.

**Like arithmetic.** Someone discovered it, at a date, in a language. None of that touches the content. Restate it in other words, renumber it, deduce it in a better order, and nothing is lost, because the truths were never *his*. On this picture, history is decoration.

**Like a recipe handed down.** The words survive intact while the thing they were for quietly drops out, and no one notices, because the words still make sense. On this picture, the only way to find out whether you still have it is to go back to the original and look.

Three Thomists, one programme, and this disagreement underneath it.

**Réginald Garrigou-Lagrange (1877–1964)**, a French Dominican, taught at the Angelicum in Rome from 1909 to 1959, holding from 1917 the first chair of ascetical and mystical theology. His first book, *Le sens commun, la philosophie de l'être et les formules dogmatiques*, attacked a Bergsonian reading of dogma that would let the Church's formulas shift with the times. That fight set his course: if the formulas are to hold, the metaphysics they are stated in must hold too, always. He took the Twenty-Four Thomistic Theses approved by the Congregation of Studies in 1914 ([6.2](06-02-leo-xiii-and-the-thomist-revival.md)) as the system's backbone, headed by act and potency ([2.1](02-01-act-and-potency-extended.md)), and laid it out as one deductive order in *La synthèse thomiste* (1946). He also wrote the century's most influential Catholic book on the spiritual life, *The Three Ages of the Interior Life* (1938); "strict observance" never meant dry. His 1946 polemic against the *nouvelle théologie* is [6.4](06-04-transcendental-thomism-and-the-ressourcement-quarrel.md)'s business.

**Jacques Maritain (1882–1973)** was a layman who came to Aquinas sideways: disillusioned at the Sorbonne by a scientism that answered none of his questions, rescued by Bergson's lectures, received into the Church in 1906, and handed the *Summa* a few years later. His central book, *Distinguer pour unir, ou Les degrés du savoir* (1932; in English *The Degrees of Knowledge*, 1937), does what its title says. Knowledge comes in orders — natural science, mathematics, metaphysics, and above them theology and the mystical knowledge of the saints — each with its own object and way of reaching it. Distinguish them and they stop competing: physics cannot refute metaphysics, and metaphysics does not do physics. Underneath the whole structure he put the **intuition of being**, a direct intellectual grasp of being as being, prior to any demonstration. He founded the Thomist Study Circles in 1922, whose annual retreats Garrigou-Lagrange preached. His political and aesthetic work belongs to [`catholic-social-teaching`](../../catholic-social-teaching/syllabus.md).

**Étienne Gilson (1884–1978)** arrived by a third route: he was a historian. His doctoral work on Descartes turned up scholasticism inside Descartes — which sent him to the Middle Ages and to Aquinas. He founded the Pontifical Institute of Mediaeval Studies in Toronto in 1929. His thesis, worked out in *L'être et l'essence* (1948) and *Being and Some Philosophers* (1949), is that Aquinas's discovery is not act and potency, which is Aristotle's, but the primacy of [the act of existing](../reference.md#the-act-of-existing) — *esse* as act, not as a fact tacked onto an essence ([2.2](02-02-esse-and-essence-the-real-distinction.md)). And his charge is that this discovery was lost almost at once, including by the commentators of [6.1](06-01-the-commentators.md).

## The argument

### The perennialist argument

Garrigou-Lagrange's position, reconstructed. Call a philosophy *perennial* when its truth does not depend on when or by whom it was reached.

1. **A philosophical claim is made true by what things are, not by what anyone thought.** *In words:* the object settles it, not the author.
2. **What things are does not change.** *In words:* if act limited by potency is how finite being works, it was so in 1250 and is so now.
3. **Aquinas's principles are true.** *In words:* the premise the whole revival rests on, and the one Leo XIII's programme presupposed.
4. **So those principles are perennially true — a *philosophia perennis*, of which Aquinas is the fullest statement rather than the owner.** *In words:* Thomism is not one man's system but the truth he happened to see best.
5. **A body of truths that does not depend on its date can be extracted from the texts and set out as ordered propositions.** *In words:* you can write the theses down, in a better order than the original, and lose nothing.
6. **So the right form for teaching Thomism is a numbered, ordered set of theses; and the commentators, who did that, were transmitters rather than distorters.** *In words:* the Twenty-Four Theses are the system, and [6.1](06-01-the-commentators.md)'s tradition is Aquinas continued.

### The premise Gilson denies

Gilson grants 1, 2 and 3. He denies **5**, and everything follows from that.

His reason is not a general worry about history. It is a claim about *this* content. What Aquinas saw was that existing is an act, and every apparatus philosophy has for stating things — definitions, theses, *what* a thing is — is built to state essences. So the one doctrine most likely to evaporate in a restatement is precisely the one that matters. The words survive; the *esse* becomes a fact about whether the essence is instantiated, and you are back in the metaphysics Aquinas broke with.

If that is right, then "is this still Thomism?" is not a question a thesis list can answer, because the thesis list is the suspect. It is a historical question — did this restatement keep what Aquinas had? — and only reading the texts in their own order and at their own date answers it. **Reading Aquinas historically is therefore not a preliminary to being a Thomist; on Gilson's view it is what being one requires.** That is a thesis about method, and it is the crux.

### Maritain's amendment

Maritain holds 1 through 4 with Garrigou-Lagrange and agrees with Gilson that *esse* is the heart of it. On 5 he splits the difference: the principles are perennial, but a list of conclusions is a summary of a knowledge, never the way into one. You reach the metaphysical order by the intuition of being, which no proposition can hand you. So he needs neither the thesis list nor the archive — and he goes on using John of St Thomas, whom Gilson's charge condemns.

## Map of positions

| | **Garrigou-Lagrange** | **Maritain** | **Gilson** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **The core of Thomism** | Act and potency, first in an ordered set of theses | The intuition of being, and the distinction of the orders of knowing | The primacy of *esse*, Aquinas's own discovery |
| **What he fears losing** | The stability of dogmatic formulas, hence of the metaphysics they use | The peace between the orders: science, metaphysics and mysticism swallowing one another | The discovery itself, silently, while the vocabulary survives |
| **The commentators** | Transmitters who drew out what was there | A resource, used freely and critically | Essentialists who kept the words and lost the thing |
| **Is Thomism perennial?** | Yes, and that is why it can be taught as theses | Perennial in its nature; always dated in its exercise | Its truth is not dated; the achievement is, and can be lost |
| **How you check a Thomism** | Against the theses, deductively | Against the intuition, and by keeping the orders distinct | Against the texts, historically |

Read the rows, not the columns: these are three answers to premise 5, not three parties with fixed membership.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean): "act and potency is the first principle of the system."**

Garrigou-Lagrange: yes, and near the head of the Twenty-Four. The system descends from the principle of non-contradiction through the [real distinction](../reference.md#real-distinction) of act and potency to God as [pure act](../reference.md#pure-act), and a student who has the order has the system.

Gilson: half right and therefore wrong. Act and potency is Aristotle's, and a Thomism headed by it has put the borrowed frame where the Christian discovery belongs. The first principle is that *esse* is the act of every act, from which act and potency in creatures follows as the way a finite thing receives it ([2.1](02-01-act-and-potency-extended.md), [2.2](02-02-esse-and-essence-the-real-distinction.md)).

Maritain: the principle is true and perennial, and it is not where you start. You start from the intuition of being; act and potency is what that intuition, worked over, yields. Put the thesis first and you produce students who can deduce and have seen nothing.

Notice that all three assert the same proposition about act and potency. They disagree about its *rank*, and rank is what premise 5 is about.

**Example 2 (hard): the Christian philosophy debate, where the map strains.**

In the 1930s French philosophy argued over whether "Christian philosophy" is a coherent phrase. The objection, pressed by Émile Bréhier and Léon Brunschvicg, is clean: if a doctrine's warrant is revelation it is theology, not philosophy; if its warrant is reason, calling it Christian adds nothing. Either way the phrase names no third thing.

Gilson's answer, from his Aberdeen Gifford Lectures (*L'esprit de la philosophie médiévale*, 1932): the category is historical. Revelation worked as an aid to reason. Notions such as creation from nothing, God as He Who Is ([4.3](04-03-the-names-of-god.md)) and the person entered philosophy because faith proposed them, and once there they are defensible on rational grounds — which the Greeks did not do. "Christian philosophy" names philosophies whose *genesis* required faith and whose *structure* is rational.

Maritain's answer (*De la philosophie chrétienne*, 1933): distinguish a philosophy's **nature** from its **state**. Its nature is fixed by its object, and objects are not Christian; its state is the concrete condition under which a person actually philosophizes, and that can be. The nature answers Bréhier; the state saves the phrase.

Now the strain. Gilson and Maritain are allies here, writing within a year of each other. But look at what each answer does to the crux. Gilson's makes the Christian character of a philosophy a fact about its history, which is one more reason history cannot be skipped. Maritain's keeps a nature that stands clear of history, conceding only the circumstances. They agreed on the conclusion while disagreeing on premise 5: agreement on a verdict is not agreement on a crux — and Thomists were divided on this one too, some holding that a philosophy is simply true or false and that "Christian" can only describe the philosopher.

## Watch out

- **"Perennial philosophy" is not a level of authority.** The Church's recommendation of Aquinas is ordinary magisterium and Church law ([6.2](06-02-leo-xiii-and-the-thomist-revival.md), [6.5](06-05-what-the-church-has-said-about-thomism.md)). Whether his metaphysics is perennially true is a [school thesis](../reference.md#school-thesis), freely disputed — the bottom rung of [`fundamental-theology` 4.4](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-04-the-ladder-of-doctrinal-authority.md). You might think Garrigou-Lagrange's confidence about it reflects the Church's; it does not, and the Twenty-Four Theses' own binding force is separate and contested ([6.2](06-02-leo-xiii-and-the-thomist-revival.md)).
- **"Existential Thomism" has nothing to do with existentialism.** The word marks *esse*, the act of existing, against a metaphysics of essences. It does not mean anguish, authenticity, or existence preceding essence. Gilson wrote at the height of the fashion and was arguing against it, not joining it.
- **"The commentators lost *esse*" is a thesis, not a finding.** Gilson's charge is disputed by Thomists who read Cajetan and John of St Thomas as developing Aquinas rather than replacing him; [6.1](06-01-the-commentators.md) states their side. Likewise, whether Aquinas has anything answering to Maritain's intuition of being is contested. Neither charge is settled here.

## One-liner

> Garrigou-Lagrange asks whether the theses are true, Gilson asks whether they are still Thomas's, and Maritain answers that a philosophy has a nature that can be perennial and a state that is always dated.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢) *(Exegetical.)*** Three remarks invented for this problem, overheard in a seminar on Aquinas:

> (i) "Start them on the order of the theses. If a student can derive pure act from act and potency, he has the system; the rest is application."
> (ii) "Before he derives anything, he has to have *seen* being. Otherwise you have produced a very fluent man who has looked at nothing."
> (iii) "Ask a narrower question first. Is the doctrine in that textbook the one in the *Summa*, or the one Cajetan left behind? You cannot answer that from the textbook."

Assign each remark to Garrigou-Lagrange, Maritain or Gilson, and for each name the single move that decides it. One sentence per remark.

**P2 (🟡) *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)***
(a) Give the level of each claim and name the act or text that fixes it, without overstating in either direction: (i) seminary theology is to be taught with St Thomas as teacher; (ii) Thomism is a *philosophia perennis*; (iii) Cajetan's commentaries lost Aquinas's doctrine of *esse*.
(b) Take the crux of this lesson: is the Thomist system a perennial philosophy whose truth is independent of its history, or a historically located achievement that must be recovered historically? Argue for either answer in 150 words or fewer. Say which numbered premise of "The perennialist argument" your answer turns on, and give one thing that would count as evidence against you.

**P3 (🔴, optional) *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)***
(a) State Bréhier and Brunschvicg's objection to the phrase "Christian philosophy" at its strongest, in two sentences, without using the words "Gilson" or "Maritain".
(b) Gilson and Maritain both answer it, and both answers have a cost. In 150 words or fewer, say which answer concedes more to the objection and why, and name the cost of the answer you think concedes less. Any verdict.

<details>
<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical.)*

**Must hit, strict:** (i) **Garrigou-Lagrange** — the deciding move is that the *order of theses* is the system, which is premise 5 in its strongest form: the propositions carry everything, so teaching is deduction from them. (ii) **Maritain** — the deciding move is that the intuition of being comes before and grounds any derivation; a thesis list summarizes a knowledge rather than producing one. (iii) **Gilson** — the deciding move is that the test of a Thomism is a *historical* comparison with the text, which the restatement itself cannot settle; the mention of Cajetan is the essentialism charge.

**Wrong turns:** assigning (ii) to Gilson because it criticizes deduction — Gilson's objection is that you cannot check the deduction against the original, not that the student has not intuited anything; assigning (iii) to Garrigou-Lagrange because Cajetan is named, when the remark treats Cajetan as the suspect rather than the transmitter; treating (i) as merely a remark about pedagogy, when the claim is that the theses *are* the system.

**Model answer:** (i) Garrigou-Lagrange: the ordered theses are the system itself, so mastering the deductive order is mastering Thomism. (ii) Maritain: nothing in metaphysics is had by derivation until being has been grasped in the intuition that underlies the derivation. (iii) Gilson: whether a restatement is still Aquinas is decided by going back to the texts, and the commentators are the ones on trial.

---

**P2** *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)*

**Must hit, strict (a):**
(i) **A disciplinary norm of Church law, not a doctrine.** It is fixed by the Church's legislation on the formation of clergy — the 1917 Code carried such a norm and the 1983 Code can. 252 §3 carries it now — reinforced by *Optatam Totius* 16, which is conciliar teaching about how theology is to be taught, not a definition that any Thomist thesis is true ([6.5](06-05-what-the-church-has-said-about-thomism.md)). A Catholic who thought the norm unwise would be disagreeing about discipline, not denying a doctrine.
(ii) **Freely disputed — rung 5** on [`fundamental-theology` 4.4](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-04-the-ladder-of-doctrinal-authority.md)'s ladder. No magisterial act proposes it. *Aeterni Patris* and the documents after it recommend Aquinas and praise the perennial value of scholastic wisdom, but a recommendation of a master is not a definition that his philosophy is perennially true; that is exactly the conflation this module grades strictly. Denying it is taking a side.
(iii) **Not a doctrinal claim at all** — a disputed historical and interpretive thesis about a fifteenth- and sixteenth-century commentator, argued by evidence from texts. No level applies. Naming Gilson (and, on analogy, de Lubac) as its proponents is credited; saying the Church has settled it is an error.

**Must hit, any verdict (b):** identify the premise the answer turns on — premise 5 (that a dateless body of truths can be extracted and set out as ordered propositions) is the expected one, and answers that turn on 1 or 3 must say why. Then give the evidence that would count against you: for the perennial side, a worked case where a faithful-looking restatement demonstrably drops something (Gilson's charge about *esse* is the standing candidate, so name what would make it stick); for the historical side, a case where a restatement in other words provably preserved the doctrine, or an account of how history alone identifies "the doctrine" without a criterion that is itself doctrinal. Either answer must keep the level assignments of (a) intact and not smuggle in the Church's authority.

**Wrong turns:** promoting (ii) to the level of the recommendation in (i), or demoting the recommendation in (i) to a mere opinion — the error runs both ways; treating (iii) as though the Church had adjudicated it; in (b), arguing that Thomism is *true* rather than that it is *perennial*, which is a different claim and not the crux; in (b), claiming the question is settled by any magisterial document.

**Model answer (b), one of several:** Historically located. The answer turns on premise 5. Aquinas's own texts show a man revising, arguing with Avicenna and the Augustinians, and choosing words against live alternatives; strip that and the surviving propositions under-determine which alternative he was refusing. The concrete test is *esse*: "in creatures essence and existence are really distinct" is a sentence a metaphysics of essences can also say, by treating existence as whether the essence is instantiated, which is not what [2.2](02-02-esse-and-essence-the-real-distinction.md) means. So the theses do not fix their own reading, and something outside them — the texts, read in order — has to. Against me: exhibit a restatement of the real distinction so worded that no essentialist reading survives, and premise 5 is back.

---

**P3** *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** the dilemma, both horns stated: if what makes the philosophy Christian is that its warrant comes from revelation, the claims are theology and not philosophy at all; if the warrant is reason alone, the adjective describes the philosopher's religion and adds nothing to the philosophy. Credit for noting that the objection concedes that Christians have done philosophy — it denies only that "Christian" names a kind of philosophy.

**Must hit, any verdict (b):** state each answer correctly first — Gilson locates the Christian character in the historical genesis of concepts that are rationally defensible once possessed; Maritain distinguishes the nature of philosophy, fixed by its object and not Christian, from its state, the concrete conditions of its exercise. Then argue about *concession*, not about who is nicer to faith, and name a real cost: Gilson's answer makes "Christian philosophy" a claim in the history of ideas that the historical record could falsify, and invites the reply that genesis is irrelevant to warrant; Maritain's grants the objection's main point — that the nature of philosophy is not Christian — and must then show that "state" is more than biography.

**Wrong turns:** treating the two answers as the same answer in different words; deciding it by whether one sounds more orthodox; forgetting that both men are defending the phrase, so the question is about the price each pays; answering (b) without having stated the objection accurately in (a).

**Model answer (b), one of several:** Maritain concedes more. He grants the objection's own premise — that a philosophy's nature is set by its object, and objects are not Christian — and keeps the phrase only for the state in which philosophizing is done. That is close to conceding that "Christian" qualifies the philosopher. Gilson concedes less: he claims something about the philosophy itself, that particular concepts entered it through revelation and are now rationally held. The cost is that his claim is now historical and therefore checkable, and hostage to whether those concepts really are absent from Greek philosophy and really are demonstrable without faith. Bréhier can also reply that where a concept came from says nothing about whether it is justified.

## Flashback

**From Lesson [6.1](06-01-the-commentators.md) (The commentators):** *(Exegetical.)* Three features come with the commentary form: it follows the master's order, article by article; it answers the commentator's opponents rather than the master's; and it is a teaching instrument for a school with an order of study. Run them on a figure the lesson only lists. John Capreolus (d. 1444) wrote four books of *Defensiones theologiae divi Thomae Aquinatis* — cast not on the order of the *Summa* but on the order of Lombard's *Sentences* — defending Aquinas against Scotus, Durandus, Aureol and Henry of Ghent, none of whom Aquinas answers anywhere in his own works. (a) Which of the three features does the *Defensiones* display most strongly, and which is strained? (b) Name one thing the three features let you predict about Capreolus's Aquinas, and one verdict they do not license. **Two sentences per part.**

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):** strongest is the **second**, and here at full strength — the opponents are the commentator's, not the master's, and Aquinas never met these critics, so every objection the *Defensiones* answers is a fourteenth- or fifteenth-century question put to a thirteenth-century text. Strained is the **first**: a defence cast on Lombard's order is not following Aquinas's own arrangement at all, and the strain is sharper than it looks, since by the end of the fourteenth century the *Summa* had displaced the *Sentences* as the theology textbook in Dominican houses — so the order being followed is neither the master's nor, by then, his order's own successor.

**Must hit, strict (b):** a prediction, any one of these — expect an Aquinas fitted out in advance to answer objections he never faced; expect the opponents' own distinctions and vocabulary inside the defence, since you cannot answer Scotus while refusing his terms; expect a position Aquinas took once in passing to acquire a name, a canonical argument and a standard list of objections. Not licensed: **any verdict of distortion, and equally any verdict of fidelity.** The features predict a shape, not a grade, and inferring that a doctrine is false from the pedigree of the reading that carries it is the genetic move, as bad against Capreolus as against anyone else.

**Wrong turns:** reading "answers the commentator's opponents" as an accusation of bad faith, when it describes what any defence in a live fight has to do; taking the departure from the *Summa*'s order as showing that Capreolus is not really on this line, when the point is only that the arrangement is not the master's; sliding from the prediction in (b) to the essentialism charge, which is a further thesis needing its own evidence.

**Model answer:** (a) The second: Capreolus answers Scotus, Durandus, Aureol and Henry of Ghent, none of them opponents Aquinas ever faced, so the questions put to the text are his own century's rather than Aquinas's. The first is strained, the work being arranged on Lombard's order and not the *Summa*'s — and the Dominican schools had already moved the other way. (b) Predict an Aquinas equipped against objections he never saw, with his critics' distinctions absorbed into the defence and his passing positions hardened into named theses. Do not predict a verdict: the lens says what shape to expect, not whether the result is Aquinas handed on or Aquinas replaced.

</details>
