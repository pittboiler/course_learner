# The Thomistic Synthesis · Lesson 6.1: The commentators

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 6: The Thomist tradition and its authority · Builds on: [2.2](02-02-esse-and-essence-the-real-distinction.md), [4.4](04-04-analogy.md), [5.4](05-04-a-map-of-the-second-and-third-parts.md) · Unlocks: [6.2](06-02-leo-xiii-and-the-thomist-revival.md)

## Why this matters

Modules 1 to 5 read Aquinas. From here the course reads his readers, and it has to, because nobody after 1500 met Aquinas cold. When Leo XIII sent the schools back to him in 1879 ([6.2](06-02-leo-xiii-and-the-thomist-revival.md)), what actually reached the classroom was a Latin manual with four centuries of commentary behind it. Every twentieth-century quarrel in [6.3](06-03-strict-observance-and-existential-thomism.md) and [6.4](06-04-transcendental-thomism-and-the-ressourcement-quarrel.md) is a quarrel about those four centuries. And the first question anyone asks about them — did the commentators hand Aquinas on or quietly replace him? — is still open among Catholics in good standing, which makes it a clean case of this course's rule: a school's reading of the master binds nobody.

## The idea

Aquinas died in 1274. He was canonized by John XXII on 18 July 1323, and Pius V proclaimed him a Doctor of the universal Church in 1567 and had the first complete edition of his works printed at Rome in 1570 — with Cajetan's commentary running alongside the text of the *Summa*. Between those dates a genre grew up around him, and the genre shapes everything the tradition later fought about.

A commentary is not a modern monograph. Three features come with the form:

1. **It follows the master's order**, article by article, question by question. It cannot reorganize; it can only expound, extend, and defend at the point where the text raises the matter.
2. **It answers the commentator's opponents, not the master's.** The questions put to the text are the questions of the commentator's own century: Scotus and the nominalists for Capreolus and Cajetan; Luther; Molina on grace; Baius on nature.
3. **It is a teaching instrument for a school with an order of study.** By the fourteenth century's end the *Summa* had displaced Lombard's *Sentences* as the theology textbook in Dominican houses, and the commentary became what a student actually read.

The consequence that matters later: **a commentator systematizes.** A position Aquinas takes once, in passing, in one work acquires a name, a canonical argument, a standard list of objections and a slot in the order of study. The school's vocabulary also absorbs its opponents', because you cannot answer Scotus without using Scotus's distinctions. None of that is dishonest. It does mean the Aquinas you meet in a commentary is an Aquinas already fitted out to answer somebody.

## The roster

| Who | When | What | Why he is on the line |
|---|---|---|---|
| John Capreolus, OP | d. 1444 | *Defensiones theologiae divi Thomae Aquinatis*, four books, cast on the order of the *Sentences* | the first sustained defence of Aquinas against Scotus, Durandus, Aureol and Henry of Ghent; called in the school the prince of Thomists |
| Thomas de Vio Cajetan, OP | 1469-1534 | commentary on the whole *Summa Theologiae*, begun 1507 and finished 1522; a short treatise on the names said analogically, written in the 1490s; a commentary on *De Ente et Essentia* | the first commentary on the entire *Summa*, and primarily a defence of Aquinas against the attacks of Scotus; his readings became the school's default, including on analogy ([4.4](04-04-analogy.md)) |
| Sylvester of Ferrara, OP | d. 1528 | commentary on the *Summa contra Gentiles* | the companion volume to Cajetan's; the two were later printed with the Leonine text |
| Domingo Banez, OP | 1528-1604 | Salamanca commentaries on the *Summa*; the Dominican side against Molina's *Concordia* (1588) | physical premotion, and the Roman congregations on the aids of grace (1598-1607); that controversy is [`creation-grace-last-things`](../../creation-grace-last-things/syllabus.md)'s to settle, and this course only names it |
| John of St Thomas, OP | 1589-1644 | the *Cursus philosophicus* and the *Cursus theologicus* | the school as a complete course of studies: logic, natural philosophy and metaphysics first, then theology — the shape the manuals of [6.2](06-02-leo-xiii-and-the-thomist-revival.md) inherit |
| Francisco Suarez, SJ | 1548-1617 | the *Disputationes Metaphysicae* (1597), fifty-four disputations | not a commentary at all, and the rival scholasticism: metaphysics ordered by topic, standing free of any master's text |

Suárez is the odd entry and belongs there. His book could be taught from directly, in Protestant universities as well as Catholic ones, which is how scholastic vocabulary reached Descartes, Leibniz and Wolff ([`modern-philosophy`](../../modern-philosophy/syllabus.md)). Much of what "scholastic" means in modern philosophy is his, not Aquinas's. His denial of the [real distinction](../reference.md#real-distinction) is stated at its best in [2.2](02-02-esse-and-essence-the-real-distinction.md).

## The argument

Twentieth-century critics — Gilson in *Being and Some Philosophers* (1949) and *Le Thomisme*, de Lubac in *Surnaturel* (1946) — pressed a charge. Paraphrased, in premises:

**C1.** For Aquinas, *esse* is [the act of the existing thing](../reference.md#the-act-of-existing), the actuality of everything the thing is, not one more item on its list of features. *In words:* his metaphysics is built round the act of being.
**C2.** The commentators had to defend that against Scotus, inside a taxonomy of distinctions Scotus set. So the question became how many distinct somethings a creature contains. *In words:* the interesting question quietly changed from "what is it for a creature to be?" to "real, formal, or of reason?"
**C3.** A metaphysics organized round essence can still reach the right conclusions — God's essence is his existence — while the reason Aquinas cared never surfaces. *In words:* the thesis survives; the centre of gravity moves to essence.
**C4.** The commentators also read sixteenth-century problems back into the text. De Lubac's case is "pure nature", a construction he traced to Cajetan's handling of the natural desire to see God and found nowhere in Aquinas ([6.4](06-04-transcendental-thomism-and-the-ressourcement-quarrel.md)).
**∴** What the nineteenth century received as Thomism is an essentialist system under Aquinas's name.

The answer, at its own strength:

**R1.** A commentary is a weapon in a live fight. Cajetan's is principally a defence of Aquinas against Scotus, and you cannot answer an opponent while refusing his vocabulary; you forfeit instead.
**R2.** New questions are not distortions. Baius, Molina and Luther asked things Aquinas was never asked. A tradition that declined to answer would have been dead, not faithful.
**R3.** "Back to the text itself" is itself a modern instinct — historical, philological, about a century old when Gilson used it. *In words:* the charge grades the commentators by a standard invented after them, for reading a master as a dated document rather than as a living authority.
**R4.** And the charge is a reading too. Gilson's Aquinas, the discoverer of *esse* against Greek essentialism, is a twentieth-century portrait doing philosophy in the costume of history, and should be judged by the standard it applies.

This lesson takes no side, and cannot: the whole dispute is a [school thesis](../reference.md#school-thesis) on both sides, freely disputed, with no act of the Church touching it. The levels come only from [`fundamental-theology` 4.4](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-04-the-ladder-of-doctrinal-authority.md).

## The case: what happened to *esse*

**Aquinas.** You can know what a phoenix is without knowing whether any exists, so a creature's essence is not its existence; at most one thing can be its own existence; everything else has *esse* from another (*De Ente et Essentia* ch. 4 — the chapter numbering differs between editions). In God essence and *esse* are the same (ST I q.3 a.4). And *esse* is the actuality of all acts (*De Potentia* q.7 a.2 ad 9). The doctrine is scattered across genres and years, and it is [2.2](02-02-esse-and-essence-the-real-distinction.md)'s.

**Cajetan and the school.** It acquires a name — *the* real distinction — a canonical argument, and two named rivals with their own labels: Scotus's formal distinction, later Suárez's distinction of reason with a foundation in the thing. What is gained is portability. The thesis can now be carried into the treatise on angels, on the soul, on the *esse* of Christ, without anyone re-reading *De Ente*. What the critics say is lost is the question underneath: a student can answer "really distinct" correctly and never be told that what is at stake is a creature receiving its whole actuality, at every instant, from subsisting being itself.

**Gilson.** Open a nineteenth-century manual and the real distinction is there, correct and inert, in a metaphysics whose working parts are essences, with existence recorded as a fact about them. He pressed the point hardest against Suárez, who drops the distinction and orders metaphysics round the concept of being as such.

**The reply.** Portability is exactly what a theology faculty needs, and inertness is a complaint about manuals rather than a demonstration about Cajetan. Nor was the charge levelled evenly: Gilson could praise Bañez on the act of being while faulting others in the same line.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean: run the genre lens on a known case).** Analogy. Aquinas says in ST I q.13 a.5 that what is said of God and creatures is said neither univocally nor purely equivocally, and reaches for the health example. Cajetan writes a separate short treatise that sorts analogy into kinds and makes analogy of proper proportionality the one carrying theological language. Now apply the three features. *Order:* he is not commenting on q.13 but writing a classroom treatise, so he is free to systematize. *Opponent:* the live threat is Scotus's univocity ([`ancient-medieval-philosophy` 8.2](../../ancient-medieval-philosophy/lessons/08-02-scotus-the-univocity-of-being.md)), and beating it needs a positive account of what analogy is, not a denial. *Systematization:* hence a taxonomy where the article gives an example. The lens does not deliver "distortion" or "fidelity". It predicts something narrower and more useful: expect a sharper and more classifiable doctrine than the text has, aimed at an opponent the text never faced. Whether q.13 a.5 will bear it is [4.4](04-04-analogy.md)'s question, settled there against the article.

**Example 2 (hard: where the lens strains).** De Lubac's charge does not fit the pattern, and that is why it cut deepest. He is not saying Cajetan sharpened Aquinas; he is saying a distinction introduced to solve a sixteenth-century problem about Baius was then read back into Aquinas as his own doctrine, changing what "nature" means in him. Notice that R1 and R2 *concede the history* — of course the commentators answered their own century — and reply that this is what a living tradition does. So the two sides agree about what happened and disagree about whether the result may still be called Aquinas's teaching, which no amount of further history will settle. Hence the bitterness in [6.4](06-04-transcendental-thomism-and-the-ressourcement-quarrel.md). And note the level trap sitting inside it: whether "pure nature" is Aquinas's doctrine is a school question, while what *Humani Generis* (1950) teaches about the gratuity of the supernatural is not, and the doctrine of grace itself belongs to [`creation-grace-last-things`](../../creation-grace-last-things/syllabus.md).

## Watch out

- **You might think printing is endorsement.** Leo XIII directed that the Leonine edition carry Cajetan's commentary with the *Summa Theologiae* and Sylvester's with the *Summa contra Gentiles*. That is an editorial decision about an edition. It makes them the standard companions; it does not make any reading in them the Church's own, and no level follows from it.
- **You might think "commentator Thomism" names a position.** It is a polemical label, and people mean different things by it: an essentialist metaphysics, a manual's house style, or simply a school that read Aquinas through later disputes. Ask which before agreeing or objecting.
- **You might think Suárez is a commentator who drifted.** He is not on this line at all. The *Disputationes* are a rival architecture, and treating them as bad Thomism concedes the very point at issue between him and Aquinas ([`metaphysics` 2.5](../../metaphysics/lessons/02-05-essence-and-existence.md)).
- **You might think that if the commentators misread Aquinas, what they taught falls with them.** "Cajetan misread q.13 a.5" and "analogy is the right account of theological language" are independent claims. Arguing from the bad pedigree of a reading to the falsity of the doctrine is a genetic move, and it is as bad when a Thomist uses it against Suárez as when a critic uses it against Cajetan.

## One-liner

> A commentary is the master's text answering the commentator's enemies — which is why the school got an Aquinas sharper than the *Summa*, and why "sharper" has been read ever since as either fidelity or replacement.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢) *(Exegetical.)*** Assign the level of each claim, naming the act or text that fixes it, and do not overstate in either direction. Use the ladder in [`fundamental-theology` 4.4](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-04-the-ladder-of-doctrinal-authority.md). **One or two sentences each.**

(a) In every creature essence and *esse* are really distinct.
(b) A study guide written for this problem states: "Leo XIII had Cajetan's commentary printed with the *Summa* in the Leonine edition, so Cajetan's readings carry the Church's authority."

**P2 (🟡) *(Exegetical.)*** The essentialism charge (C1–C4) and the genre defence (R1–R4) are not simply opposed: the defence grants part of the charge. (a) Name the premise the defence concedes and the single claim the two sides actually part on. (b) Say what kind of evidence would move someone on that claim, and name one thing that would *not*, because both sides already grant it. **150 words or fewer in total.**

**P3 (🔴, optional) *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)*** An invented handout for a parish reading group:

> "Skip the manuals. Every one of them is Cajetan's system with Aquinas's name on the cover, and anything written after 1274 is somebody's agenda. If you want the real Aquinas, read the *Summa* by itself and let the text speak for itself."

(a) Name the two distinct claims run together here, and say which one R3 targets. **Two sentences.** (b) Is "let the text speak for itself" a neutral instruction? **150 words or fewer**, any verdict, provided you say what the instruction assumes and name one thing a reader of the *Summa* alone would miss and one thing a reader of the manuals alone would miss.

<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical — strict, in both directions.)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** a school thesis, **freely disputed** among Catholics — the bottom rung of the ladder in [`fundamental-theology` 4.4](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-04-the-ladder-of-doctrinal-authority.md). No magisterial act proposes it; Scotists and Suárezians deny it and are Catholics in good standing ([2.2](02-02-esse-and-essence-the-real-distinction.md)). Neither Aquinas's holding it nor the Church's recommendation of Aquinas raises the level: that is the course's standing rule.

**Must hit, strict (b):** the inference fails, and the right answer is that **no level is fixed at all**. Directing which commentary accompanies a critical edition is an editorial and disciplinary decision, not an act proposing a doctrine, so step one of the procedure — find the act — comes up empty. A recommendation of an author is also not teaching of his opinions.

**Wrong turns:** promoting (a) on the strength of the Twenty-Four Thomistic Theses of 1914 — a list issued for schools, whose own binding force is disputed and is [6.2](06-02-leo-xiii-and-the-thomist-revival.md)'s business, and in no case a definition; sliding the other way and calling (a) "merely one opinion, so not worth holding" — freely disputed means no act binds, not that the arguments are weak; answering (b) with "rung 3, religious submission", which treats an edition's apparatus as authentic teaching.

**Model answer:** (a) Freely disputed: a Thomist school thesis, held against Scotus and Suárez, with no magisterial act proposing it, and neither Aquinas's authorship nor the Church's recommendation of him changes that. (b) No level: which commentary is printed with a critical edition is an editorial decision, and a recommendation of an author does not teach his readings.

---

**P2** *(Exegetical — strict.)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** the defence **concedes C2 (and C4) as history** — the commentators did frame Aquinas through later opponents' questions and vocabulary, because that is what commentary in a live dispute is. What the two sides part on is **whether restating a doctrine in an opponent's terms preserves it or replaces it** — equivalently, whether the sharpened school thesis still carries the content of C1. That is the load-bearing claim, and it is evaluative, not archival.

**Must hit, strict (b):** evidence that could move it is textual and comparative: places where the commentators do or do not treat *esse* as the act of the essence rather than as a term in a taxonomy; a conclusion of the school that actually contradicts something Aquinas asserts (which would settle it for the critics); or a showing that the school's later use of the thesis does the same work Aquinas's did. What would *not* move it is any further demonstration that the commentators answered sixteenth-century questions — both sides already grant this, and a dispute about the same conceded fact is where the argument is, not evidence in it.

**Wrong turns:** naming C1 as the crux (both sides accept it; Gilson asserts it and the defenders do not deny it); treating the crux as a purely historical question that more archival work would settle; saying the defence denies C4, when it concedes the episode and disputes what follows.

**Model answer:** The defence grants C2 and C4 as history: the commentators did read Aquinas through Scotus, Baius and Molina. They part on whether a doctrine restated in an opponent's vocabulary is preserved or displaced — whether the school's real distinction still carries C1's act of being. Evidence that bears on it: passages where a commentator treats *esse* as act rather than as one term in a taxonomy of distinctions, or a school conclusion that contradicts Aquinas outright. Evidence that does not: more proof that the commentators answered their own century's questions, which both sides already concede.

---

**P3** *(a) exegetical — strict; (b) evaluative — any verdict.*

**Must hit, strict (a):** two claims are fused. The first is **historical**: the manuals are Cajetan's system rather than Aquinas's — the essentialism charge, an arguable thesis. The second is **methodological**: that the original text alone gives the real author, and later reading is "agenda". **R3 targets the second**, by pointing out that reading a master historically, stripped of his tradition, is itself a modern instinct with its own commitments, not a neutral default. Credit for noting that the handout's "after 1274" also sweeps in Capreolus, Banez and John of St Thomas indiscriminately, which the charge's own authors did not do.

**Must hit, any verdict (b):** say what the instruction assumes — that a text can be read without a question put to it, so that the reader's own century supplies the questions silently rather than the commentator supplying them openly. Then one loss on each side. Reading the *Summa* alone: the objections and replies presuppose live disputes and a cast of authorities, so a reader who does not know them supplies his own, and the order of the articles, which carries the argument ([1.2](01-02-reading-a-whole-question.md)), goes unnoticed. Reading the manuals alone: you get the theses without the text they answer to, and no way to check a reading against the article — which is exactly the check Gilson's side demanded. Any verdict passes if both losses are named.

**Wrong turns:** treating the handout's history as settled and only grading the manuals; concluding that because commentary is unavoidable, all commentaries are equally good — the crux of P2 is precisely that this is arguable; reading R3 as the claim that historical scholarship is illegitimate, when it says only that it is a method with commitments rather than the absence of one.

**Model answer (b), one of several:** Not neutral. "Let the text speak" assumes a reader can approach an article with no question of his own, when in fact he will supply one — and the twenty-first-century question is no more Aquinas's than Cajetan's sixteenth-century one, with the difference that Cajetan says out loud what he is asking. Read alone, the *Summa* hides this: its objections answer opponents you cannot see, its authorities carry weights you have to be told, and the order of its articles, which is half the argument, looks like a list. Read the manuals alone and you have the theses without the text, unable to check whether q.13 a.5 supports the account of analogy built on it. The honest procedure uses both, and says which is doing the work.

## Flashback

**From Lesson [5.3](05-03-the-human-soul-in-the-system.md) (The human soul in the system):** *(Exegetical.)* A donor kidney is removed and kept perfused in a cold machine for twelve hours before it is transplanted. ST I q.76 a.8 supplies a test for such cases in passing: when the soul departs we call the hand, the eye, the flesh and the bones by those names only equivocally, and the proof Aquinas gives is that on the soul's withdrawal no part of the body retains its proper action. (a) Which way does the test point for the organ in the cooler, and which thesis of q.76 a.4 is doing the work? **Two sentences.** (b) The soul is likewise a part, and is not the human being (q.75 a.4). Does it follow that it can no more exist apart from the body than the kidney can? **One sentence, naming the article that decides it.**

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):** it points to "not in the same sense". Separated from the composite, the organ is no longer informed by the soul, so "kidney" is said of it equivocally, as "animal" is said of a painted or a stone animal; whatever activity the machine keeps going in it is not the proper action of a part of this man. The thesis doing the work is the unicity of substantial form (q.76 a.4) — the intellectual soul is the only substantial form in a human being — together with a.8's own point that a substantial form perfects not the whole alone but each part, so nothing lower is left to inform the hand as a hand or the kidney as a kidney once the soul is gone. Credit for noting that Aquinas's own cases are the corpse and its members, so the perfused organ is our extension of his doctrine and not his verdict on modern surgery.

**Must hit, strict (b):** no, and q.75 a.2 decides it: the soul has an operation of its own, understanding, in which no bodily organ shares, and what operates of itself has existence of itself, so it subsists. The kidney has no operation of its own apart from the composite and so nothing to subsist by, whereas the soul retains the very existence it had communicated to the matter (q.76 a.1 ad 5) — a form's way of surviving, not a second substance's.

**Wrong turns:** concluding from a.8 that the corpse or the separated organ is nothing at all, when matter with its accidents and dimensions remains, which is why Aquinas says "equivocally" and not "not at all"; using q.75 a.4 ad 2's denial that the soul is a person as though it also denied that the soul subsists, when that reply grants particular substance and refuses only the complete nature of a species; answering (b) from the immortality of the soul as doctrine — whose level belongs to [`creation-grace-last-things`](../../creation-grace-last-things/syllabus.md) — rather than from the article.

**Model answer:** (a) Not in the same sense: cut off from the composite the organ is no longer informed by the soul, so "kidney" is said of it as "animal" is said of a painted animal, and what it does in the machine is not the proper action of a part of this man. Unicity of substantial form is doing the work — the soul is the only substantial form in a man, and it perfects each part and not merely the whole, so no lower form is left to keep the part what it was. (b) No: by q.75 a.2 the soul has an operation, understanding, that no organ performs, and what operates on its own has existence on its own, which the kidney has not.

</details>
