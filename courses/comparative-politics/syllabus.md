# Comparative Politics — Syllabus

> Politics & Society · Tier 1 · ~25 lessons · Prereqs: [`political-institutions`](../political-institutions/syllabus.md) · Roadmap id: `comparative-politics`

## Goal

[`political-institutions`](../political-institutions/syllabus.md) takes the democratic frame as given and asks what each design does. This course asks what that course assumed away: why some places have effective states and others do not, how regimes are told apart and measured, how dictatorships actually run, why countries become democracies and why democracies fail or erode, where nations and ethnic groups come from politically, why revolutions happen, and what culture and religion do in politics. It works through cases throughout: early modern Europe, Latin America and post-colonial Africa for the state; Mexico under the PRI, Russia, Malaysia and China for authoritarian rule; Spain, Chile, Poland, South Korea, India and Singapore for democratization; Weimar Germany, Chile in 1973, Hungary, Turkey and Venezuela for breakdown and backsliding; Zambia and Malawi, Tanzania and Kenya, Lebanon, Northern Ireland and Bosnia for ethnic politics; France, Russia, China, Iran and East Germany for revolution. You will finish able to design a small-N comparison and say what it cannot show, code a regime under the major indices and explain why they disagree, name the survival strategy an autocrat is using, place a transition on the structure-versus-agency map, diagnose executive aggrandizement in a news report, and name the premise two rival explanations of the same political outcome disagree about. The course is descriptive and comparative-historical, and verdict-neutral: it reports explanations and the evidence for and against them, never which regime is best. It deliberately skips the formal models of democratization, selectorate theory and credible commitment (`institutions-and-development`), causal-identification technique ([`econometrics`](../econometrics/syllabus.md), `empirical-political-economy`), the international system, interstate war and international law ([`international-relations`](../international-relations/syllabus.md)), the design of democratic institutions ([`political-institutions`](../political-institutions/syllabus.md)), and area-studies coverage for its own sake — each case appears because it shows a mechanism.

## How practice works in this course

Problems follow [HUMANITIES-BUILD-BRIEF.md](../../HUMANITIES-BUILD-BRIEF.md): every part declares its kind, and every solution is a rubric (must-hit moves, wrong turns) plus a model answer. Five archetypes carry the course, because comparative politics is an argument about explanations of cases:

- **Find the crux** (exegetical) — two explanations of the same outcome (Tilly vs Herbst on African states, modernization vs Moore on democracy, Skocpol vs Kuran on revolution) and the single premise one side must deny, plus the case evidence that would move it.
- **Apply to a hard case** (exegetical, then evaluative) — run a theory on a real case it was not built for (Tilly on Latin America, modernization on India and Singapore, the transitions paradigm on post-Soviet Central Asia) and say where it stops determining an answer.
- **Diagnose** (exegetical) — an invented news report, memo or op-ed describes a regime's behavior; name the survival strategy, the type of manipulation or the form of backsliding. Invented material is never attributed to a real person.
- **Close reading** (exegetical) of public-domain texts: Mill's *A System of Logic* (1843) on the methods of experimental inquiry and on their limits in social science; Mill's *Considerations on Representative Government* (1861) chapter XVI on nationality; Acton's essay "Nationality" (1862); Tocqueville's *The Old Regime and the Revolution* (1856, Bonner translation). Weber's "Politics as a Vocation" (1919) and Renan's "What is a Nation?" (1882) are read in the lesson's own short translations from the public-domain German and French originals, and say so. Modern scholarship (Tilly, Moore, Skocpol, Levitsky and Way, Geddes, Svolik, Gellner, Anderson) is copyrighted and paraphrased with citations.
- **Design a comparison** (evaluative, built on counterexample and apply-to-a-hard-case) — choose cases to test a claim, say which design it is, and name the rival explanation it cannot rule out. Graded on the moves; any case choice that makes them passes.

**Formal** parts are rare and short: coding a regime under an index's aggregation rule (3.2), an ethnic fractionalization index (5.2), and process-tracing tests in odds form (1.3). Every number in a lesson is recomputed by script before it ships. Evaluative parts are verdict-neutral: arguing that structure or that leaders explain a transition, that a backsliding case is or is not a democracy, or that religion helped or hindered democratization passes equally if the required moves are made. Recent cases (backsliding since 2010, contested elections) are taught with the measurement dispute attached and dated.

## Dangerous Checklist

When you finish, you can:

- [ ] Set up a most-similar or most-different systems comparison for a political question, and name the rival explanation and the selection problem it leaves open
- [ ] Say whether a piece of within-case evidence is a hoop test, a smoking gun or neither, and how much it should move belief in a causal story
- [ ] State Tilly's war-making thesis precisely, and test it on Latin America and post-colonial Africa against Centeno's and Herbst's accounts
- [ ] Distinguish coercive, extractive and administrative capacity, and despotic from infrastructural power, in a weak or fragile state
- [ ] Code a country-year under Polity, Freedom House, V-Dem's Regimes of the World and a dichotomous alternation rule, and explain why the codings diverge
- [ ] Tell a competitive authoritarian regime from a flawed democracy and from a hegemonic-party regime, naming the features of the playing field that decide it
- [ ] Classify an autocracy as personalist, dominant-party, military or monarchic, and predict how its type shapes how it is likely to end
- [ ] Diagnose which survival tool an autocrat is using — repression, coup-proofing, co-optation through a party or legislature, managed elections — and what problem it solves
- [ ] Separate the endogenous and exogenous versions of modernization theory and say which the evidence supports, then place a transition on the structure-versus-agency map
- [ ] Recognize executive aggrandizement, autocratic legalism and strategic election manipulation in a news report, and explain why backsliding is harder to measure than a coup
- [ ] Compare primordialist, modernist and ethnosymbolist accounts of a nation's origin, and use a constructivist case to show when ethnicity becomes politically salient
- [ ] Explain a revolution with Skocpol's structural account and with a preference-falsification account, and say why the second predicts that revolutions surprise everyone

## Modules

### Module 1: The comparative method

How to learn about causes from a handful of countries, and the specific ways that goes wrong.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1.1 | Mill's methods and the two designs | Set up a most-similar and a most-different comparison and say what each can show | Mill's methods of agreement and difference; most-similar vs most-different systems designs (Przeworski and Teune); many variables, small N (Lijphart 1971); why Mill thought his methods failed for societies; cases vs variables; evidence standards cited to [`social-theory`](../social-theory/syllabus.md) 1.4 |
| 1.2 | Case selection and its traps | Choose cases for a claim and spot selection on the dependent variable | selecting on the outcome (Geddes, "How the cases you choose..."); typical, deviant, most-likely and least-likely cases; scope conditions; Galton's problem (cases that are not independent); when large-N or synthetic control does better (cited to [`econometrics`](../econometrics/syllabus.md) 4.6) |
| 1.3 | Process tracing | Weigh within-case evidence for a causal story | causal mechanisms; straw-in-the-wind, hoop, smoking-gun and doubly decisive tests (Van Evera, Collier); the tests as likelihood ratios in odds form (cited to [`philosophical-method`](../philosophical-method/syllabus.md) 2.4); critical junctures and path dependence |

**Boss problem 1:** A researcher claims that countries with large oil revenues stay authoritarian, and supports it with Saudi Arabia, Iran and Venezuela since 2000. (a) Close-read the passage of Mill's *A System of Logic*, Book VI, chapter VII (public domain) in which he argues that the method of difference cannot be applied to political phenomena: what condition does he say social cases fail to meet, and which words decide it? (b) Diagnose the design flaw in the researcher's case selection, and name the design it approximates. (c) Redesign the study as a most-similar comparison and as a most-different comparison, naming real cases for each, and say which rival explanation each design still cannot exclude. (d) Norway is proposed as a counterexample; say what kind of case it is and whether it refutes the claim or narrows its scope, any verdict, if scope conditions are used. (e) Formal: a smoking-gun test is described with given probabilities of observing the evidence if the claim is true and if it is false; compute how far it moves prior odds of 1:1. Parts (a)-(c) are exegetical; (d) is evaluative; (e) is formal.

### Module 2: The state

What a state is, how the European state was forged, and why the model travelled badly.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2.1 | What a state is, and state capacity | Define the state and measure how much of one a country has | Weber's definition (monopoly of legitimate violence over a territory), in the lesson's own translation; legitimacy types cited to [`social-theory`](../social-theory/syllabus.md) 4.3; coercive, extractive and administrative capacity; Mann's despotic vs infrastructural power; tax-to-GDP and census coverage as proxies |
| 2.2 | War made the state: Tilly | Reconstruct Tilly's thesis and his three paths of European state formation | *Coercion, Capital, and European States* (1990); extraction to fund war; coercion-intensive (Russia), capital-intensive (Venice, the Dutch Republic) and capitalized-coercion (England, France) paths; the fiscal-military state cited to [`history-of-debt`](../history-of-debt/syllabus.md) 3.1; Spruyt's rival account (sovereign states vs city-leagues) |
| 2.3 | Beyond Europe: weak, fragile and failed states | Test Tilly outside Europe and explain why some states never gained reach | Herbst on Africa (low population density, inherited borders, few interstate wars); Centeno on Latin America (limited wars, borrowing instead of taxing); Migdal's strong societies and weak states; limited statehood; fragility indices and their critics; the rentier state and the resource curse named, formal and identification work cited to `institutions-and-development` |

**Boss problem 2:** (a) Close-read the lesson's translation of Weber's definition of the state from "Politics as a Vocation": which three elements must all be present, and would a territory where a government and an insurgent movement each tax and police different districts meet it? (b) Reconstruct Tilly's argument from military competition to strong states in numbered premises. (c) Name the crux between Tilly and Herbst on why most post-colonial African states have limited reach, and say what a case would have to look like to discriminate between them. (d) Apply Tilly's thesis to nineteenth-century Latin America as Centeno does, and say where the thesis stops determining a prediction. (e) In 300-500 words, assess whether "war makes states" is a general mechanism or a European path — any verdict, if at least two non-European cases are used and the strongest case for the other side is stated. Parts (a)-(d) are exegetical; (e) is evaluative.

### Module 3: Regimes and authoritarian rule

Drawing the line between democracy and dictatorship, measuring it, and opening up the dictatorships.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3.1 | What counts as a democracy | Choose a definition of democracy and say what it includes and excludes | Schumpeter's minimalism; Dahl's polyarchy (contestation and participation); Przeworski's "parties lose elections"; thin vs thick definitions; conceptual stretching and the ladder of abstraction (Sartori); diminished subtypes, "democracy with adjectives" (Collier and Levitsky); Aristotle's six regimes cited to [`history-of-political-thought`](../history-of-political-thought/syllabus.md) 1.4 |
| 3.2 | Measuring regimes | Code a country-year under the major indices and explain why they disagree | Polity (democracy minus autocracy, -10 to +10, the anocracy band); Freedom House political rights and civil liberties; V-Dem's expert coding and its Regimes of the World categories; dichotomous coding with an alternation rule (Cheibub, Gandhi and Vreeland) and the Botswana problem; continuous vs categorical measures; **formal** coding problems |
| 3.3 | Hybrid regimes | Tell competitive authoritarianism from both democracy and closed autocracy | electoral authoritarianism and the menu of manipulation (Schedler); competitive authoritarianism and the uneven playing field (Levitsky and Way); hegemonic-party regimes; cases: Mexico under the PRI, Russia since 2000, Malaysia before 2018 |
| 3.4 | Kinds of dictatorship | Classify an autocracy and say what its type predicts | Linz's totalitarian vs authoritarian regimes, and sultanism; Geddes's personalist, dominant-party and military regimes, plus monarchies; how type predicts duration and manner of exit (Geddes, Wright and Frantz); cases: the Soviet Union, Franco's Spain, Argentina's juntas, Saudi Arabia |
| 3.5 | How dictators survive: coercion and the palace | Explain how an autocrat handles the elite and the military | Svolik's two problems (authoritarian power-sharing and authoritarian control); most leaders fall to insiders, not crowds; coups and coup-proofing (counterbalancing, family and sectarian loyalists); the dictator's dilemma of repression (Wintrobe); selectorate theory named and cited to `institutions-and-development` |
| 3.6 | How dictators survive: parties, legislatures and elections | Explain why autocrats build nominally democratic institutions | parties as credible promises to elites (Magaloni on the PRI, Brownlee); legislatures as co-optation (Gandhi); elections as information and displays of strength; China's party institutionalization and the 2018 removal of presidential term limits; when these institutions become the regime's undoing |

**Boss problem 3:** An invented country report describes a ruler in office for fifteen years: regular multiparty elections the ruling party always wins, opposition parties legal but denied broadcast access, two opposition leaders jailed on tax charges, an army commanded by the ruler's relatives alongside a separate presidential guard, and a ruling party whose central committee chooses candidates for every office. (a) Formal, then exegetical: code the country-year under a Polity-style and a Freedom House-style rule from given component scores, place it in a Regimes of the World category, and code it under an alternation rule; say which feature makes the codings diverge. (b) Classify the regime under Geddes's typology and under Levitsky and Way's, and name the features of the playing field that decide the second. (c) Diagnose each survival tool in the report and say which of Svolik's two problems it addresses. (d) Name the crux between reading the ruling party as a genuine constraint on the ruler and reading it as a façade, and say what event would discriminate. (e) In 150 words, steelman the claim that the country is a flawed democracy rather than an autocracy, then reply. Part (a) is formal then exegetical, as labelled; (b)-(d) are exegetical; (e) is evaluative.

### Module 4: Democratization, breakdown and backsliding

Why countries become democracies, why some stay that way, and how democracies die — once by coup, now more often by elected incumbents.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4.1 | Modernization theory | Separate modernization's two claims and say which the evidence supports | Lipset (1959) and the social requisites of democracy; endogenous (development causes transitions) vs exogenous (rich democracies survive) versions (Przeworski and Limongi); the income-democracy dispute (Acemoglu et al. vs Boix and Stokes) reported in words, identification cited to `empirical-political-economy`; hard cases: India, Singapore, the Gulf monarchies |
| 4.2 | Structural paths: classes and democracy | Reconstruct Moore's three routes to the modern world and the class-coalition tradition after it | Barrington Moore's *Social Origins* (1966): bourgeois revolution, revolution from above, peasant revolution; landed elites and labour-repressive agriculture; the working class as democratizer (Rueschemeyer, Stephens and Stephens); inequality-based formal models (Acemoglu-Robinson, Boix) cited to `institutions-and-development`, and Meltzer-Richard to [`political-economy`](../political-economy/syllabus.md) 5.3 |
| 4.3 | Agency and pacts: the transitions paradigm | Place a transition on the structure-versus-agency map | O'Donnell and Schmitter's hardliners and softliners, moderates and radicals; pacted transitions: Spain after Franco, Poland's Round Table (1989), Chile's 1988 plebiscite; consolidation as "the only game in town" (Linz and Stepan); Carothers's critique of the transition paradigm |
| 4.4 | Waves of democratization | Describe the waves in the regime data and weigh the diffusion explanation | Huntington's *Third Wave* (1991), dated from Portugal in 1974; reverse waves; the 1989 cascade and the Arab Spring; diffusion vs common shocks (Galton's problem again, from 1.2); disputes over how waves are counted; Western linkage and leverage (Levitsky and Way); democratic-peace and intervention questions cited to [`international-relations`](../international-relations/syllabus.md) 3.2, 4.4 |
| 4.5 | Democratic breakdown | Explain a classic breakdown by coup or collapse | Linz's *Breakdown of Democratic Regimes* (1978): loyal, semi-loyal and disloyal oppositions; Weimar Germany, Spain in 1936, Chile in 1973; bureaucratic authoritarianism in the Southern Cone (O'Donnell); presidentialism cited to [`political-institutions`](../political-institutions/syllabus.md) 3.3; party-system polarization cited to its 4.4 |
| 4.6 | Backsliding by elected incumbents | Recognize the modern forms of backsliding and weigh the explanations and the measurement dispute | Bermeo's shift from coups to executive aggrandizement and strategic election manipulation; autocratic legalism (Scheppele); forbearance, mutual toleration and gatekeeping parties (Levitsky and Ziblatt); populism as a thin ideology (Mudde); polarization and voters trading democracy for partisanship (Svolik); cases: Venezuela under Chávez, Hungary after 2010, Turkey, Poland 2015-2023; whether expert-coded indices over- or under-state recent decline, and the "democratic recession" debate |

**Boss problem 4:** India and Singapore are standard hard cases: a poor country that became and stayed a democracy, and a rich one that has not had an alternation in power. (a) State what the endogenous and exogenous versions of modernization theory each predict for both, and say which version each case troubles. (b) Name the crux between a structural explanation of India's democracy (class coalitions, the Congress party's organization at independence) and an agency explanation (leaders' choices in 1947-1950), and say what within-case evidence would be a hoop test for each. (c) An invented news report describes a newly re-elected government that replaces the election commission, lowers the judicial retirement age, and moves public broadcasting under a ministry, all by lawful statute. Diagnose each step under Bermeo's forms and Scheppele's autocratic legalism, and say which of Linz's opposition types the governing party now resembles. (d) Say why an index built from expert coders and one built from observable events would code the case in (c) differently in its first two years. (e) In 300-500 words, assess whether structure or agency better explains the difference between breakdown in Chile in 1973 and survival in Spain after 1981's failed coup — any verdict, if both explanations are stated at full strength and within-case evidence is used. Parts (a)-(d) are exegetical; (e) is evaluative.

### Module 5: Nations and ethnic politics

Where nations come from, how states make them, and when ethnic difference becomes the axis of politics and conflict.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5.1 | Theories of nationalism | Compare the main accounts of where nations come from and test them on a case | primordialism; modernism: Gellner (industrialization and a standardized high culture), Anderson (print capitalism and imagined communities); ethnosymbolism (Smith); Renan's nation as a daily plebiscite, in the lesson's own translation; Mill's chapter on nationality cited to [`history-of-political-thought`](../history-of-political-thought/syllabus.md) 6.4 |
| 5.2 | Making nations and making ethnicity salient | Explain how states build national identity, and when a cleavage becomes political | civic vs ethnic nationhood (Brubaker on France and Germany); Eugen Weber's *Peasants into Frenchmen*; nation-building by language, schooling and conscription, Tanzania vs Kenya (Miguel); constructivism: Chewas and Tumbukas in Zambia and Malawi (Posner); ethnic head counts in patronage democracies (Chandra); the fractionalization index as one minus the sum of squared group shares, and its link to the effective number of parties in [`political-institutions`](../political-institutions/syllabus.md) 2.1 (**formal**) |
| 5.3 | Ethnic conflict and power-sharing | Weigh the explanations of ethnic conflict and the two families of institutional response | Horowitz's *Ethnic Groups in Conflict*; the finding that ethnic diversity predicts civil war less well than state weakness and terrain (Fearon and Laitin), identification cited to `empirical-political-economy` and bargaining models to `conflict-and-bargaining`; consociationalism (Lijphart) vs centripetalism (Horowitz); Lebanon, Northern Ireland after 1998, Bosnia after Dayton; secession and holding-together federalism cited to [`political-institutions`](../political-institutions/syllabus.md) 5.1; group rights as a normative question cited to [`political-philosophy`](../political-philosophy/syllabus.md) 6.3 |

**Boss problem 5:** Mill and Acton disagreed in the 1860s about whether free government needs a single nationality. (a) Close-read the passage of Mill's *Considerations on Representative Government*, chapter XVI (public domain), in which he argues that free institutions are next to impossible in a country made up of different nationalities: reconstruct the argument in numbered premises and say whether its central claim is empirical or normative, pointing to the words that decide it. (b) Acton's "Nationality" (1862, public domain) defends states containing several nations; state his reply and name the crux between the two. (c) Apply Gellner's and Posner's accounts to the claim that Mill's "nationalities" are fixed facts, and say what each would predict about a change in state borders. (d) Formal: compute the fractionalization index for two invented countries from group shares, and say why an equal index can hide very different political situations. (e) In 300-500 words, assess whether the record of Lebanon, Northern Ireland and Bosnia favours consociational or centripetal designs — any verdict, if each design's best defender is represented and at least two cases are used. Parts (a)-(c) are exegetical; (d) is formal; (e) is evaluative.

### Module 6: Revolution, culture and religion

The dramatic breaks in politics, and the slower forces — values and faith — that some think explain regimes better than institutions do.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 6.1 | Social revolutions: the structural account | Explain a social revolution by state breakdown and peasant revolt | what makes a revolution social vs political; Skocpol's *States and Social Revolutions* (1979) on France, Russia and China; international pressure and fiscal crisis; state autonomy; Tocqueville's *Old Regime* as the comparative ancestor, cited to [`history-of-political-thought`](../history-of-political-thought/syllabus.md) 6.3; Marx on revolution cited to its 6.6; Goldstone's demographic-structural account |
| 6.2 | Why revolutions surprise: grievance, expectation and cascades | Explain why revolutions are hard to predict and when crowds win | relative deprivation and the J-curve (Davies); the collective-action problem of revolt cited to [`political-economy`](../political-economy/syllabus.md) 3.2; preference falsification and cascades (Kuran) in East Germany in 1989; ideology and religion in Iran in 1979, and Skocpol's own later revision; nonviolent resistance and military defection (Chenoweth and Stephan); Tunisia, Egypt and Syria in 2011 |
| 6.3 | Political culture | Say what a cultural explanation of a regime claims and what would test it | *The Civic Culture* (Almond and Verba, 1963): participant, subject and parochial cultures across five countries; the Inglehart-Welzel value map and self-expression values; culture as cause vs consequence of democracy; Putnam cited to [`social-theory`](../social-theory/syllabus.md) 6.3-6.4 |
| 6.4 | Religion in politics | Weigh the main claims about religion's effect on regimes and parties | the rise of Christian Democracy (Kalyvas); the Catholic Church's shift and the third wave (Huntington, Philpott); Stepan's twin tolerations; debates over Islam and democracy, including the state-ulema alliance thesis (Kuru); the inclusion-moderation hypothesis and Ennahda in Tunisia; secularization models cited to [`social-theory`](../social-theory/syllabus.md) Module 7 |

**Boss problem 6:** (a) Close-read the chapter of Tocqueville's *The Old Regime and the Revolution* (Bonner translation, public domain) arguing that the prosperity of Louis XVI's reign hastened the Revolution, and the passage there on the danger to a bad government when it begins to reform: reconstruct the mechanism in numbered premises. (b) Name the crux between Tocqueville's mechanism and Skocpol's structural account of 1789, and say what evidence from France would move it. (c) Apply Kuran's preference-falsification account to East Germany in 1989: why does it imply that neither the regime nor its opponents could have predicted the timing, and what does that mean for evaluating Skocpol's theory by its predictions? (d) Diagnose two invented op-eds on Iran in 1979, one attributing the revolution to Shi'a religious ideas and one to the Shah's rentier state; say which lesson's framework each assumes and what Skocpol's later revision conceded. (e) In 300-500 words, assess whether religion in the third wave and the Arab Spring worked mainly as an ideology or as an organization — any verdict, if Catholic and Muslim cases are treated by the same standard. Parts (a)-(d) are exegetical; (e) is evaluative.
