# Institutions & Development — Syllabus

> Politics & Society · Tier 2 · ~24 lessons · Prereqs: [`political-economy`](../political-economy/syllabus.md) · Roadmap id: `institutions-and-development`

## Goal

Why are some countries rich and well governed and others poor and badly governed? The answer this literature gives is *institutions* — and this course is about taking that answer apart until it is a model with assumptions you can check and an empirical claim you can argue with. [`political-economy`](../political-economy/syllabus.md) solved politics inside a settled democracy, where the rules are fixed and the question is what equilibrium they produce. Here the rules themselves are the outcome: who gets to make them, why anyone with power would accept a rule that binds him, and what happens when he cannot credibly promise to. You will finish able to solve a predatory-ruler model and find the discount factor at which secure property rights become self-enforcing; solve the Acemoglu-Robinson model of democratization, show why a promise of redistribution fails where an extension of the franchise succeeds, and derive the inverted-U in inequality; compute a selectorate equilibrium and read off the public-good share, the leader's rent and what an aid windfall does to each; solve for optimal fiscal capacity and the wage that buys an honest bureaucrat; find the corruption equilibrium with and without theft and show why fragmented bribe-taking is worse than a single monopolist; compute when clientelism survives the secret ballot; and show why an elite blocks a reform that would enrich the country. Alongside each model sits the evidence: the colonial-origins argument and the fights over it, the resource curse and its critics, the aid debate, persistence estimates and the culture-versus-institutions dispute — every one presented as live, with what the estimate would have to show spelled out and no verdict delivered. It deliberately skips the identification toolkit itself ([`empirical-political-economy`](../empirical-political-economy/syllabus.md); machinery in [`econometrics`](../econometrics/syllabus.md)), descriptive regime typologies, democratization narratives and the history of state formation ([`comparative-politics`](../comparative-politics/syllabus.md)), the historical debt episodes ([`history-of-debt`](../history-of-debt/syllabus.md)), electoral and legislative models ([`political-economy`](../political-economy/syllabus.md)), growth theory ([`grad-macro`](../grad-macro/syllabus.md)), and every normative question about whether the institutions modelled here are just ([`political-philosophy`](../political-philosophy/syllabus.md), [`philosophy-of-economics`](../philosophy-of-economics/syllabus.md)).

## How practice works in this course

Problems follow [HUMANITIES-BUILD-BRIEF.md](../../HUMANITIES-BUILD-BRIEF.md). This is a **formal-plus-empirical** course, and the two halves are graded differently. Every part declares its kind and every solution is a rubric (must-hit moves, wrong turns) plus a model answer.

- **Formal model (archetype 8), formal.** The backbone. Solve the ruler's revenue problem, the democratization game, the selectorate allocation, the fiscal-capacity investment, the bribe-setting equilibrium, the reform-blocking condition. Graded as in any math course. **Every number in a lesson or boss problem is recomputed by script before it ships**, and where a closed form exists the script checks the equilibrium against a brute-force search as well.
- **Diagnose (archetype 4), exegetical, strict.** An invented country report, reform memo, donor strategy paper or investment-bank note leans on a model without naming it. Say which model, which assumption carries the prediction, and what would have to be true for it to transfer. Invented material is written for the problem and never attributed to a real person.
- **Find the crux (archetype 5), exegetical, strict.** This field is a stack of live disputes — settler mortality as an instrument, resource rents and regime type, aid and growth, culture against institutions. The task is to name the single premise one side must deny and the evidence that would move it, not to settle it.
- **Steelman and reply / apply to a hard case (archetypes 6 and 3), evaluative, verdict-neutral.** Does the colonial-origins evidence support the institutions-first claim? Is a resource windfall bad for democracy? Should a donor give budget support or run projects? **Any conclusion passes if the moves are made,** and the must-hit moves always include stating what the model assumes, what the estimate actually compares, and which assumption or comparison the verdict turns on.

Two disciplines hold the course together. First, **exegetical parts about what a model says are strict inside an evaluative answer**: misstating the timing, who moves first, who can commit, or what is common knowledge is an error even in a part where the verdict is free. Second, **evidence is discussed, never estimated**: a problem may ask what a settler-mortality instrument has to assume, or what a within-country boundary comparison holds fixed, but never to derive a standard error — that is [`empirical-political-economy`](../empirical-political-economy/syllabus.md), and it is cited by name each time. Modern scholarship is paraphrased and cited, never quoted at length.

## Dangerous Checklist

When you finish, you can:

- [ ] State North's definition of an institution precisely, separate institutions from organizations and from policies, and say what the "deep determinants" claim is asserting against geography and trade
- [ ] Solve a predatory-ruler model for the revenue-maximizing tax, show that without commitment investment collapses, and find the discount factor at which secure property rights become self-enforcing
- [ ] Reconstruct the colonial-origins argument as an identification claim, state its exclusion restriction in one sentence, and say exactly what the settler-mortality and human-capital critiques would have to establish
- [ ] Solve the Acemoglu-Robinson democratization game: compute the revolution constraint, show that a promised tax cut is not credible where franchise extension is, find the repression threshold, and derive the inverted-U in inequality
- [ ] Separate de jure from de facto power, and explain why an institutional reform can leave the distribution of outcomes unchanged
- [ ] Compute a selectorate equilibrium — public goods, private goods per coalition member, leader's rent — and predict how each moves with coalition size, selectorate size and a revenue windfall
- [ ] Explain why an autocrat builds a party, a legislature and an election, and say which of the regime's two commitment problems each institution addresses
- [ ] Solve for optimal fiscal capacity as an investment, say how political instability, common interests and cohesive institutions move it, and compute the wage that makes a bureaucrat refuse a bribe
- [ ] Find the corruption equilibrium with and without theft, show why independent bribe-takers do more damage than one monopolist, and compute when clientelism survives a secret ballot
- [ ] Price a resource or aid windfall inside a political model, and state the resource-curse and aid-effectiveness disputes as claims that evidence could settle
- [ ] Show why an elite blocks a reform that raises total income, say what compensation would fix it, and explain why the compensation is not credible
- [ ] Tell path dependence from mere persistence, and say what a persistence estimate does and does not identify about the mechanism

## Modules

### Module 1: Institutions as a cause

The claim that institutions are the deep determinant of prosperity: what it means, the model of the state underneath it, and the evidence fight it started.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1.1 | What an institution is, and the deep-determinants claim | State the institutions-first hypothesis precisely and distinguish it from its rivals | North's definition (humanly devised constraints structuring interaction); formal vs informal institutions; institutions vs organizations vs policies; the unexplained residual (growth accounting cited to [`grad-macro`](../grad-macro/syllabus.md) 2.6) and conditional convergence (its 2.2); geography, trade and institutions as rival deep determinants; why "good institutions cause growth" is nearly unfalsifiable without an exogenous source of variation |
| 1.2 | The predatory state: rulers, bandits and property rights | Solve a revenue-maximizing ruler's problem and state the fundamental political dilemma | North's neoclassical theory of the state (ruler as a discriminating monopolist selling protection); Olson's roving vs stationary bandit; the encompassing interest; the revenue-maximizing tax rate and its gap from the efficient one; a state strong enough to protect property is strong enough to take it (**formal**) |
| 1.3 | The commitment problem | Show that investment collapses when the ruler cannot commit, and find what restores it | time inconsistency of expropriation and of the capital levy; the holdup problem with sunk investment; reputation as a repeated game (trigger strategies cited to [`grad-game-theory`](../grad-game-theory/syllabus.md) 3.3) and its critical discount factor; constitutional constraints that bind the ruler and lower his revenue; the sovereign-debt version cited to [`economics-of-debt`](../economics-of-debt/syllabus.md) 6.2-6.3, the 1688 case to [`history-of-debt`](../history-of-debt/syllabus.md) 3.2, and the international twin to [`conflict-and-bargaining`](../conflict-and-bargaining/syllabus.md) 4.2 |
| 1.4 | Colonial origins, and the argument about it | Reconstruct the settler-mortality argument as an identification claim and state its critiques at full strength | extractive vs inclusive institutions; settler mortality as an instrument for early institutions and the reversal of fortune in former colonies; the exclusion restriction said in one sentence; Albouy's challenge to the mortality data and the standard-error dispute; Glaeser and co-authors' human-capital alternative; Sachs's geography-and-disease reply; presented as **live**. The design itself and the data dispute are cited to [`empirical-political-economy`](../empirical-political-economy/syllabus.md) 5.3 and the IV machinery to [`econometrics`](../econometrics/syllabus.md) 3.6-3.8; what is taught here is what the rival *theories* claim and what each critique would have to establish |

**Boss problem 1:** A ruler taxes output at rate $t$. Each of a continuum of producers chooses effort $x \ge 0$ at cost $x^2/2$ and produces $x$, so a producer maximizes $(1-t)x - x^2/2$. **(a)** Suppose the ruler commits to $t$ before producers invest. Find the producers' effort, the revenue-maximizing tax rate, revenue, producer surplus and total surplus, and compare total surplus with the no-tax benchmark. **(b)** Now suppose the ruler sets $t$ *after* effort is sunk. Find the subgame-perfect outcome and explain in one sentence which feature of (a) it destroys. **(c)** The stage game of (b) is repeated forever with discount factor $\delta$; producers play a grim trigger that reverts to zero effort permanently after any deviation from the tax rate of (a). Find the smallest $\delta$ that sustains it, and say what in a real polity the punishment corresponds to. **(d)** A constitution caps the tax rate at $\bar t = 1/4$. Recompute effort, revenue, producer surplus and total surplus, and say why the ruler resists a constraint that makes the country richer. *(a)-(d) formal.* **(e)** Reconstruct the colonial-origins argument in numbered premises, ending in the conclusion that early institutions cause present income, and mark which premise is the exclusion restriction. *(Exegetical.)* **(f)** In 300-500 words, assess whether the colonial-origins evidence establishes that institutions are the deep determinant of income. Any verdict passes if it states what the instrument must satisfy, presents the mortality-data and human-capital critiques as their authors would, and names the evidence that would move you. *(Evaluative.)*

### Module 2: Democratization and credible commitment

The course's centrepiece, inherited from [`political-economy`](../political-economy/syllabus.md)'s former 5.5 and cited by three other syllabi: why an elite ever hands over the vote, and why a promise will not do instead.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2.1 | The elite, the citizens and the revolution constraint | Set up the redistribution game between a rich elite and poor citizens and locate the threat of revolution | two groups with unequal income shares; a distortionary linear tax with lump-sum transfer, the citizens' preferred rate cited to [`political-economy`](../political-economy/syllabus.md) 5.3; the revolution payoff and what it destroys; when the revolution constraint binds; the collective-action problem behind a revolution as a reduced form (cited to [`political-economy`](../political-economy/syllabus.md) 3.1-3.2 and [`comparative-politics`](../comparative-politics/syllabus.md) 6.2); the historical franchise extensions that test the redistribution prediction cited to [`empirical-political-economy`](../empirical-political-economy/syllabus.md) 3.4 |
| 2.2 | Why promises fail and the franchise does not | Show that a concession today is not credible and that extending the vote is | the transitory nature of de facto power (today's threat, tomorrow's calm); the elite's promise as a non-credible announcement in a game where it moves again next period; democratization as a transfer of *future* decision rights, not of income; the window of opportunity; the parallel with 1.3 and with the international commitment problem in [`conflict-and-bargaining`](../conflict-and-bargaining/syllabus.md) 4.2 (**formal**) |
| 2.3 | Repression, concession, democracy: the inverted U | Solve the elite's three-way choice and derive the non-monotone effect of inequality | the cost of repression as a share of elite income; the repression threshold; low inequality (no threat), middling (democratization), high (repression or revolution); Boix's alternative mechanism through asset specificity and capital mobility; consolidation, coups and redemocratization; the cases are cited to [`comparative-politics`](../comparative-politics/syllabus.md) 4.1-4.3 |
| 2.4 | De jure and de facto power | Explain why an institutional reform can leave outcomes unchanged | de jure power allocated by institutions vs de facto power from resources, organization and violence; investment in de facto power and the group-size logic ([`political-economy`](../political-economy/syllabus.md) 3.2); captured democracy; the seesaw effect, in which a group compensates for lost de jure power; persistence of the equilibrium under changed rules; what this predicts for institutional-reform programmes |

**Boss problem 2:** A society has a rich elite of population share $0.2$ and citizens of share $0.8$; total income is $1$, of which the elite hold a share $\theta$. A linear tax $\tau$ is rebated lump sum with deadweight cost $\tau^2/2$ per unit of mean income, so a person with income $y$ gets $y(1-\tau) + \tau - \tau^2/2$. If the citizens revolt, a fraction $\mu$ of all income is destroyed and the rest is divided among the citizens forever, with the elite getting nothing. There are two periods, discounted at $\beta = 0.9$; the revolution threat exists only in period 1. **(a)** For $\theta = 0.6$, compute both groups' incomes, the citizens' preferred tax rate and their payoff at it, and their payoff with no redistribution. **(b)** With $\mu = 0.52$, compute the citizens' two-period payoffs under revolution, under a maximal concession in period 1 followed by no redistribution in period 2, and under democratization (the citizens' preferred rate in both periods). Show that the elite cannot buy off the revolution with a promise but can with the franchise, and compute the elite's payoff under democratization. **(c)** Repression costs the elite a fraction $\kappa$ of their income in every period and removes the threat permanently. Find the $\kappa$ below which the elite represses instead of democratizing. **(d)** Redo (b) for $\theta = 0.4$ and for $\theta = 0.9$, holding $\mu = 0.52$, and state the inverted-U result the three cases exhibit. *(a)-(d) formal.* **(e)** In 150 words, say precisely why the concession in (b) is not credible while the franchise extension is, naming what each does to the elite's period-2 choice set. *(Exegetical, strict.)* **(f)** An invented briefing note argues that a government facing street protests should "announce a permanent redistribution package rather than constitutional change, which is irreversible." Diagnose which feature of the model the note has got right and which it has ignored, and say what the model predicts the protesters will do. *(Exegetical.)*

### Module 3: The logic of political survival

Open the dictatorship: who the ruler actually has to pay, what that does to policy, and why autocrats build institutions that look democratic.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3.1 | Selectorate theory | Compute the equilibrium mix of public and private goods and the leader's rent | nominal selectorate, real selectorate $S$, winning coalition $W$; the loyalty norm $W/S$; public goods as the cheap way to deliver utility when $W$ is large, private goods when it is small; the leader's rent as $(R - W^2)(1 - W/S)$ in the lesson's parameterization; predictions for survival, public-good provision and windfalls (**formal**) |
| 3.2 | Authoritarian power-sharing: the ruler and his coalition | Model the regime's internal commitment problem and see what a party solves | Svolik's two problems (power-sharing with the elite, control of the masses) recalled from [`comparative-politics`](../comparative-politics/syllabus.md) 3.5; the elite's coup threat as the enforcement device, and the ruler's incentive to shrink the coalition once secure; the party as a commitment to future rents (Magaloni); legislatures as co-optation (Gandhi); repeated-game machinery cited to [`grad-game-theory`](../grad-game-theory/syllabus.md) 3.3-3.5 |
| 3.3 | Repression, loyalty and the information problem | Trade repression off against loyalty, and see why autocrats need information they suppress | Wintrobe's repression-loyalty trade-off and the tinpot-totalitarian map; the dictator's dilemma (the more he represses, the less he learns); preference falsification and cascades cited to [`comparative-politics`](../comparative-politics/syllabus.md) 6.2; partially free media and protest as monitoring devices for the ruler (Egorov, Guriev and Sonin; Lorentzen); censorship as a choice with a cost |
| 3.4 | Elections and manipulation under autocracy | Explain why a regime that cannot lose still holds elections, and what fraud signals | elections as information about local agents and about regime strength; oversized majorities as deterrence (Magaloni); the menu of manipulation and the trade-off between margin and legitimacy; when a managed election becomes a focal point for the opposition; the descriptive typology cited to [`comparative-politics`](../comparative-politics/syllabus.md) 3.3, 3.6; vote-buying machinery deferred to 5.2 |

**Boss problem 3:** A leader controls revenue $R = 100$. She spends $g$ on a public good worth $2\sqrt{g}$ to every citizen and gives $x$ in private goods to each of the $W$ members of her winning coalition, keeping the remainder as rent. A defector from the coalition joins a successful challenger's coalition with probability $W/S$; the challenger, who keeps no rent, offers the bundle that maximizes a coalition member's utility subject to his budget $R$ and coalition size $W$, and a defector who fails to join receives only the challenger's public good. **(a)** Show that both leaders set $g = W^2$ whenever $W^2 \le R$, and derive the defector's expected payoff, the incumbent's minimum offer $x$ and her rent $(R - W^2)(1 - W/S)$. **(b)** Compute $g$, $x$, total private spending and rent for three regimes: $(W, S) = (2, 20)$, $(2, 200)$ and $(8, 16)$. Say which regime provides most public goods, which leaves the leader richest, and which feature of the parameters does each job. **(c)** Revenue jumps from 100 to 150. Recompute $g$ and the rent for $(2, 200)$ and $(8, 16)$, and state the general result for $\partial\rho/\partial R$. Say what the model predicts about resource windfalls and foreign aid — and name one prediction of the model you should distrust, given that $g$ does not depend on $R$ at all. *(a)-(c) formal; the last sentence of (c) exegetical.* **(d)** An invented country report describes a ruler who has just enlarged his ruling party's membership tenfold while leaving the politburo at nine men. Diagnose the move in the model's terms: which parameter moved, in which direction, and what happens to the ruler's rent and to the loyalty of politburo members. *(Exegetical.)* **(e)** In 300-500 words, assess whether selectorate theory explains autocratic behaviour better than an account built on repression and information (3.3). Any verdict passes if both accounts are stated at the strength their authors give them, at least one autocratic institution from 3.2 or 3.4 is explained by each, and you name the observation that would discriminate. *(Evaluative.)*

### Module 4: State capacity

Behind every institutional claim is a state that can actually tax, enforce a contract and staff an office. Capacity is not given; it is bought.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4.1 | Fiscal capacity as an investment | Solve for how much tax capacity a government builds, and say what makes it build more | Besley and Persson's framework: the maximum feasible tax rate as a state variable, built at a convex cost; common-interest spending (war, a public good) vs group-specific transfers; political stability and the probability of holding power tomorrow; cohesive institutions that limit what the incumbent can do with revenue; why high capacity is not the same as good institutions (**formal**) |
| 4.2 | Legal capacity and the enforcement of contracts | Explain what a state adds over private enforcement, and why the private alternatives cap the market | legal capacity as a complement to fiscal capacity; private-order enforcement: the Maghribi coalition and the Genoese contrast (Greif), merchant guilds, the law merchant as a reputation mechanism (Milgrom, North and Weingast); why reputation-based enforcement bounds the set of trading partners; formal courts, registries and collateral; doctrine cited to [`constitutional-law`](../constitutional-law/syllabus.md) |
| 4.3 | Bureaucrats: selection, wages and the honest official | Compute the wage that buys honesty and trade it against monitoring | Weber's bureaucracy as a personnel system, cited to [`social-theory`](../social-theory/syllabus.md) 4.4; the efficiency-wage condition for an official who can take a bribe $b$, be caught with probability $p$ and lose a rent; selection vs incentives (who applies when the wage rises); multitasking and what gets measured, cited to [`grad-micro`](../grad-micro/syllabus.md) 5.4; patronage vs merit recruitment; audits and their political limits |
| 4.4 | Taxation and the fiscal contract | Explain the bargain that taxation forces, and what happens where the state needs no taxpayers | "no taxation without representation" as a bargaining claim; quasi-voluntary compliance and tax morale (Levi); third-party information and withholding as the real drivers of compliance; the informal sector as a capacity constraint; the rentier negation of the bargain, which sets up 5.3; the fiscal-military state cited to [`history-of-debt`](../history-of-debt/syllabus.md) 3.1, tax design to [`public-economics`](../public-economics/syllabus.md) 4.1, 5.1 |

**Boss problem 4:** **(a)** A government invests today in fiscal capacity $\tau$ (the maximum tax rate it can levy tomorrow) at cost $2\tau^2$. Tomorrow, with probability $0.5$ a common interest arises and all revenue goes to a public good worth $2$ per unit to everyone; otherwise revenue is transferred to whichever of two equal-sized groups holds power, which the incumbent group does with probability $\gamma$, and a transfer is worth $2$ per unit to its recipients because the group is half the population. Find the incumbent group's optimal $\tau$ for $\gamma = 0.1$, $0.5$ and $0.9$, and state the comparative static in one line. **(b)** Now suppose cohesive institutions forbid group-specific transfers, so all revenue always goes to the public good, worth $2$ per unit in the common-interest state and $0.5$ otherwise. Find $\tau$ and compare it with each case in (a). Explain why the case with the highest capacity is not obviously the best-governed. **(c)** An official can take a bribe worth $b = 100$ once. He is caught with probability $p = 0.1$, and if caught is dismissed forever to an outside wage of $50$; he discounts at $\delta = 0.9$. Find the smallest wage at which he refuses the bribe, then recompute when monitoring doubles to $p = 0.2$, and say what the comparison implies for the cost of an honest bureaucracy. *(a)-(c) formal.* **(d)** Name the crux between an account of weak states as *underinvestment* (4.1) and one as *missing private-order substitutes being enough* (4.2), and say what evidence about a specific country would move it. *(Exegetical.)* **(e)** An invented donor strategy paper proposes to raise a country's tax-to-GDP ratio by five points in three years through a new semi-autonomous revenue authority staffed at private-sector salaries. In 150 words, name which parameter of (a) and which of (c) the plan is trying to move, and the one assumption of each model the plan quietly needs. *(Exegetical.)*

### Module 5: Corruption, clientelism and unearned revenue

Bad governance modelled as an equilibrium rather than a failing, and what happens when the money arrives without a taxpayer attached.

**Boss problem 5:** Demand for a licence is $q = 10 - P$, where $P$ is the total price a buyer pays. **(a)** The official price is $4$ and one corrupt official adds a bribe $b$, remitting the official price to the treasury. Find the bribe, the quantity, the total price and the official's take. **(b)** Now the official pockets the whole payment and issues the licence off the books, so the buyer pays only $b$. Recompute all four, and explain why buyers may prefer corruption *with* theft and what that does to the chance of detection. **(c)** Three officials must each stamp the licence and each independently sets his own bribe, with $q = 10 - \sum_i b_i$. Find the symmetric Nash equilibrium, total bribes, quantity and each official's take, and compare with a single official who sets all three stamps jointly. Say which arrangement is worse for the buyer, for the officials, and for output. **(d)** A machine offers a voter a benefit worth $10$ each election in return for a vote that costs the voter $4$ in foregone preference; the voter discounts at $\delta = 0.9$. Under perfect monitoring, find the smallest $\delta$ that sustains the exchange. Then suppose the machine learns how the voter voted only with probability $\pi$, and an undetected defector keeps receiving benefits: find the smallest $\pi$ that sustains the exchange at $\delta = 0.9$, and say what the answer implies for the secret ballot as a cure. *(a)-(d) formal.* **(e)** Name the crux between Ross's cross-country resource-curse finding and Haber and Menaldo's within-country challenge, and say what a country's history would have to look like to move it. *(Exegetical.)* **(f)** In 300-500 words, assess whether a donor facing the government of (b)-(d) should give budget support or fund discrete projects. Any verdict passes if it uses the windfall result from Boss 3(c), states the conditionality commitment problem, says what the evidence on aid can and cannot establish, and answers the strongest case for the other option. *(Evaluative.)*

### Module 6: Persistence, culture and change

Why the past keeps acting on the present, whether the acting thing is institutions or culture, and what it would take to change either.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 6.1 | Colonial legacies and persistence | Say what a persistence estimate identifies and what it leaves open | the boundary-discontinuity design in words (the Peruvian mita, Dell) and the long-run effects of colonial land-tenure and administrative systems (Banerjee and Iyer on India; indirect rule; Nunn and Wantchekon on the slave trade and trust); the three candidate carriers — institutions, culture, capital — and why an estimate of the total effect rarely separates them (the estimand-versus-mechanism distinction cited to [`empirical-political-economy`](../empirical-political-economy/syllabus.md) 1.1, design to its Module 2); the historical narratives cited to [`comparative-politics`](../comparative-politics/syllabus.md) Module 2 |
| 6.2 | Culture or institutions? | State the two explanations as rivals and say what would tell them apart | culture as informal institution and as shared beliefs sustaining an equilibrium (Greif's cultural-beliefs account); civic capital and trust as productive assets (cited to [`social-theory`](../social-theory/syllabus.md) 6.3-6.4, and the Protestant-ethic dispute to its 4.1-4.2); Tabellini's culture-and-institutions results; the endogeneity problem (culture is downstream of institutions and upstream of them); the epidemiological approach using migrants and what it does and does not hold fixed; **verdict-neutral** |
| 6.3 | Path dependence and critical junctures | Tell genuine path dependence from mere persistence and model the lock-in | increasing returns, coordination and self-reinforcement (David, Arthur; Pierson's application to politics); a Polya-urn model of adoption where early accidents decide the limit (**formal**); critical junctures and institutional drift; the Liebowitz-Margolis caution that lock-in claims need a counterfactual, and what an inefficient lock-in would have to show |
| 6.4 | Why bad institutions survive, and when they change | Show why an elite blocks a surplus-increasing reform and what would unblock it | political losers vs economic losers; the reform that raises income but lowers the probability of holding power; why a Coasean side payment fails — the beneficiaries cannot commit once the elite's power is gone (1.3 again); elite splits, external shocks and windows of opportunity (2.2); transplanted best-practice institutions and why form outruns function; what the models jointly say a durable reform needs (**formal**) |

**Boss problem 6:** **(a)** An elite receives a fixed share $0.3$ of national income plus a rent $B$ from holding power, which it holds with probability $0.9$ under the status quo. A reform raises national income from $100$ to $130$ but cuts the elite's probability of holding power to $0.5$. Find the elite's payoff with and without the reform for $B = 20$ and $B = 40$, find the critical $B$ above which the elite blocks, and compute the transfer that would make the elite indifferent at $B = 40$. Show that the transfer is affordable out of the reform's gains. **(b)** Explain in three sentences why the transfer in (a) is not paid in equilibrium, naming the model from 1.3 that the argument reuses. **(c)** Two institutional forms compete. An urn starts with one ball of each; four times, a ball is drawn at random and returned with another of the same colour. Compute the distribution of the number of balls added of the first colour, and say what the answer shows about whether the surviving institution is the better one. *(a) and (c) formal; (b) exegetical.* **(d)** An invented reform memo proposes to end a country's persistent regional income gap by rewriting its land-registration law along "international best practice" lines, citing evidence that the gap traces to a colonial-era administrative boundary. Diagnose it: what does the boundary estimate identify, which of the three carriers in 6.1 does the memo assume, and what does 6.4 predict about who resists. *(Exegetical.)* **(e)** In 300-500 words, assess whether the persistence of underdevelopment in a region of your choice is better explained by institutions or by culture. Any verdict passes if it states both explanations as their best defenders would, says what each predicts for migrants who leave and for outsiders who arrive, names the endogeneity problem in the evidence, and answers the strongest objection. *(Evaluative.)*
