# Deep Learning · Lesson 3.1: RNNs & backprop through time

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 3: Sequence models & attention · Builds on: [1.3 (backpropagation and automatic differentiation)](01-03-backpropagation-and-automatic-differentiation.md), [2.1 (the convolution operation)](02-01-the-convolution-operation.md) · Unlocks: [3.2 (LSTMs, GRUs and vanishing gradients)](03-02-lstms-grus-and-vanishing-gradients.md)

## Why this matters

Module 2 asked how to share parameters across **space**. This module asks the same question about **time**: text, audio, and time series have order, they have variable length, and a dense layer that expects a fixed-size input cannot handle either.

The recurrent neural network is the first answer, and it is the direct analogue of convolution. Where a convolution applies one kernel at every position, an RNN applies one weight matrix at every time step, carrying a hidden state forward. Parameters are independent of sequence length, exactly as convolution's are independent of image size.

It also fails in a specific, quantifiable way, and that failure is what motivates everything else in this module — the gating of [3.2](03-02-lstms-grus-and-vanishing-gradients.md) and the wholesale replacement by attention in [3.4](03-04-self-attention-queries-keys-values.md).

## The idea

Keep a **hidden state**, a vector that summarises everything seen so far. At each step, combine it with the new input to produce the next hidden state:

$$h_t = \phi(W_h h_{t-1} + W_x x_t + b).$$

That single equation is the whole model. The same $W_h$, $W_x$ and $b$ are used at every step, so a sequence of length 5 and one of length 5,000 use identical parameters — and the model can be *applied* to lengths it never saw in training.

Training unrolls the recurrence into a feedforward graph with one copy of the layer per step, and runs ordinary backpropagation on it. That is **backpropagation through time**, and it is not a new algorithm: it is [1.3](01-03-backpropagation-and-automatic-differentiation.md)'s shared-weight rule with $T$ uses instead of two. Each parameter's gradient is the **sum** of its contributions from every step.

Now the problem. To send a gradient from step $T$ back to step $t$, the chain rule passes it through the recurrence $T - t$ times, multiplying by the same Jacobian each time. That is a matrix raised to a power, and matrix powers do one of two things: **shrink to nothing or blow up**. At a per-step factor of 0.9 over 100 steps the gradient is $2.7\times10^{-5}$; at 1.1 it is $13{,}781$.

There is no safe setting. The boundary is exactly 1, and a product of a hundred factors sitting exactly on 1 is not something training maintains.

**Exploding gradients are easy to fix** — clip the gradient norm and carry on. **Vanishing gradients are not**, because a gradient that arrives as $10^{-5}$ is not distinguishable from one that arrives as zero, and there is nothing to rescue. Vanishing is the real problem, and it means an RNN cannot learn dependencies much longer than a few dozen steps.

One more cost, which turned out to matter as much as the gradients: $h_t$ requires $h_{t-1}$, so **the $T$ steps are strictly sequential**. A convolution computes every position at once; an RNN cannot. On hardware built for parallel arithmetic, that is a decisive disadvantage.

## The formal version

> **Elman RNN.** With input $x_t \in \mathbb{R}^{n}$ and hidden state $h_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d}$,
> $$h_t = \phi\big(W_h h_{t-1} + W_x x_t + b\big), \qquad y_t = W_y h_t + b_y,$$
> with $h_0$ usually zero. Parameters: $W_h \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$, $W_x \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$, $b \in \mathbb{R}^{d}$.

> **Parameter count.** $d n + d^{2} + d$, **independent of $T$.**

At $n = 256$, $d = 512$: $131{,}072 + 262{,}144 + 512 = 393{,}728$ parameters, whether the sequence is 10 steps or 10,000.

> **Backpropagation through time.** Unroll to a feedforward graph of depth $T$ and apply reverse-mode autodiff. Since $W_h$ is used at every step,
> $$\frac{\partial L}{\partial W_h} = \sum_{t=1}^{T} \frac{\partial L}{\partial h_t}\,\frac{\partial h_t}{\partial W_h}\bigg|_{\text{direct}}.$$

This is [1.3](01-03-backpropagation-and-automatic-differentiation.md)'s Example 2 with $T$ uses rather than 2. **Nothing new is required**; the name exists because the graph is generated by unrolling rather than written down.

> **The recurrent Jacobian.** For $t < T$,
> $$\frac{\partial h_T}{\partial h_t} = \prod_{k=t+1}^{T} \frac{\partial h_k}{\partial h_{k-1}} = \prod_{k=t+1}^{T} \operatorname{diag}\big(\phi'(z_k)\big)\,W_h.$$

Taking norms, $\left\|\partial h_T/\partial h_t\right\| \le \big(\|W_h\|\cdot\max\phi'\big)^{T-t}$.

| per-step factor | $T-t = 20$ | $T-t = 100$ |
|---|---|---|
| 0.9 | 0.12 | $2.7\times10^{-5}$ |
| 1.0 | 1 | 1 |
| 1.1 | 6.7 | $1.4\times10^{4}$ |

**The condition for neither is $\|W_h\|\max\phi' = 1$ exactly**, which is a measure-zero knife edge. With tanh, $\max\phi' = 1$ only at zero and falls away fast, so the realistic regime is decay — and an RNN's effective memory is a few dozen steps regardless of how long you train it.

> **Gradient clipping.** If $\|g\| > \tau$, rescale $g \leftarrow \tau g/\|g\|$.

Clipping preserves the gradient's *direction* and caps its magnitude. It fully solves explosion and does nothing for vanishing, which is the asymmetry that makes the two problems so different in practice.

> **Truncated BPTT.** Process the sequence in chunks of $k$ steps, carrying the hidden state forward but detaching it from the graph at each boundary, so gradients flow back at most $k$ steps.

This bounds memory at $O(k)$ rather than $O(T)$ and makes long sequences trainable — at the cost of making dependencies longer than $k$ **unlearnable by construction**. Given that the gradient had already vanished by then, the practical cost is usually small, which is itself a comment on how limited the architecture is.

> **Sequential cost.** A forward pass is $T$ dependent steps, each $O(d^{2})$. Wall-clock time scales with $T$ and cannot be reduced by adding hardware.

Compare: a convolution over the same sequence computes every position simultaneously, and so does self-attention ([3.4](03-04-self-attention-queries-keys-values.md)). **This is the reason transformers displaced RNNs even before their accuracy advantage was clear** — the same compute budget buys far more training on a parallel architecture.

> **Shapes of task.**

| Pattern | Example |
|---|---|
| many-to-one | sentiment of a sentence |
| many-to-many, aligned | part-of-speech tagging |
| one-to-many | image captioning |
| many-to-many, unaligned | translation — needs the encoder–decoder of [3.3](03-03-seq2seq-and-the-attention-mechanism.md) |

## Picture

![An unrolled recurrent network drawn left to right. Four hidden-state boxes are connected by horizontal arrows, each labelled with the same recurrent weight matrix. Each box takes an input arrow from below and emits an output arrow above. Annotations note that the same matrix is used at every step so its gradient accumulates one contribution per step, that the gradient from a late step to an early one is multiplied by the recurrent Jacobian once per intervening step, and that the hidden states must be computed strictly in order.](assets/03-01-fig1.svg)

Every horizontal arrow carries the **same** matrix, which is the parameter-sharing that makes the model length-agnostic. Read the diagram backwards and that same repetition becomes the problem: the gradient travelling right to left is multiplied by that matrix once per arrow it crosses.

And note what the horizontal arrows mean for computation: each box needs the one before it. **The diagram is a chain, not a fan**, so there is no way to compute the boxes in parallel — which is precisely what [3.4](03-04-self-attention-queries-keys-values.md) changes.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 — a two-step recurrence by hand, forwards and backwards.**

Take $d = 1$, $\phi = $ identity for clarity, $W_h = 0.5$, $W_x = 2$, $b = 0$, $h_0 = 0$, inputs $x_1 = 1$, $x_2 = 3$, and loss $L = \tfrac12(h_2 - y)^{2}$ with target $y = 4$.

*Forward.* $h_1 = 0.5(0) + 2(1) = 2$, and $h_2 = 0.5(2) + 2(3) = 7$. Loss $= \tfrac12(7-4)^{2} = 4.5$.

*Backward.* $\bar h_2 = h_2 - y = 3$.

$W_h$ is used at both steps, so its gradient has two contributions:

- **Step 2, directly:** $\partial h_2/\partial W_h = h_1 = 2$, contributing $3 \times 2 = 6$.
- **Step 1, through $h_1$:** $\bar h_1 = \bar h_2 \cdot W_h = 3(0.5) = 1.5$, and $\partial h_1/\partial W_h = h_0 = 0$, contributing $1.5 \times 0 = 0$.

$$\bar W_h = 6 + 0 = 6.$$

And for $W_x$, also used twice: step 2 gives $\bar h_2 x_2 = 3(3) = 9$; step 1 gives $\bar h_1 x_1 = 1.5(1) = 1.5$. So $\bar W_x = 10.5$.

Check analytically: $h_2 = W_h(W_x x_1) + W_x x_2$, so $\partial h_2/\partial W_h = W_x x_1 = 2$ and $\partial h_2/\partial W_x = W_h x_1 + x_2 = 0.5 + 3 = 3.5$. Multiplying by $\bar h_2 = 3$ gives 6 and 10.5. Both match.

**Notice the $\bar h_1 = 1.5$ against $\bar h_2 = 3$** — one step back and the gradient has halved, because $W_h = 0.5$. Extend to 100 steps and that halving compounds into $2^{-100}$.

**Example 2 — how far back an RNN can actually see.**

A language model must learn that the verb in "The **keys** to the cabinet in the hallway **are** on the table" agrees with *keys*, not *hallway* — a dependency spanning 8 tokens. Take $\|W_h\|\max\phi' = 0.8$, a realistic value for a trained tanh RNN.

| Distance | Gradient factor |
|---|---|
| 5 | 0.33 |
| 10 | 0.11 |
| 20 | 0.012 |
| 50 | $1.4\times10^{-5}$ |
| 100 | $2.0\times10^{-10}$ |

At distance 8 the factor is about 0.17 — attenuated but present, so this dependency is learnable. At distance 50 the gradient is $10^{-5}$ of its value at the loss, competing against gradients from *nearby* tokens that arrive at full strength.

**That competition is the real mechanism.** It is not that the long-range gradient is zero; it is that it is swamped. The model can always reduce its loss faster by improving its use of the last few tokens, so that is where gradient descent spends the capacity, and the long-range structure is never learned even though it is representable.

The consequence for the field: RNNs work well on tasks with local structure and fail on ones needing genuine long-range reference. Gating ([3.2](03-02-lstms-grus-and-vanishing-gradients.md)) pushes the usable range from tens to hundreds of steps by giving the gradient an additive path — the same idea as [2.3](02-03-deep-architectures-and-residual-connections.md)'s residual connection, applied along time. Attention ([3.3](03-03-seq2seq-and-the-attention-mechanism.md)) removes the distance dependence altogether by giving every position a direct connection to every other.

## Watch out

- You might think backpropagation through time is a distinct algorithm. It is ordinary reverse-mode autodiff on the unrolled graph, with the shared-weight accumulation of [1.3](01-03-backpropagation-and-automatic-differentiation.md). Treating it as special obscures that the same rule governs convolution.
- You might think gradient clipping addresses vanishing gradients. It addresses only explosion. Clipping caps a gradient's size; it cannot manufacture one that has decayed to $10^{-5}$.
- You might think a bigger hidden state extends the memory. It increases capacity per step and does nothing about the exponent: the gradient still decays as $\|W_h\|^{T-t}$, and the fix has to change the *form* of the recurrence, not its size.

## One-liner

> One matrix applied at every time step makes the model length-agnostic and makes its gradient a matrix power, which either vanishes or explodes — and the steps cannot be computed in parallel either.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢)** An RNN has input dimension $n = 100$ and hidden dimension $d = 256$, with an output layer to 10 classes. (a) Give the parameter count of the recurrent layer and of the output layer. (b) State how the recurrent count changes if the training sequences go from length 20 to length 2,000. (c) State how the *training cost* changes.

**P2 (🟡)** A trained RNN has $\|W_h\|\max\phi' = 0.85$. (a) Give the gradient attenuation at distances 10, 30 and 100 steps. (b) The team needs dependencies at distance 80 and proposes doubling the hidden size. Evaluate the proposal. (c) Name two changes that would genuinely help and say what each does to the recursion.

**P3 (🔴, optional)** A team trains on sequences of length 10,000 and runs out of memory. (a) Explain why memory scales with $T$, referring to what the backward pass needs. (b) They adopt truncated BPTT with $k = 100$. State what this fixes, what it costs, and why the cost may be smaller than it sounds. (c) Give the one thing that must still be carried across chunk boundaries, and what must not be.

<details>
<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** (a) Recurrent layer: $W_x$ is $256\times100$, $W_h$ is $256\times256$, $b$ is $256$:

$$25{,}600 + 65{,}536 + 256 = \mathbf{91{,}392}.$$

Output layer: $256 \times 10 + 10 = \mathbf{2{,}570}$.

(b) **It does not change at all.** The parameter count is $dn + d^{2} + d$, in which $T$ does not appear — the same matrices are reused at every step. This is the defining property of the architecture and the direct analogue of convolution's independence from image size ([2.1](02-01-the-convolution-operation.md)).

(c) Training cost changes a great deal. **Compute** is $O(Td^{2})$ per sequence, so it rises by a factor of 100. **Memory** rises by the same factor, because the backward pass needs every hidden state retained ([1.3](01-03-backpropagation-and-automatic-differentiation.md)). And **wall-clock time** rises by 100 in a way extra hardware cannot fix, since the steps are sequential.

**P2** (a) Attenuation is $0.85^{T-t}$:

| Distance | Factor |
|---|---|
| 10 | $0.85^{10} = 0.197$ |
| 30 | $0.85^{30} = 7.6\times10^{-3}$ |
| 100 | $0.85^{100} = 8.7\times10^{-8}$ |

(b) **The proposal does not help.** Doubling $d$ from 256 to 512 changes the model's capacity per step and the cost per step; it does not change the *form* of the gradient, which is still $\prod \operatorname{diag}(\phi')W_h$ — a product of $T-t$ factors.

If anything the larger matrix is likely to have a **larger** spectral norm at initialization unless rescaled, which moves the problem toward explosion rather than away from decay. At distance 80 the factor is $0.85^{80} = 2.3\times10^{-6}$ either way.

The general principle: **the exponent is set by the distance and the base by the recurrence; changing the size changes neither.**

(c) Two changes that genuinely help:

- **Gating** — an LSTM or GRU ([3.2](03-02-lstms-grus-and-vanishing-gradients.md)). The cell state's update is *additive*, $c_t = f_t \odot c_{t-1} + i_t \odot \tilde c_t$, so the recursion's Jacobian along the cell path is $\operatorname{diag}(f_t)$ rather than a full matrix product. With the forget gate near 1 the factor is near 1 per step, so the product decays slowly instead of geometrically. **This changes the recursion from multiplicative to nearly additive** — the same trick as [2.3](02-03-deep-architectures-and-residual-connections.md)'s residual connection, applied along time rather than depth.
- **Attention** ([3.3](03-03-seq2seq-and-the-attention-mechanism.md), [3.4](03-04-self-attention-queries-keys-values.md)). Give position $T$ a direct weighted connection to position $t$, so the gradient path has length 1 regardless of distance. **This removes the product entirely** rather than improving its base, which is why it worked where every incremental fix had failed.

A third, weaker option: an **orthogonal or unitary parameterization** of $W_h$, constraining $\|W_h\| = 1$ so the product neither grows nor shrinks. It works in principle and sits on the knife edge in practice, and it has not displaced gating.

**P3** (a) The backward pass needs every intermediate value that the forward pass used ([1.3](01-03-backpropagation-and-automatic-differentiation.md)). For an RNN unrolled to length $T$ that means all $T$ hidden states — and all $T$ pre-activations — must be retained from the forward pass until the backward sweep reaches them. Memory is therefore $O(T \cdot d \cdot B)$ for batch size $B$, and at $T = 10{,}000$ that is 500 times the footprint of a 20-step sequence.

(b) **What it fixes:** memory falls from $O(T)$ to $O(k)$, a hundredfold reduction at $k=100$, and the backward pass becomes short and fast.

**What it costs:** gradients flow back at most $k$ steps, so any dependency longer than 100 steps is **unlearnable by construction** — not merely hard, but structurally excluded.

**Why the cost may be smaller than it sounds:** the gradient had already vanished. With a per-step factor of 0.85, the gradient at distance 100 is $8.7\times10^{-8}$ of its value at the loss — indistinguishable from zero against the gradients arriving from nearby steps. Truncation discards a signal the architecture was not going to use. That is a real defence of the technique and also a damning statement about the architecture: **it is safe to throw away long-range gradients precisely because they were never going to teach anything.**

(c) **The hidden state must be carried** across the boundary, so the forward computation is unbroken and the model's *representation* of the distant past still influences the present. Truncation limits the flow of gradients, not of information.

**The graph must not be carried**: the hidden state has to be detached from the autodiff graph at each boundary. Failing to detach is the classic bug — memory grows without bound as the graph accumulates across chunks, and the run dies with an out-of-memory error some way into the first epoch, which looks like a capacity problem and is not.

</details>

## Flashback

**From Lesson 2.1 (the convolution operation):** Convolution shares one kernel across spatial positions and an RNN shares one matrix across time steps. (a) State the corresponding gradient rule in each case. (b) Give the one structural difference that makes the RNN's version dangerous and the convolution's version harmless. (c) Name a third architecture that shares parameters across position and say what it shares.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

(a) **Both are the same rule**: a parameter used at many places accumulates one gradient contribution per use, summed ([1.3](01-03-backpropagation-and-automatic-differentiation.md)).

For convolution, $\bar W = \sum_{i,j} \bar Y[i,j]\,X[\text{patch at } i,j]$ — one term per output position. For the RNN, $\bar W_h = \sum_{t} \bar h_t\,h_{t-1}^{\top}$ — one term per time step.

(b) The structural difference is **whether the uses are independent or chained**.

A convolution's positions are computed in **parallel from the same input**: the value at position $(i,j)$ does not depend on the value at $(i-1,j)$. So the kernel's gradient is a sum of terms that each travelled one layer, and no term is a product of many Jacobians.

An RNN's steps are **chained**: $h_t$ depends on $h_{t-1}$, which depends on $h_{t-2}$. So the contribution from step $t$ to a loss at step $T$ has passed through the recurrence $T-t$ times, and each term in the sum carries a factor $\|W_h\|^{T-t}$. **The sum is the same; the terms are exponentially unequal**, and that inequality is the vanishing-gradient problem.

Put compactly: convolution shares a parameter across a *fan*, an RNN shares it across a *chain*. Depth in a convolutional network does create a chain, of course — which is exactly why [2.3](02-03-deep-architectures-and-residual-connections.md) was needed, and why the fix there and the fix in [3.2](03-02-lstms-grus-and-vanishing-gradients.md) are the same idea.

(c) **Self-attention** ([3.4](03-04-self-attention-queries-keys-values.md)) shares the query, key and value **projection matrices** across all positions — the same three matrices applied to every token.

What is different is that the *mixing* between positions is not a fixed learned kernel or a fixed recurrence but a **content-based weighting** computed from the data at run time. So attention shares the machinery for deciding what to attend to, while the attention pattern itself varies per input. And, crucially for this lesson, every position connects to every other in one step, so the gradient path length between any two positions is 1 rather than their distance.

</details>

## Connections

- **Backward:** backpropagation through time is [1.3](01-03-backpropagation-and-automatic-differentiation.md)'s shared-weight accumulation with $T$ terms, and parameter sharing across time is the exact analogue of [2.1](02-01-the-convolution-operation.md)'s sharing across space.
- **Forward:** [3.2](03-02-lstms-grus-and-vanishing-gradients.md) replaces the multiplicative recurrence with a gated additive one, reusing [2.3](02-03-deep-architectures-and-residual-connections.md)'s insight along the time axis; [3.3](03-03-seq2seq-and-the-attention-mechanism.md) and [3.4](03-04-self-attention-queries-keys-values.md) abandon recurrence entirely.
- **Sideways:** the recurrence $h_t = \phi(W_h h_{t-1} + W_x x_t)$ is a nonlinear state-space system, the discrete-time model of [`signals-systems` 4.3](../../signals-systems/lessons/04-03-difference-equations-realizations.md), and $\|W_h\| < 1$ is its stability condition; the same eigenvalue-magnitude criterion decides stability of linear maps in [`dynamical-systems` 1.3](../../dynamical-systems/lessons/01-03-trace-determinant-classification.md).
