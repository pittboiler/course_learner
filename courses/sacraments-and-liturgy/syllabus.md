# Sacraments & Liturgy — Syllabus

> Catholic Theology · Tier 2 · ~25 lessons · Prereqs: [`ecclesiology-and-mariology`](../ecclesiology-and-mariology/syllabus.md) · Roadmap id: `sacraments-and-liturgy`

## Goal

This course teaches what the Catholic Church holds about the seven sacraments and about the liturgy in which they are celebrated — from within the tradition, with the weight of every claim marked, and with the disputed parts left disputed. It begins with the sacramental principle itself, because almost every error downstream is an error here: what a sign that causes what it signifies actually is, what matter, form, minister and intention each contribute, what *ex opere operato* does and does not claim, and why the Church counts seven and not five or thirty. You will finish able to take any sacrament and state its matter, form, minister, recipient, effects and the defining texts, marking each claim's level; to run the *sacramentum tantum / res et sacramentum / res tantum* analysis on a concrete celebration and say what each layer picks out; to read Trent Session XIII on the real presence and Session XXII on the sacrifice of the Mass closely enough to say what each canon anathematizes and what it leaves open; to state transubstantiation in its own metaphysical vocabulary and say precisely what the doctrine is and is not committed to about Aristotle; to explain penance in its three acts and indulgences as a practice with its actual conditions; to lay out the three degrees of Holy Orders and state the reservation of priestly ordination at exactly the level the Church assigns it, no higher and no lower; to state matrimony's sacramentality and indissolubility, tell a declaration of nullity from a dissolution, and lay out the pastoral debates of the last forty years with each side at full strength; and then to say what the liturgy *is* before saying what it looks like — how the Roman Rite came to have the shape it has, what the Eastern rites are, what the twentieth-century reform did, and which of the arguments about that reform are settled and which are not.

It deliberately skips a great deal that neighbours own. **It does not argue against other traditions.** The Eucharistic, penitential, sacerdotal and matrimonial disputes with the Reformation traditions and with Orthodoxy — real presence against receptionism, the Mass as sacrifice, the validity of Anglican orders, the Orthodox practice of *oikonomia* in second marriages — are argued in `apologetics-catholic-claims`, which takes this course as a prerequisite for exactly that reason. Here those positions are **stated as their own best defenders state them, and left standing**, on the rule [`ecclesiology-and-mariology`](../ecclesiology-and-mariology/syllabus.md) established and this course inherits. The events — Lateran IV, Constance, Trent, Vatican II, the post-conciliar decades — belong to [`church-history`](../church-history/syllabus.md), which is cited, not retold. Grace and justification as doctrine belong to [`creation-grace-last-things`](../creation-grace-last-things/syllabus.md); a sacrament here is an instrumental cause of the grace that course describes. The Church as the sacrament of salvation, who belongs to her and who may receive, belong to [`ecclesiology-and-mariology`](../ecclesiology-and-mariology/syllabus.md). The levels of doctrinal authority belong to [`fundamental-theology`](../fundamental-theology/syllabus.md). It also skips canon law as a discipline (procedure appears only where a doctrine is unintelligible without it), sacred music and church architecture as arts, the liturgies of the Reformation traditions for their own sake, and any survey of comparative ritual studies.

## How practice works in this course

Problems follow [HUMANITIES-BUILD-BRIEF.md](../../HUMANITIES-BUILD-BRIEF.md), with the Catholic Theology stance: taught from within the tradition, every claim carrying its level from [`fundamental-theology`](../fundamental-theology/syllabus.md) 4.4. This course's characteristic discipline is that **sacramental theology has an unusually sharp line between what is defined and what is a school's explanation of it, and the defined side is short.** That Christ is truly, really and substantially present under the species is defined; the Aristotelian machinery by which Aquinas explains how accidents persist is not. That the Mass is a true and propitiatory sacrifice is defined; the twentieth-century accounts of *how* the one sacrifice is made present are not. That there are seven sacraments instituted by Christ is defined; that each was instituted by an identifiable act during the public ministry is not. Lessons keep the two columns visibly apart, and problems test whether the student can.

- **Close reading (archetype 7), exegetical and strict, is the workhorse**, on public-domain texts only: Trent in Waterworth's translation (1848) — above all Session VII on the sacraments in general, Session XIII on the Eucharist, Session XIV on penance and extreme unction, Session XXI on communion under both kinds, Session XXII on the sacrifice of the Mass, Session XXIII on order and Session XXIV on matrimony; Aquinas in the English Dominican Province translation (*Summa Theologiae* III qq.60-65 and qq.73-83, and the Supplement where the *Summa* breaks off); the Didache, Ignatius, Justin's *First Apology*, Irenaeus and Cyprian in the *Ante-Nicene Fathers*; Cyril of Jerusalem's *Mystagogical Catecheses* and Ambrose's *On the Mysteries* in the *Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers*; Augustine on the word joined to the element (*Tractates on the Gospel of John*); scripture in the Douay-Rheims (Challoner), with the lesson's own translation where a Greek or Hebrew word decides a reading (*mysterion*, *anamnesis*, *eucharistia*, *epiklesis*, *presbyteros*, *cheirotonia*, *oikonomia*). **Every conciliar text after Trent, every modern papal and curial document — *Mediator Dei*, *Sacrosanctum Concilium*, *Mysterium Fidei*, *Indulgentiarum Doctrina*, *Ordinatio Sacerdotalis*, *Familiaris Consortio*, *Amoris Laetitia*, *Summorum Pontificum*, *Traditionis Custodes*, *Desiderio Desideravi* and the instructions — is paraphrased and cited by name**, never quoted from a copyrighted translation.
- **Apply to a hard case (archetype 3), exegetical then evaluative,** is the second workhorse and the one this course exists for. Given a celebration described in detail, decide whether the sacrament was valid, whether it was licit, and whether it was fruitful, and say which of matter, form, minister, intention, recipient or rite decides. Cases include: a baptism using "we baptize you"; an emergency baptism by a layperson who does not believe; a confirmation by a presbyter without a faculty; a Mass at which the priest alters the words of consecration; a general absolution given outside the conditions; a marriage between two baptized non-Catholics; a marriage attempted without canonical form. The three verdicts are kept apart; collapsing them is the error the rubric names most often.
- **Find the crux (archetype 5), exegetical.** Paschasius against Ratramnus; Berengarius against the formula he was made to sign; the Council of Constance against the Utraquists on the cup; Luther and Trent on whether the Mass is a sacrifice; Aquinas against Scotus on how orders are conferred; *Inter Insigniores* against the arguments its critics make; continuity against rupture in the liturgical reform.
- **Diagnose (archetype 4), exegetical.** Invented parish bulletins, homilies, catechist's handouts, wedding-preparation leaflets and traditionalist and progressive blog posts that get the sacramental theology wrong — "the Eucharist is a symbol of Christ's presence in the community"; "*ex opere operato* means the sacraments work like magic"; "an annulment is a Catholic divorce"; "the Mass repeats the sacrifice of Calvary"; "the pope could ordain women tomorrow if he wanted"; "Vatican II abolished the Latin Mass"; "Paul VI's Missal is invalid". Invented material is never attributed to a real person.
- **Reconstruct (archetype 1), exegetical.** Aquinas on why a sacrament must be a sign that causes (III q.60, q.62); on the conversion of the whole substance (III q.75); on why the accidents remain without a subject (III q.77); Trent's reasoning in the chapters that precede each set of canons.

**Authority is graded strictly, and this course's overstatements are specific.** The defined content of Session XIII against the explanatory apparatus around it; that the Church's power over the sacraments extends to the manner of their dispensation but not to their substance; that the reservation of priestly ordination is taught as definitively to be held and that the CDF's 1995 response says it has been infallibly proposed by the ordinary and universal magisterium — and that whether that response settles the level is itself discussed by Catholic theologians; that celibacy in the Latin Church is discipline and not doctrine; that the validity and lawfulness of the reformed Roman Missal is not an open question, while prudential judgements about the reform's execution are. Overstating a level and understating one are both errors.

**Evaluative parts, verdict-neutral, are reserved for questions genuinely open among Catholics:** whether the institution of each sacrament by Christ was immediate or mediate; whether attrition suffices with the sacrament; whether transubstantiation is intelligible outside an Aristotelian account of substance; which twentieth-century account of how the one sacrifice becomes present is best; the right order and age of the sacraments of initiation; whether the diaconate could be opened to women; the disputed readings of *Amoris Laetitia* ch. 8; and nearly everything about the liturgical reform's execution.

## Dangerous Checklist

When you finish, you can:

- [ ] Say what a sacrament is on the tradition's own definition — a sign that effects what it signifies — and run the *sacramentum tantum / res et sacramentum / res tantum* analysis on any of the seven, naming what each layer picks out
- [ ] Take a described celebration and return three separate verdicts — valid, licit, fruitful — saying which of matter, form, minister, intention, recipient or rite decides each, and why the Donatist principle settles the minister's holiness but not his intention
- [ ] State what *ex opere operato* claims and what it denies, and what a sacramental character is, is not, and does in baptism, confirmation and orders
- [ ] Say how the number seven was arrived at, what Lyons II, Florence and Trent each did with it, and what "instituted by Christ" is and is not committed to
- [ ] State for each of the seven: matter, form, minister, recipient, effects, and the text that fixes each, with the level of every claim
- [ ] Read Trent Session XIII closely enough to say what "truly, really and substantially" excludes, what the word transubstantiation is said to do, and what the decree does not attempt to explain — and state transubstantiation in the vocabulary of substance and accident without smuggling in claims the doctrine does not make
- [ ] Explain in what sense the Mass is a sacrifice, using Trent Session XXII, without either repeating Calvary or reducing the Mass to a memorial, and lay out the main twentieth-century accounts of how the one sacrifice is made present
- [ ] Say what Trent Session XXI taught about communion under both kinds, what the doctrine of concomitance contributes, and what the Church claims power over in the dispensation of the sacraments
- [ ] Explain penance in its three acts and its form, tell contrition from attrition and say what is settled about each, state the seal without qualification, and explain what an indulgence is, what its conditions actually are, and how one is gained
- [ ] Lay out the three degrees of Holy Orders and their matter and form, and state the reservation of priestly ordination at exactly its level — naming the arguments given for it, the objections Catholic theologians have raised about its level, and what remains genuinely open about the diaconate
- [ ] State matrimony's sacramentality, ends and properties; distinguish a declaration of nullity from a dissolution and both from divorce; name the grounds and the privileges; and lay out the debate over *Amoris Laetitia* ch. 8 with each reading at full strength
- [ ] Say what the liturgy is before saying what it looks like — Christ's priestly act, the Church's public worship, *lex orandi lex credendi* — and explain what *participatio actuosa* does and does not ask
- [ ] Navigate the liturgical year and the Liturgy of the Hours: the temporal and sanctoral cycles, the lectionary, the structure of the office, and the two psalm numberings
- [ ] Trace the Roman Rite from the Roman Canon to the Missal of Pius V to the Missal of Paul VI, name the major Eastern rites and the Eastern Catholic churches, and say what a rite is
- [ ] State the twentieth-century reform's mandate, what it actually changed, and the arguments for and against its execution with each side at full strength — separating what is settled from what is not, and giving the legal history from the indults to *Traditionis Custodes*
- [ ] Say what a sacramental is, how it differs from a sacrament, and on what criteria the Church judges a popular devotion

Before any of the seven, the thing they have in common. This module is the course's grammar: almost every later error is a mistake made here and carried forward.

**Boss problem 7:** (a) State, in paraphrase, what *Sacrosanctum Concilium* mandates about the revision of the rites, about Latin and the vernacular, and about active participation. Then list four concrete features of the Missal of Paul VI and say, for each, whether the constitution mandates it, permits it, or is silent about it. *(Exegetical, strict.)* (b) Assign a level and say what assent each asks: *Sacrosanctum Concilium*; *Mediator Dei*; the apostolic constitution promulgating the Missal of Paul VI; *Summorum Pontificum*; *Traditionis Custodes*; *Redemptionis Sacramentum*. Use [`fundamental-theology`](../fundamental-theology/syllabus.md) 4.5, and say in one sentence each what kind of claim a pastoral constitution, a motu proprio and a curial instruction can and cannot make. *(Exegetical, strict.)* (c) Diagnose two invented blog posts, written for this problem and attributed to no one: the first argues that "the Missal of Paul VI is a fabrication with no claim on Catholics, and the 1570 Missal was canonized in perpetuity by *Quo Primum*"; the second argues that "Vatican II abolished Latin, made the older rites illegal, and turned the Mass into a community meal, so any nostalgia for the old rite is disobedience." For each, name every factual and every authority-level error, and give the correct statement. *(Exegetical, strict.)* (d) In 400-500 words, any verdict: was the liturgical reform of the 1960s a legitimate development of the Roman Rite? Separate the three questions that get run together — whether the Church had authority to reform, whether the reform as executed followed *Sacrosanctum Concilium*, and whether particular changes were prudent — and answer each. Apply at least two of Newman's notes ([`fundamental-theology`](../fundamental-theology/syllabus.md) 5.2), state the strongest case against your verdict, and concede what the record makes hard for your side. Denying the validity or lawfulness of the reformed Missal, or denying that any criticism of the reform is legitimate, fails the part regardless of the rest. *(Evaluative.)*

## Notes

- **Stance.** Taught from within the tradition, with authority marked, and historically honest. The hard things are said as hard: that the number seven was not counted before the twelfth century and that the Church holds it was always there; that a Roman synod made Berengarius sign a formula so crude that the tradition itself had to refine it twenty years later; that the West separated confirmation from baptism for reasons of episcopal availability and has been explaining the result ever since; that indulgences were sold, and that Trent said so before Luther's critics did; that the Church withheld the cup from the laity for centuries and then restored it; that the liturgical reform of the 1960s was carried out faster and more extensively than most council fathers expected, and that serious Catholics have argued about it ever since. None of these is softened before it is stated, and none is stated to score a point.
- **The no-argument rule, inherited.** This course states other traditions' positions and does not argue with them, on exactly the rule [`ecclesiology-and-mariology`](../ecclesiology-and-mariology/syllabus.md) set and for the same reason: `apologetics-catholic-claims` takes both courses as prerequisites so that it can argue from a base where the Catholic doctrine has been taught properly and the alternatives have been stated without a reply attached. Problems here have **no apologetic parts**. Where a rubric asks for another tradition's position — Zwingli and Calvin on the supper, the Lutheran confessions on the Mass as sacrifice, the Baptist objection to infant baptism, the Orthodox on *oikonomia*, the arguments other communions give for ordaining women — it grades fidelity alone, and names "volunteering a rebuttal" as a wrong turn.
- **Authority discipline for this course's hardest claims.** (1) Session XIII defines the real presence and says the Church suitably and properly calls the conversion transubstantiation; it does not canonize Aristotle, and lessons say so in the same breath. (2) Session XXII defines that the Mass is a true and propitiatory sacrifice; every account of *how* the one sacrifice is made present is theology, and none is magisterial. (3) *Ordinatio Sacerdotalis* is taught as definitively to be held, and the 1995 CDF response says it has been infallibly proposed by the ordinary and universal magisterium; lessons state both, state that the level itself is discussed by Catholic theologians, and adjudicate nothing. (4) The female diaconate is open in a way the priesthood is not; lessons keep the two questions apart and never let an answer to one settle the other. (5) Celibacy in the Latin Church is discipline. (6) The validity and lawfulness of the reformed Roman Missal, and the Church's authority to reform her rites, are not open questions; the prudential assessment of the reform is entirely open, and lessons hold both halves at once. (7) *Amoris Laetitia* ch. 8 is currently read in incompatible ways by Catholics in good standing; lessons present the readings and do not pick. (8) Sacramentals do not confer grace *ex opere operato*, and saying they do is an exegetical error graded strictly.
- **Accuracy: verify before writing each lesson; none of these should be asserted from memory.** Session and canon numbers, and the exact wording in Waterworth, for every Trent citation above; DH numbers for Trent, Florence, Lateran IV and the Berengarian oaths; the canon number of Lateran IV's precept of annual confession (named above without one); the year and exact title of Florence's Decree for the Armenians; the dates of the Berengarian formulas; the titles and dates of Pius X's decrees on sacred music, frequent communion and the age of first communion; the apostolic constitutions revising the forms of confirmation (1971) and anointing (1972) and of the Liturgy of the Hours (1970), each named above without full confidence in title or date; article numbers in *Sacrosanctum Concilium* (used above only where a lesson will verify them, and otherwise paraphrased without a number); the date and precise content of the 2020 CDF response on the baptismal formula; the exact operative sentence of *Ordinatio Sacerdotalis* and of the 1995 CDF response; the years and remits of the 2016 and 2020 commissions on the women's diaconate and the title of the ITC's 2002 study; the title, issuing body and date of the 2001 guidelines on the Anaphora of Addai and Mari; the year of the Directory on Popular Piety and of the revised rite of exorcism; the article numbers of *Familiaris Consortio* 84 and of *Amoris Laetitia* ch. 8; all Catechism paragraph numbers. Where a number cannot be confirmed, the document is named without it — the rule the whole field follows.
- **Ownership collisions found and resolved.** (1) [`theology-of-debt`](../theology-of-debt/syllabus.md) 3.5-3.6 owns the treasury of the Church, Trent Session XXV and Luther's objection as *debt theology*, and its scope table cedes indulgences as *practice* here. 4.2 therefore teaches only the practice — definition, kinds, conditions, the *Enchiridion*, application to the dead — and cites its 3.5-3.6 for the doctrine rather than restating it; its Boss 3 already runs the Lutheran objection as an apologetic part, and this course does not repeat that exercise. (2) [`metaphysics`](../metaphysics/syllabus.md) 2.1 and [`thomistic-synthesis`](../thomistic-synthesis/syllabus.md) 2.1 both cede transubstantiation by name and both flag the bridge in their Notes; 3.3 crosses it, owns the doctrine, and takes the general theory of substance from them without re-teaching it — including the question [`metaphysics`](../metaphysics/syllabus.md)'s Notes raise and decline to answer, which becomes Boss 3 (d). (3) [`moral-theology`](../moral-theology/syllabus.md) cedes penance, the seal, marriage as sacrament, indissolubility and nullity here, and keeps *Amoris Laetitia* ch. 8 only as a conscience question (its 2.5); 5.4 owns the sacramental and disciplinary question and cites its conscience apparatus. (4) [`creation-grace-last-things`](../creation-grace-last-things/syllabus.md) cedes baptism and penance as sacraments here and hands over its Module 4 as the doctrine of the grace a sacrament causes; 2.2 cites its 7.3 on limbo rather than opening it, and 4.1 cites [`theology-of-debt`](../theology-of-debt/syllabus.md) 3.4 for the debt of punishment. (5) [`ecclesiology-and-mariology`](../ecclesiology-and-mariology/syllabus.md) cedes the seven sacraments, Holy Orders as a sacrament, the Eucharist as the act that makes the Church and the reservation of priestly ordination here; 3.5 completes its 3.2, 1.2 works out its 2.2, and 5.1 begins from its 3.1 and 3.3. Who may be admitted to communion is **its** 5.2 and is cited, not re-argued. (6) [`church-history`](../church-history/syllabus.md) cedes transubstantiation at Lateran IV and Trent, and the Tridentine and Pauline missals, here by name. (7) [`new-testament`](../new-testament/syllabus.md) marks "sacrament ceded" on John 6, 1 Corinthians 11 and James 5:14, and [`old-testament`](../old-testament/syllabus.md) cedes the Psalms in the liturgy; 6.2 is where that lands. (8) [`patristics`](../patristics/syllabus.md) reads the Didache, Ignatius, Justin, Cyril and Ambrose as texts and sends their sacramental doctrine here; this course does not repeat its Boss 1 exercise on church offices, and reads Justin's liturgical chapters and Cyril's mystagogy, which it does not. (9) No built lesson anywhere in the library touches a sacramental or liturgical topic.
- **Where this goes next.** `apologetics-catholic-claims` takes this course whole: Module 3 for the Eucharistic disputes, 4.1-4.2 for confession and indulgences, 5.1 for orders and *Apostolicae Curae*, 5.4 for divorce and remarriage, and every fair statement of another tradition's position as its starting material.
- **Lesson count.** The roadmap estimated ~22; the syllabus runs 25 (+14%, inside the band). The Eucharist takes five lessons because the witness, the controversies, the real presence, the sacrifice and the sacrament outside Mass are five separate skills and collapsing any pair is how this subject is usually taught badly. The liturgy takes six because the steering names six distinct topics and each carries its own texts. Merges already made to hold the line: baptism and confirmation share one module of three rather than four; anointing shares the healing module rather than standing alone with a history lesson; orders takes two lessons only because the reservation question needs a lesson to itself to be handled at the right level; the liturgical year and the Liturgy of the Hours share a lesson, as do language and *ars celebrandi*. If a lesson overflows when built, split it and merge elsewhere rather than grow past 26.
