# Sacraments & Liturgy · Lesson 1.3: *Ex opere operato*, character, and fruitfulness

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 1: The sacramental principle · Builds on: [1.1 What a sacrament is](01-01-what-a-sacrament-is.md), [1.2 Matter, form, minister, intention](01-02-matter-form-minister-intention.md), [`patristics` 4.4](../../patristics/lessons/04-04-augustine-against-the-donatists.md) · Unlocks: [1.4 Why seven](01-04-why-seven.md), [2.1 Baptism](02-01-baptism-the-rite-and-its-effects.md), [7.3 Sacramentals](07-03-sacramentals-and-popular-devotion.md)

## Why this matters

[1.2](01-02-matter-form-minister-intention.md) said when a sacrament happens. This lesson asks what it does, and for whom. The Catholic answer carries a Latin tag, *ex opere operato*, caricatured from both sides: by critics as magic, by some Catholics as a guarantee that asks nothing of them. Two ideas ride with it into every later module: the *character* that makes three sacraments unrepeatable, and the line between valid and fruitful.

## The idea

**Two questions about any sacrament.** Whose act makes it work? And what does it do for this person?

The schools answered the first with a phrase, coined around 1200 (Peter of Poitiers is usually credited). A sacrament works [*ex opere operato*](../reference.md#ex-opere-operato), "from the work worked" (lesson's gloss): from the rite done as Christ instituted it, because Christ acts in it ([1.1](01-01-what-a-sacrament-is.md)). Its partner, [*ex opere operantis*](../reference.md#ex-opere-operantis), "from the work of the one working", is what the minister's or recipient's own faith and devotion contribute; the two are not rivals (*Mediator Dei*, 1947, §36).

So the first question is answered without inspecting anyone's soul: a priest in mortal sin baptizes truly, as Augustine argued against the Donatists ([`patristics` 4.4](../../patristics/lessons/04-04-augustine-against-the-donatists.md); [`ecclesiology-and-mariology` 2.2](../../ecclesiology-and-mariology/lessons/02-02-holy.md)): the minister is the pipe, not the spring.

The second question does look at the recipient. An adult who comes unbelieving, or clinging to grave sin, receives a real sacrament and none of its grace: [valid but unfruitful](../reference.md#valid-licit-fruitful), Augustine's "having" without "profiting". What stands in the way is the [*obex*](../reference.md#obex), the bar (lesson's gloss). Aquinas's image: a heavy body held up falls the moment the support goes, because the tendency was there all along (ST III q.69 a.10). Remove the obstacle and the sacrament already received begins to work. That is [reviviscence](../reference.md#reviviscence).

**What *ex opere operato* does not mean.**

1. **Not magic.** Magic bends a power to the operator's will. Here the binding runs the other way: Christ bound his own action to rites he instituted, and the Church does only what she received.
2. **Not a power in the rite apart from God.** The efficacy flows from Christ's passion (ST III q.62 a.5); the rite is his instrument.
3. **Not "your disposition doesn't matter."** The fruit depends on it (CCC 1128).
4. **Not "the minister's intention doesn't matter."** His *holiness* doesn't; his intention to do what the Church does is required (Trent, Session VII, can. 11; [1.2](01-02-matter-form-minister-intention.md)).

## In their own words

The Lutheran confessions, as they state themselves. The Augsburg Confession (1530), article 13: the sacraments were instituted not only as marks of profession among men but chiefly as signs and testimonies of God's will toward us, to awaken and confirm faith, so they must be used with faith in the promises they offer; it condemns those who teach that they justify *ex opere operato* without requiring that faith (Schaff, *Creeds of Christendom* III). Melanchthon's Apology (1531), article 13, names its target:

> we condemn the whole crowd of scholastic doctors, who teach that the Sacraments confer grace ex opere operato, without a good disposition on the part of the one using them, provided he do not place a hindrance in the way.

(Bente and Dau, *Triglot Concordia*, 1921.) Its reason: the sacraments are signs and seals of God's promises, and a promise profits no one unless received by faith. Luther had argued in *The Babylonian Captivity of the Church* (1520) that not baptism but faith in the word of promise, to which baptism is added, justifies.

None of this makes the sacraments bare symbols. Luther's Small Catechism (1529) says baptism works forgiveness, delivers from death and the devil, and gives everlasting salvation to all who believe. How can water do such great things?

> It is not water, indeed, that does it, but the Word of God which is with and in the water, and faith, which trusts in the Word of God in the water. (Part IV, Schaff's translation)

## The doctrine

Trent's [Session VII](../reference.md#trent-session-vii) (1547), canons on the sacraments in general (Waterworth), with later teaching:

1. **The sacraments contain and confer the grace they signify** on those who place no obstacle. They are not merely outward signs of a grace already received through faith, nor badges that mark believers off from unbelievers (can. 6). As far as God's part goes, grace is given always and to all who receive rightly (can. 7). *In words:* the rite is a cause, not a receipt.
2. **Grace is conferred "through the act performed"** (*ex opere operato*), and faith alone in the divine promise does not suffice to obtain it (can. 8). *In words:* the sacrament gives; faith does not fetch the gift from elsewhere.
3. **In an adult, the fruit depends on disposition.** Session VI ch. 7: justice is received "according to each one's proper disposition and co-operation", and baptism, its instrumental cause, is "the sacrament of faith". An adult's obstacle is unbelief or attachment to mortal sin (ST III q.69 a.9 and ad 3), and mortal sin forfeits grace in any case (VI ch. 15, cans. 27–28; [`moral-theology` 3.4](../../moral-theology/lessons/03-04-sin-mortal-and-venial.md)). *In words:* the act is Christ's; the fruit is received, not extracted.
4. **Baptism, confirmation and order imprint a [character](../reference.md#sacramental-character)**, "a certain spiritual and indelible Sign", on account of which they cannot be repeated (can. 9). One who has once been a priest cannot become a layman again (XXIII can. 4). *In words:* three sacraments mark you for good, whatever you later do.
5. **Each sacrament gives a grace proper to it**: [sacramental grace](../reference.md#sacramental-grace), the Spirit's grace given by Christ and proper to each (CCC 1129). Grace itself is [`creation-grace-last-things`](../../creation-grace-last-things/syllabus.md) 4.1–4.3.

**What the character is, on Aquinas's account.** Deputation to worship needs power, active to give and passive to receive; so the character is a spiritual power, a participation in Christ's priesthood (ST III q.63 aa.2–3). Order deputes to confer sacraments, baptism to receive them, confirmation in its way likewise; the Eucharist imprints none, being the end of the others (a.6). It is indelible because Christ's priesthood is eternal (a.5). Aquinas's warrant: God "hath sealed us" (2 Cor 1:22, DR; *sphragizō*, to set a seal, lesson's gloss). Vatican II takes the core without the metaphysics: the baptismal character destines the faithful for Christian worship (*Lumen Gentium* 11); the Catechism calls it an indelible configuration to Christ (CCC 1121); the Code draws the consequence (can. 845 §1).

So character and grace come apart. Must an adult have right faith to be baptized? For the grace, yes; for the character, no, since

> the sacrament is not perfected by the righteousness of the minister or of the recipient of Baptism, but by the power of God. (ST III q.68 a.8)

**Reviviscence.** One baptized in deceit who later repents is not baptized again; what was given becomes effective once the deceit is gone (Augustine, *On Baptism* I.12.18; Aquinas, III q.69 a.10). No council defined it. Beyond baptism it thins out (see the picture); in penance the penitent's acts are "as it were the matter" (Session XIV can. 4), so the question hardly arises.

| Defined or taught (with level) | A school's explanation (no magisterial level) |
|---|---|
| Grace contained and conferred on those who place no obstacle, *ex opere operato*; faith in the promise alone does not suffice (VII cans. 6–8): **rung 1** | *How* the rite causes grace: Aquinas's instrumental causality (III q.62 a.4); other schools gave other accounts |
| An adult's fruit depends on his disposition (VI ch. 7; *Mediator Dei* 31; CCC 1128): **at least rung 3** | Reviviscence: common teaching for baptism (Augustine, Aquinas); disputed beyond it |
| A character in baptism, confirmation, order; indelible; hence no repetition (VII can. 9; XXIII can. 4): **rung 1** | What the character *is*: a spiritual power in the soul's powers, a participation in Christ's priesthood (III q.63 aa.2–4) |
| A grace proper to each sacrament (CCC 1129): **at least rung 3** | Aquinas: a "Divine assistance in obtaining the end of the sacrament" (III q.62 a.2); whether a new habit, a vigour within sanctifying grace or a claim to actual graces divided the schools |

**Mark the levels** ([`fundamental-theology` 4.4](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-04-the-ladder-of-doctrinal-authority.md); [levels](../reference.md#levels-of-authority)). Session VII cans. 6–9 are **rung 1**, like can. 1 ([`fundamental-theology` 5.1](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/05-01-vincent-of-lerins.md)) and can. 12 (E&M 2.2); so are XXIII can. 4 and the loss of grace by mortal sin (VI ch. 15, cans. 27–28). Canon 9 defines *that* an indelible sign is imprinted and why the three are not repeated, not what kind of thing the character is. The dependence of fruit on disposition (a doctrinal chapter of Trent, *Mediator Dei* 31, CCC 1128), LG 11, CCC 1121 and 1129: **at least rung 3**. Reviviscence of baptism: **rung 4**, common teaching, no magisterial act. Its extension beyond baptism, the nature of character and of sacramental grace, and the mode of causality: **rung 5**. Cann. 290, 845, 916 and 976: **law, no rung**. The Lutheran confessions are stated, not graded.

## Worked examples

**Ex 1 (clean): the roadside absolution.** Invented: Bastian Kroll, a priest returned to the lay state by rescript twelve years ago, stops at a motorway crash, where a dying driver asks for a priest and confesses. *Valid:* the character is indelible (VII can. 9, XXIII can. 4); ordination once validly received never becomes invalid, and what Bastian lost is the clerical state (can. 290). Penance also needs the faculty, which in danger of death the law gives any priest who lacks it (can. 976). *Licit:* yes, by the same canon. *Fruitful:* that turns on the driver's sorrow ([4.1](04-01-penance-the-sacrament-and-its-history.md)), not on Bastian's soul, which nobody can see and *ex opere operato* makes irrelevant.

**Ex 2 (strain): two communicants.** Invented: Ngozi and her brother Chidi receive Communion at the same Mass, both, before God, in mortal sin. Chidi knows it and means to stay as he is. Ngozi confessed a grave sin last month, sincerely but without the sorrow the sacrament needs; she believes herself forgiven and clings to nothing. For both the sacrament is valid: it is Christ they receive ([3.3](03-03-real-presence-and-transubstantiation.md)). Chidi's reception is illicit (can. 916 requires confession first) and unfruitful: conscious mortal sin is an obstacle (ST III q.79 a.3). Of Ngozi's case the same article says that one in mortal sin "of which he is not conscious, and for which he has no attachment", approaching devoutly, receives the charity that perfects contrition, and is forgiven. The strain: the obstacle is the will's conscious attachment, not the bare objective state, and nobody can audit that. Aquinas's verdict is explanation, at no level; the Catechism keeps the order of things, saying the Eucharist is not *ordered* to forgiving mortal sin (CCC 1395). Preparing to receive is [3.5](03-05-communion-reservation-adoration.md)'s.

## Watch out

- **You might think *ex opere operato* means "works no matter what."** It works whatever the minister's holiness, but only if matter, form and intention are there, and it bears fruit in an adult only without an obstacle.
- **You might think valid-but-unfruitful means nothing happened.** In baptism, confirmation and order the character was imprinted, so there is something to revive.
- **Levels, both ways.** Saying Trent defined Aquinas's account of the character, or reviviscence, overstates (canon 9 defines that the sign is imprinted, not what it is; reviviscence is rung 4). Calling the character "a scholastic theory" understates: its existence and indelibility are rung 1.
- **You might think sacramentals work the same way.** They act *ex opere operantis Ecclesiae*, through the Church's prayer (*Mediator Dei* 27; CCC 1670): [7.3](07-03-sacramentals-and-popular-devotion.md).

## One-liner

> A sacrament is Christ's act and waits on no one's holiness; its fruit is received, not extracted; and three of them leave a seal no later choice can erase.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢)** *(Exegetical)* An **invented** transcript from a diocesan radio phone-in, written for this problem:

> **Caller:** My son says *ex opere operato* means the sacraments work like magic. Is he right?
>
> **Host:** Closer than he thinks! It means the rite works all by itself, like a spell. It doesn't matter what the priest intends, as long as the words are right. It doesn't matter how you come either: go up to Communion in serious sin and you get exactly the same grace as your neighbour. Trent defined all of this, including Aquinas's teaching that the character is a power seated in the soul's powers. Holy water and blessed medals work the same way. And a priest who walks away from ministry stops being a priest.

Find **four** errors. For each, give the correction, and give the level wherever a level is involved. 150 words or fewer.

**P2 (🟡)** *(Exegetical (a) · Exegetical (b))* An **invented** case, written for this problem. Emeka Nwosu, 29, asks for baptism because his fiancée's family will not otherwise accept the marriage. He completes the catechumenate, means to receive the sacrament, and is baptized with the approved rite by a priest who suspects nothing. Privately he believes none of it and intends to keep skimming money from his employer. Seven years later he comes to faith, repays what he took, and goes to confession. Aquinas, ST III q.69 a.10, body and replies 2–3 (English Dominican Province):

> ... when a man is baptized, he receives the character, which is like a form; and he receives in consequence its proper effect, which is grace whereby all his sins are remitted. But this effect is sometimes hindered by insincerity. Wherefore, when this obstacle is removed by Penance, Baptism forthwith produces its effect. ... when it is removed, Baptism takes away all guilt, and all debt of punishment due to sins, whether committed before Baptism, or even co-existent with Baptism. ... Baptism as the direct efficient cause, Penance as the indirect cause, i.e. as removing the obstacle. ... when the insincerity passes away, subsequent sins are indeed remitted, but by Penance, not by Baptism. Wherefore they are not remitted, like the sins which preceded Baptism, as to the whole debt of punishment.

(a) Using only the passage, say what Emeka's baptism gave him on the day, what blocked the rest, what removed the block, and which cause remits which of his sins, with what difference. Three sentences.

(b) Give three separate verdicts on the baptism as celebrated (valid, licit, fruitful), naming the deciding element for each. Then give the level of (i) "Emeka must not be baptized again" and (ii) "his baptism now takes its full effect". One sentence each.

**P3 (🔴)** *(Exegetical)* Set Trent, Session VII, canon 8 (Waterworth) beside the Apology passage quoted in **In their own words**:

> If any one saith, that by the said sacraments of the New Law grace is not conferred through the act performed, but that faith alone in the divine promise suffices for the obtaining of grace; let him be anathema.

(a) Name one thing the Apology and Trent (canon 6, with Session VI ch. 7) both hold about an adult who receives a sacrament without the required disposition, and one view of the sacraments that the Augsburg Confession and canon 6 both reject in nearly the same words. Two sentences.

(b) Name the single premise on which they divide, say which side affirms it, and state the Apology's reason for denying it as a Lutheran theologian would sign it. Three sentences.

<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical)*

**Accept:** any four of the six below.

**Must hit, strict:**

- **"Works all by itself, like a spell."** The efficacy is Christ's act through the rite he instituted, by virtue of his passion (CCC 1127–1128), not a technique that bends power to the operator. *Ex opere operato* itself is **rung 1** (Session VII can. 8).
- **"Doesn't matter what the priest intends."** His holiness doesn't matter; his intention at least to do what the Church does is required for validity (VII can. 11, **rung 1**; [1.2](01-02-matter-form-minister-intention.md)).
- **"Same grace in serious sin."** The sacrament is valid, but an adult's obstacle blocks the fruit (VII can. 6, **rung 1**; CCC 1128, **at least rung 3**); conscious mortal sin is such an obstacle (ST III q.79 a.3), and can. 916 forbids receiving so.
- **"Trent defined ... Aquinas's teaching."** Overstated. Canon 9 defines that an indelible character is imprinted (**rung 1**); that it is a power seated in the soul's powers is Aquinas's explanation, at **no magisterial level**.
- **"Holy water and medals the same way."** Sacramentals act *ex opere operantis Ecclesiae*, through the Church's prayer (*Mediator Dei* 27; CCC 1670), not *ex opere operato*.
- **"Stops being a priest."** One once a priest cannot become a layman again (XXIII can. 4, **rung 1**). He can lose the clerical state, but ordination never becomes invalid (can. 290, law).

**Wrong turns:** "correcting" the host by saying the minister's holiness matters, which is the Donatist error (VII can. 12, rung 1); treating "Communion in serious sin" as invalid rather than unfruitful; grading the character's existence as mere school opinion.

**Model answer:** The rite does not work like a spell: its power is Christ's, acting through what he instituted, and *ex opere operato* (VII can. 8) is rung 1. The priest's holiness is irrelevant but his intention to do what the Church does is required (can. 11, rung 1). Communion received in conscious mortal sin is valid but unfruitful, because an adult's obstacle blocks the grace (can. 6; CCC 1128). Trent defined that the character exists and is indelible (can. 9, rung 1), not Aquinas's account of what it is, which has no magisterial level.

---

**P2** *(Exegetical (a) · Exegetical (b))*

**Must hit, strict (a):**

- On the day: the character, "like a form". Grace, its proper effect, was blocked by insincerity.
- Penance removed the obstacle, and then baptism "forthwith" produced its effect, remitting all guilt and all debt of punishment for sins before and during the baptism, as the direct cause; penance is the indirect cause.
- The seven years' sins are remitted by penance, not baptism, and not as to the whole debt of punishment.

**Must hit, strict (b):**

- **Valid:** yes. Matter, form, minister and his intention, and Emeka's intention to receive, were all present; his unbelief does not block the character (ST III q.68 a.8). Deciding element: the recipient's intention to receive.
- **Licit:** yes as celebrated. The approved rite and the required preparation were kept by a minister who could not see the deceit; deciding element: rite and law. Emeka's deceit is his own grave sin, a sacrilegious reception, which bears on the fruit and on his guilt, not on the rite.
- **Fruitful:** no. Deciding element: the recipient's disposition, here unbelief and attachment to grave sin (q.69 a.9).
- **(i)** **Rung 1**: the baptismal character is indelible and baptism is not repeated (VII can. 9).
- **(ii)** **Rung 4**: reviviscence of baptism is common teaching (Augustine, *On Baptism* I.12.18; Aquinas), defined by no council.

**Accept:** for "licit", an answer that calls Emeka's *reception* illicit or sacrilegious passes, provided it places the fault in his act and not in the rite, and keeps it apart from validity and fruit.

**Wrong turns:** calling the baptism invalid because he lacked faith (that confuses character and grace); saying the seven years' sins were washed away by the baptism; grading (ii) rung 1 on the strength of canon 6, which presupposes the obstacle but does not define reviviscence; grading (ii) "freely disputed", which understates it for baptism.

**Model answer:** (a) On the day Emeka received the character, which is like a form, but its effect, grace, was hindered by his insincerity. When penance removed that obstacle, baptism at once remitted all guilt and punishment for the sins before and during it, baptism as the direct cause and penance as the indirect one. The sins of the seven years are remitted by penance, not baptism, and not as to the whole debt of punishment. (b) Valid, since every element including his intention to receive was present; licit as celebrated, the rite and law having been kept; unfruitful, because his disposition placed an obstacle. (i) Rung 1, by Session VII can. 9. (ii) Rung 4, common teaching from Augustine and Aquinas with no definition behind it.

**P3** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict (a):**

- Both affirm that such an adult receives no grace from the rite: canon 6 confers grace on those who "do not place an obstacle"; the Apology requires faith in the user.
- Both reject the sacraments as mere marks of profession: the Augsburg Confession says "not only to be marks of profession among men"; canon 6 rejects "certain marks of the Christian profession".

**Must hit, strict (b):**

- The premise: the rite itself confers the grace "through the act performed" on those who place no obstacle, so that the adult's faith and repentance dispose him for a gift the rite gives rather than being what obtains it. Trent affirms it (cans. 6, 8).
- The Apology denies it: the sacraments are signs and seals of God's promises; a promise profits only when received by faith; so the faith that believes forgiveness is offered is what receives the promised thing, and the absence of a hindrance is not enough.

**Wrong turns:** volunteering a rebuttal, such as "but Trent also requires disposition, so the Apology attacks a straw man"; stating the Lutheran view as "the sacraments are only symbols", which the Small Catechism and CA 13 both deny; making Trent say the recipient's part is "only" to place no obstacle, which overstates canon 6 the other way; locating the crux in infant baptism or in whether grace is needed at all.

**Model answer:** (a) Both hold that an adult who comes without the required disposition gains no grace from the rite. Both refuse to make the sacraments mere marks of profession, the Augsburg Confession saying they are "not only" that and canon 6 anathematizing the view that they are merely that. (b) They divide on whether the rite itself confers grace through the act performed on those who place no obstacle, the recipient's faith disposing him for a gift the rite gives; Trent affirms it. The Apology denies it because the sacraments are signs and seals of God's promise, and a promise profits no one unless it is received by faith. So faith that believes forgiveness is offered must be added in the use of the sacrament, and the absence of a hindrance is not enough.

## Flashback

**From Lesson [1.1](01-01-what-a-sacrament-is.md) (What a sacrament is):** *(Exegetical.)* Close reading. Trent, Session VII, canon 6 anathematizes whoever says "that the sacraments of the New Law do not contain the grace which they signify". In what sense do they *contain* it? Aquinas, ST III q.62 a.3, from the body of the article and the reply to the first objection (English Dominican Province):

> A thing is said to be in another in various ways; in two of which grace is said to be in the sacraments. First, as in its sign … Secondly, as in its cause; for … a sacrament of the New Law is an instrumental cause of grace. Wherefore grace is in a sacrament … as to a certain instrumental power transient and incomplete in its natural being.
>
> Grace is said to be in a sacrament not as in its subject; nor as in a vessel considered as a place, but understood as the instrument of some work to be done …

(a) Name the two ways the passage says grace is "in" a sacrament, and the idea from Lesson 1.1 that each rests on. Then name two things "contain" does not mean here.

(b) An **invented** catechist, written for this problem, tells a class: "Canon 6 says the sacraments contain grace, so the grace is stored in the font water, waiting for whoever is dipped in it." Which words of the passage rule that picture out? And what does canon 6 make binding, at what level, compared with Aquinas's account of what containing is?

**Two sentences per part.**

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Accept:** for the second sentence of (a), any two of the three exclusions below.

**Must hit, strict (a):**

- **As in its sign:** a sacrament signifies grace. This is Augustine's sacred sign.
- **As in its cause:** the sacrament is grace's *instrumental* cause, God its principal cause. This is Aquinas's instrumental causality.
- **Not**, any two: as in its subject, since grace inheres in the soul, not in the rite; as in a vessel that holds it in a place; as a permanent form abiding in the thing, since the power is "transient and incomplete".

**Must hit, strict (b):**

- The words: "transient and incomplete", and "not ... as in a vessel considered as a place, but ... as the instrument of some work to be done". Nothing is stored in standing water: the power is in the rite only as the instrument of a work being done. (Credit, not required: 1.1's point that the sign is the washing joined to the words, not the water in the font.)
- Canon 6 binds, at **rung 1**, *that* the sacraments contain and confer the grace they signify and are not merely outward signs of it.
- That containing is a passing instrumental power is Aquinas's explanation, at **no magisterial level**: *how* the sacraments cause grace is **rung 5, freely disputed**.

**Wrong turns:** reading "contain" as storage, the vessel sense the article itself rejects; answering the catechist by calling "contain" a mere Thomist metaphor, which understates a rung-1 canon; saying that canon 6, by using the word, defined Aquinas's account of it, which overstates; making the sacrament the subject in which grace inheres.

**Model answer:** (a) Grace is in a sacrament as in its sign, because a sacrament signifies grace, and as in its cause, because the sacrament is grace's instrumental cause under God, the principal cause. It is there neither as in its subject, which is the soul, nor as in a vessel that holds it, but as a transient and incomplete instrumental power. (b) "Transient and incomplete" and "not ... as in a vessel ... but ... as the instrument of some work to be done" rule out grace stored in the font, since the power is in the rite only while the work is being done. Canon 6 binds at rung 1 that the sacraments contain and confer the grace they signify; that containing is a passing instrumental power is Aquinas's explanation, with no magisterial level, because how the sacraments cause grace is freely disputed.
