# General Biology · Lesson 4.3: The tree of life

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 4: Evolution & the diversity of life · Builds on: [4.2](04-02-evolution-in-populations.md), [1.3](01-03-cell-theory-two-kinds-of-cell.md) · Unlocks: 4.4 (a taste of ecology)

## Why this matters

If every population descends from earlier populations and they keep splitting, then the history of life has a shape — a branching tree — and every organism alive sits at the tip of one branch. Classification stops being a filing system imposed by humans and becomes a **claim about history** that can be tested and can be wrong.

This lesson is also where the evidence for common descent gets assembled. That evidence is unusually strong, and its strength comes from a specific feature: **independent lines of evidence converge on the same tree.**

## The idea

**Classification should reflect descent.** Before Darwin, organisms were grouped by resemblance, which is a reasonable filing system and a poor theory. After Darwin, a group is only meaningful if it consists of an ancestor and *all* of its descendants — a **clade**. Groups that fail this test get dismantled, and several familiar ones have been.

"Fish" is the standard example. If you take the common ancestor of all fish and follow every descendant, you sweep in amphibians, reptiles, birds and mammals — because a coelacanth is more closely related to *you* than it is to a trout. There is no way to draw a boundary around "fish" that excludes tetrapods without cutting a branch in the middle. **"Fish" is a shape of animal, not a branch of the tree.**

**Reading a tree correctly.** Trees are misread constantly, so it is worth being explicit:

- **Branch points are common ancestors**, not living species. You did not descend from chimpanzees; you and chimpanzees share an ancestor that was neither.
- **Relatedness is measured by the most recent shared branch point**, not by how close two tips are drawn on the page. Tips can be rotated freely about any node without changing the tree's meaning.
- **There is no main line and no goal.** Every living tip is equally distant in time from the root. A bacterium has been evolving exactly as long as you have.

**The evidence, and why it is convincing.** Any one line of evidence could be explained away. What is hard to dismiss is that they **agree**:

- **Fossils** — showing transitional forms, in the right rock layers, in the right order.
- **Anatomy** — homologous structures built from the same parts for different jobs, plus vestigial features that make sense only as leftovers.
- **Embryology** — related organisms following similar developmental paths.
- **Biogeography** — species distributions tracking geography and continental drift rather than habitat suitability.
- **Molecular sequences** — DNA and protein similarity tracking the same relationships.

**The molecular evidence deserves special weight**, because it was not available when the tree was first drawn. Anatomists built a tree from bones in the nineteenth century; sequencing rebuilt it independently in the twentieth from a completely different data source. **The two trees largely match.** That agreement is the sort of thing a true theory produces and a false one has no reason to.

**Distinguishing inheritance from coincidence.** The one trap: similarity comes in two flavours. **Homology** is similarity from shared ancestry — the same structure repurposed. **Analogy** is similarity from shared *problem* — different structures converging on the same solution. Only homology carries information about the tree, and telling them apart is most of the practical work of building one.

## The formal version

**The taxonomic hierarchy**, broadest to narrowest:

$$\textbf{Domain} \to \text{Kingdom} \to \text{Phylum} \to \text{Class} \to \text{Order} \to \text{Family} \to \text{Genus} \to \textbf{Species}$$

**Binomial nomenclature** names a species by genus and species, italicized with the genus capitalized: *Homo sapiens*, *Escherichia coli*.

**The three domains**, established by Carl Woese in 1977 from ribosomal RNA sequences:

| Domain | Cell type | Distinguishing features |
|---|---|---|
| **Bacteria** | prokaryotic | peptidoglycan cell walls |
| **Archaea** | prokaryotic | distinct membrane lipids and wall chemistry; transcription machinery resembling eukaryotes' |
| **Eukarya** | eukaryotic | nucleus and membrane-bound organelles ([1.3](01-03-cell-theory-two-kinds-of-cell.md)) |

**Woese's result was genuinely startling and is worth dwelling on.** Under a microscope, archaea and bacteria are indistinguishable — small, wall-bound, no nucleus — and were classified together for a century. Sequence comparison showed archaea are a separate lineage, and in several respects (transcription, translation, DNA replication machinery) **archaea are closer to eukaryotes than to bacteria.** A whole domain of life was discovered by molecular comparison, not by looking.

It also relocates you: the deepest split in life is not plant-versus-animal or even prokaryote-versus-eukaryote, but between these three lineages, and eukaryotes are a relatively recent development on one of them.

**The species concept.** The **biological species concept** defines a species as a group whose members can interbreed and produce fertile offspring. It is the standard definition and it fails in several important cases:

- **Asexual organisms** — most bacteria never interbreed with anything, so the criterion cannot be applied.
- **Fossils** — you cannot test whether extinct forms interbred.
- **Ring species** — adjacent populations interbreed all around a ring, but the two ends where they meet do not.
- **Hybridization** — many plants, and some animals, produce fertile hybrids across nominal species boundaries.

*In words: species are real enough to be useful and fuzzy enough to resist a single definition* — which is what you should expect if speciation is a gradual process rather than an event.

**Homology versus analogy:**

| | Homology | Analogy |
|---|---|---|
| Cause | shared ancestry | similar selective pressure |
| Structure | **same underlying parts**, different function | **different parts**, same function |
| Example | whale flipper, bat wing, human hand — same bones | bird wing and insect wing — unrelated construction |
| Use in a tree | **builds it** | must be excluded |
| Also called | — | convergent evolution |

**The test is to look at the construction, not the function.** A bat wing and a bird wing both fly, but the bat's is built from elongated fingers and the bird's from a fused forelimb — analogous as wings, homologous as forelimbs. Both statements are true at different levels, which is why homology must always be stated with respect to a specific structure.

**Molecular clocks and sequence comparison.** Mutations accumulate at a roughly steady rate in sequences under little selective constraint ([4.1](04-01-natural-selection.md)'s silent sites), so **the number of differences between two species estimates time since divergence**. Comparing many genes across many species produces trees that can be cross-checked against fossils, and the calibration largely holds. See [molecular clock](../reference.md#molecular-clock).

**Vestigial structures** are reduced remnants of features that were functional in an ancestor: whale pelvic bones, human wisdom teeth and tailbone, eye structures in blind cave fish. **They are evidence precisely because they are useless** — a designed organism has no reason for them; an inherited one has no easy way to discard them.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (mechanical — homology or analogy?).** Classify each pair and say what it does or does not tell you.

| Pair | Verdict | Reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| Bat wing and human arm | **Homologous** | Same bones — humerus, radius, ulna, carpals, digits — in the same arrangement, different functions. Inherited from a shared tetrapod ancestor. |
| Bird wing and butterfly wing | **Analogous** | Both fly; the bird's is a modified vertebrate forelimb with bones, the butterfly's is a chitinous outgrowth with no bones at all. |
| Shark fin and dolphin fin | **Analogous** (as fins) | Both are streamlined swimming surfaces, but the dolphin's contains a modified tetrapod forelimb — its ancestors walked on land. |
| Whale flipper and bat wing | **Homologous** | Same forelimb bones, both descended from a shared mammalian ancestor. |

**The dolphin case is the instructive one.** Superficially a dolphin resembles a shark far more than it resembles a bat. But open the flipper and you find the same bones as a bat wing and a human hand. **Internal structure tracks ancestry; external form tracks lifestyle** — which is exactly why anatomists build trees from skeletons rather than silhouettes.

**Example 2 (why you'd care — endogenous retroviruses).** Perhaps the sharpest single line of evidence for common descent comes from an unexpected place.

Retroviruses insert their DNA into the host genome. If the infected cell is a sperm or egg precursor, the inserted sequence is passed to offspring and becomes a permanent, inherited feature. **About 8 percent of the human genome consists of such viral remnants.**

Here is why the evidence is so strong. Insertion position is essentially random across billions of possible sites. So finding **the same viral sequence at the same genomic position** in two species has only two explanations:

1. A common ancestor was infected once, and both species inherited the insertion.
2. The same virus independently inserted at exactly the same base in both lineages.

The probability of the second is vanishingly small for one insertion. **Humans and chimpanzees share hundreds of endogenous retroviruses at identical positions.**

And the pattern goes further, which is what makes it a genuine test rather than an observation. The insertions form a **nested hierarchy**: some are shared by humans, chimps, gorillas and orangutans; others only by humans, chimps and gorillas; others only by humans and chimps. That nesting **independently reproduces the primate tree** built from anatomy and from ordinary gene sequences.

**This is a prediction that could have failed and didn't.** If the tree were wrong, the insertion pattern would conflict with it — you would find a virus shared by humans and orangutans but absent in chimps. Such conflicts are essentially never observed. Common descent is not an interpretation laid over the data; it is what makes independent datasets agree.

## Watch out

- **You might think humans evolved from chimpanzees.** We share a common ancestor with them, roughly 6 to 7 million years ago, which was neither a human nor a chimpanzee. Both lineages have been evolving since.
- **You might read a tree as a ladder with humans at the top.** Every tip is a present-day organism, equally far from the root in time. There is no direction to the tree.
- **You might judge relatedness by how close two tips look.** Only branch points count. Tips can be rotated about any node freely, so adjacency on the page means nothing.
- **You might use analogous traits to build a tree.** Convergent evolution is exactly what makes unrelated organisms look similar. Wings evolved independently at least four times.
- **You might expect the biological species concept to always apply.** It fails for asexual organisms, fossils, ring species and freely hybridizing groups — which is what you would expect from a gradual process.
- **You might think "just a theory" means uncertain.** In science a theory is an explanatory framework supported by extensive evidence — the status of atomic theory and gravitation, not of a guess.

## One-liner

> Classification is a claim about history: groups must be ancestor-plus-all-descendants, homology builds the tree while analogy misleads, and the tree's strength is that anatomy, fossils, biogeography and sequences independently agree on it.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢)** Explain why the group "fish" is not a valid clade. What would you have to include to make it one?

**P2 (🟡)** A bird wing and a bat wing both serve flight. Are they homologous, analogous, or both? Justify your answer at each level of structure.

**P3 (🔴)** Humans and chimpanzees share hundreds of endogenous retroviruses at identical genomic positions. Explain why this is powerful evidence for common descent, and describe the observation that would have falsified it.

<details>
<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** A valid clade contains an ancestor **and all of its descendants**. "Fish" fails this test.

The common ancestor of all animals called fish is also the ancestor of **all tetrapods** — amphibians, reptiles, birds and mammals. Lobe-finned fish (coelacanths, lungfish) are more closely related to tetrapods than they are to ray-finned fish like trout. So a coelacanth's nearest relatives include *you*, not a salmon.

Drawing a boundary around "fish" that excludes tetrapods therefore cuts a branch in the middle, leaving out some of the ancestor's descendants. Such a group is **paraphyletic** — not a clade.

**To make it a clade you would have to include all tetrapods**, giving the group Vertebrata (or Osteichthyes for the bony-fish-plus-tetrapods clade). In other words, the only valid version of "fish" is one in which humans are fish.

*Check.* The same problem afflicts "reptile" — the ancestor of all reptiles is also the ancestor of birds, so a valid clade must include birds, which is why birds are now classified as dinosaurs ✓. It also afflicts "invertebrate" and "algae." The pattern is general: groups defined by **lacking** a feature, or by retaining an ancestral form, are usually paraphyletic.

**P2** **Both — and the answer depends on which structure you ask about.**

**Analogous as wings.** As flight surfaces they evolved independently. The shared ancestor of birds and bats was a small terrestrial reptile-like animal that did not fly, so flight arose separately in the two lineages. The wings' *construction* differs accordingly:

| | Bird wing | Bat wing |
|---|---|---|
| Flight surface | feathers | a skin membrane |
| Skeletal support | fused and reduced hand bones | four hugely elongated fingers |

**Homologous as forelimbs.** Strip away the flight adaptations and both contain the same bones in the same order — humerus, radius, ulna, carpals, digits — inherited from the common tetrapod ancestor. That ancestral forelimb is homologous across birds, bats, whales and humans.

**The general principle: homology is a claim about a specific structure, not about an organism.** The forelimb is homologous; its modification into a wing is convergent. Both statements are true at once because they refer to different levels.

*Check.* This is exactly why the dolphin fin in Example 1 is analogous to a shark fin but homologous to a bat wing ✓ — you must always specify *which* structure, or the question has no answer.

**P3** **Why it is powerful evidence** rests on the randomness of insertion.

A retrovirus inserts into the host genome at an essentially arbitrary site among roughly three billion possible positions. For a given insertion to appear at **the same position** in two species, there are two possibilities:

1. **Common ancestry** — the ancestral population was infected once, the insertion became fixed, and both descendant species inherited it at the position it already occupied.
2. **Independent insertion** — the same virus inserted at exactly the same base in both lineages separately.

The probability of option 2 for a single insertion is roughly 1 in $3\times10^9$. For **hundreds** of shared insertions, the combined probability is smaller than any number worth writing down. Common ancestry is not merely the better explanation; it is the only surviving one.

**And it is a genuine test, not a post-hoc fit,** because the insertions form a **nested hierarchy** that independently reproduces the primate tree:

| Insertion shared by | Implies infection occurred |
|---|---|
| Humans, chimps, gorillas, orangutans | before the orangutan lineage split |
| Humans, chimps, gorillas | after orangutans split, before gorillas |
| Humans, chimps only | after gorillas split |

**The falsifying observation:** a retroviral insertion at an identical genomic position shared by **humans and orangutans but absent in chimpanzees and gorillas**. Under common descent that is impossible without invoking an implausible independent insertion or a loss in two separate lineages at exactly that site. The tree built from anatomy and from ordinary gene sequences forbids it.

Systematic surveys of endogenous retroviruses find essentially no such conflicts. **The pattern could have come out any way and came out matching the tree**, which is precisely what distinguishes a tested prediction from an accommodation of the data.

*Check.* The same nested-hierarchy logic applies to other shared "errors" that carry no function and hence no reason for convergence — pseudogenes, transposon insertions, and the broken vitamin C synthesis gene shared by primates with **the same disabling mutation** ✓. Shared functional traits could reflect similar selection; shared *identical mistakes* cannot.

## Flashback

**From Lesson 4.2 (Evolution in populations):** Explain how a geographic barrier splitting one population can, over time, produce two species. Name the mode of speciation.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

This is **allopatric speciation** — speciation with geographic separation.

The sequence:

1. **A barrier arises** — a river changes course, sea level rises, a mountain range forms — dividing one interbreeding population into two.
2. **Gene flow stops.** The two gene pools no longer exchange alleles, so nothing keeps them synchronized ([4.2](04-02-evolution-in-populations.md)).
3. **They diverge independently.** Different mutations arise in each; genetic drift takes each in its own random direction; and selection differs because the two environments differ.
4. **Reproductive isolation accumulates** as a by-product. Differences in timing, mating behaviour, or genetic compatibility build up — not because they are selected for, but simply because the populations are no longer constrained to remain compatible.
5. **The test:** if the barrier is later removed and the two populations can no longer produce fertile offspring, they are separate species.

*Check.* This lesson supplies the aftermath — repeated over billions of years, this branching is what generates the tree ✓. And it explains one of the biogeographic patterns listed as evidence: island archipelagos are full of closely related endemic species (Darwin's finches, Hawaiian honeycreepers) because islands supply exactly the barriers this process requires ✓.

</details>
