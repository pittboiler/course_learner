# General Biology · Lesson 1.4: A tour of the organelles

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 1: The cell & the molecules of life · Builds on: [1.3](01-03-cell-theory-two-kinds-of-cell.md) · Unlocks: 2.1 (energy and enzymes)

## Why this matters

This is the lesson most often reduced to a list to memorize, which wastes it. The organelles are worth learning as **answers to problems**: the surface-area problem from [1.3](01-03-cell-theory-two-kinds-of-cell.md), the problem of running incompatible chemistry in one bag, and the problem of keeping destructive enzymes from destroying their owner.

Learn them that way and you can predict which organelles a cell has from what it does for a living — which is exactly what Boss problem 1 asks, and what a cell biologist actually does at a microscope.

## The idea

A prokaryote runs every reaction in one shared compartment. That works, but it forces every reaction to tolerate the same pH, the same ion concentrations, and every other reaction's intermediates. **Compartments buy you three things:**

1. **Incompatible conditions side by side.** A lysosome runs at pH 5 while the cytoplasm sits at 7.2. In one shared bag you could have one or the other, never both.
2. **Concentration.** Confining an enzyme and its substrate to a small volume raises both concentrations enormously, and reaction rates go up with them. Diluting the same molecules across the whole cell would make the chemistry crawl.
3. **Containment.** Digestive enzymes that would eat the cell are kept behind a membrane. A cell that hydrolyses proteins for a living needs somewhere to do it that isn't "everywhere".

And the fourth, from [1.3](01-03-cell-theory-two-kinds-of-cell.md): **organelles are surface area folded inward**, which is what lets a eukaryotic cell be large at all.

Beyond the individual parts, there's one structure worth seeing whole — the **endomembrane system**, a physically connected production line. Instructions leave the nucleus; ribosomes on the rough ER build proteins straight into it; vesicles bud off and carry them to the Golgi, which modifies, tags and sorts; more vesicles carry the finished product to the membrane or to a lysosome. It is a factory with a shipping department, and tracing one protein along it explains half the organelles at once.

Two organelles stand apart from that system: mitochondria and chloroplasts. Both have **double membranes, their own circular DNA, and prokaryote-sized ribosomes** — because, as [1.3](01-03-cell-theory-two-kinds-of-cell.md)'s problem P3 argued, they *were* prokaryotes.

## The formal version

### The endomembrane system

| Organelle | Structure | Job |
|---|---|---|
| **Nucleus** | double membrane with pores; holds chromosomes and the nucleolus | stores DNA; transcription happens here ([3.4](03-04-central-dogma.md)); the nucleolus builds ribosomes |
| **Rough ER** | membrane sheets studded with ribosomes, continuous with the nuclear envelope | synthesizes and folds proteins destined for membranes, lysosomes, or export |
| **Smooth ER** | tubes, no ribosomes | makes lipids and steroids; detoxifies drugs; stores $\ce{Ca^2+}$ |
| **Golgi apparatus** | stacked flattened sacs, with a receiving and a shipping face | modifies, tags and sorts proteins; dispatches vesicles |
| **Lysosome** | single-membrane sac, interior at pH 5 | hydrolytic digestion of macromolecules, debris and worn organelles |
| **Vesicles** | small membrane spheres | transport between all of the above, and to the plasma membrane |
| **Vacuole** | large sac; in plants, most of the cell's volume | storage; in plants, water pressure (turgor) against the wall |

**Why the lysosome runs acidic** is a nice piece of design. Its enzymes work best near pH 5 and poorly at the cytoplasm's 7.2. So if a lysosome leaks, the escaping enzymes find themselves in the wrong pH and largely stop working. **The compartment is a safety feature twice over** — a physical barrier, plus a chemical one that fails safe.

### The energy organelles

| Organelle | Where | Job |
|---|---|---|
| **Mitochondrion** | all eukaryotes | cellular respiration — makes most of the cell's ATP ([2.2](02-02-cellular-respiration.md)) |
| **Chloroplast** | plants and algae | photosynthesis — light and $\ce{CO2}$ into sugar ([2.3](02-03-photosynthesis.md)) |

Both have an outer membrane and a heavily folded inner one — the mitochondrion's **cristae**, the chloroplast's stacked **thylakoids**. That folding is the surface-area trick again, and it is not decorative: the reactions in question happen *on* those membranes, so area is throughput.

### Everything else

- **Ribosomes** — not membrane-bound, so not organelles in the strict sense; two subunits of RNA and protein. **Free** ribosomes make proteins for use in the cytosol; **bound** ones (on the rough ER) make proteins for export or for membranes. Same ribosome; the destination is set by a signal sequence on the protein itself.
- **Cytoskeleton** — protein filaments giving shape, anchoring organelles, and providing tracks for motor proteins. It also pulls chromosomes apart in division ([2.5](02-05-mitosis-meiosis.md)).
- **Cell wall** — outside the membrane where present: cellulose in plants, chitin in fungi, peptidoglycan in bacteria. Never in animal cells.
- **Centrosome / centrioles** — organize the spindle in animal cell division.

### Plant versus animal, at a glance

| Feature | Plant | Animal |
|---|---|---|
| Cell wall | yes, cellulose | no |
| Chloroplasts | yes | no |
| Large central vacuole | yes | no (small vesicles only) |
| Centrioles | usually absent | yes |
| Mitochondria | **yes** | yes |

**Plants have mitochondria too** — this catches people out constantly. Photosynthesis makes sugar; the plant still has to respire that sugar to get ATP, and it must do so at night and in every non-green tissue. Chloroplasts are an *addition*, not a replacement.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (mechanical — follow one protein).** Trace insulin, a hormone secreted by pancreatic beta cells, from gene to bloodstream. Name the organelle at each step.

1. **Nucleus** — the insulin gene is transcribed to messenger RNA, which exits through a nuclear pore.
2. **Rough ER** — a ribosome begins translating; a signal sequence on the growing chain directs it to the rough ER, and the protein is threaded into the ER interior as it is made. Initial folding and quality control happen here.
3. **Vesicle** — buds off the ER carrying the protein.
4. **Golgi** — receives it, trims and modifies it (insulin is cut from a longer precursor), tags it for secretion.
5. **Secretory vesicle** — buds off the Golgi and waits near the plasma membrane.
6. **Plasma membrane** — on a blood-glucose signal, the vesicle fuses and releases insulin outside.

**The protein never once crosses a membrane after step 2.** It is threaded into the ER lumen at synthesis and stays inside the endomembrane system the whole way, which is topologically already "outside" the cytosol. Fusing with the plasma membrane simply opens that compartment to the world. *The endomembrane system is a folded-in piece of the outside* — the cleanest way to hold the whole picture.

**Example 2 (why you'd care — read the cell, name the job).** Cell biologists infer function from organelle proportions. Three real cases:

| What you see | What the cell does |
|---|---|
| Enormous rough ER, prominent Golgi, many secretory vesicles | **Secretes protein for a living** — a pancreatic acinar cell (digestive enzymes) or a plasma cell (antibodies) |
| Mitochondria packed shoulder to shoulder, little ER | **Burns energy constantly** — cardiac muscle, where mitochondria can reach 35 percent of cell volume |
| Abundant smooth ER, few ribosomes | **Processes lipids or detoxifies** — a liver hepatocyte; chronic drug exposure visibly expands its smooth ER |

Run it in reverse and you can predict: a cell specializing in **phagocytosis** — engulfing bacteria, like a macrophage — should be rich in **lysosomes**, and it is.

**Now the diagnostic use.** Tay-Sachs disease is caused by one missing lysosomal enzyme. Without it, a specific lipid cannot be broken down, so it accumulates inside lysosomes, which swell until the cell — most severely, a neuron — fails. Under a microscope the cells are full of enlarged, engorged lysosomes.

**The general shape of a lysosomal storage disease: one missing enzyme, one substrate that never gets digested, one organelle that fills up.** There are dozens of such diseases and they all follow this template — a direct consequence of the fact that digestion was confined to a compartment in the first place.

## Watch out

- **You might think plant cells lack mitochondria.** They have them. Photosynthesis makes fuel; respiration spends it, and roots and night-time leaves do nothing else.
- **You might call ribosomes organelles.** They have no membrane. The distinction matters because it is exactly why prokaryotes, which have no membrane-bound organelles, still have ribosomes.
- **You might think free and bound ribosomes are different machines.** They're identical and interchangeable. A ribosome becomes "bound" when the protein it happens to be making carries an ER signal sequence — the *cargo* decides, not the ribosome.
- **You might treat the double membrane as a structural detail.** It's evidence. Two membranes is what engulfment predicts and what nothing else easily explains — it's the strongest structural argument for endosymbiosis ([1.3](01-03-cell-theory-two-kinds-of-cell.md), P3).
- **You might assume every eukaryotic cell has every organelle.** Mature red blood cells eject their nucleus and mitochondria to make room for haemoglobin — and consequently cannot divide, cannot repair themselves, and die after about 120 days.

## One-liner

> Organelles are not a parts list but a set of answers: rooms for incompatible chemistry, concentration for speed, containment for danger, and folded-in membrane for the surface area a large cell cannot get from its own boundary.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢)** A cell is packed with rough ER and Golgi and studded with secretory vesicles. What is it most likely doing? Name a real cell type that fits.

**P2 (🟡)** Explain why a lysosome's interior is acidic, and what would happen to the cell if a single lysosome ruptured. Why is that less catastrophic than it sounds?

**P3 (🔴)** A drug blocks vesicle budding from the Golgi. Predict the consequences for a pancreatic cell secreting digestive enzymes — for the Golgi itself, the ER, and the cell's function. Would a red blood cell be affected? Explain.

<details>
<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** It is a **protein-secreting cell**. Each observation points the same way:

- **Rough ER** in bulk means large-scale synthesis of proteins destined for export (free ribosomes would suffice for cytosolic proteins).
- **Prominent Golgi** means heavy modification, tagging and sorting traffic.
- **Secretory vesicles** are the finished product waiting at the door.

Real examples: a **pancreatic acinar cell** (digestive enzymes), a **plasma cell** (antibodies), or a **goblet cell** in the gut lining (mucus).

*Check.* The contrast confirms it — a cell making protein only for its own use would show many *free* ribosomes and little rough ER, since no protein needs to enter the endomembrane system ✓.

**P2** **Why acidic:** proton pumps in the lysosomal membrane actively pump $\ce{H+}$ inward, holding the interior near pH 5. The hydrolytic enzymes inside have evolved a pH optimum matching that — they work well at 5 and poorly at 7.

**If one ruptures:** its enzymes spill into cytoplasm at pH 7.2, which is *not* their optimum, so their activity drops sharply. Combined with dilution into a much larger volume, a single lysosome's contents do little damage; the cell repairs and carries on.

**Why the design is clever:** the pH mismatch is a **fail-safe**. The cell is not relying solely on the membrane holding — it has arranged for the enzymes to be near-inactive anywhere except where they belong. Two independent safeguards, one structural and one chemical.

*The caveat:* massive simultaneous rupture overwhelms both, and the released enzymes do digest the cell. This is one route to **necrosis**, and it is why widespread lysosomal damage from toxins or radiation kills tissue.

*Check.* Consistent with the enzyme behaviour of [2.1](02-01-energy-atp-enzymes.md), where pH shifts a protein's charge and therefore its shape and activity ✓.

**P3** **For the pancreatic cell** — a cascading traffic jam:

1. **Golgi:** vesicles cannot leave, so processed protein accumulates and the Golgi swells.
2. **ER:** with the Golgi backed up, incoming ER vesicles cannot be received. Protein accumulates in the ER lumen, triggering the **unfolded protein response** — the cell slows translation and ramps up chaperones; if the stress persists, it initiates apoptosis.
3. **Function:** secretion stops. For a pancreatic acinar cell that means no digestive enzymes reaching the gut, so the animal cannot digest food — and, because activated enzymes accumulate inside the pancreas instead, a risk of the organ digesting itself.

Note that transcription and translation continue for some time — the blockage is in *shipping*, not manufacture, which is precisely why the pressure builds.

**For a red blood cell: essentially no effect.** A mature mammalian red blood cell has ejected its nucleus, mitochondria, ER and Golgi. It secretes nothing and synthesizes no protein; it is a bag of haemoglobin whose only job is to carry $\ce{O2}$ and to deform through capillaries. With no secretory pathway to block, the drug has no target.

*Check.* This contrast is the general principle in action — **a drug's effect depends on which organelles the target cell actually has** ✓. It is also why chemotherapy hits rapidly dividing tissues hardest, and why a lysosomal enzyme deficiency ([Example 2](#worked-examples)) strikes neurons worst.

</details>

## Flashback

**From Lesson 1.2 (The four biomolecules):** A protein is denatured by heating. Which level or levels of its structure are lost, which survive, and why?

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Lost:** secondary, tertiary and quaternary structure. **Survives:** primary structure.

The reason is which bonds hold each level. Primary structure is the sequence, held by **peptide bonds** — covalent, roughly 20 times stronger than the weak interactions, and unaffected by the heat that denatures a protein. Every other level is held by **weak interactions**: hydrogen bonds along the backbone (secondary), R-group interactions and the hydrophobic effect (tertiary), and the same forces between chains (quaternary). Heat supplies enough thermal energy to break those, and the chain unravels into a random coil.

Function goes with the shape, so a denatured enzyme stops working — but the information is intact, which is why some small proteins refold spontaneously when returned to mild conditions.

*Check.* Same logic as the lysosome's pH safeguard in P2 above: extreme pH denatures by changing R-group charges, so the interactions holding the fold change too ✓. And it explains the boiled egg — the albumin unfolds and the exposed hydrophobic patches stick together irreversibly, exactly the aggregation mechanism from [1.2](01-02-four-biomolecules.md)'s sickle-cell example ✓.

</details>
