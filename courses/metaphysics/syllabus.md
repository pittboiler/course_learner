# Metaphysics — Syllabus

> Philosophy · Tier 1 · ~26 lessons · Prereqs: [`philosophical-method`](../philosophical-method/syllabus.md) · Roadmap id: `metaphysics`

## Goal

This course owns the live problems of metaphysics for the whole library: what there is, what properties are, what a substance is and what makes it the kind of thing it is, what possibility and necessity come to, what causation is, whether time passes, how things persist, whether we act freely, and what a person is. Three traditions get equal strength throughout. The **Quinean** tradition treats ontology as a question about what our best theories quantify over. The **Humean** tradition builds the world from local matters of particular fact, with laws, causes and possibilities as patterns in that mosaic. The **neo-Aristotelian** tradition treats substance, essence, act and potency, powers and grounding as real structure, and it is taught here in its contemporary form (Fine, Lowe, Koslicki, Koons, Pruss, Vetter), not as history. You will finish able to state each position so its defenders would sign it, run it on the standard puzzles (the Ship of Theseus, preemption, fission, Frankfurt cases), and name the premise two rival views actually disagree about. Two downstream courses lean hard on this one: `thomistic-synthesis` needs substance, hylomorphism, act and potency, essence and existence, and ordered causal series (Module 2, 4.4), and [`philosophy-of-religion`](../philosophy-of-religion/syllabus.md) needs necessity, possible worlds, brute facts and necessary existence (Module 3) and the structure of causal series (4.4). It deliberately skips formal modal logic and the philosophy of reference (`philosophy-of-language-and-logic`), the mind-body problem and consciousness (`philosophy-of-mind`), spacetime physics (`philosophy-of-physics`), the historical exegesis of Plato, Aristotle and the scholastics (`ancient-medieval-philosophy`), and the arguments for God themselves (`philosophy-of-religion`).

## How practice works in this course

Problems follow [HUMANITIES-BUILD-BRIEF.md](../../HUMANITIES-BUILD-BRIEF.md): every part declares its kind, and every solution is a rubric (must-hit moves, wrong turns) plus a model answer. Here that means:

- **Exegetical parts are strict.** What Quine's criterion commits a speaker to, what a perdurantist says the Ship of Theseus is, what Lewis's counterfactual analysis says about a preemption case, what Frankfurt's case is designed to refute. These have right answers.
- **Evaluative parts are verdict-neutral.** No rubric rewards concluding that universals exist, that the A-theory is true, that hylomorphism beats mereological nihilism, or that compatibilism is right. Must-hit moves are ones every side has to make: state the view precisely, run it on the case, name the premise the objection attacks, and say what the reply costs.
- **Counterexample, apply-to-a-hard-case and find-the-crux are the workhorses.** Metaphysics is tested by puzzle cases (the statue and the clay, Billy and Suzy's rocks, fission), and most disputes bottom out in one premise. Every module also uses reconstruct and steelman-and-reply, and each uses at least three archetypes. **Diagnose** uses invented passages (a physicist's op-ed, a science-fiction transporter ad) that smuggle in a metaphysical commitment.
- **Close reading** uses public-domain texts only: Plato, *Parmenides* (Jowett); Aristotle, *Categories*, *Physics* and *Metaphysics* (Oxford translations of 1908–1930); Boethius, *Contra Eutychen* (Stewart and Rand, 1918); Aquinas, *Summa Theologiae* (English Dominican Province); Hobbes, *De Corpore*; Locke, *Essay*; Leibniz, *Monadology* (Latta); Butler and Reid on personal identity; Hume, *Enquiry*; McTaggart, "The Unreality of Time" (1908). Modern authors (Quine, Carnap, Armstrong, Lewis, Kripke, Fine, van Inwagen, Frankfurt, Parfit, Pruss) are paraphrased and cited, never quoted at length.
- **Formal parts are rare:** de re and de dicto readings written out in possible-worlds terms and an inference checked for validity on each (3.1, Boss 3). The formal machinery is in `philosophy-of-language-and-logic`.

## Dangerous Checklist

When you finish, you can:

- [ ] Read the ontological commitments off a theory with Quine's criterion, offer a paraphrase that avoids one, and say what Carnap's and the neo-Aristotelian grounding approach each think is wrong with the whole exercise
- [ ] State the one-over-many problem and give the realist, trope and resemblance-nominalist answers, with the regress or companionship objection each has to survive
- [ ] Explain the Aristotelian analysis of change (substrate, form, privation; act and potency) and say what a Humean who denies unactualized potencies must say about the builder who is not building
- [ ] Compare substratum, bundle and hylomorphic theories of substance, and answer the special composition question from nihilism, universalism, organicism and hylomorphism
- [ ] Tell a modal account of essence from Fine's definitional one, and state the distinction between essence and existence along with the main positions on whether it is real
- [ ] Separate logical, metaphysical and physical necessity and de re from de dicto modality, and judge when conceivability is evidence of possibility
- [ ] Say what a possible world is on Lewis's modal realism, on abstractionist ersatzism and on a powers-based account, and give the hardest objection to each
- [ ] Say what a brute fact is, state the principle of sufficient reason in strong and weakened forms, and assess whether anything could exist necessarily against the objection that whatever exists can be conceived not to exist
- [ ] Run a preemption or overdetermination case against the regularity and counterfactual theories of causation, and explain how a powers theorist diagnoses it
- [ ] Tell an essentially ordered causal series from an accidentally ordered one, and say why the difference matters to arguments that an infinite regress is impossible
- [ ] Reconstruct McTaggart's argument, place presentism, the growing block and eternalism against it, and say which theories of persistence each combines with
- [ ] Give the endurantist, perdurantist and stage-theoretic verdicts on the Ship of Theseus and the statue and the clay
- [ ] State the consequence argument, the compatibilist replies (hierarchical and reasons-responsive), Frankfurt's case against the principle of alternate possibilities, and the luck objection to libertarianism
- [ ] Apply the psychological, animalist and hylomorphic views of personal identity to fission and to a transporter case, and say what each implies for responsibility

## Modules

### Module 1: What there is

The oldest question and its modern method: how to tell what a theory says exists, whether numbers and properties are among the things that do, and what it would take to settle it.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1.1 | Existence and ontological commitment | Read off what a theory says exists, and say whether the question is a real one | Quine's criterion ("to be is to be the value of a variable"), Plato's beard and Meinongian objects, paraphrase, Carnap's internal vs external questions, the neo-Aristotelian shift from what exists to what grounds what |
| 1.2 | Abstract objects | Weigh the case for and against numbers and other abstracta | abstract vs concrete, the Quine–Putnam indispensability argument, Benacerraf's access problem, fictionalism (Field), platonism's causal-inertness worry |
| 1.3 | Universals: the realist's case | Say what problem universals solve and what kind of realist one might be | one over many, predication and resemblance, transcendent (Platonic) vs immanent (Aristotelian, Armstrong) universals, moderate realism (universals in things, abstracted by the mind), Lewis's natural properties, the multiple-location worry |
| 1.4 | Nominalism and tropes | Explain properties without universals, and say what each attempt costs | predicate, class and resemblance nominalism, Goodman's imperfect community and companionship difficulties, "ostrich" nominalism, tropes (D. C. Williams, Campbell), Bradley's regress |

**Boss problem 1:** Take the passage of Plato's *Parmenides* (Jowett) in which Parmenides asks whether a thing shares in the whole of a Form or in a part of it, Socrates answers with the analogy of a day present in many places, and Parmenides replaces it with a sail spread over many people. (a) Close-read: state the dilemma, and say which words show why the sail undoes the day. (b) Reconstruct the dilemma as an argument against any universal wholly present in many things, and say whether Armstrong's immanent universals are caught by it or deny a premise. (c) Diagnose an invented op-ed by a physicist who says numbers are "a useful fiction" and then argues that the number of quark generations is odd: apply Quine's criterion, and either paraphrase the commitment away or say why no paraphrase is available. (d) In 300–500 words, assess whether the immanent realist, the trope theorist or the resemblance nominalist best answers the one-over-many problem, stating the strongest objection to your choice (a regress, the companionship difficulty, or the dilemma from (a)). Parts (a)–(c) are exegetical; (d) is evaluative.

### Module 2: Substance, change, and essence

What bears properties, how anything can change and still be itself, and what makes a thing the kind of thing it is. This module is the foundation `thomistic-synthesis` builds on.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2.1 | Substance and accident | Compare the three main answers to "what has the properties?" | Aristotle's primary substance vs accidents (*Categories*), Locke's substratum, bundle theory, Black's two spheres and the identity of indiscernibles, substance as independent and a unified subject |
| 2.2 | Change, act and potency | Explain how change is possible, and why the Aristotelian says potency is real | Parmenides' argument that change is impossible, substrate, form and privation, act and potency, accidental vs substantial change, the Megarian denial of unactualized potency, the Humean conditional analysis |
| 2.3 | Hylomorphism and composition | Say when parts make a whole, and give the hylomorphist's answer | van Inwagen's special composition question, nihilism, universalism, organicism, matter and form, substantial form and prime matter, contemporary hylomorphism (Fine, Koslicki, Koons, Jaworski), the four causes as a map |
| 2.4 | Essence and grounding | Tell what a thing is essentially from what is merely necessarily true of it | real vs nominal essence (Locke), sortal essentialism, modal essence vs Fine's definitional essence (Socrates and his singleton), grounding and metaphysical explanation (Fine, Schaffer), priority monism as a test case |
| 2.5 | Essence and existence | State the distinction between what a thing is and that it is, and the dispute over whether it is real | Avicenna's and Aquinas's distinction (knowing what a phoenix is without knowing that one exists), existence as a first-order property vs Frege's and Kant's view that it is not a real predicate, real vs conceptual distinction (Thomists vs Suárez; Scotus's formal distinction), a being whose essence is to exist |

**Boss problem 2:** Take Aristotle's *Metaphysics* IX.3 (Ross, 1908), where he argues against the Megarian view that a thing can act only when it is acting. (a) Close-read: state the Megarian thesis and reconstruct at least two of Aristotle's objections (the builder who is not building, sensible qualities, motion and becoming) in numbered premises. (b) A contemporary Humean analyzes "the builder can build" as "the builder would build if he tried." Name the crux between this analysis and Aristotle's: the premise about what makes the unexercised capacity true. (c) Build a counterexample to the conditional analysis (a disposition that is masked, or one that a "fink" would remove just when the condition obtains), and say whether the Humean can repair the analysis without conceding real powers. (d) A log burns to ash. In 300–500 words, describe the event twice, once as substantial change on the hylomorphic account and once on a mereological nihilist's account, and assess which better explains that something continues through the change and something does not. Any verdict, if both accounts are stated at full strength. Parts (a)–(b) are exegetical; (c)–(d) are evaluative.

### Module 3: Modality and necessary existence

What it means for something to be possible or necessary, what possible worlds are, and whether some facts are brute and some beings necessary. [`philosophy-of-religion`](../philosophy-of-religion/syllabus.md) 2.4 builds on 3.3.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3.1 | Kinds of necessity and how we know them | Separate the strengths of necessity and judge a conceivability argument | logical, metaphysical and physical (nomological) necessity; de re vs de dicto; Kripke's necessary a posteriori (water and H2O), metaphysical side only (the epistemic side is `epistemology` 4.1); conceivability and possibility (Yablo, Chalmers), and when the inference fails |
| 3.2 | What possible worlds are | Compare the main theories of what grounds modal truth | possible worlds as a tool vs an ontology, Lewis's modal realism and the "incredulous stare", counterpart theory, abstractionist ersatzism (Plantinga, Adams), modal fictionalism, powers-based modality (possibility grounded in potencies: Vetter, Pruss, Jacobs) |
| 3.3 | Brute facts and necessary existence | Say whether everything must have an explanation, and whether anything could exist necessarily | the Humean mosaic and brute contingency vs rationalism, causal vs grounding explanation, the PSR stated in strong and weakened forms (its use and the modal-collapse objection go to `philosophy-of-religion` 2.4), necessary vs contingent existence, the objection voiced by Hume's Cleanthes and by Kant that any existent can be conceived not to exist, candidates for necessary existence (abstracta; a being whose essence is to exist, from 2.5), van Inwagen on why there is anything at all |

**Boss problem 3:** Take the opening of Section IV, Part I of Hume's *Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding*, where he divides relations of ideas from matters of fact and says the contrary of a matter of fact is always possible. (a) Close-read: state the principle linking conceivability, contradiction and possibility that the passage relies on, and name the words that carry it. (The passage's role in the problem of induction belongs to `epistemology`; here only its modal principle is at issue.) (b) Use the necessary a posteriori (water is H2O) to build a case where the contrary of a matter of fact seems conceivable yet is impossible, and say whether the case refutes Hume's principle or only forces a restatement of what "conceivable" means. (c) Formal: take "the number of planets is necessarily greater than 7." Write out its de dicto and de re readings in terms of possible worlds, say which is true, and show that the inference "8 is necessarily greater than 7; the number of planets is 8; so the number of planets is necessarily greater than 7" is valid on one reading and invalid on the other. (d) It seems conceivable that there should have been nothing concrete at all. In 300–500 words, assess whether that shows an empty world is possible, and so that no concrete being exists necessarily, stating how a Lewisian realist, an ersatzist and a powers theorist (for whom every possibility needs a ground in something actual) would each answer. Any verdict, if all three are given at full strength. Part (a) is exegetical; (b) is evaluative; (c) is formal; (d) is evaluative.

### Module 4: Causation

What it is for one thing to bring about another: regularities, counterfactual dependence, and powers, plus the laws that govern causes and the order that causal series can have.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4.1 | Hume's challenge and the regularity theory | State Hume's problem about necessary connection and the regularity theory it produced | constant conjunction, necessary connection as projection, Hume's two definitions of cause, Mackie's INUS conditions, Humean supervenience, the problem of accidental regularities |
| 4.2 | Counterfactual causation | Run Lewis's analysis on clean and hard cases | counterfactual dependence, causal chains and transitivity, early and late preemption (Billy and Suzy), overdetermination, causation by absence, trumping |
| 4.3 | Powers, dispositions, and laws | Compare Humean and anti-Humean accounts of what makes laws and causes hold | dispositions and their manifestations, powers as directed ("physical intentionality"), mutual manifestation (C. B. Martin; Mumford and Anjum), efficient and final causes, laws as best system (Lewis) vs necessitation (Armstrong, Dretske, Tooley) vs dispositional essentialism (Bird) |
| 4.4 | Ordered causal series and conservation | Tell an essentially ordered series from an accidentally ordered one | per se vs per accidens order (the hand, stick and stone vs a line of fathers), derived vs underived causal power, simultaneous causation, becoming vs being (a cause of coming-to-be vs a cause of continuing-to-be), why the order matters to regress arguments; the arguments themselves go to `philosophy-of-religion` 2.3 and `thomistic-synthesis` |

**Boss problem 4:** Take Hume's *Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding*, Section VII, where he gives two definitions of cause (read historically in [`modern-philosophy`](../modern-philosophy/syllabus.md) 4.2). (a) Close-read: state both definitions, and say whether they are equivalent, pointing to the words that make the second one different in kind. (b) Billy and Suzy both throw rocks at a bottle; Suzy's arrives first and breaks it, and Billy's would have broken it a moment later. Apply the regularity theory and Lewis's simple counterfactual analysis; say what each implies about whether Suzy's throw caused the breaking, and which verdict is wrong. (c) Steelman a powers theorist's diagnosis of the case in 150 words, then give the best Humean reply in 150 more. (d) A hand moves a stick that moves a stone; a grandfather begets a father who begets a son. In 150 words, explain the difference in order between the two series, then say whether a regularity theory has the resources to mark that difference. Parts (a)–(b) are exegetical; (c) is evaluative; (d) is exegetical, then evaluative.

### Module 5: Time and persistence

Whether time passes and only the present is real, and how a thing can survive the loss of its parts and properties.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5.1 | The A-theory and the B-theory | Reconstruct McTaggart's argument and say what each theory of time makes of it | the A-series (past, present, future) and B-series (earlier, later), McTaggart's contradiction and the regress of tenses, tensed vs tenseless facts, temporal passage, the moving spotlight |
| 5.2 | Presentism, the growing block, and eternalism | Compare the ontologies of time and the objections each has to survive | presentism, growing block (Broad), eternalism, the truthmaker objection to presentism, the "how do you know it's now?" objection to the block, the relativity objection (cites [`relativity`](../relativity/syllabus.md) 1.1 and `philosophy-of-physics`) |
| 5.3 | Coincidence and the Ship of Theseus | Give the main verdicts on the classic puzzles of material constitution | Leibniz's law, the Ship of Theseus with Hobbes's reassembled planks, the statue and the lump of clay, Tibbles and Tib, constitution is not identity, dominant kinds, mereological essentialism, sortal-relative identity |
| 5.4 | Endurance, perdurance, and stages | Say how a thing persists through change on each theory | endurantism (wholly present), perdurantism (temporal parts), stage theory (Sider), Lewis's problem of temporary intrinsics, which theories of time each pairs with, hylomorphic endurance |

**Boss problem 5:** Take McTaggart's "The Unreality of Time" (*Mind*, 1908). (a) Reconstruct his argument that the A-series is contradictory in numbered premises, including the step where the A-theorist's reply generates a regress. (b) Name the crux between McTaggart and an A-theorist who holds that tense is primitive, and say what that A-theorist must deny. (c) Hobbes's version of the Ship: the planks of Theseus's ship are replaced one by one, and someone reassembles the discarded planks into a second ship. Give the verdict on which ship is Theseus's for an endurantist who follows spatiotemporal continuity, a perdurantist, and a mereological essentialist. (d) In 300–500 words, assess whether a view about time settles the persistence question: can a presentist be a perdurantist, and does perdurance dissolve the Ship or merely relocate it? Any verdict, if each pairing is tested against the problem of temporary intrinsics. Parts (a)–(c) are exegetical; (d) is evaluative.

### Module 6: Free will and persons

Whether an agent who acts in a determined, or an undetermined, world can be free and responsible, and what the agent is that persists to bear the responsibility.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 6.1 | Determinism and the consequence argument | State determinism precisely and reconstruct the strongest argument that it rules out free will | causal determinism, alternative possibilities, van Inwagen's consequence argument, the transfer principle, compatibilism vs incompatibilism vs hard determinism, determinism vs fatalism |
| 6.2 | Compatibilism | Give the best compatibilist accounts of freedom and responsibility | the classical conditional analysis and its failure, Strawson's reactive attitudes, Frankfurt's hierarchical theory (wholehearted identification with first-order desires), reasons-responsiveness (Fischer and Ravizza), manipulation cases (Pereboom's four-case argument) |
| 6.3 | Frankfurt cases and alternate possibilities | Say whether responsibility requires being able to do otherwise | the principle of alternate possibilities, Frankfurt's counterfactual intervener, the flicker-of-freedom reply, the dilemma defense (Kane, Widerker), source vs leeway incompatibilism |
| 6.4 | Libertarianism and its critics | Compare the libertarian accounts and the luck objection they share | event-causal libertarianism (Kane's self-forming actions), agent causation (O'Connor, Clarke), the luck and rollback objections, hard incompatibilism (Pereboom), where indeterminism might enter; the theological bridge to grace goes to `creation-grace-last-things` |
| 6.5 | What a person is | Compare the main answers to "what are we?" | Boethius's definition (an individual substance of a rational nature), Locke's person as a "forensic term", animalism (Olson, van Inwagen), the constitution view (Baker), the hylomorphic view (the soul as the form of the body) and the corruptionist vs survivalist dispute |
| 6.6 | Personal identity over time | Apply the psychological and biological criteria to fission and transporter cases | Locke's memory criterion, Butler's circularity and Reid's brave officer objections, psychological continuity, fission and Parfit's claim that identity is not what matters, Williams's "The Self and the Future", the too-many-thinkers problem |

**Boss problem 6:** (a) Close-read the passages in Locke's *Essay* II.xxvii ("Of Identity and Diversity"; read historically in [`modern-philosophy`](../modern-philosophy/syllabus.md) 3.3) that define a person and call "person" a forensic term: what does Locke make personal identity consist in, and which words tie it to responsibility? (b) Reconstruct Reid's brave-officer objection (the boy flogged, the officer who remembers the flogging, the general who remembers the officer but not the flogging) in numbered premises, and name the Lockean premise it attacks. (c) Build a Frankfurt-style case in which an agent could not have done otherwise yet seems responsible; then give the strongest incompatibilist reply (flicker of freedom or the dilemma defense), 150 words. (d) An invented case: overnight, a team of neuroscientists rewrites Kai's values so that he now wholeheartedly endorses a plan he previously found abhorrent, and he carries it out while fully responsive to reasons. In 300–500 words, give the verdicts on Kai's responsibility of a hierarchical compatibilist, a libertarian and a hard incompatibilist, and say whether a psychological-continuity view even counts the morning's Kai as the same person. Any verdict, if you say whether responsibility requires identity or only continuity. Parts (a)–(b) are exegetical; (c)–(d) are evaluative.

## Notes

- **Verdict-neutral across three traditions.** Every module sets a Humean or Quinean view against a neo-Aristotelian one at equal strength: nominalism vs moderate realism (1.3–1.4), bundle vs hylomorphic substance (2.1, 2.3), conditional analysis vs real potency (2.2), Lewisian worlds vs powers-based modality (3.2), best-system laws vs dispositional essentialism (4.3), perdurance vs hylomorphic endurance (5.4), psychological continuity vs animalism vs hylomorphism (6.5–6.6). Lessons may report where the profession sits (the PhilPapers surveys show most philosophers leaning compatibilist) but never argue for it in the lesson's voice.
- **For `thomistic-synthesis`.** That course should cite 2.1–2.5 and 4.4 rather than re-teach substance, act and potency, hylomorphism, essence and existence, or ordered series, and start from Aquinas's systematic use of them: pure act, analogy, participation, and the real distinction's role in the Five Ways. The division with `ancient-medieval-philosophy` is that it reads Aristotle and Aquinas historically, in their texts and contexts; this course asks whether their positions are true now, against their contemporary rivals. `ancient-medieval-philosophy` had no syllabus on disk when this was written; when it is written, it should cite 1.3 and 2.1–2.3 for the live debates and keep the historical exegesis.
- **For `philosophy-of-religion`.** Its syllabus lists metaphysics as a prerequisite and assumes modality, causation and free will from it, while owning the PSR as a premise (2.4) and the per se regress argument (2.3). The split: this course supplies kinds of necessity and possible worlds (3.1–3.2), brute facts, the PSR stated, and necessary existence (3.3), and per se vs per accidens order as a theory of causal structure (4.4). That course weighs the PSR (Hume–Edwards, modal collapse) and runs the regress arguments. Its 2.3 lists the per se/per accidens distinction among its key concepts; when built, it should reload the distinction from 4.4 rather than re-teach it.
- **Bridge to Catholic theology.** The substance and accident distinction of 2.1 is the philosophical vocabulary in which Lateran IV and Trent state the doctrine of transubstantiation, which `sacraments-and-liturgy` owns. Worth knowing there: whether that doctrine depends on any one philosophy of substance is itself a question Catholic theologians discuss. This course does not teach the doctrine. Similarly, 6.4's libertarian and compatibilist accounts are the philosophical background to the Thomist vs Molinist dispute over grace and freedom in `creation-grace-last-things`, and 6.5's hylomorphic view of persons bears on the soul and the resurrection there.
- **Personal identity ownership.** The roadmap blurb for `philosophy-of-mind` lists personal identity, and the steering for this course assigns it here. Resolution: this course owns it as a problem of persistence (6.5–6.6); `philosophy-of-mind` should cite those lessons and add only questions specific to consciousness.
- **Lesson count.** The roadmap estimated ~22; this runs 26 (+18%, inside the ±25% band). The steering adds two topics beyond the roadmap blurb that need real space: the neo-Aristotelian tradition in contemporary form (act and potency, hylomorphism, essence and existence, powers, ordered series: five lessons that `thomistic-synthesis` depends on) and time (two lessons). Nothing is crammed: universals take two lessons so tropes are not a footnote to nominalism, and personal identity takes two so that "what are we?" and "what makes us the same?" are not run together.
