# Metaphysics · Reference Card

> Open book. This card is meant to be open while you work — including during
> quizzes. Nothing here is something you're expected to recall cold.

Three traditions run through this course, and most entries below belong to one of them: the **Quinean** (ontology is what our best theory quantifies over), the **Humean** (a mosaic of local particular facts, with laws, causes and modality as patterns in it), and the **neo-Aristotelian** (substance, essence, act and potency, powers and grounding as real structure). The card is for mid-problem lookup: which position says what, what each takes as primitive, and what each owes.

## Positions and distinctions

### Quine's criterion

To be is to be the value of a bound variable: a theory is committed to whatever must be in the range of its quantifiers for it to be true. Commitment is read off quantifiers, never off names. Two honest responses to an unwanted commitment — pay it, or paraphrase.

*Introduced:* [1.1](lessons/01-01-existence-and-ontological-commitment.md)

### Internal and external questions

Carnap's distinction between questions asked inside a framework and questions about whether to adopt it, the second being practical rather than factual. Quine denies the seam. Whether it can be drawn is the crux of whether this course's questions are substantive.

*Introduced:* [1.1](lessons/01-01-existence-and-ontological-commitment.md)

### The indispensability argument

We ought to believe in whatever our best scientific theories indispensably quantify over; mathematics is indispensable; so we ought to believe in mathematical objects. Field attacks the second premise (nominalistic science plus conservativeness); Melia attacks the first (weaseling — assert the theory, then take the abstracta back).

*Introduced:* [1.2](lessons/01-02-abstract-objects.md)

### Benacerraf's dilemma

A uniform semantics makes mathematical truth about objects; a causal-contact requirement then makes mathematical knowledge inexplicable. The best account of truth and the best account of knowledge pull apart.

*Introduced:* [1.2](lessons/01-02-abstract-objects.md)

### The one-over-many problem

Many things are F; what explains the sameness? The realist posits a universal. Each opponent attacks a different premise, which is how to tell the nominalisms apart.

*Introduced:* [1.3](lessons/01-03-universals-the-realists-case.md)

### Transcendent and immanent realism

Two questions, not one: can a universal exist uninstantiated, and where is it? Transcendent realism says yes and nowhere; immanent realism (Armstrong) says no and wholly present in each instance. **Moderate realism** keeps an individualized nature abstracted by the mind.

*Introduced:* [1.3](lessons/01-03-universals-the-realists-case.md)

### Bradley's regress

If instantiation is a relation, what relates the universal to the relation? Replies: a non-relational tie, states of affairs as basic, or a benign regress. It also hits tropes plus compresence.

*Introduced:* [1.3](lessons/01-03-universals-the-realists-case.md)

### The imperfect community and companionship difficulties

Goodman's two problems for resemblance nominalism. **Imperfect community:** a class can be pairwise resembling with no property common to all. **Companionship:** if every F is also G, the F-class is never maximal, so the property is lost. Tropes escape both structurally, since exact resemblance needs no respect.

*Introduced:* [1.4](lessons/01-04-nominalism-and-tropes.md)

### Substance, substratum, bundle

Three answers to "what has the properties?" — the Aristotelian individual substance with its *said-of* / *present-in* tests; a bare particular that bears properties without being any of them; or a bundle of properties, which entails the identity of indiscernibles if the bundle is of universals but not if it is of tropes.

*Introduced:* [2.1](lessons/02-01-substance-and-accident.md)

### The identity of indiscernibles

If two things share every property they are one thing. Black's two spheres is the standard counterexample. Distinguish it from **Leibniz's law** (the indiscernibility of identicals), which is far less controversial and does the work in [5.3](lessons/05-03-coincidence-and-the-ship-of-theseus.md).

*Introduced:* [2.1](lessons/02-01-substance-and-accident.md)

### Act and potency

Aristotle's answer to Parmenides: what comes to be comes from what is *potentially* it, not from nothing, so potency is a third thing between being and non-being. **First act** (a capacity possessed) and **second act** (its exercise) make the distinction relative, not absolute. The Megarian denies unexercised potencies.

*Introduced:* [2.2](lessons/02-02-change-act-and-potency.md)

### Substantial and accidental change

A change in which a thing of a kind comes to be or ceases to be, versus a change a thing survives. Accidental change needs something that persists through it — which is the neo-Aristotelian's argument for substance.

*Introduced:* [2.2](lessons/02-02-change-act-and-potency.md)

### The special composition question

Under what conditions do some things compose a further thing? **Nihilism:** never (only simples arranged thus). **Universalism:** always (any things compose something). **Restricted:** sometimes — van Inwagen's answer is when their activity constitutes a life; the hylomorphist's is when a form unifies them into one thing of a kind. Sider's vagueness argument is the pressure on restricted answers.

*Introduced:* [2.3](lessons/02-03-hylomorphism-and-composition.md)

### Hylomorphism

A substance is matter structured by form, not a heap of parts. Contemporary versions treat structure as real and sometimes as a proper part (Koslicki), against mereological reductionism (Fine). The **homonymy principle** — a severed hand is a hand in name only — is what it buys and what it costs.

*Introduced:* [2.3](lessons/02-03-hylomorphism-and-composition.md)

### Essence is finer than necessity

Fine's argument: Socrates is necessarily a member of his singleton and necessarily distinct from the Eiffel Tower, but neither is part of what he is. So essence is not the modal profile; it is real definition, and necessity is explained by essence rather than the reverse.

*Introduced:* [2.4](lessons/02-04-essence-and-grounding.md)

### Grounding

Non-causal metaphysical dependence — what holds in virtue of what. **Full** and **partial** grounds; usually taken to be irreflexive, asymmetric, transitive and well-founded, each of which has been challenged. Wilson's scepticism: there is no one big-G relation, only the specific small-g ones.

*Introduced:* [2.4](lessons/02-04-essence-and-grounding.md)

### Essence and existence

What a thing is, versus that it is: you can know what a phoenix is without knowing whether any exists. The **strong** claim is that in creatures the two are really distinct, existence being the act by which an essence is. Three readings of the distinction's status — Thomist (real), Scotist (formal), Suárezian (of reason with a foundation in the thing) — adjudicated in [`thomistic-synthesis`](../thomistic-synthesis/syllabus.md).

*Introduced:* [2.5](lessons/02-05-essence-and-existence.md)

### Existence is not a real predicate

The Kant–Frege–Quine point that existence is expressed by the quantifier, not by a property of individuals. The neo-Aristotelian reply: that is a claim about how we express existence, not about whether a thing's being is distinct from its nature.

*Introduced:* [2.5](lessons/02-05-essence-and-existence.md), building on [1.1](lessons/01-01-existence-and-ontological-commitment.md)

### Kinds of necessity

Logical (narrow), conceptual/analytic, metaphysical, physical (nomological). They nest, and two boundaries are contested: metaphysical necessity is not logical necessity in the narrow sense, and the physical/metaphysical line collapses if dispositional essentialism is right ([4.3](lessons/04-03-powers-dispositions-and-laws.md)).

*Introduced:* [3.1](lessons/03-01-kinds-of-necessity.md)

### The necessary a posteriori

Water is H2O: necessary, but discoverable only by experience. Kripke's schema — an a priori conditional plus an a posteriori antecedent — explains why experience is needed without making the necessity empirical.

*Introduced:* [3.1](lessons/03-01-kinds-of-necessity.md)

### Conceivability as a guide to possibility

Qualified three ways: conceiving a description is not conceiving a scenario; prima facie is not ideal conceivability; and an apparent conceiving may be a misdescription of something else. Assess a conceivability argument by asking which is in play.

*Introduced:* [3.1](lessons/03-01-kinds-of-necessity.md)

### What possible worlds are

**Tool** (a way of stating modal claims) versus **ontology** (a theory of what modal truth consists in) — the distinction that organizes the whole debate. Lewis's modal realism: concrete isolated worlds, indexical actuality, counterparts. Ersatzism: worlds as maximal consistent sets, with the worry that "consistent" reintroduces modality. Modalism: modality is primitive. Powers-based accounts (Vetter, Pruss): possibility grounded in what actual things can do.

*Introduced:* [3.2](lessons/03-02-what-possible-worlds-are.md)

### The principle of sufficient reason

Every fact has an explanation — in forms of very different strength (unrestricted, contingent facts only, causal, restricted, modest-defeasible). The strength chosen is the whole game. A **brute fact** is one with no explanation; whether admitting one is a cost or plain honesty is the dispute.

*Introduced:* [3.3](lessons/03-03-brute-facts-and-necessary-existence.md)

### Necessary existence

Existing in every possible world. Abstracta are the cleanest candidates, if they exist at all; whether any concrete thing could exist necessarily is contested, and the **subtraction argument** is the standard case for the possibility of an empty world.

*Introduced:* [3.3](lessons/03-03-brute-facts-and-necessary-existence.md)

### The regularity theory of causation

Hume's challenge: we observe constant conjunction, priority and contiguity, never necessary connection. The regularity theory reduces causation to law-like patterns and faces four problems — imperfect, irrelevant and accidental regularities, and asymmetry. **Mackie's INUS** account is its sophisticated form, with the **causal field** fixing what counts as background.

*Introduced:* [4.1](lessons/04-01-humes-challenge-and-the-regularity-theory.md)

### Counterfactual causation

Lewis: c causes e when e counterfactually depends on c — with causation defined as the **ancestral** of dependence, precisely to handle transitivity. Early preemption is handled by the chain; **late preemption** and **trumping** are not. Repairs: quasi-dependence, event fragility (which trivializes if pushed), and structural-equations interventionism.

*Introduced:* [4.2](lessons/04-02-counterfactual-causation.md)

### Three accounts of laws

**Humean best system:** laws are the axioms of the best systematization of the mosaic, balancing simplicity and strength — laws describe. **DTA nomic necessitation:** a relation between universals, facing van Fraassen's inference problem. **Dispositional essentialism:** properties are essentially powers, so laws follow from what the properties are and come out metaphysically necessary. Compare them by what each takes as primitive.

*Introduced:* [4.3](lessons/04-03-powers-dispositions-and-laws.md)

### Essentially and accidentally ordered series

*Per se:* later members exercise a **derived** power, dependent here and now on the earlier (the hand, the stick, the stone). *Per accidens:* each member's power is its own (fathers and sons). Marks: concurrence, derived power, immediate collapse. **The derived-power claim is the substance; the others are diagnostics.** Whether a per se series can regress infinitely — and what would follow — is evaluated in [`philosophy-of-religion`](../philosophy-of-religion/syllabus.md), not here.

*Introduced:* [4.4](lessons/04-04-ordered-causal-series.md)

### Cause of becoming and cause of being

A cause of a thing's coming to be (the builder) versus a cause of its continuing to exist (conservation). The **existential inertia thesis** denies the second is needed.

*Introduced:* [4.4](lessons/04-04-ordered-causal-series.md)

### McTaggart's argument

Change requires the A-series; A-determinations are mutually incompatible yet every event has all of them; the natural repair (at different times) regenerates the problem at the next level. The **A-theory** rejects the incompatibility or takes tense as primitive; the **B-theory** rejects the claim that change requires the A-series, analysing tensed talk by token-reflexive truth conditions.

*Introduced:* [5.1](lessons/05-01-the-a-theory-and-the-b-theory.md)

### Presentism, growing block, eternalism

Three answers to what exists: only present things; past and present; all times tenselessly. **This is a different axis from the A/B question** — the moving spotlight is eternalist about ontology and A-theoretic about tense. Presentism's hardest problem is the truthmaker objection; the growing block's is the epistemic objection; eternalism's is the loss of passage.

*Introduced:* [5.2](lessons/05-02-presentism-growing-block-eternalism.md)

### Coincidence and material constitution

The statue and the clay differ in historical and modal properties, so by Leibniz's law they are not identical — yet they coincide exactly. The menu, each with its price: coincidence (and the **grounding problem**: what makes them differ?), counterpart-relative modal properties, sortal-relative identity, dominant sortals, mereological essentialism, nihilism, conventionalism, and temporal parts.

*Introduced:* [5.3](lessons/05-03-coincidence-and-the-ship-of-theseus.md)

### Endurance, perdurance, stages

A thing is wholly present at each moment; or it has temporal parts and persists as a four-dimensional worm; or it is the momentary stage, with persistence handled by temporal counterparts. The **problem of temporary intrinsics** is the standard test: each view denies a different premise of it.

*Introduced:* [5.4](lessons/05-04-endurance-perdurance-and-stages.md)

### Determinism, and what it is not

A complete state plus the laws entails every later state. Not **fatalism** (which says choices don't matter — determinism says they do), not **predictability**, and not the mere truth of future-tensed propositions.

*Introduced:* [6.1](lessons/06-01-determinism-and-the-consequence-argument.md)

### The consequence argument

If determinism is true, our acts are consequences of the remote past and the laws; we have no power over those; so we have no power over our acts. Two attack surfaces: the **transfer principle** (Beta), challenged by agglomeration counterexamples, and the **ability to do otherwise**, where Lewis distinguishes being able to act such that a law *would* have been broken from being able to break a law — he accepts the first and denies the second.

*Introduced:* [6.1](lessons/06-01-determinism-and-the-consequence-argument.md)

### Compatibilist accounts

The **conditional analysis** (and why it fails on the compelled chooser); **hierarchical/mesh** views (Frankfurt's second-order volitions, the willing and unwilling addict); **guidance control** (Fischer and Ravizza: a moderately reasons-responsive mechanism that is the agent's own); and **reactive attitudes** (Strawson: responsibility is grounded in the participant stance). The **manipulation argument** (Pereboom's four cases) is the common challenge; hard-line and soft-line replies are the two ways out.

*Introduced:* [6.2](lessons/06-02-compatibilism.md)

### Frankfurt cases

A counterfactual intervener stands ready but never acts, so the agent could not have done otherwise yet seems responsible — aimed at the **principle of alternate possibilities**. Objections: the **flicker of freedom** (is the remaining alternative robust?) and the **dilemma defence** (either the prior sign is deterministic, which begs the question, or it is not, and the intervener cannot rely on it). Buffer and blockage cases are the attempted escapes. The debate's result was to move the question from leeway to **source**.

*Introduced:* [6.3](lessons/06-03-frankfurt-cases-and-alternate-possibilities.md)

### Libertarian accounts and the luck objection

**Event-causal** (Kane's self-forming actions, indeterminism located in the effort), **agent-causal** (the agent as substance causes the decision), **non-causal**. All face the **luck objection** in its rollback form: replay the moment with everything fixed, and which way it goes is chance — which is not obviously control.

*Introduced:* [6.4](lessons/06-04-libertarianism-and-its-critics.md)

### What we are

**Animalism** (we are human animals; the thinking-animal argument), the **psychological/Lockean** view, the **constitution** view (Baker: constituted by an animal via a first-person perspective), **substance dualism**, and the **hylomorphic** view (a rational animal whose form is the soul — not dualism). Whether "person" is a substance sortal or a phase sortal decides how the persistence question is posed.

*Introduced:* [6.5](lessons/06-05-what-a-person-is.md)

### Personal identity over time

Locke's **memory criterion**, broken by Butler's circularity charge and Reid's brave officer, repaired as **psychological continuity** (overlapping chains, with a causal clause and usually a non-branching clause); the **biological criterion**; and **fission**, which breaks continuity accounts formally, since identity is one-one and continuity is not. Responses: both, neither, closest continuer, or Parfit's — that identity is not what matters in survival.

*Introduced:* [6.6](lessons/06-06-personal-identity-over-time.md)

## What each tradition takes as primitive

The honest comparison in this course is not which view explains most but which primitive you can live with.

| Question | Quinean | Humean | Neo-Aristotelian |
|---|---|---|---|
| Properties | whatever the theory quantifies over; often classes | classes or resemblance, in the mosaic | universals or tropes, sparse, in things |
| Modality | quantification over worlds, or paraphrase | worlds as a device; recombination of the mosaic | powers of actual things |
| Laws | the best system's axioms | the best system's axioms | consequences of essences |
| Causation | regularity or counterfactual dependence | regularity, dependence, or influence | derived and underived powers |
| Persistence | temporal parts, usually | temporal parts | substance enduring, with form |

## Pitfalls

### Ontology

- Commitment is read off quantifiers, not off names — a theory can name Pegasus and be committed to nothing. *([1.1](lessons/01-01-existence-and-ontological-commitment.md))*
- A paraphrase must support the same inferences, not merely avoid the word. *([1.1](lessons/01-01-existence-and-ontological-commitment.md))*
- Every view of properties takes something primitive; a primitive is an invoice, not a refutation. *([1.4](lessons/01-04-nominalism-and-tropes.md))*
- Leibniz's law (indiscernibility of identicals) is not the identity of indiscernibles — the second is the controversial one. *([2.1](lessons/02-01-substance-and-accident.md), [5.3](lessons/05-03-coincidence-and-the-ship-of-theseus.md))*

### Substance and modality

- The substratum is not prime matter, and hylomorphism is not substance dualism. *([2.1](lessons/02-01-substance-and-accident.md), [2.3](lessons/02-03-hylomorphism-and-composition.md), [6.5](lessons/06-05-what-a-person-is.md))*
- Necessarily true of a thing is not the same as part of what it is. *([2.4](lessons/02-04-essence-and-grounding.md))*
- Conceiving a description is not conceiving a scenario. *([3.1](lessons/03-01-kinds-of-necessity.md))*
- Possible worlds as a tool commits you to nothing; as an ontology they commit you to a great deal. *([3.2](lessons/03-02-what-possible-worlds-are.md))*
- The strength of the PSR you assume decides the argument before it starts. *([3.3](lessons/03-03-brute-facts-and-necessary-existence.md))*

### Causation, time and freedom

- A counterexample to a causal analysis must share its structure, not its subject matter. *([4.2](lessons/04-02-counterfactual-causation.md))*
- Making events maximally fragile saves the analysis by trivializing it. *([4.2](lessons/04-02-counterfactual-causation.md))*
- In a per se series the claim is derived power; simultaneity is a diagnostic, and attacking it is not yet attacking the claim. *([4.4](lessons/04-04-ordered-causal-series.md))*
- Eternalism is not the B-theory, and the A-theory is not presentism — two axes. *([5.2](lessons/05-02-presentism-growing-block-eternalism.md))*
- Temporal parts are not photograph slices, and they are not what a thing looks like at a moment. *([5.4](lessons/05-04-endurance-perdurance-and-stages.md))*
- Determinism is not fatalism, and an invalid consequence argument would not show that we are free. *([6.1](lessons/06-01-determinism-and-the-consequence-argument.md))*
- A Frankfurt case shows at most that responsibility does not require alternatives — not that compatibilism is true. *([6.3](lessons/06-03-frankfurt-cases-and-alternate-possibilities.md))*
- The luck objection is about control, not about predictability. *([6.4](lessons/06-04-libertarianism-and-its-critics.md))*
