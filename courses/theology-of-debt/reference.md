# Theology of Debt · Reference Card

> Open book. This card is meant to be open while you work — including during
> quizzes. Nothing here is something you're expected to recall cold.

Every problem in this course asks one of three things: **what does the text say**
(the Hebrew, Greek or Latin word it turns on, read in the Douay–Rheims or the
public-domain translation), **what does the tradition do with it** (release law,
sin as debt, satisfaction, usury and its titles, modern teaching on credit), and
**how much weight does each claim carry**. The card is the lookup surface for all
three: the vocabulary, the documents with their dates and rungs, the scripture
references with their Douay numbering, the arithmetic of the formal parts, and the
level of every teaching whose weight matters. Levels come from
[`fundamental-theology` 4.4](../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-04-the-ladder-of-doctrinal-authority.md),
which owns the ladder; where that course and this card differ, that course wins.
Levels are graded **strictly both ways**: promoting a claim past its act and
demoting it below its act are the same error facing opposite directions. Where the
build's verification corrected the syllabus, this card follows the correction.

## Levels at a glance

Rung 1 is **defined** or otherwise **proposed as divinely revealed**; rung 2
**definitive**, to be held; rung 3 **authentic ordinary magisterium**, owed
religious submission; rung 4 **common teaching** of the schools; rung 5 **freely
disputed**. **Discipline** (canons, penalties, Jubilee conditions, codes of law) is
off the ladder: it binds as law and can change. A scholar's thesis has **no rung**.

### The levels in this course

**Find the act; the rung follows from it.** The six authority items of the syllabus
Notes come first, as corrected by the build, then the other claims the lessons grade.
A catechism paragraph keeps its sources' weight
([`fundamental-theology` 4.5](../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-05-reading-a-documents-weight.md));
a Doctor's authority and a famous Summa article are not acts; a canon presupposes
teaching without raising it.

## Lending and release in the Old Testament

### Neshek and tarbit

**The Torah's two interest words: *neshek*, "bite", and *tarbit* (or *marbit*),
"increase".** Leviticus 25:36–37 names both. What distinguishes them is disputed:
interest deducted in advance vs added at repayment, or money vs food (25:37's
pairing). The Talmud (Bava Metzia 60b) concludes that by Torah law they are one
prohibition under two names, the bite to the borrower and the increase to the
lender.

- **Any increase, no threshold.** Ten measures lent, twelve asked back: forbidden
  whatever the rate. "Usury" as an excessive rate is a modern sense.
- **Ezekiel 18:8, 13, 17** lists both as marks of the wicked man, with no "to thy
  brother" and no foreigner clause. **Habakkuk 2:7** puns on the root: "they that
  shall bite thee" also means those who exact interest from you (Hebrew only).
- **Nehemiah 5:7's "usury" is *massa'*,** a loan claim or its charge, not *neshek*.
- The ban is what the hidden-usury reading of Luke 16:1–9 presupposes
  ([the unjust steward's reductions](#the-unjust-stewards-reductions)).
- **Level:** word meanings are exegesis, not doctrine.

Lessons: [1.1](lessons/01-01-lending-in-the-covenant.md), [1.4](lessons/01-04-creditors-under-judgment.md), [2.3](lessons/02-03-the-parables-of-debt.md)

### The pledge laws

**Security is limited by the debtor's life.** Exodus 22:26–27: the cloak comes back
by sunset. Deuteronomy 24:6: no millstone, for that "takes a life in pledge"
(Hebrew). 24:10–13: the creditor stands outside while the debtor brings out the
pledge; the poor man's pledge is returned before night. 24:17: no widow's garment.
Aquinas (ST I-II q.105 a.2 ad 4): the Law "permits the debtor to give what he needs
least".

- **Breach charged by the prophets:** Amos 2:8 (garments laid to pledge by every
  altar); Ezekiel 18:7, 12, 16.
- The cloak-by-sunset rule is the model for *Centesimus Annus* 35's limit at the
  scale of a nation ([6.2](#centesimus-annus-35)).
- **Level:** the pledge rules are judicial determinations no longer binding as such
  (Aquinas; common teaching).

Lessons: [1.1](lessons/01-01-lending-in-the-covenant.md), [1.4](lessons/01-04-creditors-under-judgment.md), [6.2](lessons/06-02-international-debt-and-the-jubilee-call.md)

### The foreigner exception

**Deuteronomy 23:20 (DR; Hebrew 23:21): interest may be taken from the *nokri*,
the foreigner, not from a brother.** The *ger*, resident alien, is a different
word, protected in Exodus 22:21; the Douay's "stranger" hides the distinction.

| Reading | Who | Claim |
|---|---|---|
| a command | Sifre Deut 263; Maimonides, positive commandment 198 | lending to a gentile at interest is commanded |
| a permission | Nahmanides and others | allowed, not commanded |
| scope | Mishnah Bava Metzia 5:6 | "brother" = fellow Jew; a resident alien may be charged |
| toleration | Aquinas I-II q.105 a.3 ad 3; II-II q.78 a.1 ad 2 | tolerated to avoid a greater evil; under the Gospel every man is a neighbour |
| dispensation | Challoner's note | God, Lord of all goods, gave a title to another's goods |
| the war exception | Ambrose, *On Tobias* | interest only from one you may lawfully kill ([4.1](#the-war-exception)) |

- Aquinas's "greater evil" is a charge of Jewish avarice (Isaiah 56:11): teach it as
  hard ([`church-history` 5.3](../church-history/lessons/05-03-heresy-inquisition-and-the-jews.md)).
- **Level:** all the Catholic readings are theologians' readings, common teaching at
  most. "The Church defined the exception a toleration" overstates.

### Shemittah

**The seventh-year "letting drop" of debts between Israelites** (Deut 15:1–3; the
verb of Exodus 23:11's fallow year). "The year of remission of the Lord" (15:2 DR).
The foreigner may still be pressed (15:3).

- **The ground:** "Remember that thou also wast a bondservant in the land of Egypt"
  (15:15 DR). Released people release.
- **Before the release:** 15:9 forbids the hard heart that refuses to lend as year 7
  nears; 15:10 switches to "give". See [the seventh-year incentive](#the-seventh-year-incentive).
- **15:4 vs 15:11:** "there shall be no poor nor beggar among you" vs "There will
  not be wanting poor in the land" (DR). Rashi/Sifre make 15:4 conditional;
  Challoner reads it as a command to keep brethren from want.
- **Cancel or suspend?** For suspension: the fallow verb, "cannot demand". For
  cancellation: 15:9's logic; Mishnah *Shevi'it* 10:1, 10:9; Aquinas (I-II q.105
  a.2 ad 4) reads cancellation, as charity to the insolvent. **Freely disputed.**
- The debt release is Deuteronomy's; the Jubilee releases land and persons.
- **Jeremiah 34:** Zedekiah's slave release, revoked when the Chaldeans withdrew
  (Jer 37:5 = DR 37:4); "defiled my name" (34:16); "a liberty ... to the sword"
  (34:17). Servants, not loans.
- **Level:** the calendar is a [judicial precept](#judicial-precepts) (4); the open
  hand is taught (CCC 2449, rung 3).

Lessons: [1.2](lessons/01-02-the-seventh-year-deuteronomy-15.md)

### Prosbul

**Hillel's declaration before a court handing over one's debts so that they survive
the seventh year** (Mishnah *Shevi'it* 10:3–4; debts delivered to a court already
exempt, 10:2). *Gittin* 4:3 counts it among enactments for *tikkun ha-olam*;
*Gittin* 36a–b records that after the Jubilee lapsed the release held only by
rabbinic enactment.

- **Fidelity:** it obeys 15:9, which forbids the credit freeze Hillel saw.
- **Evasion:** it turns a fixed release into a default the stronger party waives.
- **Crux:** which verse states the law's purpose when they conflict, credit for the
  poor (15:7–11) or release (15:1–2).
- Mark 7:13 concerns *corban*, not the *prosbul*.
- **Level:** freely disputed; a question inside Jewish law with its own answer; no
  Catholic act addresses it. The practice is [`history-of-debt` 1.3](../history-of-debt/lessons/01-03-release-laws-in-ancient-israel.md).

Lessons: [1.2](lessons/01-02-the-seventh-year-deuteronomy-15.md)

### Deror

**Hebrew "release, liberty".** Proclaimed in the fiftieth year (Lev 25:10; DR
"remission", Vulgate *remissio*); Jeremiah 34:8, 15, 17 (Zedekiah's release, and the
"liberty ... to the sword"); Isaiah 61:1 (the anointed herald "proclaims *deror*",
the same verb-noun pair as Lev 25:10); Ezekiel 46:17's "year of release". Cognate of
Akkadian *andurarum*, the royal clean slate
([`history-of-debt` 1.2](../history-of-debt/lessons/01-02-debt-bondage-and-the-royal-clean-slate.md)).
The Septuagint renders the release as [*aphesis*](#aphesis).

Lessons: [1.2](lessons/01-02-the-seventh-year-deuteronomy-15.md), [1.3](lessons/01-03-the-jubilee-the-land-is-mine.md), [2.1](lessons/02-01-the-acceptable-year-of-the-lord.md)

### Judicial precepts

**Aquinas's class of Old Law precepts binding by institution: they lapsed with
Christ, though it is not sinful to imitate them** (ST I-II q.104 a.3). The release
calendar is one. So "the Church teaches debts must be cancelled every seven years"
is wrong; the moral core (the open hand, CCC 2449) binds. *Tertio Millennio
Adveniente* 51 appeals to the "spirit" of Leviticus 25:8–12, not the law re-enacted.
**Level:** common teaching (4), not defined. Owner of the scheme:
[`old-testament` 2.6](../old-testament/lessons/02-06-covenant-and-law.md).

Lessons: [1.2](lessons/01-02-the-seventh-year-deuteronomy-15.md), [6.2](lessons/06-02-international-debt-and-the-jubilee-call.md)

### The seventh-year incentive

**A loan made just before the release is partly a gift, and Deuteronomy 15:9 asks
the lender to make it anyway.** With principal $P$ and probability $p$ of repayment
before the release:

$$\text{expected recovery} = pP, \qquad \text{expected loss} = (1-p)P.$$

Example: $P = 100$ measures in year 6, $p = 0.3$: recovery 30, loss 70. With
interest forbidden, nothing in the contract offsets the 70. Aquinas's middle
sentence: those able to pay would probably have paid before the seventh year, so the
release costs little against the solvent. Generalized as pricing under a known
release date in [`economics-of-debt`](../economics-of-debt/syllabus.md) 8.2.

Lessons: [1.2](lessons/01-02-the-seventh-year-deuteronomy-15.md)

### The Jubilee

**Leviticus 25:8–55: after seven sabbath-years, the trumpet sounds on the Day of
Atonement (seventh month, tenth day, 25:9) and the fiftieth year is holy; land
returns to its families and Israelites who sold themselves go out.**

- **Sales are leases:** "he shall sell to thee the time of the fruits" (25:14–16);
  price by harvests left ([Jubilee arithmetic](#release-and-jubilee-arithmetic)).
- **Redemption order:** the [go'el](#goel) first (25:25), then the seller refunding
  unused years (25:26–27), then the Jubilee itself (25:28); persons likewise
  (25:47–52).
- **It cancels no money debt.** It returns land and persons; 25:35–37 forbids
  interest mid-chapter. Challoner, Josephus (*Ant.* III.12), TMA 12 and CCC 2449
  ("the jubilee year of forgiveness of debts") fold it together with Deuteronomy 15;
  the Catechism's list does not rule on which law released debts.
- **The release is not universal:** 25:44–46 allows permanent bondmen from the nations.
- Lev 25:9 does not put the trumpet in year 49; it sounds after 49 years are counted.
- **Afterlife:** Isaiah 61, Daniel 9, *Jubilees*, [11QMelchizedek](#11qmelchizedek),
  Luke 4 ([the Nazareth reading](#the-nazareth-reading)); the Christian Jubilee of
  1300 borrowed the name, not the institution ([the treasury](#the-treasury-of-the-church)).
- Read three ways in the debt thread: law ([`history-of-debt` 1.3](../history-of-debt/lessons/01-03-release-laws-in-ancient-israel.md)),
  theology (here), relief device ([`economics-of-debt`](../economics-of-debt/syllabus.md) 8.2).

Lessons: [1.3](lessons/01-03-the-jubilee-the-land-is-mine.md), [2.1](lessons/02-01-the-acceptable-year-of-the-lord.md), [3.5](lessons/03-05-the-christian-jubilee-and-the-treasury.md)

### The land is mine

**Leviticus 25:23–24 grounds the Jubilee: "because it is mine, and you are
strangers and sojourners with me" (DR), so every holding is "under the condition of
redemption".** Israel stands to God as the resident alien stands to a landholder.
The parallel ground for persons is the Exodus: "they are my servants, and I brought
them out of the land of Egypt" (25:42 DR; the "whom I brought forth" wording is
25:55).

- **Level:** that God owns the earth and destines its goods for all, owners being
  stewards, is rung 3 (CCC 2402–2403; TMA 13's *dominium altum*; SNC 16 grounds its
  debt appeal here). It protects family tenure; it does not abolish property.

Lessons: [1.3](lessons/01-03-the-jubilee-the-land-is-mine.md), [6.2](lessons/06-02-international-debt-and-the-jubilee-call.md)

### Practiced or ideal

**Was a Jubilee ever held? Freely disputed.**

- **Not kept:** no narrative records one; Josephus describes it only as law.
- **The seventh year is attested:** Nehemiah 10:31 [DR 2 Esdras]; 1 Maccabees
  6:49, 53; Josephus *Ant.* XI.8, XIII.8. The exile as the land's unpaid sabbaths:
  Leviticus 26:34–35; 2 Chronicles [DR 2 Paralipomenon] 36:21.
- **Kept, or meant to be:** *go'el* practice (Ruth 4, Jeremiah 32); Near Eastern
  releases; Ezekiel 46:17; Bergsma's case for a pre-exilic setting.
- **Jewish reading:** lapsed with the exile of the Transjordan tribes (*Arakhin* 32b).
- **TMA 13** says the prescriptions "largely remained ideals": a papal historical
  judgment, not doctrine. Inerrancy is not at stake: Leviticus asserts a law, not
  that it was kept. The theology of 25:23 does not wait on the answer.

Lessons: [1.3](lessons/01-03-the-jubilee-the-land-is-mine.md)

### Goel

**The *go'el*, kinsman-redeemer, who buys back land or a person sold for poverty**
(Lev 25:25, 47–52): Boaz (Ruth 4), Hanamel's field (Jeremiah 32); later a title of
God. Nehemiah 5:8 appeals to it: "we have redeemed ... our brethren the Jews, that
were sold to the Gentiles" (DR 2 Esdras). The Old Testament background many prefer
to Delphic manumission for the New Testament purchase words
([lytron](#lytron-and-redemption-words)).

Lessons: [1.3](lessons/01-03-the-jubilee-the-land-is-mine.md), [1.4](lessons/01-04-creditors-under-judgment.md), [2.4](lessons/02-04-the-bond-and-the-ransom.md)

### The hundredth part

**Nehemiah 5:11 (DR 2 Esdras 5:11) orders creditors to restore "the hundredth part"
(*mĕʾat*).** Read as monthly, it is the Roman *centesima*:

$$12 \times 1\% = 12\% \text{ a year simple}, \qquad (1.01)^{12} - 1 \approx 12.68\% \text{ compounded monthly}.$$

- **The period is an interpretation, not in the text;** some take the word as the
  loan charge itself. Show the arithmetic and label it.
- **Nicaea c.17** forbids clerics "the hundredth"; the bracket "[as monthly
  interest]" is the NPNF translator's, not the canon's ([Nicaea canon 17](#nicaea-canon-17)).
- Nehemiah's grounds: brotherhood and redemption (5:8), fear of God and the
  reproach of the nations (5:9); his remedy is a one-time restoration under oath,
  closer to a royal clean slate than to the calendar; he pays first (5:10, 14–18).

## Debt and release in the New Testament

### Aphesis

**Greek "release"; its verb *aphiemi* is "release, forgive".** The Septuagint's word
for the Jubilee (Lev 25:10–13) and the seventh-year remission (Deut 15:1–2: "release
every debt your neighbour owes you", *aphiemi* + *opheilei*).

- **Luke 4:18** uses it twice: "deliverance to the captives" (Isa 61:1) and "to set
  at liberty them that are bruised" (Isa 58:6 LXX, inserted). The Douay's two English
  words hide one Greek word.
- Elsewhere in Luke it is remission of sins (1:77; 3:3; 24:47).
- **The Lord's Prayer:** *aphes hemin ta opheilemata*, the sabbatical verb applied to
  sin ([opheilema](#opheilema)).
- **Matthew 18:27:** the king "forgave him the debt", literally released the
  *daneion*, the loan.
- **Ephesians 1:7** (and Col 1:14): *apolytrosis* joined to *aphesis*, "redemption
  ... the remission": purchase and release as one gift.

### The seventy weeks

**Daniel 9:24–27: Jeremiah's seventy years become seventy weeks of years,
$70 \times 7 = 490 = 10 \times 49$, ten Jubilee periods (7 + 62 + 1 weeks), "that
sin may have an end".**

- **Christian reading (Challoner):** ends in Christ; 483 years from Artaxerxes'
  decree to his baptism. Commentary, no magisterial weight.
- **Jewish reading** (*Seder Olam* 28; Rashi on 9:24): $70 + 420 = 490$, exile plus
  Second Temple, from the first destruction to the second. A reading in its own
  tradition, counting a different span; not a failed version of the Christian one.
- *Jubilees* 50:4: $49 \times 49 + 7 + 2 + 40 = 2{,}450 = 50 \times 49$, entry to
  the land at the fiftieth Jubilee.
- The book's date: [`old-testament` 6.3](../old-testament/lessons/06-03-daniel-and-apocalyptic.md).

Lessons: [2.1](lessons/02-01-the-acceptable-year-of-the-lord.md)

### 11QMelchizedek

**Qumran text 11Q13, about 100 BC, paraphrased only.** It reads Leviticus 25:13,
Deuteronomy 15:2 and Isaiah 61:1 (with 52:7) as one prophecy of a last Jubilee: the
heavenly Melchizedek proclaims liberty to the captives and releases them from their
iniquities; the Day of Atonement falls at the end of the tenth Jubilee, Daniel's
figure. Before Jesus spoke, Isaiah 61 was already read as a Jubilee release from sin.

### The Nazareth reading

**Luke 4:16–21: Jesus reads Isaiah 61:1–2 (LXX) with Isaiah 58:6 inserted and
declares "This day is fulfilled this scripture in your ears."**

- "To heal the contrite of heart" is Vulgate/Byzantine, omitted by critical editions

### Opheilema

### Sin as debt

**Sin named as a debt owed to God.** Its history runs through the whole of Module 3.

- **Origin:** the Aramaic [*hoba*](#hoba) means both debt and sin; Matthew's "debts"
  is probably the older idiom, Luke's "sins" its explanation
  ([the Anderson thesis](#the-anderson-thesis)).
- **The petition:** our forgiving is a **condition** (Matt 6:14–15) and a **measure**
  (Matt 7:2), never a **price** (Cyprian, *On the Lord's Prayer* 22–23; CCC
  2838–2845). "We are always debtors" (CCC 2845, quoting Rom 13:8).
- **Anselm (CDH I.11):** to sin is not to render God his due, the due being a will
  wholly subject to God's; the idiom becomes a doctrine of restitution.
- **Aquinas:** literal enough to transfer like a surety's: "men take the debts of
  another upon themselves" (I-II q.87 a.7); the price paid "not to the devil, but to
  God" (III q.48 a.4 ad 3).
- **Level:** no rung. The Church prays it daily but defines no metaphor as *the*
  account of sin; the burden idiom (the Lamb who *takes away*) stands beside it.

### Luke 7:47 love as cause or sign

**"Many sins are forgiven her, because she hath loved much" (DR).** Two readings of
the *hoti*, both held by Catholic exegetes:

- **Cause:** her love is why she is forgiven; the natural grammar of the clause;
  congenial to contrition perfected by charity (Trent XIV ch. 4, which does not cite
  this verse).
- **Sign:** her love is the evidence. The parable (7:41–43) runs forgiveness first,
  love after; 7:47b "to whom less is forgiven, he loveth less"; 7:50 credits her
  **faith**.
- **Challoner's note** joins them: love, faith and sorrow concur in a true conversion.
- Luke 7:42's verb is *echarisato*, "graciously forgave", the verb of Col 2:13
  (*charisamenos*); *aphiemi* comes in 7:47–48.
- **Level:** freely disputed; the Challoner note is not a magisterial act.

Lessons: [2.3](lessons/02-03-the-parables-of-debt.md)

### The unjust steward's reductions

**Luke 16:5–8: 100 baths of oil rewritten as 50, 100 cors of wheat as 80** (DR
"barrels", "quarters"; Greek *batous*, *korous*), and "the lord commended the unjust
steward, forasmuch as he had done wisely" (16:8).

| Reading | Source | Claim |
|---|---|---|
| shrewd fraud | long-standing | he cut his master's due to buy friends; foresight praised, not fraud |
| his own cut | M. D. Gibson (*Expository Times*, 1902–3) | he forwent a steward's markup |
| hidden usury | Derrett (NTS, 1960–61); Fitzmyer (TS, 1964) | the markup was interest written into the bond, and was the agent's profit; he renounced it |

Implied markups on the usury reading: $50/50 = 100\%$ on oil, $20/80 = 25\%$ on
wheat; Fitzmyer says not to press them. "The lord" of 16:8 is most often the master;
Jesus's voice begins in 16:9. **Level:** freely disputed. The parable can illustrate
the usury doctrine; it cannot carry it.

Lessons: [2.3](lessons/02-03-the-parables-of-debt.md)

### The Matthew 18 ratio

**Ten thousand talents against a hundred denarii.** Taking a talent as 6,000
denarii (the Attic convention, and the Challoner note's: talent 750 oz of silver,
penny 1/8 oz) and a denarius as a day's wage (Matt 20:2):

$$\frac{10{,}000 \times 6{,}000}{100} = \frac{60{,}000{,}000}{100} = 600{,}000.$$

About 200,000 years of labour at 300 days a year, against about four months. Peter's
"seventy times seven" such debts total $490 \times 100 = 49{,}000$ denarii,
$\approx 0.08\%$ of the king's release. Luke 7's debts (500, 50) differ only tenfold.

- The release was free (18:27); 18:34–35 teaches it can be **forfeited** by a heart
  that refuses to pass it on, not that it was bought.
- **Level:** forgiving from the heart is the Lord's teaching read by CCC 2840, 2843
  (3); the scenery has no weight.

Lessons: [2.3](lessons/02-03-the-parables-of-debt.md)

### Cheirographon

**Colossians 2:14's "handwriting": in the papyri, a note of hand written by the
debtor ("I will repay").** Key words (lesson's glosses): *charisamenos* "granting as
a favour"; *exaleipsas* "wiping clean"; *tois dogmasin* "with the decrees" (the
crux); *proselosas* "nailing".

| Reading | Leans on |
|---|---|
| our IOU to God | *cheirographon*; "against us" |
| the Law's decrees | *tois dogmasin*; Eph 2:15; Col 2:16 |
| a heavenly record of sins | "contrary to us"; 2:15's powers; *Apocalypse of Zephaniah* |

- **All three make it a remission, not a payment:** the creditor cancels; nobody pays.
- **Philemon 18–19** is a real bond: *elloga* ("put that to my account"), "written
  with my own hand", *apotiso* ("I will repay", stronger than the stock *apodoso*).
- Deissmann: cancelled bonds were crossed with a *chi*; he admits this does not
  explain the nailing. Anselm (CDH I.7) reads the decree as God's, not a bond the
  devil holds.
- **Level:** identity freely disputed; authorship is [`new-testament` 6.1](../new-testament/lessons/06-01-the-disputed-pauline-letters-and-the-pastorals.md)'s.

Lessons: [2.4](lessons/02-04-the-bond-and-the-ransom.md), [3.3](lessons/03-03-anselm-cur-deus-homo.md)

### Lytron and redemption words

**The purchase family.** *Lytron*, price of release (Mark 10:45); *antilytron*,
"a redemption for all" (1 Tim 2:6); *agorazo*, buy in the market (1 Cor 6:20, 7:23
"bought with a price"); *exagorazo*, buy out (Gal 3:13, 4:5); *lytroo* (1 Pet 1:18,
"not with ... gold or silver"); *apolytrosis* + *aphesis* (Eph 1:7; Col 1:14, quoted
at Trent VI ch. 3).

- **A purchase has captive, price, payer, new owner.** Revelation 5:9 "redeemed us to
  God" names the new **owner**. **No verse names a payee**, which is why the Fathers
  asked "paid to whom?" ([the devil's rights](#the-devils-rights)).
- **Deissmann's Delphic sacral manumission:** the slave pays his own price to the god.
  Critics: in Paul another pays; Delphic purchase verbs are not *agorazo*; the freed
  man becomes Christ's bondman. Many prefer the Exodus and the [go'el](#goel). The
  vocabulary is certain; the rite behind it is a hypothesis.
- **Level:** that Christ redeemed us is of faith (rung 1); the payee is never defined.

## Sin as debt from the Second Temple to Trent

### The Anderson thesis

**Gary Anderson, *Sin: A History* (2009), paraphrased: sin as a *burden* in earlier
Hebrew becomes sin as a *debt* in the Second Temple period, with consequences.**

1. **Burden first:** [*nasa' 'awon*](#nasa-awon), "bear iniquity".
2. **Debt later:** Aramaic [*hoba*](#hoba) becomes the ordinary word for sin; the
   Targums render Hebrew sin-words with it; Matthew's "debts" is its Greek sound.
3. **Consequences:** the exile as a repayment term ([*rasah*](#rasah)); alms as
   credit stored with God ([almsgiving as deposit](#almsgiving-as-deposit)).

**Fair critiques:** the images **coexist** (Isaiah 53:11–12 is pure burden; the
scapegoat stayed); the Targum evidence is **late**; a word is not a live metaphor
(James Barr). Best defended as a claim about what the idiom *made available*.
**Level:** a historian's thesis, freely disputed; a Catholic may reject all three
steps.

Lessons: [3.1](lessons/03-01-from-burden-to-debt.md)

### Hoba

**Aramaic *ḥôbāʾ*, "debt", the ordinary Second Temple Aramaic word for sin.** Behind
Matthew's *opheilemata* (6:12) and Luke 11:4's "every one that is indebted to us".
The build notes this as standard scholarship, not checked against a lexicon.

Lessons: [2.2](lessons/02-02-forgive-us-our-debts.md), [3.1](lessons/03-01-from-burden-to-debt.md)

### Nasa awon

**Hebrew "bear iniquity": the burden idiom.** The sinner bears it; with God as
subject it means "take away, forgive" (Exodus 34:7). Cain's "my iniquity is too great
to bear" (Gen 4:13; DR "greater than that I may deserve pardon", following the
Vulgate); the scapegoat (Lev 16:22); Psalm 38:4 [DR 37:5]; Isaiah 53:11–12.

Lessons: [3.1](lessons/03-01-from-burden-to-debt.md)

### Rasah

**Hebrew root *rṣh* in its second sense, "pay off, make good".** The land *makes up*
its sabbaths (Lev 26:34, 43; 2 Chr 36:21); the exiles *pay off* their iniquity
(Lev 26:41, DR "pray for their sins"); Jerusalem's iniquity *is paid* (Isa 40:2, DR
"is forgiven"; "double for all her sins"). The Douay follows the Vulgate and hides the
word: quote the English, reason from the Hebrew.

Lessons: [3.1](lessons/03-01-from-burden-to-debt.md)

### Almsgiving as deposit

**Alms as credit stored with God.** Proverbs 19:17 ("lendeth to the Lord: and he
will repay him"); Daniel 4:24 ("redeem thou thy sins with alms": Aramaic and DR
4:24, many English Bibles 4:27); Tobit 4:10 DR ("reward for the day of necessity"),
4:11, 12:9 ("alms delivereth from death ... purgeth away sins"); Sirach 3:30 [DR 3:33]
("alms resisteth sins"); 29:11–13 [DR 29:14–17]; Matthew 6:19–21, 19:21 (treasure in
heaven).

- **Catholic reading, exact:** Trent XIV can. 13 defines that satisfaction **as to
  temporal punishment** is made to God **through the merits of Christ** by works
  including almsdeeds (rung 1). Guilt is forgiven by grace. CCC 1434 names fasting,
  prayer and almsgiving as the three forms of penance (rung 3).
- **Lutheran reading:** the Apology (Art. III, *Triglot*) reads Daniel 4:24 as
  repentance and faith. **Jewish reading:** Proverbs 10:2's *ṣedāqāh* read of
  charity (Bava Batra 10a).
- The idiom long predates indulgences.

Lessons: [3.1](lessons/03-01-from-burden-to-debt.md), [3.2](lessons/03-02-the-fathers-alms-satisfaction-and-ransom.md)

### Redemptive almsgiving

**Cyprian, *On Works and Alms* (early 250s; ANF 5): alms cleanse sins committed
after baptism.** §2: pre-baptismal sins are "purged by the blood ... of Christ"; §4:
God "appeased by almsgiving alone"; §5: "by works of righteousness God is satisfied";
§26: the giver "accounts God his debtor" (Proverbs 19:17). Proof texts: Daniel 4:24,
Tobit 12:9. **Read through Trent XIV can. 13**, not the other way round: Cyprian's
unqualified phrasing is one bishop's preaching. Tertullian likewise says a good deed
"has God as its debtor": a debt God owes only because he promised, the seed of
**merit**.

Lessons: [3.2](lessons/03-02-the-fathers-alms-satisfaction-and-ransom.md)

### Satisfaction

**Settling a claim by something the creditor accepts in place of strict payment.**
Four stages:

| Stage | Text | Claim |
|---|---|---|
| Tertullian | *On Repentance* 9 (ANF 3) | "by confession satisfaction is settled"; temporal mortification expunges *eternal* punishments (the later distinction is not yet his) |
| Cyprian | *On Works and Alms* 5 | "God is satisfied" by works of mercy |
| Anselm | CDH I.11 | restitution of the honour withheld **plus more**, "considering the contempt offered" |
| Aquinas | I-II q.87 a.6–7 | punishment accepted voluntarily to restore the order of justice; "loses somewhat of the nature of punishment"; can be borne for another united in love |

- **Trent VI ch. 7:** Christ "made satisfaction for us unto God the Father" (doctrinal
  chapter; mechanism not defined).
- **Trent XIV ch. 8–9, cann. 12–15:** our satisfactions are made through Christ, "in
  whom we satisfy", and are a worship of God (defined).
- **Satisfaction is not penal substitution:** for Anselm they are the two branches of
  the fork ([satisfaction or punishment](#satisfaction-or-punishment)); Aquinas says
  penal punishment is personal (q.87 a.8). Theories compared:
  [`christology` 5.4](../christology/lessons/05-04-penal-substitution-and-the-catholic-assessment.md).

Lessons: [3.2](lessons/03-02-the-fathers-alms-satisfaction-and-ransom.md), [3.3](lessons/03-03-anselm-cur-deus-homo.md), [3.4](lessons/03-04-aquinas-satisfaction-and-the-debt-of-punishment.md), [3.6](lessons/03-06-luther-trent-and-the-reform-of-indulgences.md)

### Temporal punishment

**The finite debt of punishment that can remain after guilt and eternal punishment
are forgiven.** Defined: Trent VI can. 30 (no justified penitent has guilt and
eternal debt so blotted out that no temporal debt remains, in this life or in
purgatory) and XIV ch. 8, can. 12 (God does not always remit the whole punishment
with the guilt). Ch. 14 assigns post-baptismal satisfaction to it. CCC 1472: a
consequence following from the nature of sin, purification from unhealthy attachment,
not "vengeance inflicted by God from without".

- **Not yet in Tertullian**, whose penance "expunges eternal punishments".
- Aquinas's ground: the turning-away (eternal debt) lifts with the guilt; the finite
  turning-toward can leave a temporal debt (III q.86 a.4). Example: David after
  Nathan (2 Sam [DR 2 Kings] 12:13–14).
- **Bill or wound?** Debt language says what is owed; purification language says what
  paying does. No mechanism is defined.

Lessons: [3.1](lessons/03-01-from-burden-to-debt.md), [3.2](lessons/03-02-the-fathers-alms-satisfaction-and-ransom.md), [3.4](lessons/03-04-aquinas-satisfaction-and-the-debt-of-punishment.md), [3.5](lessons/03-05-the-christian-jubilee-and-the-treasury.md), [3.6](lessons/03-06-luther-trent-and-the-reform-of-indulgences.md)

### The devil's rights

**The patristic opinion that sin gave the devil a claim on humanity, and the ransom
was paid to him.**

| Father | Position |
|---|---|
| Origen, *Comm. Matt.* 16.8 (paraphrased) | not paid to God, so to the evil one, who held us until Jesus's soul was handed over |
| Gregory of Nyssa, *Great Catechism* | the fishhook: divinity hidden in the bait of flesh ([`christology` 5.2](../christology/lessons/05-02-ransom-christus-victor-and-sacrifice.md)) |
| Gregory Nazianzen, *Or.* 45.22 | refuses both: not the robber, and the Father "accepts" but did not demand |
| Augustine, *De Trin.* XIII.12–15 | a hold "by the justice of God in some sense" (12.16), forfeited by killing the one who owed nothing (14.18); the devil "not enriched, but bound" (15.19) |
| Anselm, CDH I.7 | no right at all: devil and man both belong to God |
| Aquinas, III q.48 a.4 ad 3 | the price paid "not to the devil, but to God" |

**Level:** rung 5 opinion of orthodox Fathers, never taught, later abandoned; **not
heresy**. Its rejection became common teaching; no act defines it.

### Cur Deus Homo

**Anselm, c. 1098 (Deane tr., 1903): the argument from an unpayable debt to the
God-man, *remoto Christo*.** The debt chain, chapters checked against Deane:

| Chapter | Step |
|---|---|
| I.7 | the devil has no rights; Col 2:14's decree is God's |
| I.11 | sin = not rendering God his due; satisfaction = restitution plus surcharge |
| I.12 | mercy alone leaves the account "undischarged"; injustice would be "more free than justice" |
| I.13–15 | satisfaction **or** punishment; I.15 "not only unfitting, but consequently impossible" |
| I.20 | every offered payment (contrition, alms, obedience) is already owed |
| I.21 | one look against God's will outweighs the universe and infinitely many worlds |
| I.24 | self-caused insolvency is no excuse |
| II.5 | the only "necessity" in God is the immutability of his goodness |
| II.6 | a satisfaction "which none but God can make and none but man ought to make"; price greater than "all the universe besides God" |
| II.11 | his death is not owed: given "not of debt but freely" |
| II.19–20 | the reward passes to his brethren; a reward "greater than all debt" remits all debt |

- Deane numbers Book II ch. 18 as (a) and (b); other editions differ.
- **Critics:** Harnack (a juridical construction), Aulén (the "Latin" type), Southern
  (the feudal-honour setting, and its limits). I.15: nothing can be added to or taken
  from God's honour; it names the order of the universe.
- **Level:** that Christ made satisfaction is Trent VI ch. 7; the mechanism is
  theology with no act; the **necessity** is freely disputed; a Doctor's title does
  not raise a thesis. Boss 3's premise reconstruction is not reproduced here.
- Anselm as philosopher: [`ancient-medieval-philosophy` 6.1](../ancient-medieval-philosophy/lessons/06-01-anselm-and-the-proslogion-as-history.md).

Lessons: [3.3](lessons/03-03-anselm-cur-deus-homo.md)

### Satisfaction or punishment

**Anselm's fork, CDH I.13–15: the honour taken away "must be repaid, or punishment
must follow".** Satisfaction is honour repaid **freely**; punishment is God taking
back from the **unwilling** (I.14). I.15's "unfitting ... consequently impossible"
is the step that turns **fittingness into necessity**, the step Aquinas refuses: God
could have forgiven without satisfaction, since he has no one above him (III q.46 a.2
ad 3), and chose satisfaction as "more copious mercy" (a.1 ad 3). Christ's death is on
the satisfaction branch; this is not penal substitution.

Lessons: [3.3](lessons/03-03-anselm-cur-deus-homo.md), [3.4](lessons/03-04-aquinas-satisfaction-and-the-debt-of-punishment.md)

### Reatus poenae

**The debt of punishment: the liability a disturbed order owes back to justice**
(ST I-II q.87 a.1: three orders, three punishments, from conscience, human authority
and God).

- It **outlasts the act** until "penal compensation" restores equality (a.6).
- **Stain and debt come apart** (a.6): the stain goes when the will is reunited to
  God; a debt may remain, "not indeed of punishment simply, but of satisfactory
  punishment".
- **Eternal and temporal separate** (III q.86 a.4).
- **Transferable in love** as satisfactory, never as penal (q.87 aa.7–8).
- Christ's Passion frees from it **directly** (payment) and **indirectly** (removing
  the sin it rests on) (III q.49 a.3); baptism imposes no satisfaction; after a second
  fall the member is conformed to the head by a lighter penalty (ad 2).
- **Level:** Aquinas's machinery is theology; the survival of temporal debt is defined
  (Trent VI can. 30).

Lessons: [3.4](lessons/03-04-aquinas-satisfaction-and-the-debt-of-punishment.md), [3.5](lessons/03-05-the-christian-jubilee-and-the-treasury.md)

### The treasury of the Church

**Christ's superabundant satisfaction and merits, with the saints' works added through
him, the common property of the mystical body, dispensed by the keys.**

- **Supplement q.25 a.1** (compiled after Aquinas's death from *In IV Sent.* d.20):
  saints satisfied "exceeding the requirements of their debts"; the merits are "the
  common property of the whole Church"; distributed by "him who rules them all".
  Ad 2: the penitent is "given the means whereby he may pay"; ad 3: never guilt.
- **History:** Hugh of St Cher usually credited (c. 1230); Alexander of Hales,
  Albert, Aquinas. *Unigenitus* (Clement VI, 27 Jan 1343): not a drop but a torrent of
  blood, an infinite treasure entrusted to Peter. *Cum postquam* (1518).
  *Indulgentiarum Doctrina* 5 (1967). CCC 1476–1477 (communion of saints, 1474–1475).
- **Level: authentic ordinary magisterium (rung 3), NOT defined.** A bull teaching a
  doctrine is not an *ex cathedra* definition; Trent XXV never uses the word; *Exsurge*
  prop. 17 is censured globally; the manuals graded it variously; CE 1912's
  "dogmatically set forth" overreaches. Calling it "just a scholastic theory"
  understates just as badly.
- **Commutation vs indulgence:** Egbert's twenty-six solidi re-denominate self-payment;
  an indulgence pays from the common fund. **Restitution to a neighbour is never
  commuted** (Cummian's penitential).

Lessons: [3.5](lessons/03-05-the-christian-jubilee-and-the-treasury.md), [3.6](lessons/03-06-luther-trent-and-the-reform-of-indulgences.md)

### Indulgence

**A remission before God of the temporal punishment due to sins whose guilt has
already been forgiven** (CCC 1471; *Indulgentiarum Doctrina*). It never remits
guilt (*a culpa et a poena* is the error Nicholas of Cusa condemned at a Magdeburg
synod). *Cum postquam*: to the living *per modum absolutionis*, to the dead *per
modum suffragii*.

- **Defined:** the Church's power to grant them and their usefulness (Trent XXV,
  anathema). Catholics may choose not to seek one; denying the power falls under the
  anathema.
- **Rung 3:** the treasury as mechanism.
- **Discipline:** the Jubilee intervals (100, 50 from 1343, 25 by the later fifteenth
  century), the 1967 norms (no days and years, norm 4; partial measured by the act's
  charity, norm 5; one plenary a day, norm 6), the Jubilee indulgence of 2000.
- **The hard record:** Clovesho 747, Lateran IV's cap, Boniface IX 1392, Cusa, Swanson's
  pardoners. Practice belongs to [`sacraments-and-liturgy`](../sacraments-and-liturgy/syllabus.md);
  purgatory to [`creation-grace-last-things`](../creation-grace-last-things/syllabus.md).

Lessons: [3.5](lessons/03-05-the-christian-jubilee-and-the-treasury.md), [3.6](lessons/03-06-luther-trent-and-the-reform-of-indulgences.md)

### Trent on indulgences

**Session XXV, Decree concerning Indulgences, 4 December 1563 (Waterworth).** Defines
with anathema that the power was granted by Christ to the Church and that the use is
"most salutary"; urges moderation; orders "all evil gains for the obtaining thereof
... be wholly abolished"; sends other abuses to bishops and pope. **It never names the
treasury.** The year before, Session XXI reform ch. 9 (16 July 1562) abolished the
*quaestores* and had indulgences published by bishops, "not for gain". Trent conceded
the abuse at the level of fact, defined the power at rung 1, and reformed by discipline.

### The Lutheran objection to satisfactions

**As the Lutheran confessions state it:**

1. Christ's satisfaction covers guilt **and** eternal death (Apology XII, *Triglot*
   §43, from Hosea 13:14).
2. Forgiveness by faith is total; afflictions and works are fruits, not compensation
   (§46, §50).
3. Canonical satisfactions are human traditions "not necessary by divine Law" for
   guilt, eternal punishment or purgatory (§50); counting them as payment obscures
   Christ.
4. The keys do not reach purgatory; indulgences once remitted public church penances
   (§78–79).
5. The Church's true treasure is "the Most Holy Gospel of the glory and the grace
   of God" (thesis 62, Philadelphia 1915).

- *Triglot* prints this part of Apology XII under its own head, "Of Confession and
  Satisfaction", with separate paragraph numbers.
- **1517 is not 1531:** the Theses still allow the pope to remit penalties he imposed
  (theses 5, 20, 61); thesis 58 denies the treasury, not Christ's merits.
- **Rome's answers:** *Cum postquam* (1518), *Exsurge Domine* (15 June 1520, prop. 17 =
  thesis 58), Trent VI can. 30, XIV ch. 8 ("in whom we satisfy"), XXV. The Catholic
  reply rests on temporal punishment, the communion of saints and "one mystical
  person" (III q.48 a.2 ad 1). Separability of doctrine and abuse is argued in
  [`apologetics-catholic-claims`](../apologetics-catholic-claims/syllabus.md).
  Boss 3's apologetic answer is not reproduced here.

## Usury: the prohibition

### Usury

**The classical sense: any gain demanded beyond the principal (*sors*) of a *mutuum*
by reason of the loan itself, however small.** Its statements, in order:

| Source | Formulation |
|---|---|
| Torah | any "bite" or "increase" to the poor brother ([neshek and tarbit](#neshek-and-tarbit)) |
| Fathers | taking interest (Ps 14:5 DR, Ezek 18:8, 13, Luke 6:35); Jerome: any surplus, in kind too |
| Gratian (c. 1140) | anything demanded beyond the principal |
| *Consuluit* | the **intention** of gain is the sin, even without a contract |
| Aquinas q.78 a.1 | payment taken for the use of money lent: selling what does not exist, or the same thing twice |
| Lateran V (reported) | gain from the use of a thing that does not bear fruit, without labor, expense or risk ([Inter multiplices](#inter-multiplices)) |
| *Vix Pervenit* 3.I | gain claimed *ipsius ratione mutui*; moderation, a rich borrower, productive use excuse nothing (3.II) |
| Propaganda 1873 nos. 1–2; CIC 1917 can. 1543 | nothing by force of the loan itself |
| CCC 2269, 2438 | "usurious" used, never defined |

- ***Usura* is not "excessive interest".** In q.78 any agreed surplus is usury;
  a 40% charge fully covered by real titles is not.
- **The modern magisterial sense** is lending that exploits need
  ([usury in modern teaching](#usury-in-modern-teaching)). Always ask which sense a
  text uses.
- **The 5.4 crux:** does "usury" in Vienne and VP 3.I denote every charge above
  principal on a money loan as then practised (wide, Noonan) or gain claimed by reason
  of the loan, excluding titles (narrow, continuity)? The wording *ipsius ratione
  mutui* favours the narrow; Noonan answers from what the tradition *did*.
- Luke 16 can illustrate the doctrine, not carry it.

Lessons: [2.3](lessons/02-03-the-parables-of-debt.md), [4.1](lessons/04-01-the-fathers-against-usury.md), [4.2](lessons/04-02-from-canon-to-council.md), [4.3](lessons/04-03-aquinas-on-usury-ii-ii-q78.md), [4.4](lessons/04-04-the-extrinsic-titles.md), [5.1](lessons/05-01-the-monti-di-pieta-and-lateran-v.md), [5.2](lessons/05-02-vix-pervenit.md), [5.3](lessons/05-03-not-to-be-disturbed.md), [5.4](lessons/05-04-did-the-usury-doctrine-change.md), [6.1](lessons/06-01-usury-and-finance-in-modern-teaching.md)

### Mutuum

**The loan of a consumptible: ownership and risk pass to the borrower, who owes back
an equal quantity whatever happens** (ST II-II q.78 a.2 ad 5; a.1 ad 5). A charge for
the loan as such is usury. The moralists' three questions for any contract paying a
return on money: **who owns the capital, who bears the risk, can the investor demand
the principal regardless?** A loan answers: borrower; borrower; yes. What is lent is
the lender's, so a borrower who can repay and does not keeps another's property
([excusing causes](#excusing-causes)).

Lessons: [4.3](lessons/04-03-aquinas-on-usury-ii-ii-q78.md), [4.4](lessons/04-04-the-extrinsic-titles.md), [4.5](lessons/04-05-contracts-that-are-not-loans.md), [5.2](lessons/05-02-vix-pervenit.md), [6.3](lessons/06-03-the-ethics-of-personal-debt.md)

### Consumptibles

**Things whose use is their consumption: wine, wheat, and (as Aquinas reads
Aristotle) money, whose proper use is to be spent.** For them use cannot be sold apart
from the thing, so lending transfers ownership; a house or cart survives its use and
may be let for rent (a.1 body; ad 6, the silver vessel). Lateran V's *quae non
germinat*, "that does not bear fruit", is the point in a clause. The argument's
soundness as philosophy: [`philosophy-of-debt`](../philosophy-of-debt/syllabus.md) 2.2.

Lessons: [4.3](lessons/04-03-aquinas-on-usury-ii-ii-q78.md), [5.1](lessons/05-01-the-monti-di-pieta-and-lateran-v.md)

### Commutative justice

**Justice in exchanges between persons, measured by equality of thing to thing**
(ST II-II q.61 a.1). Usury breaks it; [restitution](#restitution) is its act (q.62
a.1). *Vix Pervenit* 3.II: its office is to keep each contract's equality and repair
it exactly. It forbids the lender to end up richer by lending, and equally poorer
(the ground of the [titles](#the-extrinsic-titles)).

- A debt owed a **neighbour** is not a debt of punishment owed **God**: no
  commutation or indulgence touches it.
- CA 35 opens by affirming that "debts must be paid" before its limit; CCC 2410–2411:
  contracts bind to the extent just, and commutative justice obliges strictly,
  including paying debts.
- **Owned here, applied to debt;** the virtue itself is
  [`moral-theology` 3.3](../moral-theology/lessons/03-03-the-cardinal-virtues-and-the-gifts.md).

Lessons: [3.5](lessons/03-05-the-christian-jubilee-and-the-treasury.md), [4.3](lessons/04-03-aquinas-on-usury-ii-ii-q78.md), [5.2](lessons/05-02-vix-pervenit.md), [6.2](lessons/06-02-international-debt-and-the-jubilee-call.md), [6.3](lessons/06-03-the-ethics-of-personal-debt.md)

### Restitution

**The act of commutative justice returning what is owed to the one wronged**,
necessary for salvation (II-II q.62 a.2). Absolution does not remit it; no
penitential commutation or indulgence does either (Cummian shortened a thief's
penance only if he restored).

| Case | Rule |
|---|---|
| usury taken in money, wheat, wine (q.78 a.3) | restore the usury plus any loss caused; keep profits of one's own industry |
| house or land extorted by usury | restore the thing and its fruits |
| goods bought with usurious money (ad 2, *Cum tu sicut asseris*) | the usurer's, but liable up to what was taken |
| *Vix Pervenit* 3.II, 3.IV, 10 | usury and any other excess over a contract's equality restored in both fora; licit fruit kept |
| a loan (q.62 a.6) | owed by reason of the taking, not only the thing, even if the money is lost |
| timing (q.62 a.8) | at once if possible; else ask remission or respite |
| sharing in injustice (CCC 2412) | restitution in proportion to one's part |

Lessons: [3.4](lessons/03-04-aquinas-satisfaction-and-the-debt-of-punishment.md), [3.5](lessons/03-05-the-christian-jubilee-and-the-treasury.md), [4.3](lessons/04-03-aquinas-on-usury-ii-ii-q78.md), [5.2](lessons/05-02-vix-pervenit.md), [6.3](lessons/06-03-the-ethics-of-personal-debt.md)

### The patristic arguments against usury

1. **Scripture:** Psalm 15:5 [DR 14:5], Ezekiel 18, Luke 6:34–35 ("lend, hoping for
   nothing thereby").
2. **Cruelty to the poor:** Basil, homily on Psalm 14, the *tokos* pun (money breeds;
   the brood ruins the poor); he tells the poor to sell or work rather than borrow.
3. **Increase without labor:** Gregory of Nyssa, *Against Usurers*: the usurer ploughs
   with pen and paper. (Not yet "money is sterile": that is Aristotle via the
   scholastics.)
4. **Charity as the norm:** Leo the Great, Sermon 17: "holy usury" is lending to the
   Lord; "usury of money is the death of the soul" (Latin *fenus* / *funus*, in the
   sermon, not Letter 4).
5. **[The war exception](#the-war-exception)** (Ambrose).

Basil, Nyssa and Ambrose are **paraphrased only**; no public-domain English is relied
on. **Level:** near-unanimous consent on morals, locus 6, a heavy witness; Vincent's
test is itself not magisterial. What the consensus condemned (any gain, or gain wrung
from need) is 5.4's question. See [`patristics` 3.2](../patristics/lessons/03-02-basil-the-great.md), [3.3](../patristics/lessons/03-03-the-two-gregories.md), [4.1](../patristics/lessons/04-01-ambrose-of-milan.md), [5.2](../patristics/lessons/05-02-leo-the-great.md).

Lessons: [4.1](lessons/04-01-the-fathers-against-usury.md)

### The war exception

**Ambrose, *On Tobias* (paraphrased): Deuteronomy 23:20's "foreigner" is the enemy;
take interest only from one you could lawfully kill, since the right of usury reaches
exactly as far as the right of war.** "Brother" is first the fellow in the faith, then
every fellow under Roman law. Not an ethnic rule; its later use in framing
Christian–Jewish lending belongs to [`church-history` 5.3](../church-history/lessons/05-03-heresy-inquisition-and-the-jews.md)
and [`history-of-debt` 2.2](../history-of-debt/lessons/02-02-jewish-lenders-and-the-monti-di-pieta.md).

Lessons: [4.1](lessons/04-01-the-fathers-against-usury.md)

### Nicaea canon 17

**Nicaea (325; NPNF2-14): clergy who take "the hundredth", the *hemiolia* (repay the
whole and one half) or "any other contrivance whatever for filthy lucre's sake" are
deposed.**

- Binds **clergy** only; ground: Psalm 15 [DR 14]:5 ("He hath not given his money upon
  usury"), not a prophet.
- "[as monthly interest]" is the translator's bracket: 1% a month, 12% simple,
  ≈12.7% compounded ([the hundredth part](#the-hundredth-part)).
- Kin canons: Apostolic Canon 44 (depose unless he desists); Carthage 419 c.5 (lay
  usury reprehensible, sharper for clergy). Leo, Letter 4 (10 Oct 443, to bishops of
  Campania, Picenum, Etruria and all the provinces): lay usury punished sharply.
- Early practice: generally no penalty on laymen; Desideratus of Verdun borrowing *cum
  usuris legitimis* (Gregory of Tours, HF III.34).
- **Level:** a disciplinary canon, off the ladder; it defines nothing and presupposes
  the moral judgment. "Nicaea defined interest sinful" overstates.
- The event: [`church-history` 2.1](../church-history/lessons/02-01-nicaea-and-the-imperial-church.md).

Lessons: [4.1](lessons/04-01-the-fathers-against-usury.md)

### Consuluit

**Urban III (pope 1185–87), decretal *Consuluit*, X 5.19.10 (universal law via the
*Liber Extra*, 1234).** A man who lends without agreement but would not lend unless he
expected more back, and a merchant who sells dearer on credit, both sin **by the
intention of gain**, for Luke 6:35 says "lend, hoping for nothing thereby". Usury
becomes a sin of the will before it is a clause: a confessor could find it where a
judge found no contract. Noonan cites it for the precept reading of Luke 6:35.
**Level:** authentic papal teaching in juridical form; defines nothing.

Lessons: [4.2](lessons/04-02-from-canon-to-council.md), [5.4](lessons/05-04-did-the-usury-doctrine-change.md)

### Lateran IV canon 67

**Lateran IV (1215): Christians are compelled by censure to cut off dealings with
Jewish lenders who take "oppressive and excessive" interest, until satisfaction;
princes may not punish the boycott; Jews holding once-tithed property must make the
tithes good.** Its language about Jews is hostile, and it stands beside canon 68 on
distinctive dress. Jews were not under the Christian ban, so moderate Jewish interest
was in effect tolerated while any Christian interest stayed forbidden: "excessive" is
**not** a moderate-interest rule for Christians. **Level:** disciplinary law. The
Church's later judgment on such hostility: *Nostra Aetate* 4 (1965). History:
[`history-of-debt` 2.2](../history-of-debt/lessons/02-02-jewish-lenders-and-the-monti-di-pieta.md),
[`church-history` 5.3](../church-history/lessons/05-03-heresy-inquisition-and-the-jews.md).

Lessons: [4.2](lessons/04-02-from-canon-to-council.md)

### The Council of Vienne on usury

**Decree *Ex gravi ad nos* (Clementines 5.5, sole chapter; decree 29 in Tanner),
1311–12. Three parts:**

- **(a)** officials who make, keep or apply statutes compelling debtors to pay usury
  are excommunicated; statutes struck within three months;
- **(b)** money-lenders compelled by censure to open their account books;
- **(c)** whoever **pertinaciously asserts that practising usury is not a sin** is
  punished as a heretic; ordinaries and inquisitors proceed against suspects.

**Level:** (a) and (b) are discipline; (c) is the heaviest act in the series and
places "usury is a sin" within the faith: **at least theologically certain, *de fide*
argued.** Heresy attaches to the **assertion**, not the practice. What "usury"
**denotes** is **not settled** by the decree: that is 5.4's crux. Demoting (c) to
discipline and stretching it to "all interest" are equal errors. Paraphrased, not
quoted.

Lessons: [4.2](lessons/04-02-from-canon-to-council.md), [4.3](lessons/04-03-aquinas-on-usury-ii-ii-q78.md), [4.4](lessons/04-04-the-extrinsic-titles.md), [5.2](lessons/05-02-vix-pervenit.md), [5.4](lessons/05-04-did-the-usury-doctrine-change.md)

### Disciplinary canon and doctrinal declaration

**A disciplinary canon is law: it binds those under the legislator's jurisdiction,
carries a sanction, and can be changed by the same authority; it presupposes moral
teaching without raising it. A doctrinal declaration says what belongs to faith and is
graded by the ladder.** One document can hold both (Vienne (a)–(b) vs (c)). Test each
text: **is it imposing a penalty, or declaring what must be believed?** Escalating
penalties show the teaching was thought settled; they do not raise its level.

| Text | Date | Kind | Content |
|---|---|---|---|
| *Admonitio generalis* c.5 (Aachen) | 789 | royal law, no rung | usury "altogether forbidden to everyone", citing Nicaea, Leo, the apostolic canons, the Law |
| Gratian, *Decretum* C.14 qq.3–4 | c. 1140 | private compilation | anything beyond the principal |
| Lateran II c.13 | 1139 | conciliar discipline | usurers infamous for life, no Christian burial unless repentant |
| Lateran III c.25 | 1179 | conciliar discipline | *manifest* usurers denied communion and burial; clergy who bury them give back what they received, suspended |
| *Consuluit* | 1185–87 | papal decretal, universal law 1234 | intention of gain is the sin |
| Lateran IV c.67 | 1215 | conciliar discipline | boycott of Jewish lenders taking excessive interest |
| Lyons II, *Usurarum voraginem* + next constitution (26–27 Tanner) | 1274 | conciliar discipline | no letting houses to foreign usurers, expulsion in three months; no burial or valid will without restitution |
| Vienne, *Ex gravi ad nos* | 1311–12 | discipline (a–b) + doctrinal (c) | see [Vienne](#the-council-of-vienne-on-usury) |

"Manifest" in Lateran III is a juridical test (notoriety): a secret usurer sinned no
less but fell outside the canon.

Lessons: [4.1](lessons/04-01-the-fathers-against-usury.md), [4.2](lessons/04-02-from-canon-to-council.md)

### The extrinsic titles

**Grounds for receiving more than the principal from a circumstance outside the loan,
which can be named, measured and proved apart from it.** Compensating a real harm
restores equality; it does not exceed it. *Interesse* ("what the difference is", traced
to Azo c. 1200) named the **licit** payment, the opposite of *usura*.

| Title | Means | History |
|---|---|---|
| [*damnum emergens*](#damnum-emergens) | loss incurred | admitted by Aquinas, even agreed in advance |
| [*lucrum cessans*](#lucrum-cessans) | profit forgone | refused by Aquinas; admitted narrowly, then widely |
| [*periculum sortis*](#periculum-sortis) | real above-ordinary risk to principal | contested by *Naviganti*; later common |
| [*poena conventionalis*](#poena-conventionalis) | penalty for culpable delay | valid only for real, unexpected delay |

- **Four conditions for every title:** real, caused by the loan, proportionate
  (ceiling = the harm), not presumed.
- **VP 3.III** does not deny that titles "not innate and intrinsic to the nature of the
  loan" can ground a surplus; **3.V** forbids presuming them "always found and
  everywhere at hand"; it names none.
- Lateran V's labor/expense/risk tests anticipate them; its approval covered expense
  only, up to cost.
- **Not the same as non-loan contracts:** titles justify something extra *on a loan*;
  partnership, census and exchange are not loans.
- **Level:** common teaching (4); CE 1912 reports the four admitted without dispute
  since the late sixteenth century (Langholm dates full acceptance of *lucrum cessans*
  to c. 1642). "Titles are dogma" overstates; "mere loopholes" understates.
- A modern rate split into titles: [rate decomposition](#titles-and-contracts).

Lessons: [4.4](lessons/04-04-the-extrinsic-titles.md), [4.5](lessons/04-05-contracts-that-are-not-loans.md), [5.1](lessons/05-01-the-monti-di-pieta-and-lateran-v.md), [5.2](lessons/05-02-vix-pervenit.md), [5.4](lessons/05-04-did-the-usury-doctrine-change.md), [6.1](lessons/06-01-usury-and-finance-in-modern-teaching.md)

### Damnum emergens

**Loss actually incurred by lending: an outlay or loss the lender suffers because he
lent.** Admitted by Aquinas, and contractable in advance: "this is not to sell the use
of money but to avoid a loss" (q.78 a.2 ad 1). Many medieval canonists admitted it
only for loss arising after the loan, typically through delay. Ceiling: the loss.
Example: a calf sold at 250 dollars in November instead of 300 in spring to fund the
loan: compensation up to 50 is just.

Lessons: [4.3](lessons/04-03-aquinas-on-usury-ii-ii-q78.md), [4.4](lessons/04-04-the-extrinsic-titles.md)

### Lucrum cessans

**Profit forgone by lending.**

- **Refused** by Aquinas: the lender "must not sell that which he has not yet and may
  be prevented in many ways from having" (a.2 ad 1). The later debate is whether that
  reason forbids the title or only valuing an uncertain gain as certain.
- **Admitted narrowly** by Hostiensis (d. 1271): a merchant habitually trading at
  fairs lends, from charity, money he would have traded with, absent fraud.
- **With conditions:** Soto (*De iustitia et iure*, 1553); Molina treated it as loss in
  a wider sense; **Lessius, the Louvain Jesuit** (*De iustitia et iure*, 1605), extended
  it to any lender where a money market makes lacking money a real cost (not
  "Salamanca").
- **Nineteenth century:** the "productive capital" rationale made it the normal
  condition of every loan, in tension with VP 3.V.
- Economically, opportunity cost. **The strain:** a title present in every loan looks
  like a price for the loan itself.

Lessons: [4.3](lessons/04-03-aquinas-on-usury-ii-ii-q78.md), [4.4](lessons/04-04-the-extrinsic-titles.md), [5.3](lessons/05-03-not-to-be-disturbed.md), [5.4](lessons/05-04-did-the-usury-doctrine-change.md)

### Periculum sortis

**A premium for real, above-ordinary risk to the principal.** Gregory IX's decretal
*Naviganti* (X 5.19.19, Decretals 1234) called a sea-loan lender who takes a fixed
increase *because he bears the risk* a usurer; later moralists admitted risk
nonetheless, and how they squared it with *Naviganti* was argued. Break-even premium at
zero recovery: $p/(1-p)$ ([rates and ratios](#rates-and-ratios)). Lateran V approved no
payment for risk.

Lessons: [4.4](lessons/04-04-the-extrinsic-titles.md), [5.1](lessons/05-01-the-monti-di-pieta-and-lateran-v.md), [5.4](lessons/05-04-did-the-usury-doctrine-change.md)

### Poena conventionalis

**An agreed penalty for culpable delay past the due date.** Must be a real penalty for
real delay. If both parties expect the "late" payment, or it runs whether or not
there is delay, it is interest *in fraudem usurarum*. "Late fee" is not a magic word.

Lessons: [4.4](lessons/04-04-the-extrinsic-titles.md)

### Societas

**Partnership: the investor keeps ownership, so the money trades at his risk and his
profit share is fruit of his own property** (q.78 a.2 ad 5). Corollary: a "partner"
whose capital is guaranteed by the working partner is a lender. Share the loss or you
are not a partner. **Level:** common teaching (4).

Lessons: [4.3](lessons/04-03-aquinas-on-usury-ii-ii-q78.md), [4.5](lessons/04-05-contracts-that-are-not-loans.md)

### Census

**A redeemable rent charge: a lump sum buys a yearly rent charged on fruitful
property.** Martin V, *Regimini universalis* (1425, answering doubts in the Empire),
reissued under the same title by Callixtus III (1455): not usurious. Conditions on the
moralists' reading: real fruitful property; rent proportionate to its yield; the
**seller** may redeem, the **buyer** cannot call the principal; the rent fails if the
property perishes. Same cash as a loan (60 on 1,000 = 6%), different rights when
things go wrong. **Level:** rung 3 for the practice under its conditions; defines
nothing about interest in general. A personal rent redeemable at the buyer's demand
fails the conditions.

Lessons: [4.5](lessons/04-05-contracts-that-are-not-loans.md)

### Dry exchange

**A bill of exchange drawn and returned with no real transfer between places, settled
at a pre-agreed sum:** the "exchange" is a form around a time-payment, a disguised
loan. A real exchange (money truly moved between places, return uncertain) was judged
licit. **Level in the lesson:** the moralists' condemnation, common teaching (4).

Lessons: [4.5](lessons/04-05-contracts-that-are-not-loans.md)

### The triple contract

**Partnership + insurance of the principal + sale of the uncertain profit for a fixed
return, all with one counterparty = a loan at a fixed rate.** Example: 1,000 invested,
expected profit 150, premium 40, profit sold for a fixed 90:
$1000 + 90 - 40 = 1050$ in every outcome; the merchant nets $X - 50$, having in effect
borrowed at 5%.

- **Eck's defence** (disputations 1514–16, Bologna 1515; Fugger cause): three licit
  contracts cannot become illicit by combination; about 5% is a fair price for
  giving up an uncertain gain.
- **Sixtus V, *Detestabilis avaritia* (1586):** partnerships with guaranteed capital
  or a fixed risk-free gain are usurious, with the penalties for notorious usurers.
  The bull does not use the name "triple contract". **Level:** rung 3 as a moral
  judgment; **reach argued** (Azor, Lessius read it narrowly; the defence was
  widespread by the late seventeenth century). Neither a definition nor mere advice.
- **Decisive feature:** the **same counterparty** on every leg, collapsing ownership
  and risk back onto the merchant: the waiting "has the character of a loan" (q.78 a.2
  ad 7). Boss 4(d)'s general algebra is left to the problem.

Lessons: [4.5](lessons/04-05-contracts-that-are-not-loans.md)

## Usury: development

### Monti di pieta

**Franciscan civic pawn funds lending small sums to the poor against pledges for a
moderate fee covering costs only.** Perugia 1462, after Michele Carcano's preaching;
planning credited to Barnabas of Terni and Fortunato Coppoli. Dominican critics
(Cajetan, *De monte pietatis*, 1498): the fee is fixed, grows with sum and time, and is
a condition of the loan; the city, not the poor, should carry the costs. Defenders:
*qui commodum sentit, onus quoque sentire debeat*, the one who benefits bears the
cost.

- **Lateran V (1515)** declared them not usurious and meritorious
  ([Inter multiplices](#inter-multiplices)).
- **The hard fact:** the founding preaching was also a campaign against licensed Jewish
  lenders, often openly hostile (Trent 1475, Bernardino da Feltre). The bull mentions
  only the poor and "usurers". History: [`history-of-debt` 2.2](../history-of-debt/lessons/02-02-jewish-lenders-and-the-monti-di-pieta.md).
- **Heirs:** *Caritas in Veritate* 65 traces micro-finance to "the birth of
  pawnbroking"; credit unions: Desjardins's *caisses populaires* (Quebec, from 1900),
  St. Mary's Bank, Manchester NH (1908, Msgr Pierre Hevey), the first US credit union.

Lessons: [5.1](lessons/05-01-the-monti-di-pieta-and-lateran-v.md), [6.1](lessons/06-01-usury-and-finance-in-modern-teaching.md), [6.3](lessons/06-03-the-ethics-of-personal-debt.md)

### Inter multiplices

**Lateran V, Session X, 4 May 1515, under Leo X (DH 1442–1444).** "With the approval
of the sacred council, we declare and define" that monti approved by the Holy See,
charging something moderate "solely for the expenses" and "without profit", are not
usurious, and the loan is meritorious. Anyone who henceforth preaches or disputes
against it incurs excommunication *latae sententiae*. Counsel: fully free monti are
"more perfect", with indulgences for donors covering at least half the wages.

- **The "proper interpretation of usury"** (gain sought from the use of a thing that
  does not bear fruit, "with no labor, no expense and no risk") sits **in the recital
  of the critics' case**, introduced by *enim*. "Declare and define" governs the ruling
  on the monti. Reading (A): the council's own definition, since the ruling depends on
  it. Reading (B): a reported premise, one party's gloss. **Its conciliar weight is
  argued.** Say "the definition reported in Lateran V".
- **Level of the ruling:** binding, at least authentic magisterium; whether an
  infallible definition is argued (object is a verdict on a conditioned contract type;
  can. 749 §3). The critics are commended, not condemned.
- Not the 1690 bull *Inter multiplices pastoralis officii* (Alexander VIII).

Lessons: [5.1](lessons/05-01-the-monti-di-pieta-and-lateran-v.md), [5.4](lessons/05-04-did-the-usury-doctrine-change.md)

### Vix Pervenit

**Benedict XIV, encyclical to the patriarchs, archbishops, bishops and ordinaries of
Italy, 1 November 1745,** after a commission (4 July; congregations 18 July and 1
August) whose five propositions (§3) the pope "approves and confirms" (§4). Occasion:
Scipione Maffei's *Dell'impiego del danaro* (Verona 1744). Background: Calvin's letter
(usually dated 1545). Read in the lesson's own translation of the Latin.

| § | Content |
|---|---|
| 3.I | usury has its seat in the loan contract: gain claimed beyond the principal by reason of the loan itself |
| 3.II | no excuse from a moderate rate, a rich borrower, productive use; restitution by commutative justice |
| 3.III | extrinsic titles and contracts of another nature (annual revenues, lawful trade) not denied |
| 3.IV | those contracts keep their own equality; excess is "some other true injustice" |
| 3.V | no presuming titles "always found and everywhere at hand"; inquire first (cites Matt 5:42) |
| 5 | teach nothing contrary, under canonical penalties |
| 6 | the pope decides nothing about the disputed contract or others theologians dispute |
| 8 | avoid rigorist and laxist extremes; no grave censures on a supported opinion |
| 9 | declare the contract beforehand, against *palliata usura* |
| 10 | the usury question is not "of name only"; licit fruit kept, illicit restored in both fora |

- **Level:** authentic papal magisterium (3) at least; no defining formula.
  **Reach argued:** narrow (addressed to Italy; CE 1912 says not infallible) vs wider
  (Holy Office 29 July 1836 incidental declaration for the whole Church, reported by CE;
  §4's claim to restate common teaching). What §6 leaves and §8 shields is freely
  disputed.
- Names no author; Maffei reprinted his book (Rome 1746) with the encyclical bound in.
- It does not mention Lateran V or the monti.

Lessons: [5.2](lessons/05-02-vix-pervenit.md), [5.4](lessons/05-04-did-the-usury-doctrine-change.md)

### Non inquietandos

**"Not to be disturbed": the Roman formula for *practically safe*, not *true*.**

| Reply | Date, DH | What it says |
|---|---|---|
| Pius VIII to the bishop of Rennes (in audience) | 18 Aug 1830, DH 2722–2724 | **confessors** who absolve penitents lending to merchants at interest, after a promise to obey any future papal judgment, are "not to be disturbed" |
| Penitentiary to Besançon | July 1831, DH 2725–2726 | same formula for confessors following Alphonsus Liguori |
| Holy Office to the bishop of Nice | 17 Jan 1838, DH 2743 | moderate gain "on the sole title of the law" absolved without restitution: "Affirmative, *dummodo parati sint stare mandatis S. Sedis*" |
| Propaganda Fide instruction | 1873, DH 3105–3109 | nos. 1–2 restate VP; no. 3 legal title sufficient *in praxi* "so long as this question hangs under judgment"; no. 4 never toward the poor or immoderate; no. 5 no universal line |

- The 1830 reply concerns **confessors**, not penitents or interest, and does not
  mention civil law; *dummodo parati sint* is the **1838** reply.
- Reasoning: a confessor may not impose a genuinely doubtful obligation
  ([`moral-theology` 4.1](../moral-theology/lessons/04-01-casuistry-and-the-probabilism-controversy.md)).
- **Level:** pastoral directives, **not definitions, and they said so**; binding on
  confessors as practice. Under Newman's conservative action they cut both ways:
  formula kept, endorsement withheld, practice allowed. With can. 1543 they let
  moderate legal interest be taken without revising VP.

Lessons: [5.3](lessons/05-03-not-to-be-disturbed.md), [5.4](lessons/05-04-did-the-usury-doctrine-change.md), [6.1](lessons/06-01-usury-and-finance-in-modern-teaching.md)

### Titulus legis

**The legal title: the theory that civil law permitting moderate interest itself
creates a just title (or, weaker, is a fair public presumption that the usual titles
are present).** Context: French decree of October 1789; Code civil art. 1905 (1804);
law of 1807 capping 5% civil, 6% commercial. Companion rationale: money as
**productive capital**, making *lucrum cessans* universal, in tension with VP 3.V.
**Level:** tolerated in practice by the replies and the 1873 instruction, never
judged as theory; freely disputed.

Lessons: [5.3](lessons/05-03-not-to-be-disturbed.md)

### Canon 1543

**1917 Code (Book III, Part VI on temporal goods, Title XXIX on contracts): no gain on
a loan of fungibles "by reason of the contract itself"; but agreeing on the legal rate
is not in itself unlawful unless clearly immoderate, and a higher rate may be agreed on
a just and proportionate title.** Its first clause repeats the doctrine; the permission
follows the "but".

- **1983 Code:** no counterpart. Can. 1284 §2 5° (pay interest due on a loan or
  mortgage, repay capital) and 6° (invest surplus with the ordinary's consent). Its
  silence repeals the canon as law and restores nothing.
- **Level:** universal law of the Latin Church (discipline), binding until 1983; what
  it implies doctrinally (the legal title accepted, or only a presumption for practice)
  is argued.

Lessons: [5.3](lessons/05-03-not-to-be-disturbed.md), [5.4](lessons/05-04-did-the-usury-doctrine-change.md)

### Development of doctrine

**Vincent of Lérins's growth clause (the same doctrine, the same sense, the same
meaning), taken into *Dei Filius* ch. 4, and Newman's notes.** On usury three notes
bite:

- **Preservation of type:** continuity says "the loan is gratuitous; gain needs a
  title"; Noonan says the type was a *practice* of gratuitous lending, now gone.
- **Continuity of principles:** commutative justice runs through q.78, VP 3.II and the
  titles: continuity's strongest ground.
- **Conservative action upon its past:** the nineteenth-century replies cut both ways.

Parallel case: the 2018 CCC 2267 on the death penalty
([`moral-theology` 5.4](../moral-theology/lessons/05-04-the-death-penalty-and-ccc-2267.md)).
**Level:** Vincent's canon and Newman's notes are tools, no rung. Owners:
[`fundamental-theology` 5.1](../fundamental-theology/lessons/05-01-vincent-of-lerins.md),
[5.2](../fundamental-theology/lessons/05-02-newmans-theory-of-development.md).

Lessons: [5.4](lessons/05-04-did-the-usury-doctrine-change.md)

### Change or development

**Did the usury teaching change or develop? Freely disputed.**

| Position | Voice | Claim |
|---|---|---|
| change | John T. Noonan, *A Church That Can and Cannot Change* (2005) | the deposit cannot change, but moral teaching did (slavery, religious liberty, usury); forbidding X then permitting X is change |
| continuity (i) | Vermeersch (CE 1912 "Usury"); Dulles (*First Things*, Oct 2005: "clarified rather than overturned") | the principle stands; economic facts changed. Common form: titles, above all *lucrum cessans*, now nearly always present. Vermeersch's own variant rejects presumed titles and prices the general value of holding money |
| continuity (ii) | Brian McCall, *The Church and the Usurers* (2013) | the rule stands and much modern lending (mortgages for shelter included) breaks it; return to the prohibition |

- (i) and (ii) share continuity and split on facts; Noonan and (ii) share the premise
  that modern interest is what the old rule condemned, and split on whether the rule
  was right.
- **Crux:** the scope of "usury" in Vienne and VP 3.I. Evidence that would move it:
  could lenders then have claimed titles as widely as lenders now do?
- **Misreporting Noonan:** he claims *moral teaching* changed, not dogma.
- All paraphrased; no rung. Boss 5(d) verdicts are not given here.

Lessons: [5.4](lessons/05-04-did-the-usury-doctrine-change.md)

## Debt in modern Catholic teaching

### Usury in modern teaching

**Magisterial usage: lending that exploits need (rates beyond the borrower's reach,
profit drawn from another's need, debt that crushes).** Close to Italian criminal
*usura*.

| Text | Date | Content |
|---|---|---|
| *Rerum Novarum* 3 | 15 May 1891 | "rapacious usury ... under a different guise, but with like injustice" |
| *Rerum Novarum* 20 | 1891 | gathering "one's profit out of the need of another" condemned; "usurious dealing" |
| CCC 2269 | — | usurious and greedy dealings bringing hunger and death: indirect homicide (fifth commandment; fn cites Amos 8:4–10) |
| CCC 2409; 2438; 2449 | — | exploiting hardship; "abusive if not usurious" international systems; OT ban as care for the poor |
| *Caritas in Veritate* 45, 65 | 2009 | ethical finance, microcredit; credit unions; micro-finance from "the birth of pawnbroking"; protect the weak from usury |
| *Oeconomicae et Pecuniariae Quaestiones* 16 | dated 6 Jan, published 17 May 2018 | rates really beyond borrowers' reach illegitimate, alongside "usurious activities"; cooperative credit and microcredit encouraged |
| Francis to the National Anti-Usury Council | 3 Feb 2018 | usury a serious sin |
| Leo XIV to the same council | 18 Oct 2025 | a grave sin, at times very grave; quotes CCC 2269; extends to financial systems among nations |

- The two senses cut across: a cheap loan with no title is classical usury, not
  modern; a costly all-cost loan may pass the first and fail the second. The classical
  sense asks what the lender is paid *for*; the modern, what the loan *does*.
- **No act defines the modern sense or withdraws the classical one.** Whether consumer
  interest is classical usury is freely disputed. The Catechism's silence on interest
  as such repeals and reaffirms nothing.
- The CCC 2401–2463 range has no article on interest as such.

Lessons: [6.1](lessons/06-01-usury-and-finance-in-modern-teaching.md)

### Centesimus Annus 35

**John Paul II (1991): "The principle that debts must be paid is certainly just"; but
payment may not be demanded when it brings hunger and despair to entire peoples. Then
ways must be found to lighten, defer or even cancel the debt, consistent with peoples'
right to subsistence and progress.** Debtor nations owe effort, sacrifice and
stability. Its test is **effects on a people**, not a ratio (HIPC's 150%). The pledge
law at national scale; the national analogue of grave necessity
([excusing causes](#excusing-causes)). **Level:** principle rung 3; the choice of
remedy prudential. "The Church teaches all poor countries' debts must be cancelled"
overstates twice.

Lessons: [6.2](lessons/06-02-international-debt-and-the-jubilee-call.md)

### Shared responsibility for international debt

**Creditors and debtors share the causes and the remedy of unpayable debt.**
Pontifical Commission *Iustitia et Pax*, *At the Service of the Human Community: An
Ethical Approach to the International Debt Question* (27 Dec 1986; paraphrased, full
text not found); SRS 19 (1987: loans accepted "perhaps imprudent[ly]", debt service a
brake on development); Compendium 450 (creditor side: exchange rates, speculation;
debtor side: corruption, misuse; the people suffering are not responsible).

- **Moral hazard,** met without the term: premise that debts bind kept; duties laid on
  debtors; losses put on lenders who took the risk. How much imprudence relief causes
  is empirical ([`economics-of-debt`](../economics-of-debt/syllabus.md) 8.1).
- The 1986 text is curial; its principles gained papal weight via SRS 19 and CA 35.
- **Odious debt** is not Church doctrine ([`philosophy-of-debt`](../philosophy-of-debt/syllabus.md) 6.3).

Lessons: [6.2](lessons/06-02-international-debt-and-the-jubilee-call.md)

### Ecological debt

**Laudato Si' 51–52 (2015), quoted in *Spes Non Confundit* 16 (2024): a true
ecological debt runs North to South, from trade imbalances and disproportionate use of
natural resources; to be paid by limiting non-renewable energy and aiding sustainable
development.** LS 52 contrasts it with foreign debt as a means of control.

- **Level split:** the moral claim applies CCC 2439 (justice, not only charity, where
  prosperity rests on resources not fairly paid for): rung 3. Its size and accounting
  are empirical, which LS 188 says the Church does not presume to settle. The
  prescription is prudential.
- **Not an offset:** no text nets ecological against financial debt or names an
  amount.
- SNC 16 (9 May 2024, the Jubilee of 2025): forgive debts that will never be repaid,
  "justice" more than generosity; Lev 25:23; a hunger fund from military spending.

Lessons: [6.2](lessons/06-02-international-debt-and-the-jubilee-call.md)

### Excusing causes

**Inability suspends repayment while it lasts; it does not cancel the debt.**

- **Physical impossibility:** no means. **Moral impossibility:** paying would cost
  oneself or dependants the necessities of life.
- **Duties remain:** the intention to pay; ask the creditor "for remission or a
  respite" (ST II-II q.62 a.8 ad 2); pay by degrees if one can lay by a little (Slater:
  "of obligation").
- **The creditor's free remission extinguishes the debt.**
- Lending is ordinarily a **counsel** (q.78 a.1 ad 4; Matt 5:42), a duty where need is
  real and a gift would be owed anyway (almsgiving from surplus is a precept, q.32 a.5;
  Sirach 29:2).
- CCC 2408 (taking in urgent necessity is not theft) is not the Catechism's ruling on
  repayment.
- **Level:** rung 4, common teaching (Aquinas; Slater's *Manual*, 1918); the Catechism
  does not enumerate them. The duty to repay itself is rung 3 (CCC 2409–2411).

Lessons: [6.3](lessons/06-03-the-ethics-of-personal-debt.md)

### Suretyship

**Co-signing is a real promise to pay if the debtor does not.** Sirach 29:14 [DR 29:18]
praises the surety who stands for his neighbour; 29:18 [DR 29:23] warns that
suretyship has ruined many; 29:20 [DR 29:26] counsels caution; 8:13 [DR 8:16]: stand
surety as one who will pay. Both praise and warning assume the surety pays. Aquinas
uses the surety for satisfaction borne in love (I-II q.87 a.7).

Lessons: [1.1](lessons/01-01-lending-in-the-covenant.md), [6.3](lessons/06-03-the-ethics-of-personal-debt.md)

### Bankruptcy discharge and conscience

**Does a discharge leave a moral duty to pay? Freely disputed (rung 5), and the
dispute runs through one book.**

- **Slater** (*Manual of Moral Theology*, vol. 1, 1918, "Causes which excuse from
  restitution"; CE 1907 "Moral Aspect of Bankruptcy"): it depends on the civil law's
  intent. English absolute discharge frees; in most countries, the US included,
  discharge only bars suit, so the duty revives if the debtor becomes able.
- **Michael Martin SJ**, the American note in the same volume: the US Act of 1898
  releases in conscience (its word "release", its fresh-start purpose, creditors'
  presumed remission).
- "Bankruptcy is theft" and "discharge settles it in conscience" both overstate.
  Bankruptcy as an institution: [`history-of-debt` 4.2](../history-of-debt/lessons/04-02-debtors-prisons-and-the-invention-of-bankruptcy.md).

Lessons: [6.3](lessons/06-03-the-ethics-of-personal-debt.md)

### Release and Jubilee arithmetic

| Quantity | Formula | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Jubilee price of land sold in year $s$ | $P = nY$, $n = 49 - s + 1$ harvests, $Y$ = yield | sold in year 20: $30 \times 10 = 300$; year 44: $6 \times 10 = 60$ |
| Netting sabbath years | count only non-sabbath harvests in the $n$ | Boss 1(b)'s step; Leviticus does not discount |
| Redemption refund | $P - (\text{harvests used}) \times Y$ = unused years $\times Y$ | 12 used: $300 - 120 = 180$ |
| Seventh-year expected recovery / loss | $pP$ / $(1-p)P$ | $P = 100$, $p = 0.3$: 30 / 70 |
| Seventy weeks | $70 \times 7 = 490 = 10 \times 49$; Jewish count $70 + 420$ | |
| Jubilees 50:4 | $49 \times 49 + 7 + 2 + 40 = 2{,}450 = 50 \times 49$ | |

### Titles and contracts

**Triple contract:** investor's position $= 1000 + 90 - 40 = 1050$ in every outcome;
merchant's net $= X - 90 + 40 = X - 50$: a loan at 5%. (Boss 4(d)'s general
conditions on $p$ and $q$ are left to the problem.)

**Rate decomposition** (a 7.00% mortgage, illustrative; components simply added):

| Component | Rate | Candidate title |
|---|---|---|
| expected inflation | 2.50% | keeping the principal whole (*tantundem*: number or value?) |
| real cost of funds | 2.00% | *damnum emergens* |
| expected default loss $1\% \times 40\%$ | 0.40% | *periculum sortis* |
| servicing | 0.35% | expense, as with the monti |
| residual | 1.75% | *lucrum cessans*, or usury |

Inflation fits no classical title. Microcredit example: expense 20% + funds 8% +
default 3% = 31% cost-recovery; the question for the 1-point residual is the same.

## Pitfalls

### Overstating a level

- **"The treasury of merit is defined dogma."** Rung 3: *Unigenitus*, *Cum postquam*,
  *Indulgentiarum Doctrina* 5, CCC 1476–1477; Trent never names it; CE 1912 overreaches.
  *([3.5](lessons/03-05-the-christian-jubilee-and-the-treasury.md), [3.6](lessons/03-06-luther-trent-and-the-reform-of-indulgences.md))*
- **"Vienne defined that all interest is heresy."** It punishes pertinacious *assertion*
  that usury is not sinful; the scope of "usury" is exactly what is argued. Don't settle
  it by fiat. *([4.2](lessons/04-02-from-canon-to-council.md), [5.4](lessons/05-04-did-the-usury-doctrine-change.md))*
- **"The Church declared interest legitimate in 1830."** *Non inquietandos* protects
  **confessors** as a practical norm while the question is *sub iudice*; it is not
  permission in principle. *([5.3](lessons/05-03-not-to-be-disturbed.md))*
- **"Aquinas / the Summa defined that interest is sinful."** A theologian's article
  defines nothing; the conclusion rests on the canons and Vienne. Its refusal of
  *lucrum cessans* was not a ruling later theologians defied. *([4.3](lessons/04-03-aquinas-on-usury-ii-ii-q78.md), [4.4](lessons/04-04-the-extrinsic-titles.md))*
- **"The Church changed its mind about usury"** stated as fact. Changed or developed is
  freely disputed; state Noonan and a continuity reading at full strength. *([5.4](lessons/05-04-did-the-usury-doctrine-change.md))*
- **"Nicaea / Lateran III defined usury a mortal sin."** Disciplinary canons presuppose,
  they do not define. *([4.1](lessons/04-01-the-fathers-against-usury.md), [4.2](lessons/04-02-from-canon-to-council.md))*
- **"Lateran V solemnly defined usury"; "*Vix Pervenit* infallibly defined it for the
  whole Church"; "*Detestabilis avaritia* is a dogmatic definition"; "the titles are
  dogma".** *([4.4](lessons/04-04-the-extrinsic-titles.md), [4.5](lessons/04-05-contracts-that-are-not-loans.md), [5.1](lessons/05-01-the-monti-di-pieta-and-lateran-v.md), [5.2](lessons/05-02-vix-pervenit.md))*
- **"Anselm's necessity is Church teaching"** (a Doctor's title does not raise a
  thesis); **Aquinas's order-and-stain account is dogma.** *([3.3](lessons/03-03-anselm-cur-deus-homo.md), [3.4](lessons/03-04-aquinas-satisfaction-and-the-debt-of-punishment.md))*
- **"The Church teaches debts must be cancelled every seven years / all poor
  countries' debts must be cancelled."** Judicial precept; CA 35's range of remedies is
  prudential. *([1.2](lessons/01-02-the-seventh-year-deuteronomy-15.md), [6.2](lessons/06-02-international-debt-and-the-jubilee-call.md))*
- **"The Church now defines usury as rates above the legal limit."** No act defines the
  modern sense. *([6.1](lessons/06-01-usury-and-finance-in-modern-teaching.md))*
- **Promoting exegesis:** the foreigner exception as defined toleration; Luke 4 as
  defined Christological-only or as Church social program; Luke 7:47's cause reading;
  TMA 13 as a ruling that the Jubilee was never kept; the devil-payee as teaching;
  Ezekiel 18 as a definition of usury. *([1.1](lessons/01-01-lending-in-the-covenant.md), [1.3](lessons/01-03-the-jubilee-the-land-is-mine.md), [1.4](lessons/01-04-creditors-under-judgment.md), [2.1](lessons/02-01-the-acceptable-year-of-the-lord.md), [2.3](lessons/02-03-the-parables-of-debt.md), [3.2](lessons/03-02-the-fathers-alms-satisfaction-and-ransom.md))*

### Understating a level

- Calling Trent XXV's power and usefulness of indulgences, VI can. 30, XIV can. 13
  ("alms make satisfaction for temporal punishment") or VI ch. 7's satisfaction
  "pious opinion" or "just Anselm". *([3.1](lessons/03-01-from-burden-to-debt.md), [3.2](lessons/03-02-the-fathers-alms-satisfaction-and-ransom.md), [3.3](lessons/03-03-anselm-cur-deus-homo.md), [3.6](lessons/03-06-luther-trent-and-the-reform-of-indulgences.md))*
- Filing Vienne (c) as "medieval discipline, since changed". *([4.2](lessons/04-02-from-canon-to-council.md), [5.4](lessons/05-04-did-the-usury-doctrine-change.md))*
- "*Vix Pervenit* was only advice to Italian bishops"; "the 1830s replies were a dodge
  with no authority"; "Lateran V was only pastoral tolerance". *([5.1](lessons/05-01-the-monti-di-pieta-and-lateran-v.md), [5.2](lessons/05-02-vix-pervenit.md), [5.3](lessons/05-03-not-to-be-disturbed.md))*
- "The Fathers only condemned clerical lending"; "the usury ban was just Aquinas's
  opinion". *([4.1](lessons/04-01-the-fathers-against-usury.md), [4.3](lessons/04-03-aquinas-on-usury-ii-ii-q78.md))*
- "Forgiving others was one school's view"; "redemption is just one optional
  metaphor". *([2.2](lessons/02-02-forgive-us-our-debts.md), [2.3](lessons/02-03-the-parables-of-debt.md), [2.4](lessons/02-04-the-bond-and-the-ransom.md))*
- "It's just the Pope's economic opinion" (CA 35's principles, CCC 2439); "condemning
  exploitative lending is papal opinion"; "the duty to repay is just the manuals".
  *([6.1](lessons/06-01-usury-and-finance-in-modern-teaching.md), [6.2](lessons/06-02-international-debt-and-the-jubilee-call.md), [6.3](lessons/06-03-the-ethics-of-personal-debt.md))*

### Reading traps

- **Anachronism:** *neshek* and *usura* are any increase, not "excessive interest".
  *([1.1](lessons/01-01-lending-in-the-covenant.md), [4.3](lessons/04-03-aquinas-on-usury-ii-ii-q78.md))*
- **Merging releases:** the Jubilee returns land and persons; Deut 15 releases debts;
  Jer 34 concerns servants. *([1.2](lessons/01-02-the-seventh-year-deuteronomy-15.md), [1.3](lessons/01-03-the-jubilee-the-land-is-mine.md))*
- **Lateran V's definition sentence** sits in the critics' recital; cite it as
  "reported". *([5.1](lessons/05-01-the-monti-di-pieta-and-lateran-v.md))*
- **Rennes 1830 vs Nice 1838:** "not to be disturbed" (confessors, 1830); "*dummodo
  parati sint*" (1838). *([5.3](lessons/05-03-not-to-be-disturbed.md))*
- **Lessius was Louvain, not Salamanca.** *([4.4](lessons/04-04-the-extrinsic-titles.md))*
- **Leo's *fenus/funus* pun** is in Sermon 17, not Letter 4. *([4.1](lessons/04-01-the-fathers-against-usury.md))*
- **Nicaea's "[as monthly interest]"** is the translator's bracket. *([1.4](lessons/01-04-creditors-under-judgment.md), [4.1](lessons/04-01-the-fathers-against-usury.md))*
- **Same cash flows are not same morality** (census vs loan); non-loan contracts are not
  titles. *([4.5](lessons/04-05-contracts-that-are-not-loans.md))*
- **Payee:** Rev 5:9's "to God" names the owner; no text names a payee. *([2.4](lessons/02-04-the-bond-and-the-ransom.md))*
- **Indulgences never forgive guilt;** restitution is never commuted or indulged.
  *([3.4](lessons/03-04-aquinas-satisfaction-and-the-debt-of-punishment.md), [3.5](lessons/03-05-the-christian-jubilee-and-the-treasury.md), [3.6](lessons/03-06-luther-trent-and-the-reform-of-indulgences.md))*
- **1517 is not 1531:** the Theses still allow papal remission of imposed penalties.
  *([3.6](lessons/03-06-luther-trent-and-the-reform-of-indulgences.md))*
- **Parable scenery** (sale of family, torturers) carries no doctrine; Matt 18:34 means
  forfeited, not bought. *([2.3](lessons/02-03-the-parables-of-debt.md))*
- **Excusing causes suspend, they do not cancel;** bankruptcy's residue is rung 5 either
  way; CCC 2408 is not a repayment clause. *([6.3](lessons/06-03-the-ethics-of-personal-debt.md))*
- **Hard history taught as hard:** Lateran IV c.67, Aquinas's Isaiah 56:11 charge, the
  monti preaching, indulgence abuses. Don't omit, don't make it doctrine.
  *([1.1](lessons/01-01-lending-in-the-covenant.md), [3.5](lessons/03-05-the-christian-jubilee-and-the-treasury.md), [4.2](lessons/04-02-from-canon-to-council.md), [4.3](lessons/04-03-aquinas-on-usury-ii-ii-q78.md), [5.1](lessons/05-01-the-monti-di-pieta-and-lateran-v.md))*

### Douay-Rheims numbering and wording traps

- **Daniel 4:24** is the Aramaic **and** DR number; 4:27 is many English Bibles'.
- **Exodus 22:25–27** DR = English; Hebrew 22:24–26. **Deut 23:19–20** DR = English;
  Hebrew 23:20–21.
- **Psalms:** Hebrew 15 = DR 14; 37 = DR 36; 38 = DR 37.
- **Sirach:** 3:30 = DR 3:33; 8:13 = DR 8:16; 29:7 = DR 29:10; 29:11–13 = DR 29:14–17;
  29:14, 18, 20 = DR 29:18, 23, 26; 29:1–2 same.
- **Books:** 2 Samuel = DR 2 Kings; 2 Kings = DR 4 Kings; 2 Chronicles = DR 2
  Paralipomenon; Nehemiah = DR 2 Esdras. Jer 37:5 = DR 37:4. Tobit follows the Vulgate
  recension (DR 4:10–11).
- **Wording:** DR Lev 25:10 "remission" (not "liberty"); Deut 15:2 "year of remission";
  Deut 15:11 "There will not be wanting poor"; Deut 15:15 "bondservant"; Lev 25:42 vs
  25:55; Luke 4:19 adds "the day of reward"; Luke 16 "barrels", "quarters"; Isa 40:2
  "is forgiven"; Lev 26:41 "pray for their sins"; Gen 4:13 "deserve pardon"; Hab 2:6
  "thick clay". The Vulgate's choices hide the Hebrew: quote the English, reason from
  the original.

## Conventions

- **One card per course**, covering every lesson. The linter checks that every lesson
  file is cited somewhere on this card.
- **Headings are anchors.** Renaming a `###` breaks inbound lesson links. Headings are
  ASCII only.
- **No prose dollar signs** (see CLAUDE.md).
- **Corrections over syllabus.** Where the build's verification corrected the syllabus
  (Lateran V's definition in the critics' recital; Daniel 4:24 numbering; Lessius at
  Louvain; the 1830 reply about confessors; *dummodo parati sint* in 1838; Aquinas on
  foreigners at I-II q.105 a.3 ad 3), the card follows the correction.
- **Boss problems are reserved.** No boss answer is reconstructed here: Anselm's
  premises (Boss 3a), the Lutheran statement and reply (3d), the q.78 a.1 premises
  (4b), the triple-contract algebra (4d), and the evaluative verdicts are left to the
  problems.
- **Paraphrase rule.** Basil, Nyssa, Ambrose, Origen, Anderson, Noonan, McCall, the
  medieval decrees and post-1930 documents are paraphrased; quotations come from
  public-domain translations or the lessons' own translations of Latin.
