# Theology of Debt · Lesson 4.4: The extrinsic titles

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 4: Usury: the prohibition · Builds on: [4.3 Aquinas: *Summa Theologiae* II-II q.78](04-03-aquinas-on-usury-ii-ii-q78.md), [4.2 From canon to council](04-02-from-canon-to-council.md) · Unlocks: [4.5 Contracts that are not loans](04-05-contracts-that-are-not-loans.md), [5.2 *Vix Pervenit*](05-02-vix-pervenit.md), [5.4 Did the usury doctrine change?](05-04-did-the-usury-doctrine-change.md)

## Why this matters

The usury doctrine never said a lender must always walk away with exactly what he lent. It said he may take nothing *for the loan itself*. Everything else in the doctrine's history turns on that qualifier. The **extrinsic titles** are the grounds on which a lender could justly receive more than the principal because of something *outside* the loan: a loss it caused him, a gain it cost him, a risk to his capital, the borrower's culpable delay. One of them, profit forgone, was refused by Aquinas in so many words and admitted three centuries later by the best-regarded moralists. Whether that shift was a development or a reversal is the question [5.4](05-04-did-the-usury-doctrine-change.md) inherits. This lesson gives you the tools to ask it precisely.

## The idea

Your neighbor borrows your car for a week and returns it with a full tank. You have been repaid. Now suppose that while it was gone you had to rent a car to get to work. Asking her to cover that rental is not charging for the loan. It is refusing to let the favor cost you. A lender who asks for "a little something" because he was kind enough to lend is doing something different: he is pricing the kindness.

The tradition drew exactly this line. **Intrinsic** gain arises *from the loan as such* (the *mutuum*, which passes ownership of the money to the borrower, [4.3](04-03-aquinas-on-usury-ii-ii-q78.md)). That is usury, however small. **Extrinsic** gain arises from a circumstance that happens to accompany the loan and can be named, measured and proved apart from it. That can be just. The medieval lawyers had a word for such compensation: *interesse*, from the Roman-law phrase *quod interest*, "what the difference is" to the lender. Noonan traces the noun to the Bolognese jurist Azo, around 1200. Our word "interest" began as the name for the *licit* payment, the opposite of *usura*.

## Source

ST II-II q.78 a.2 ad 1 (English Dominican Province), the reply that fixes the starting line for two titles at once:

> "A lender may without sin enter an agreement with the borrower for compensation for the loss he incurs of something he ought to have, for this is not to sell the use of money but to avoid a loss. It may also happen that the borrower avoids a greater loss than the lender incurs, wherefore the borrower may repay the lender with what he has gained. But the lender cannot enter an agreement for compensation, through the fact that he makes no profit out of his money: because he must not sell that which he has not yet and may be prevented in many ways from having."

Read the three sentences as a ledger. The first admits loss incurred, and allows it to be *agreed* in advance. Its test is "not to sell the use of money": the compensation must answer to something other than the loan. The second lets the borrower share a gain *freely*, as repayment of gratitude, not as a term of the contract. The third refuses profit forgone, and the reason is not that opportunity cost is imaginary. It is that the profit is *not yet his and uncertain*. Selling it would be selling a hope as though it were a possession. Every later argument about *lucrum cessans* is an argument about that reason: does it forbid the title outright, or only forbid valuing an uncertain gain as if it were certain?

## The argument

1. **The loan itself earns nothing.** In a *mutuum* the borrower owes back the same quantity; whatever is demanded "by reason of the loan" beyond it is usury ([usury](../reference.md#usury), [4.3](04-03-aquinas-on-usury-ii-ii-q78.md)).
2. **But a loan can cause, or be accompanied by, a real harm to the lender that justice may repair.** Compensating that harm restores equality; it does not exceed it.
   *In words:* commutative justice ([`moral-theology` 3.3](../../moral-theology/lessons/03-03-the-cardinal-virtues-and-the-gifts.md)) forbids the lender to end up richer by lending, and equally forbids him to end up poorer.
3. **The four titles** ([the extrinsic titles](../reference.md#the-extrinsic-titles)):
   - ***Damnum emergens***, loss incurred ([damnum emergens](../reference.md#damnum-emergens)): lending causes the lender an actual outlay or loss. Admitted by Aquinas (a.2 ad 1). Many medieval canonists admitted it only for loss arising after the loan was made, typically through the borrower's delay; Aquinas already lets it be agreed at the outset.
   - ***Lucrum cessans***, profit forgone ([lucrum cessans](../reference.md#lucrum-cessans)): the money would otherwise have earned a gain. Refused by Aquinas. Admitted in a narrow case by the canonist Hostiensis (Henry of Susa, d. 1271): a merchant who habitually trades at the fairs lends, out of charity, money he would have traded with, and may claim his *interesse*, provided there is no fraud. Domingo de Soto (*De iustitia et iure*, 1553) admitted it with conditions; Luis de Molina treated forgone gain as a loss in a wider sense. Leonard Lessius, the Louvain Jesuit (*De iustitia et iure*, 1605), widened it to any lender, bankers included, where a market for money makes its lack a real cost.
   - ***Periculum sortis***, risk to the principal ([periculum sortis](../reference.md#periculum-sortis)): a premium for a real, above-ordinary danger of losing the capital. Gregory IX's decretal *Naviganti*, in the Decretals of 1234 (X 5.19.19), had called a lender who takes a fixed sum beyond the principal *because he bears the risk* of a sea voyage a usurer. Later moralists admitted risk as a title all the same; how they squared it with *Naviganti* was argued, and Noonan traces the readings.
   - ***Poena conventionalis***, the agreed penalty ([poena conventionalis](../reference.md#poena-conventionalis)): a penalty clause for **culpable** delay past the due date. It must be a real penalty for real delay. If both parties expect the "late" payment, it is interest *in fraudem usurarum*, in fraud of the usury law.
4. **Every title has the same four conditions.** It must be **real** (the loss, forgone gain or risk actually exists), **caused by the loan**, **proportionate** (no more than the harm), and **not presumed**.
   *In words:* a title is a fact about this lender and this loan, not a clause you can write into every contract.
5. **The hinge.** *Vix Pervenit* (1745) locates the sin in gain claimed "by reason of the loan itself", and in the same breath does not deny that other titles, in its words (lesson's translation) "not at all innate and intrinsic to the nature of the loan", may concur and justly ground something beyond the principal. It then warns that anyone who persuades himself such titles are always present is rash ([5.2](05-02-vix-pervenit.md)).
   *In words:* the Magisterium let the titles in by the door marked "extrinsic" and posted a guard at it.

**Mark the levels.** The titles, their conditions and the extension of *lucrum cessans* are **theologically certain / common teaching** of canonists and theologians. The Catholic Encyclopedia (1912, a secondary source) reports the four as admitted without dispute since the end of the sixteenth century, and no council or pope has defined any of them. Aquinas's refusal is **a theologian's teaching** (4.3), not a magisterial act, so its reversal by later theologians overturns no definition. *Naviganti* is a **papal decretal in the universal canon law**: a juridical ruling on a contract, not a doctrinal definition. *Vix Pervenit*'s recognition that extrinsic titles *can* exist is **authentic ordinary magisterium** in an encyclical addressed to the bishops of Italy; how far its authority reaches is argued, not settled ([5.2](05-02-vix-pervenit.md)). It names no list of titles, and its closing directions tell the bishops to consult the most esteemed authors and not to brand a well-supported contrary opinion with grave censures. Vienne's decree ([4.2](04-02-from-canon-to-council.md)) treated the assertion that usury is no sin as heresy. Whether the titles lie wholly outside what that decree calls usury depends on what the decree denotes, and that is [5.4](05-04-did-the-usury-doctrine-change.md)'s question, not settled here.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean: loss incurred).** A farmer lends his neighbor 300 dollars for the winter. To do it, he must sell a calf in November at 250 dollars that would fetch 300 in spring, or borrow the gap himself. Run the test. The extra is not for the use of the money; it answers to a named loss of 50 dollars. The loss is real (a known price gap), caused by the loan (he would not have sold otherwise), and he can stipulate it in advance as a.2 ad 1 allows. **Compensation up to 50 dollars is just; more is usury.** Had he sold the calf anyway, there is no loss caused by the loan and no title.

**Example 2 (strained: when the title is always present).** Now take a lender who, like Lessius's banker, lives in a city where money can always be placed at a going return. For him the alternative is never absent: every loan forgoes a gain. The four conditions still run, and they pass *every time*. That is the strain. If a title is present in every loan by a certain kind of lender, what distinguishes it from a price for the loan itself? Two answers were available. One: nothing has changed in principle; the title is still a real, measured, lender-specific cost, and it has simply become common because the economy has changed (money has become generally fruitful). The other: a title present by default is a presumption, the very thing *Vix Pervenit* calls rash, and the extrinsic has quietly become intrinsic. Which reading is right is the continuity-versus-change dispute, freely argued among Catholics ([5.4](05-04-did-the-usury-doctrine-change.md)). Notice where both readings agree: the *mutuum* still earns nothing *as such*.

## Watch out

- **"The titles are Church dogma"** overstates. They are common teaching. *Vix Pervenit* recognizes that such titles can exist; it defines none and lists none.
- **"The titles are loopholes the Church merely tolerated"** understates and misreads. They follow from commutative justice, and *Vix Pervenit*, at the level of authentic magisterium, affirms that such titles can exist. Whether they were *abused* as disguises is a separate, historical question ([`history-of-debt` 2.1](../../history-of-debt/lessons/02-01-commerce-around-the-prohibition.md)).
- **"Aquinas's refusal of *lucrum cessans* was a magisterial ruling that later theologians defied"** overstates. It is a theologian's article. What is disputed is whether his *reason* (selling what one has not yet) forbids the title or only its careless valuation.
- **"Late fee" is not a magic word.** A penalty that runs whether or not there is delay, or that both sides expect to trigger, is interest by another name.
- **A title compensates; it does not profit.** The title's ceiling is the harm. Anything above it is gain from the loan.

## One-liner

> Nothing for the loan itself, everything owed for the harm it does: the extrinsic titles repair loss, forgone gain, risk and delay when they are real, and the whole later history is the fight over when "real" became "always".

## Problems

**P1 (🟢)** *(Exegetical)* An invented contract. A lender advances 5,000 dollars for twelve months. The contract calls the extra a "late fee": 1.5% of the principal per month, charged monthly **from the first month**, "whether or not any payment is late."

(a) Which title does the label claim, and does the contract meet that title's conditions? What is the fee in fact, and what does it amount to over the year (show the arithmetic)? (b) The contract is rewritten: no fee unless payment is late, then 1.5% a month. But lender and borrower both know the borrower will pay three months late, and they have agreed privately that he will. Does the rewrite cure it? **Two sentences per part.**

**P2 (🟡)** *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b))* An invented case. Marta, a cloth merchant whose trading stock has regularly returned about 12% a year, withdraws 2,000 florins from the business to lend a neighbor in urgent need for one year, and asks for compensation for the trading profit she will miss.

(a) What does ST II-II q.78 a.2 ad 1 say to her claim, and on what ground? What does Hostiensis's case allow, and how does it fit Marta? On the conditions of the later common teaching, what may she claim, and why is 240 florins (12% of 2,000) at most a ceiling rather than the answer? (b) Is the later admission of *lucrum cessans* a faithful reading of Aquinas's *reason* in ad 1, or a departure from his conclusion that his reason does not survive? **150 words or fewer for (b).**

**P3 (🔴)** *(Exegetical (a) · Formal (b) · Exegetical (c))* Gregory IX's decretal *Naviganti* (X 5.19.19) opens (Latin, then the lesson's translation):

> *Naviganti vel eunti ad nundinas certam mutuans pecuniae quantitatem, pro eo, quod suscipit in se periculum, recepturus aliquid ultra sortem, usurarius est censendus.*
>
> "One who lends a fixed quantity of money to a man sailing or going to the fairs, intending to receive something beyond the principal because he takes the risk upon himself, is to be judged a usurer."

(a) What exactly does the decretal condemn, and on what ground? Name the words that decide it. (b) An invented case: a lender lends 1,000 dollars for one year to a borrower whose record gives a 20% chance of total default with nothing recovered. What premium $\pi$ makes the lender's *expected* repayment exactly equal to the principal? A second lender charges 25% to a borrower whose default probability is 5%. What premium would the title justify, and what is his expected gain beyond the principal on 1,000 dollars? (c) Give the level of *Naviganti* and of the later title *periculum sortis*, and say whether the second lender's extra 19.7 points can be defended as *periculum sortis*. **Two sentences each for (a) and (c).**

<details>
<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict:** (a) It claims ***poena conventionalis***, which requires a penalty for **culpable delay** past the due date. A fee that runs from month one "whether or not any payment is late" answers to no delay, so no title exists. It is a price for the loan itself, i.e. **usury**: $0.015 \times 12 = 0.18$, so $0.18 \times 5{,}000 = 900$ dollars, an 18% annual rate by another name. (b) **No.** A penalty both parties expect to trigger, with the delay privately agreed, is the classic interest *in fraudem usurarum*: the "delay" is not culpable default but a planned extension, and the penalty is its price.

**Wrong turns:** accepting the label ("it's called a late fee, so it is a penalty"). Treating (b) as licit because the text of the contract is now correct: the title must be real, and here the fact behind it is fabricated.

**Model answer:** (a) The label claims the agreed penalty, but that title requires real, culpable delay, and this fee runs from the first month regardless, so it is usury: 1.5% × 12 = 18%, or 900 dollars on 5,000. (b) No: when both parties plan the lateness, the penalty is the price of a planned extension of the loan, interest in fraud of usury.

**P2** *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b))*

**Must hit, strict (a):** Aquinas **refuses** compensation for profit forgone: she "must not sell that which [she] has not yet and may be prevented in many ways from having". The gain is uncertain and not yet hers. **Hostiensis** allows a merchant who habitually trades at the fairs, lending **out of charity** money he would have traded with, to claim his *interesse*, absent fraud. Marta fits: a habitual trader, a real withdrawal, a loan to a neighbor in need. Under the later **conditions** she may claim compensation only if the forgone gain is **real** (she would in fact have traded it), **caused by the loan**, and **proportionate**. **240 florins** ($0.12 \times 2{,}000$) is a ceiling because the 12% is an *uncertain* expected return: a fair valuation discounts it for the chance it would not have come and for the labor and risk she no longer bears. That is exactly the force of Aquinas's "may be prevented".

**Must hit, any verdict (b):** state Aquinas's reason precisely (the uncertainty and non-possession of the gain), and say whether valuing a *hope* of gain at less than certainty answers it or evades it. Then state the strongest point for the other side. Mark the question **freely disputed** (the change-or-development question, 5.4) and the levels: ad 1 is a theologian's teaching, the later titles common teaching.

**Wrong turns:** saying Aquinas "allows it if she is a merchant" (that is Hostiensis). Awarding the full 240 as certain. Treating (b) as settled by *Vix Pervenit*, which names no titles.

**Model answer (b), one of several:** Faithful reading: ad 1's reason is that she would be selling a certain claim to a gain she does not yet have. The later teaching sells no such thing. It values the *hope* of gain at what a hope is worth, discounted for risk and for labor spared, and only where the hope is real. So it keeps the reason and drops only a conclusion that assumed forgone gain could not be measured. Departure reading: Aquinas meant that forgone profit is never the lender's to claim, because money in a loan is not his; admitting it concedes that money is fruitful, the very premise of a.1 that the argument denied. The crux is whether ad 1 forbids *valuing* uncertain gain or *claiming* it at all.

**P3** *(Exegetical (a) · Formal (b) · Exegetical (c))*

**Must hit, strict (a):** It condemns lending a **fixed sum** (*certam ... quantitatem*, a *mutuum*) to a sea- or fair-bound merchant and receiving **something beyond the principal** (*ultra sortem*) **on the ground that the lender bears the risk** (*pro eo, quod suscipit in se periculum*). The deciding words are *pro eo quod suscipit in se periculum* with *ultra sortem*: risk-bearing alone does not turn a loan's increase into a just return.

**Must hit, strict (b):** Set expected repayment equal to principal: $(1-p)(1+\pi)L = L$, so $\pi = \dfrac{p}{1-p}$. With $p = 0.20$: $\pi = 0.2/0.8 = 0.25$, a **25%** premium. Check: $0.8 \times 1{,}250 = 1{,}000$. Second lender: $\pi = 0.05/0.95 = 0.0526$, about **5.26%**. His expected repayment is $0.95 \times 1{,}250 = 1{,}187.50$ dollars, an expected gain of **187.50 dollars** beyond the principal (the excess over the fair premium is $25\% - 5.26\% \approx 19.7$ points). Model: the break-even risk premium in [`economics-of-debt`](../../economics-of-debt/syllabus.md).

**Must hit, strict (c):** *Naviganti* is a **papal decretal in the universal canon law**, a juridical ruling on a contract, not a doctrinal definition. *Periculum sortis* is **common teaching** of the later moralists, admitted under conditions (real, proportionate, not presumed). The second lender's extra 19.7 points answers to **no real risk**, so no title covers it: it is gain by reason of the loan, i.e. usury on the tradition's terms.

**Wrong turns:** reading *Naviganti* as condemning any charge on a risky loan "at any rate" and stopping there (the later title then looks like defiance, when the dispute is over how to read it). Computing $\pi = p$ (5% or 20%), which leaves the lender short in expectation. Calling *Naviganti* a dogma, or calling the title "defined by *Vix Pervenit*".

**Model answer:** (a) The decretal condemns taking a fixed increase beyond the principal on a loan for the reason that the lender bears the voyage's risk; *pro eo quod suscipit in se periculum* with *ultra sortem* decides it. (b) $\pi = p/(1-p) = 25\%$ for $p = 0.2$; 5.26% for $p = 0.05$, so the second lender expects 187.50 dollars beyond principal. (c) *Naviganti* is a decretal of the canon law, not a definition, and the title is common teaching. The excess answers to no real risk, so it is usury.

</details>

## Flashback

**From Lesson [4.2](04-02-from-canon-to-council.md) (From canon to council):** *(Exegetical.)* Find the crux. An **invented** case, Siena, 1250. Rinaldo, a cloth merchant, sells cloth on six months' credit at 12 florins a bolt when his cash price is 10, and does so only because the delay lets him charge more. He makes no loans and nobody in the city thinks of him as a lender. His **confessor** tells him he is guilty of usury and must restore the difference. When a rival asks the bishop's **judge** to refuse Rinaldo communion as a usurer, the judge declines.

(a) Do the confessor and the judge disagree about whether Rinaldo sins? State the crux in one sentence. (b) Name the text each relies on and give its level, naming the act that fixes it. (c) What would have to change for the judge to act? **Two sentences per part.**

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):** **No real contradiction.** The confessor answers whether Rinaldo **sins**, which *Consuluit* locates in the intention of gain; the judge answers whether he is a **manifest** usurer liable to a public penalty, which is a juridical test of notoriety, not a moral one. The crux is the difference between the sin and the canon's jurisdictional threshold.

**Must hit, strict (b):** the confessor relies on **Urban III's *Consuluit***, whose second case is exactly a merchant who charges more for goods sold on credit, wrong because of the intention of gain (Luke 6:35). It is a papal rescript made universal law in Gregory IX's *Liber Extra* (1234): **authentic papal teaching in legal form, not a definition**. The judge relies on **Lateran III canon 25** (1179), which denies communion and Christian burial to *manifest* usurers: a **disciplinary canon of a general council**, binding law, not a definition.

**Must hit, strict (c):** Rinaldo would have to become a **manifest** (publicly notorious) usurer. Then Lateran III bars him from communion and, if he dies unrepentant, from Christian burial, and Lyons II would further require restitution made or pledged before burial.

**Wrong turns:** saying the judge thinks the markup is licit; his refusal concerns proof and notoriety, not morals. Promoting *Consuluit* to a definition, or demoting it to a private opinion. Bringing in Vienne's heresy clause, which targets pertinacious assertion that usury is no sin, and Rinaldo asserts nothing.

**Model answer:** (a) They do not disagree about the sin: the confessor judges his intention, while the judge applies a penalty reserved for usurers known as such. (b) The confessor stands on *Consuluit*, which names the credit-sale markup and made the intention of gain the sin as universal law through the *Liber Extra*, authentic papal teaching and not a definition. The judge stands on Lateran III canon 25, a general council's disciplinary law that reaches only manifest usurers. (c) Rinaldo's usury would have to become public and notorious. Then the canon bars him from communion and, if he dies unrepentant, from Christian burial.

</details>
