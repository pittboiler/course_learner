# Philosophical Method — Syllabus

> Philosophy · Tier 1 · ~16 lessons · Prereqs: none · Roadmap id: `philosophical-method`

## Goal

This is the toolkit every course in Philosophy, Politics & Society and Catholic Theology leans on: how to take a passage of prose and find the argument in it, test whether the argument works, attack it well, and defend against the attack. You will finish able to put any argument into numbered premises, find its weakest premise, build a counterexample to a principle, run and vary a thought experiment, and name the single premise two opposed positions actually disagree about. It also teaches the two ways of arguing the later fields depend on: arguing *within* a tradition from its authorities, as a scholastic disputed question does, and arguing *about* one from outside. It deliberately skips formal logic as a mathematical system (truth-table semantics, completeness, quantifier rules — owned by [`mathematical-logic`](../mathematical-logic/syllabus.md)), any survey of philosophical positions (the subject courses own those), and rhetoric and persuasion as such.

**Scope discipline.**

| Topic | Owner | Here |
|---|---|---|
| Propositional and first-order logic as formal systems | [`mathematical-logic`](../mathematical-logic/syllabus.md) | Used informally: validity by form and counterexample, no semantics or proof systems |
| Bayesian updating, likelihood ratios | [`prob-stat-refresher`](../prob-stat-refresher/syllabus.md), later `epistemology` | Odds form only, as a tool for weighing evidence in prose arguments (2.4) |
| Particular positions (utilitarianism, the Five Ways, Rawls) | the subject courses | Used only as worked material for the method |

## How practice works in this course

Problems follow [HUMANITIES-BUILD-BRIEF.md](../../HUMANITIES-BUILD-BRIEF.md). This course is the pilot for that model: it exercises every problem archetype except formal models, and it is where each **problem kind** (exegetical, evaluative, apologetic, formal) is introduced — in the lessons where the matching skill is taught. Every solution is a rubric (must-hit moves, wrong turns) plus a model answer. Worked material is drawn from public-domain texts (Plato, Aristotle, Aquinas, Hume, Mill) and from arguments written for the lesson.

## Dangerous Checklist

When you finish, you can:

- [ ] Put an argument from ordinary prose into standard form — numbered premises, conclusion, suppressed premises supplied charitably
- [ ] Say whether an argument is valid, and prove an invalid one invalid by building a same-form counterexample
- [ ] Locate an argument's weakest premise and say what evidence or argument would bear on it
- [ ] Assess an inference to the best explanation or an argument from analogy by naming what makes it stronger or weaker
- [ ] Weigh a piece of evidence in odds form and spot a base-rate error in a prose argument
- [ ] Tell a genuine informal fallacy from a legitimate move with the same shape (a relevant ad hominem, a sound slippery slope)
- [ ] Test a proposed definition with necessary-and-sufficient conditions and break it with a counterexample
- [ ] Dissolve a dispute with a distinction, and tell a verbal dispute from a substantive one
- [ ] Vary a thought experiment to isolate the factor doing the work, and say what the case can and cannot show
- [ ] Classify an objection (premise denial, counterexample, reductio, proves-too-much, self-defeat) and give the matching kind of reply
- [ ] Take two opposed positions and name the single premise their disagreement turns on
- [ ] Read a scholastic article (objections, *sed contra*, *respondeo*, replies) and reconstruct its argument from authority and from reason separately

## Modules

### Module 1: The anatomy of an argument

What an argument is, what makes one good, and how to get it out of prose and onto the page where it can be tested.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1.1 | What an argument is | Tell an argument from an explanation, a report, or a speech, and find its conclusion | premise, conclusion, inference, indicator words, argument vs explanation |
| 1.2 | Validity and soundness | Separate whether the form works from whether the premises are true | validity, soundness, form vs content, valid-with-false-premises, the counterexample method |
| 1.3 | Reconstruction and charity | Put a passage into standard form and supply what it leaves unsaid | standard form, suppressed premise, principle of charity, the weakest premise, **exegetical** problems |
| 1.4 | Argument forms and formal fallacies | Recognize the valid forms philosophy runs on and the invalid look-alikes | modus ponens/tollens, disjunctive syllogism, dilemma, reductio, affirming the consequent, quantifier-scope slips |

**Boss problem 1:** Take the Laws' speech from Plato's *Crito* (public-domain translation), in which Socrates argues he must not escape prison. (a) Reconstruct the central argument in standard form, supplying at least one suppressed premise, and say what principle of charity guided each addition. (b) Check the reconstruction for validity; if it is invalid, repair it with the weakest premise that makes it valid. (c) Identify the weakest premise and state the strongest case against it. Parts (a)–(b) are exegetical; (c) is evaluative.

### Module 2: Reasoning that isn't deduction

Most arguments in politics, history and theology are not proofs. How to weigh the ones that aren't.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2.1 | Inference to the best explanation | Judge which explanation of the evidence a rival should prefer | abduction, explanatory virtues (scope, fit, simplicity, fruitfulness), defeasibility, rival hypotheses |
| 2.2 | Arguments from analogy | Assess an analogy by its relevant similarities and its disanalogies | analogical form, relevant similarity, disanalogy objection, the design analogy as a test case |
| 2.3 | Informal fallacies, and when they aren't | Tell a real mistake from a legitimate move with the same shape | equivocation, begging the question, straw man, relevant vs irrelevant ad hominem and appeal to authority, slippery slope, false dilemma, **evaluative** problems |
| 2.4 | Weighing evidence in odds form | Say how much a piece of evidence should move you, and catch base-rate errors | prior odds, likelihood ratio, posterior odds, base-rate neglect, cumulative cases |

**Boss problem 2:** Hume argues in *Of Miracles* (public domain) that no testimony can establish a miracle unless its falsehood would be more miraculous than the fact it reports. (a) Reconstruct the argument in standard form. (b) Recast its central claim in odds form: what must be true of the prior odds and the likelihood ratio of the testimony for Hume's conclusion to follow? (c) Name the premise a defender of miracle reports must deny, and assess whether denying it is ad hoc — any verdict, if the odds-form analysis is used. Parts (a)–(b) are exegetical; (c) is evaluative.

### Module 3: Concepts, cases, and intuitions

The distinctively philosophical tools: definitions, distinctions, and imaginary cases used as evidence.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3.1 | Conceptual analysis | Propose a definition, test it, and break it | necessary and sufficient conditions, counterexample to an analysis, the justified-true-belief analysis and Gettier cases, family resemblance |
| 3.2 | Distinctions and verbal disputes | Dissolve a disagreement by drawing the right distinction, or show it was never about words | making a distinction, verbal vs substantive dispute, act/omission, intending/foreseeing, the "it depends what you mean" test |
| 3.3 | Thought experiments | Build and vary an imaginary case to isolate the factor doing the work | controlled variation, the trolley and transplant cases, what a case can show, the intuition-pump worry |
| 3.4 | Intuitions and reflective equilibrium | Decide when to revise a principle and when to bite the bullet | intuitions as evidence, reflective equilibrium, biting the bullet, "one person's modus ponens is another's modus tollens" |

**Boss problem 3:** Most people judge it permissible to divert a runaway trolley to kill one instead of five, but impermissible for a surgeon to kill one patient to harvest organs for five. (a) Propose a principle that yields both verdicts, stated precisely enough to test. (b) Build a variant case — changing one factor only — that the principle gets wrong or leaves undetermined. (c) Decide whether to revise the principle or bite the bullet on the variant, and defend the choice using reflective equilibrium. Part (a) is graded on precision and fit with both verdicts; (b)–(c) are evaluative.

### Module 4: Dialectic — engaging a position

Arguments live in exchanges. How to object well, reply well, find where two sides really part ways, and argue inside a tradition as well as about it.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4.1 | Objections and replies | Classify an objection and choose the reply that fits it | premise denial, counterexample, reductio, proves-too-much, self-defeat; concede-and-restrict, deny, distinguish |
| 4.2 | Steelmanning and the burden of proof | State an opponent's view in a form they would sign, and say who owes an argument | principle of charity revisited, steelman vs straw man, dialectical burden, presumption, the steelman-and-reply archetype |
| 4.3 | Finding the crux | Reduce a disagreement to the one premise it turns on | crux, map of positions, disagreement in belief vs in values, what would change a mind |
| 4.4 | Arguing within a tradition: the disputed question | Read and write in the scholastic form, and separate argument from authority and argument from reason | objections, *sed contra*, *respondeo*, replies; legitimate authority; arguing within vs about a tradition; close reading; **apologetic** problems |

**Boss problem 4:** Take Aquinas, *Summa Theologiae* II-II q.40 a.1, "Whether it is always sinful to wage war?" (public-domain English Dominican translation). (a) Map the article's structure: objections, *sed contra*, *respondeo*, replies. (b) Reconstruct the *respondeo*'s three conditions for a just war in standard form, marking which premises rest on authority and which on reason. (c) Take Objection 1 and Aquinas's reply to it; name the crux between them. (d) Steelman the objector against the reply in 150 words — then, in a further 150, reply as a defender of Aquinas's position would. Parts (a)–(c) are exegetical; (d) is apologetic.

## Sources of truth

- Walter Sinnott-Armstrong and Robert Fogelin, *Understanding Arguments* (reconstruction, validity, informal fallacies — the course's conventions for standard form follow it)
- Timothy Williamson, *Doing Philosophy* (thought experiments, intuitions as evidence, how philosophical disagreement actually works)
- Daniel Dennett, *Intuition Pumps and Other Tools for Thinking* (varying cases; the intuition-pump worry)
- Worked texts in public-domain translations: Plato, *Crito* (Jowett); Hume, *An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding*, §10; Aquinas, *Summa Theologiae* (English Dominican Province)

## Notes

- **The pilot for three fields.** Build this course first and stop to report before building anything else in Philosophy, Politics & Society or Catholic Theology — see the build order in [HUMANITIES-BUILD-BRIEF.md](../../HUMANITIES-BUILD-BRIEF.md).
- **Where the tools go next.** Conceptual analysis (3.1) is the method of `epistemology` and `metaphysics`; thought experiments and reflective equilibrium (3.3–3.4) are the method of `ethics` and [`political-philosophy`](../political-philosophy/syllabus.md); odds-form evidence (2.4) returns in `philosophy-of-religion` (miracles), `apologetics-foundations` (the resurrection) and `epistemology`; the disputed question (4.4) is the native form of `thomistic-synthesis` and every Catholic Theology course after it.
- **Lesson count.** The roadmap estimated ~12; the syllabus runs 16 because the method has four genuinely separate parts (form, non-deductive weight, cases, dialectic) and each needs four lessons to be practiced rather than listed. Roadmap estimate updated to match.
