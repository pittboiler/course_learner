# Philosophical Method · Reference Card

> Open book. This card is meant to be open while you work — including during
> quizzes. Nothing here is something you're expected to recall cold.

This course's machinery is a set of moves: get an argument out of prose, test it, weigh it when it isn't a proof, attack it, and answer an attack. The card is for mid-problem lookup — which form is this, what does a good analogy need, what does the rubric mean by "evaluative" — so that the problems can spend your time on judgement instead of recall.

## Positions and distinctions

### Argument and explanation

An argument offers reasons for something you might not yet accept; an explanation gives causes for something you already do. "Because" introduces either, so ask what the passage wants you to *accept*.

*Introduced:* [1.1](lessons/01-01-what-an-argument-is.md)

### Standard form

An argument written as numbered premises and a conclusion, one claim per line, with everything that does no argumentative work discarded. Getting the parts onto separate lines is what makes a gap visible.

*Introduced:* [1.1](lessons/01-01-what-an-argument-is.md); as a five-step discipline in [1.3](lessons/01-03-reconstruction-and-charity.md)

### Validity

It is impossible for all the premises to be true and the conclusion false at the same time. A promise about the *link*, not about the premises: an argument can be valid with false premises, and invalid with a true conclusion.

*Introduced:* [1.2](lessons/01-02-validity-and-soundness.md)

### Soundness

Valid *and* all premises true. The only combination that establishes its conclusion.

*Introduced:* [1.2](lessons/01-02-validity-and-soundness.md)

### Counterexample method

To show an argument invalid: strip it to its form, then build a new argument with that same form whose premises are obviously true and whose conclusion is obviously false. The counterexample must share the *form*, never the topic.

*Introduced:* [1.2](lessons/01-02-validity-and-soundness.md)

### Principle of charity

Reconstruct the strongest version of the argument that the author would still sign. Its limit: an improvement the author would disown is no longer their argument, and beating it proves nothing.

*Introduced:* [1.3](lessons/01-03-reconstruction-and-charity.md)

### Weakest-premise rule

When supplying a suppressed premise, add the one that *claims least* while still making the argument valid. Supplying something grander hands the author a burden they never took on.

*Introduced:* [1.3](lessons/01-03-reconstruction-and-charity.md)

### Load-bearing premise

The premise most likely to be false, once the argument is reconstructed — the line the whole thing rests on and the right target for an objection. Note the vocabulary trap: "weakest" means *claims least* in the rule above and *most likely false* here, so this card uses "load-bearing" for the second sense.

*Introduced:* [1.3](lessons/01-03-reconstruction-and-charity.md)

### Reductio ad absurdum

Assume the claim for the sake of argument, derive something absurd, conclude the assumption is false. What it does *not* tell you is which claim in the set must go — only that they cannot all stand.

*Introduced:* [1.4](lessons/01-04-argument-forms-and-formal-fallacies.md); deployed against a position in [4.1](lessons/04-01-objections-and-replies.md)

### Quantifier-scope slip

Swapping the order of "every" and "some" makes a much stronger claim: "everyone has a reason to obey" does not give you "there is one reason everyone has." Some…every never follows from every…some.

*Introduced:* [1.4](lessons/01-04-argument-forms-and-formal-fallacies.md)

### Inference to the best explanation

Surprising evidence; a hypothesis that would make it unsurprising; no rival does as well; so provisionally accept it. The third premise is where the work and the danger are.

*Introduced:* [2.1](lessons/02-01-inference-to-the-best-explanation.md)

### Best of a bad lot

An abductive inference is only as good as the rivals actually considered, so generating rivals is part of the method, not a courtesy.

*Introduced:* [2.1](lessons/02-01-inference-to-the-best-explanation.md)

### Argument from analogy

A and B share features F1…Fn; A has G; so B has G. Its strength rests almost entirely on the usually-unstated premise that the shared features are *relevant* to G.

*Introduced:* [2.2](lessons/02-02-arguments-from-analogy.md)

### Disanalogy objection

Name a difference between A and B that bears on G. Any difference at all is not an objection — every two things differ somewhere.

*Introduced:* [2.2](lessons/02-02-arguments-from-analogy.md)

### Equivocation

A term shifts sense between premises, and the argument needs both senses to go through. The legitimate twin is a term used in one sense throughout, even if that sense is technical.

*Introduced:* [2.3](lessons/02-03-informal-fallacies-and-when-they-arent.md)

### Begging the question

The premise is no more acceptable to this audience than the conclusion. Circularity is a property of an argument *plus an audience*, not of the argument alone.

*Introduced:* [2.3](lessons/02-03-informal-fallacies-and-when-they-arent.md)

### Straw man

Refuting a version of the position its holder would not sign. The legitimate twin is a reductio, where the absurd version is *derived from* what they do hold rather than substituted for it.

*Introduced:* [2.3](lessons/02-03-informal-fallacies-and-when-they-arent.md)

### Ad hominem

Irrelevant when the claim rests on evidence you can inspect yourself. Relevant when the claim rests on the speaker's testimony or when a conflict of interest bears on their reliability — and even then it lowers credence rather than refuting.

*Introduced:* [2.3](lessons/02-03-informal-fallacies-and-when-they-arent.md)

### Appeal to authority

Fallacious when the authority is outside their field, is a lone dissenter passed off as the field, or is silent on the question. Legitimate when a genuine expert speaks within their field and within its consensus — which is how most of what you know reached you.

*Introduced:* [2.3](lessons/02-03-informal-fallacies-and-when-they-arent.md)

### Slippery slope

Fallacious when the chain is asserted whole. Sound when each step is defended — but a chain is a conjunction of conditionals, so its probability decays multiplicatively (five defended steps at 0.8 each leaves about a third).

*Introduced:* [2.3](lessons/02-03-informal-fallacies-and-when-they-arent.md)

### False dilemma

A third option exists and is quietly dropped. The legitimate twin is a genuine dichotomy, where the options are exhaustive because they are a claim and its negation.

*Introduced:* [2.3](lessons/02-03-informal-fallacies-and-when-they-arent.md)

### Base-rate neglect

Treating a strong likelihood ratio as if it settled the matter, when the prior odds were long. The confusion is between the chance of the evidence given the hypothesis and the chance of the hypothesis given the evidence.

*Introduced:* [2.4](lessons/02-04-weighing-evidence-in-odds-form.md)

### Cumulative case

Independent items multiply their likelihood ratios. Correlated items do not — four chronicles copying one source are one item, not four, which is the standard error in cumulative arguments.

*Introduced:* [2.4](lessons/02-04-weighing-evidence-in-odds-form.md)

### Necessary and sufficient conditions

C is necessary for K when nothing is a K without C; C is sufficient when anything with C is thereby a K. An analysis claims both, which is why it can fail in two directions.

*Introduced:* [3.1](lessons/03-01-conceptual-analysis.md)

### Conceptual analysis

Propose a definition as a biconditional, then try to break it: a case that satisfies the conditions but is not a K (not sufficient), or a clear K that fails a condition (not necessary).

*Introduced:* [3.1](lessons/03-01-conceptual-analysis.md)

### Gettier case

A case of justified true belief that is not knowledge, because the justification and the truth come apart by luck. The pattern of patch-and-break that followed is itself the finding.

*Introduced:* [3.1](lessons/03-01-conceptual-analysis.md)

### Family resemblance

Some concepts have no common core — their instances overlap in a web of similarities instead. An exit from analysis, and one that can be taken too early.

*Introduced:* [3.1](lessons/03-01-conceptual-analysis.md)

### Stipulative definition

Fixing a sharp sense for the argument at hand instead of analysing the concept. Honest when announced, and smuggling when the stipulated sense quietly does work the ordinary sense would not.

*Introduced:* [3.1](lessons/03-01-conceptual-analysis.md)

### Verbal dispute

The parties agree on every non-linguistic fact and differ only in what a word means. The test: substitute the two senses and see whether the disagreement survives. Some disputes that look verbal are not — when what a word *should* mean is itself at stake, the word is the prize.

*Introduced:* [3.2](lessons/03-02-distinctions-and-verbal-disputes.md)

### Ad hoc distinction

A distinction drawn with no motivation beyond escaping the objection in front of you. Three marks separate it from a good one: independent motivation, prior availability, and generality beyond the awkward case. Ad hoc does not mean false.

*Introduced:* [3.2](lessons/03-02-distinctions-and-verbal-disputes.md)

### Act and omission

Doing something that brings about a harm versus not doing something that would prevent it.

*Introduced:* [3.2](lessons/03-02-distinctions-and-verbal-disputes.md)

### Intending and foreseeing

The effect you aim at versus the effect you accept as a side effect. The distinction underneath double effect.

*Introduced:* [3.2](lessons/03-02-distinctions-and-verbal-disputes.md)

### Negative and positive liberty

Freedom from interference by others versus the capacity to act and achieve.

*Introduced:* [3.2](lessons/03-02-distinctions-and-verbal-disputes.md)

### De dicto and de re

A claim about whatever satisfies a description, versus a claim about the particular thing that happens to satisfy it.

*Introduced:* [3.2](lessons/03-02-distinctions-and-verbal-disputes.md)

### Controlled variation

Hold everything fixed, change one factor, and see whether the verdict moves. That is what a thought experiment is for, and a pair that varies two factors at once shows nothing.

*Introduced:* [3.3](lessons/03-03-thought-experiments.md)

### Intuition pump

A case vivid enough to move a verdict by its framing rather than by the factor under test. The reply is to vary the framing and see whether the verdict holds.

*Introduced:* [3.3](lessons/03-03-thought-experiments.md)

### Reflective equilibrium

Adjust principles to fit considered judgements, adjust judgements to fit principles, and iterate until they cohere. An amendment counts as principled rather than ad hoc only if it follows from something you would have accepted anyway.

*Introduced:* [3.4](lessons/03-04-intuitions-and-reflective-equilibrium.md)

### Biting the bullet

Accepting a counterintuitive implication rather than amending the theory. Honest when the cost is stated plainly, paid in the case as described, and weighed against what the theory buys.

*Introduced:* [3.4](lessons/03-04-intuitions-and-reflective-equilibrium.md)

### Modus ponens or modus tollens

A valid argument running from a theory to a hated verdict can be read two ways: accept the theory and the verdict, or reject the verdict and the theory. Naming which way each side reads it locates the disagreement instead of repeating it.

*Introduced:* [3.4](lessons/03-04-intuitions-and-reflective-equilibrium.md)

### Debunking explanation

An account of *why* someone holds an intuition that gives no reason to think it tracks the truth. It undercuts the intuition's evidential weight rather than contradicting it — and it cuts both ways.

*Introduced:* [3.4](lessons/03-04-intuitions-and-reflective-equilibrium.md)

### Proves too much

The arguer's own reasoning, run on a parallel case, yields a conclusion they reject. The most useful objection in the set, because it uses only what they have already granted.

*Introduced:* [4.1](lessons/04-01-objections-and-replies.md)

### Self-defeat

The claim fails its own test — a standard that, applied to itself, disqualifies itself.

*Introduced:* [4.1](lessons/04-01-objections-and-replies.md)

### Concede and restrict

Grant the objection and narrow the claim to what survives it. The most common honest reply, and its price is the scope you gave up.

*Introduced:* [4.1](lessons/04-01-objections-and-replies.md)

### Steelman

A statement of a position its best defenders would sign as their own. Distinct from charity in reconstruction, where the target is a particular text: here the target is a position, and you may improve on any single defender — but not into something they would disown.

*Introduced:* [4.2](lessons/04-02-steelmanning-and-the-burden-of-proof.md)

### Dialectical burden

Who owes an argument. Presumption sits with the default or status quo until a prima facie case shifts the burden; the standard abuse is both sides claiming presumption so that neither argues.

*Introduced:* [4.2](lessons/04-02-steelmanning-and-the-burden-of-proof.md)

### Crux

The premise a disagreement turns on: if you came to believe it, you would change your view. Test it on both sides — when it works in both directions it is a double crux.

*Introduced:* [4.3](lessons/04-03-finding-the-crux.md)

### Factual, evaluative and semantic divergence

The three kinds of crux. Naming which one you have tells you what to do next: seek evidence, identify the value difference and stop pretending data will settle it, or draw the distinction. A semantic crux is not thereby a merely verbal dispute.

*Introduced:* [4.3](lessons/04-03-finding-the-crux.md)

### Argument from authority inside a tradition

Legitimate where the authority is a shared premise rather than an appeal to popularity. It has three attack surfaces: scope (is the authority speaking to this?), weight (at what level does it bind?), and reading (does it say what is claimed?).

*Introduced:* [4.4](lessons/04-04-arguing-within-a-tradition-the-disputed-question.md)

### Internal and external critique

Internal: the tradition contradicts itself or its own principles — it needs only premises the tradition grants. External: the tradition's premises are false — it needs premises the tradition denies. Conflating them wrecks the conversation, because each side then answers an argument the other did not make.

*Introduced:* [4.4](lessons/04-04-arguing-within-a-tradition-the-disputed-question.md)

## Arguments and rules

### Valid forms and their invalid look-alikes

| Form | Shape | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| Modus ponens | If *P* then *Q*; *P* ∴ *Q* | Valid |
| Modus tollens | If *P* then *Q*; not *Q* ∴ not *P* | Valid |
| Hypothetical syllogism | If *P* then *Q*; if *Q* then *R* ∴ if *P* then *R* | Valid |
| Disjunctive syllogism | *P* or *Q*; not *P* ∴ *Q* | Valid |
| Constructive dilemma | *P* or *Q*; if *P* then *R*; if *Q* then *R* ∴ *R* | Valid |
| Reductio | Assume *P*; derive an absurdity ∴ not *P* | Valid |
| Affirming the consequent | If *P* then *Q*; *Q* ∴ *P* | **Invalid** |
| Denying the antecedent | If *P* then *Q*; not *P* ∴ not *Q* | **Invalid** |
| Quantifier-scope slip | Every…some ∴ some…every | **Invalid** |

*From* [1.4](lessons/01-04-argument-forms-and-formal-fallacies.md)

### The odds rule

$$\underbrace{\frac{P(H \mid E)}{P(\neg H \mid E)}}_{\text{posterior odds}} = \underbrace{\frac{P(H)}{P(\neg H)}}_{\text{prior odds}} \times \underbrace{\frac{P(E \mid H)}{P(E \mid \neg H)}}_{L}$$

Odds $a:b$ is probability $a/(a+b)$. Independent items multiply: $L_{\text{total}} = L_1 \times L_2 \times \cdots$. A rough scale: $L$ of 1 is worthless, 3 to 10 is the honest value of most single items, above 100 is rarer than people claim, and $L = \tfrac{1}{5}$ counts against exactly as hard as $L = 5$ counts for.

*From* [2.4](lessons/02-04-weighing-evidence-in-odds-form.md)

### Explanatory virtues

| Virtue | The question it asks |
|---|---|
| Scope | How much of the evidence does it cover? |
| Fit | How much else would you have to give up to accept it? |
| Simplicity | How many independent assumptions, and are any there only to save the hypothesis? |
| Fruitfulness | Does it imply something not yet checked? |

Scope bought with patches costs simplicity; the ad hoc test asks whether the patch has any support other than saving the hypothesis.

*From* [2.1](lessons/02-01-inference-to-the-best-explanation.md)

### Objections and the replies that fit them

| Objection | What it claims | Replies that fit |
|---|---|---|
| Premise denial | A premise is false | Deny the objection's own premise; concede and restrict |
| Counterexample | A universal premise or analysis fails on a case | Restrict the principle; grant and outweigh; deny the case |
| Reductio | The premises entail something absurd | Distinguish; deny the derivation; bite the bullet |
| Proves too much | The same reasoning licenses what the arguer rejects | Distinguish the parallel case; accept the parallel conclusion |
| Self-defeat | The claim fails its own test | Restrict the standard so it exempts itself non-arbitrarily |
| So what | All true, none of it bears on the thesis | Supply the missing relevance premise |

*From* [4.1](lessons/04-01-objections-and-replies.md)

### The scholastic article

| Part | Its job |
|---|---|
| Objections (*videtur quod*) | The strongest case against the position |
| *Sed contra* | An authority on the other side — a signpost, not the argument |
| *Respondeo* | The argument itself |
| Replies | What each objection got right, and where it goes wrong |

*From* [4.4](lessons/04-04-arguing-within-a-tradition-the-disputed-question.md)

### The four problem kinds

| Kind | Asks | Graded on |
|---|---|---|
| Exegetical | What does the text, principle or doctrine say or imply? | Accuracy — strictly |
| Evaluative | Is the argument sound? Is it justified? | The required moves, never the verdict |
| Apologetic | Defend a position against a named alternative | Fidelity to the opposing view, then the quality of the reply |
| Formal | Compute, prove, solve | As in any math problem |

*Named in* [1.3](lessons/01-03-reconstruction-and-charity.md) (exegetical), [2.3](lessons/02-03-informal-fallacies-and-when-they-arent.md) (evaluative), [4.4](lessons/04-04-arguing-within-a-tradition-the-disputed-question.md) (apologetic).

## Pitfalls

### Reading an argument

- "Because" marks a cause in an explanation and a reason in an argument — the word settles nothing. *([1.1](lessons/01-01-what-an-argument-is.md))*
- The conclusion is not necessarily last; in an op-ed it is often the title. Find it by function. *([1.1](lessons/01-01-what-an-argument-is.md))*
- A rhetorical question is an assertion in disguise. Rewrite it flat before assessing it. *([1.1](lessons/01-01-what-an-argument-is.md))*
- Supplying a premise the author would disown wins against an argument nobody made. *([1.3](lessons/01-03-reconstruction-and-charity.md))*

### Testing it

- "Valid" here means only that the link holds, not that the argument is any good. *([1.2](lessons/01-02-validity-and-soundness.md))*
- A true conclusion never rescues a broken form, and an invalid argument's conclusion may still be true. *([1.2](lessons/01-02-validity-and-soundness.md))*
- A counterexample must share the form, not the topic. *([1.2](lessons/01-02-validity-and-soundness.md))*
- Repairing a fallacy by strengthening a premise is not free — say what now has to be defended. *([1.4](lessons/01-04-argument-forms-and-formal-fallacies.md))*

### Weighing it

- An explanation that fits everything only because it was patched to fit has bought scope with simplicity. *([2.1](lessons/02-01-inference-to-the-best-explanation.md))*
- The best explanation on your list may still be a bad one; generate rivals before concluding. *([2.1](lessons/02-01-inference-to-the-best-explanation.md))*
- Counting differences refutes no analogy; only a difference that bears on the conclusion does. *([2.2](lessons/02-02-arguments-from-analogy.md))*
- Naming a fallacy is not an argument, and half of all fallacy charges misfire — answer the argument. *([2.3](lessons/02-03-informal-fallacies-and-when-they-arent.md))*
- A strong likelihood ratio against long prior odds still leaves an improbable conclusion. *([2.4](lessons/02-04-weighing-evidence-in-odds-form.md))*
- Correlated evidence counted as independent inflates a cumulative case dramatically. *([2.4](lessons/02-04-weighing-evidence-in-odds-form.md))*

### Concepts and cases

- Reaching for family resemblance at the first counterexample ends the inquiry too early. *([3.1](lessons/03-01-conceptual-analysis.md))*
- A stipulation that quietly does the ordinary concept's work is smuggling. *([3.1](lessons/03-01-conceptual-analysis.md))*
- A distinction with no motivation beyond this objection is ad hoc — check all three marks. *([3.2](lessons/03-02-distinctions-and-verbal-disputes.md))*
- A pair of cases that varies two factors at once tests neither. *([3.3](lessons/03-03-thought-experiments.md))*
- A case can refute a universal principle; it cannot establish one on its own. *([3.3](lessons/03-03-thought-experiments.md))*
- Reflective equilibrium with unexamined starting judgements produces a tidy version of whatever you already believed. *([3.4](lessons/03-04-intuitions-and-reflective-equilibrium.md))*

### Exchanges

- A reply that does not fit the objection's type concedes the point without noticing. *([4.1](lessons/04-01-objections-and-replies.md))*
- Steelmanning stops where the defender would disown your version — say so when you have improved on them. *([4.2](lessons/04-02-steelmanning-and-the-burden-of-proof.md))*
- If a premise fails the crux test on both sides, it is not the crux, however loudly it is argued. *([4.3](lessons/04-03-finding-the-crux.md))*
- Answering an internal critique with external premises, or the reverse, talks past the objection. *([4.4](lessons/04-04-arguing-within-a-tradition-the-disputed-question.md))*
