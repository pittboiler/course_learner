# Church History — Syllabus

> Catholic Theology · Tier 1 · ~30 lessons · Prereqs: none · Roadmap id: `church-history`

## Goal

This course tells the story of the Catholic Church from the Jerusalem community of the apostles to the pontificate of Leo XIV: the events, persons, institutions and politics that made the Church what it is. It is written from within the tradition, but held to professional historical standards: the scandals are told plainly (persecutions the Church suffered and persecutions it ran, the tenth-century papacy, the crusaders' massacres, the Inquisition, the Renaissance popes, the Galileo affair, the abuse crisis), and wherever historians disagree the course names the disagreement instead of settling it quietly. You will finish able to place any major event in its century and its causal chain, read a primary source as a historian reads it (who wrote it, for whom, and what it can and cannot establish), find the crux between rival interpretations, and test a popular historical claim against the evidence. It deliberately skips the *content* of what councils defined (the dogmatic courses teach that), the theology of the papacy, the Reformation's doctrinal disputes argued out, the Fathers read as theologians, and any attempt at comprehensive coverage: the Eastern churches after 1054 and the Church outside Europe after 1800 appear only where they touch the main line, and whole centuries of national church history are left out.

## How practice works in this course

Problems follow [HUMANITIES-BUILD-BRIEF.md](../../HUMANITIES-BUILD-BRIEF.md). History runs on two kinds. **Exegetical** problems are strict: what a source says, when something happened, what a canon required, which words decide a reading. **Evaluative** problems are verdict-neutral: whether Constantine's conversion was sincere, whether the crusades were defensive, whether Pius XII should have spoken louder. Any conclusion passes if the required moves are made: state the rival readings at their strongest, name the evidence each leans on, say what would move the question. Four archetypes carry the course: **close reading** (the workhorse, on public-domain sources: Tacitus, Pliny, Eusebius, Athanasius, Bede, Einhard, conciliar canons in Schaff's *Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers*, papal documents before 1930, the 95 Theses); **diagnose** (an invented op-ed or museum placard, and the historical assumption doing its work); **find the crux** between named historians; and **apply to a hard case**, which here means testing a popular historical claim against the evidence ("the Inquisition burned millions", "Galileo was jailed for saying the earth moves"). There are occasional **formal** parts (checking a growth-rate estimate), and no apologetic ones: defending the Church's claims from its history belongs to `apologetics-catholic-claims`. Where a doctrinal claim's weight matters, it carries its level from `fundamental-theology`; misstating a date or overstating a level is an exegetical error.

## Dangerous Checklist

When you finish, you can:

- [ ] Put any major event from the apostles to Leo XIV in its century and its causal chain, and explain the hinge dates (312, 451, 800, 1054, 1122, 1517, 1545, 1789, 1870, 1965) as turning points rather than trivia
- [ ] Read a primary source as a historian does: author, date, audience, purpose, and what it can and cannot establish (Pliny, Eusebius, Einhard, a conciliar canon, a papal bull)
- [ ] Tell what a council *did* (who convened it, who fought, how it was received) from what it *defined*, and say which course owns the definition
- [ ] Trace the historical evidence for Roman primacy era by era (1 Clement, Victor I, Leo I, the Franks, Gregory VII, Innocent III, Boniface VIII, Constance, Vatican I) and say what each piece does and does not show
- [ ] Explain the East–West schism as a process, not a date, naming the events that made it and the ones that tried to heal it
- [ ] Name the crux between historians on the course's great disputes (why Christians were persecuted, Constantine's conversion, crusader motives, the Cathars, the English Reformation, the Galileo affair, Pius XII) and what evidence would move each
- [ ] Test a popular historical claim (the "Dark Ages", the Inquisition's body count, Galileo's prison, "Hitler's Pope") against the evidence, conceding what is true in it
- [ ] Explain the Reformation's causes as several: theological, political, economic and technological, and weigh them against each other
- [ ] Explain Catholic reform as more than a reaction, and say what Trent changed in the life of an ordinary parish
- [ ] Tell the Church's failures plainly (forced conversion, the massacres of Jews, the Inquisition, conquest in the Americas, the abuse crisis), and say how the Church itself has since acknowledged them
- [ ] Explain how the papacy lost its temporal power between 1789 and 1870, and what that loss did to its spiritual authority
- [ ] State what Vatican II did as an event, and why its interpretation (continuity or rupture) is still contested

## Modules

### Module 1: The apostolic Church to Constantine (c. 30-337)

A Jewish messianic movement becomes an empire-wide church with bishops, a canon and martyrs, and then, suddenly, the emperor's favoured religion.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1.1 | The first generation and its sources | Say what can be known about the Church from c. 30 to 70, and from which sources | Jerusalem community, Paul's mission, the Jerusalem meeting (Acts 15, c. 49), Nero's persecution (Tacitus, *Annals* 15.44), the fall of the Temple (70), source criticism |
| 1.2 | Bishops, canon, and the parting of the ways (c. 70-200) | Explain how the second-century Church organized itself, and weigh the early evidence for Rome's standing | 1 Clement, Ignatius and the single bishop, Irenaeus's succession lists, Marcion, Victor I and the Easter controversy (Eusebius, book 5), the late monepiscopate at Rome (Lampe), the partings of the ways (Dunn, Boyarin) |
| 1.3 | Persecution and martyrdom (64-311) | Say when, why and how Christians were persecuted, and why historians disagree about the "why" | Pliny and Trajan (*Letters* 10.96–97), Perpetua (203), Decius (250) and the lapsed, the Great Persecution (303), Galerius's edict (311), de Ste. Croix vs Sherwin-White |
| 1.4 | Constantine (306-337) | Tell what Constantine did for the Church and weigh the rival accounts of his conversion | Milvian Bridge (312), the "Edict of Milan" (313), the Donatist schism, Lactantius vs Eusebius on the vision, Burckhardt vs Barnes on sincerity, Stark's growth estimate (**formal**) |

**Boss problem 1:** Pliny's letter to Trajan and Trajan's reply (*Letters* 10.96–97, public-domain translation). (a) Set out the procedure Pliny followed, what he was unsure of, and what Trajan's reply permits and forbids (not seeking Christians out, anonymous accusations, pardon for those who sacrifice). (b) De Ste. Croix held that Christians were punished for the name and for refusing the gods; Sherwin-White stressed their obstinacy before the magistrate. Name the crux and the words in the letters each reading leans on. (c) Stark estimates about 40% growth per decade from roughly a thousand Christians in the year 40. Check what that gives for the year 300, then evaluate the claim that "Christians were persecuted continuously for three centuries" against the letters and the chronology of 1.3. Parts (a)–(b) are exegetical; (c) is formal, then evaluative.

### Module 2: Councils, empire, and the desert (325-553)

With imperial favour came imperial councils, imperial exiles and imperial politics, and a counter-movement of Christians who fled to the desert.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2.1 | Nicaea and the imperial Church (325-395) | Tell the story of the Arian controversy as politics, from Nicaea to Theodosius | Arius and Alexander, the emperor convenes a council, Athanasius's exiles, Julian, Theodosius and Nicene Christianity as law (380), Constantinople (381), Ambrose and Theodosius, "Arianism" as a polemical label (Ayres); doctrine ceded to `trinity-and-god` |
| 2.2 | Ephesus, Chalcedon, and Leo's Rome (431-553) | Explain why the Christological councils split the East, and what Leo I made of Rome's see | Alexandria vs Constantinople, Cyril and Nestorius, the "Robber Synod" (449), Leo's Tome, Chalcedon's canon 28, the non-Chalcedonian churches (miaphysite, not "monophysite"), the sack of Rome (410) and Gelasius's letter (494), Justinian and Vigilius (553); doctrine ceded to `christology` |
| 2.3 | The desert and the monastery (c. 270-c. 550) | Say what monasticism was for, how it spread, and how it became an institution | Antony and Athanasius's *Life*, Pachomius and the common life, Basil, women ascetics (Macrina, Paula), Cassian, Benedict's Rule, the holy man (Peter Brown) |

**Boss problem 2:** Canon 28 of Chalcedon and Leo I's rejection of it (public-domain translations in *Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers*, 2nd series: the canons in vol. 14, Leo's letters in vol. 12). (a) State what the canon grants the see of Constantinople and the ground it gives for the privileges of both Romes. (b) State the grounds on which Leo rejects the canon, and name the crux between the two theories of precedence. (c) Evaluate the claim "by 451 the bishop of Rome's authority was accepted throughout the Church" against the record of 2.1–2.2: the council's reception of the Tome, canon 28 itself, and the churches that rejected Chalcedon. What the evidence means for the doctrine of primacy is left to `ecclesiology-and-mariology`; the question here is historical. Parts (a)–(b) are exegetical; (c) is evaluative.

### Module 3: The early medieval Church (590-962)

The western empire is gone: the Church converts the new peoples, loses half the Christian Mediterranean to Islam, and turns from Constantinople to the Franks.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3.1 | Gregory the Great and the conversion of the peoples (590-c. 1000) | Explain how Europe became Christian, and why historians argue over what "conversion" meant | Gregory's mission to Kent (597) and letter to Mellitus (in Bede), Irish monks, Whitby (664), Boniface, the forced conversion of the Saxons, Cyril and Methodius, top-down vs popular Christianization (Fletcher) |
| 3.2 | The rise of Islam and the Christian Mediterranean (632-c. 900) | Say what the Arab conquests did to the Church, East and West | the conquests (Jerusalem, Alexandria, Carthage, Spain from 711), three patriarchates under Muslim rule, *dhimmi* status, Christians under the caliphate (John of Damascus), the Pirenne thesis and its archaeological critics |
| 3.3 | Popes, Franks, and emperors (726-962) | Explain how the papacy acquired a state and crowned an emperor, and what came after | iconoclasm and the Lombards push Rome toward the Franks, Pepin's anointing and donation (751–756), the forged Donation of Constantine (exposed by Valla, 1440), Christmas Day 800, the "iron century" and its hostile sources (Liutprand), Otto I (962) |

**Boss problem 3:** Einhard's account, in his *Life of Charlemagne* (public-domain translation), of Charles's reaction to the imperial title. (a) State exactly what Einhard claims Charles said and felt, and what the passage does not say. (b) Historians read the reaction as displeasure at the pope's role, displeasure at Byzantine offence, or a conventional show of modesty. Name the crux and the evidence that would decide it, including Charles's own crowning of Louis the Pious at Aachen in 813 with no pope present. (c) Pirenne argued that without Muhammad there would have been no Charlemagne. Evaluate the thesis against the evidence of 3.2–3.3 in 300 words, any verdict, stating what the archaeological critics showed and what survives of the thesis. Parts (a)–(b) are exegetical; (c) is evaluative.

### Module 4: Reform, schism, and crusade (1046-1291)

A reformed papacy claims to free the Church from lay control, breaks with Constantinople, and launches armed pilgrimages to Jerusalem.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4.1 | The reform papacy and the investiture contest (910-1122) | Explain what the Gregorian reformers wanted, and how the fight with the emperor ended | Cluny (910), Sutri (1046), Leo IX, simony and clerical marriage, the papal election decree (1059), Gregory VII and the *Dictatus Papae*, Canossa (1077), the Concordat of Worms (1122), "papal revolution" (Berman) vs "right order" (Tellenbach) |
| 4.2 | East and West: the long estrangement (726-1965) | Explain why 1054 is a symbol, not the break, and name the events that made and tried to heal the schism | iconoclasm, Photius, the filioque as a grievance (doctrine ceded), Humbert and Cerularius (1054), the sack of Constantinople (1204), Lyons II (1274) and Florence (1439), the lifting of the excommunications (1965), Chadwick's "making of a rift" |
| 4.3 | The crusades (1095-1291) | Tell what the crusades were, why men went, and what they did | Clermont (1095) and the versions of Urban's speech, the crusade indulgence, the Rhineland massacres of Jews (1096), Jerusalem (1099), military orders, Hattin (1187), Acre (1291), Riley-Smith's piety vs the materialist reading, John Paul II's acts of repentance (2000–2001) |

**Boss problem 4:** Urban II's speech at Clermont survives only in versions written after 1099, among them Fulcher of Chartres's and Robert the Monk's (public-domain translations in Munro's *Urban and the Crusaders*, 1895). (a) Compare what each version has Urban say about the Eastern Christians, about Jerusalem, and about the spiritual reward, citing the words that show it. (b) Say what the later date of writing means for using either as evidence of Urban's aims, and name the crux between the penitential-piety reading of crusader motives and the materialist one, with the evidence that bears on it. (c) Evaluate the claim "the crusades, not 1054, made the schism between East and West" in 300 words, any verdict, using 4.2–4.3. Parts (a)–(b) are exegetical; (c) is evaluative.

### Module 5: Papal monarchy and its crisis (1198-1449)

The medieval papacy at its height of power builds an order of friars, universities and inquisitors, then overreaches, moves to Avignon, and splits in three.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5.1 | Papal monarchy: Innocent III to Boniface VIII (1198-1303) | Explain how far papal power reached, and how Boniface VIII lost the confrontation with France | *plenitudo potestatis*, Lateran IV (1215): annual confession, distinctive dress for Jews, measures against heresy; Innocent III and King John; *Clericis laicos* and *Unam Sanctam* (1302); Anagni (1303) |
| 5.2 | Friars and universities (c. 1200-1323) | Say what the mendicant orders and the universities changed about the medieval Church | Francis and Dominic, poverty and preaching, Paris and Bologna and papal privileges, friars in the schools, the Franciscan poverty dispute and John XXII |
| 5.3 | Heresy, inquisition, and the Jews (c. 1140-1320s) | Tell how the medieval Church pursued dissent, and weigh the evidence of the inquisitors' own records | Cathars and Waldensians, the Albigensian Crusade (from 1209), papal inquisitors (1230s), *Ad extirpanda* (1252) and torture, the Montaillou register, the Talmud burned in Paris, expulsions; "persecuting society" (Moore) and whether the Cathar church was real (Pegg vs Hamilton) |
| 5.4 | Avignon, the Great Schism, and the councils (1305-1449) | Explain how the Church came to have three popes and how a council ended the schism | the Avignon popes and the Templars, Catherine of Siena and the return (1377), the disputed election of 1378, Pisa (1409), Constance: *Haec Sancta*, Hus, Martin V (1417); Basel and Florence; Mollat's revision of the "Babylonian captivity" |

**Boss problem 5:** Boniface VIII's bull *Unam Sanctam* (1302), in a public-domain translation. (a) Close-read the two swords passage: which sword is said to be wielded by whom, and at whose command? (b) Historians and theologians have read the bull as claiming direct papal power over temporal rulers, or only the spiritual power's superiority and right to judge. Name the words that each reading must explain away. The theological status of the bull's closing sentence belongs to `ecclesiology-and-mariology`. (c) Evaluate the claim "the Avignon popes were puppets of the French crown" in 300 words, any verdict, using the Templars, the popes' administration and the city's status (5.4). Parts (a)–(b) are exegetical; (c) is evaluative.

### Module 6: The Reformations (1500-1648)

An indulgence campaign sets off a protest that splits Western Christendom along lines drawn as much by princes as by theologians, and the Catholic Church reforms itself at Trent.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 6.1 | The late medieval Church and Luther's protest (c. 1500-1521) | Weigh the state of the Church on the eve, and tell how an academic protest became a break | Renaissance popes, lay piety and the "decay" debate (Duffy), humanism and Erasmus, the St Peter's indulgence, the 95 Theses (1517) and the posting dispute (Iserloh), printing, *Exsurge Domine* (1520), Worms (1521); doctrine argued out in `apologetics-catholic-claims` |
| 6.2 | Reformations beyond Wittenberg (1521-1648) | Map the Protestant reformations and explain why religion followed rulers | the Peasants' War, Zwingli, Calvin's Geneva, Anabaptists and Münster, the princes and the Peace of Augsburg (1555), the French wars and Nantes (1598), the Thirty Years' War and Westphalia (1648) |
| 6.3 | The English Reformations (1527-1603) | Tell how England broke with Rome and weigh fast-Reformation against slow-Reformation histories | Henry's annulment, the Act of Supremacy (1534), More and Fisher, the dissolution of the monasteries, Edward and Mary (the burnings), the Elizabethan settlement (1559), *Regnans in Excelsis* (1570), the Catholic martyrs; Dickens vs Haigh and Duffy |
| 6.4 | Trent and Catholic reform (1545-1600s) | Say what Trent did and how its reform reached parishes | Trent's three periods (1545–1563), reform decrees (residence, seminaries), the Index, the Roman Catechism and Missal, Borromeo, the Jesuits (1540) and Teresa of Ávila, "Catholic Reform" and "Counter-Reformation" (Jedin), "early modern Catholicism" (O'Malley); doctrine ceded to `creation-grace-last-things` and `sacraments-and-liturgy` |

**Boss problem 6:** Luther's 95 Theses (public-domain translation). (a) Sort a supplied set of theses into those attacking the preaching of indulgences and those questioning papal power over the penalties of purgatory, and say which words place each. (b) Dickens told the English Reformation as a fast change welcomed from below; Haigh and Duffy told it as slow and imposed on a living Catholicism. Name the crux and the kinds of evidence (wills, churchwardens' accounts, visitation records) that bear on it. (c) Evaluate the claim "Trent was a reaction to Protestantism" in 300 words, any verdict, using the reforms that predate 1517 and Jedin's two terms (6.1, 6.4). Parts (a)–(b) are exegetical; (c) is evaluative.

### Module 7: A Church in new worlds (1493-1773)

Catholic Europe meets the Americas, Asia and the new astronomy, and in each case has to judge something it has never seen before.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 7.1 | Conquest and conscience in the Americas (1493-c. 1600) | Tell the Church's part in conquest and in its critique, without flattening either | *Inter caetera* (1493) and royal patronage, Montesinos's sermon (1511), Las Casas, *Sublimis Deus* (1537), the Valladolid debate (1550–51), African slavery and the Church's complicity, the Black Legend (Hanke) |
| 7.2 | Accommodation in Asia (1542-1773) | Explain the Jesuit strategy in Asia and why Rome ruled against it | Xavier, Valignano and Ricci, the Japanese martyrs, de Nobili, the Chinese rites controversy and its reversal (1939), the suppression of the Jesuits (1773) |
| 7.3 | The Galileo affair (1610-1633, and after) | Tell what happened to Galileo and why, and test the conflict narrative against it | the telescope, the Letter to the Grand Duchess Christina and Bellarmine's letter to Foscarini (1615), the 1616 decree, the *Dialogue* and Urban VIII, the 1633 trial and abjuration, Copernican works dropped from the Index (1835), John Paul II's 1992 address, the conflict thesis (Draper, White) vs historians of science (Brooke, Numbers) |

**Boss problem 7:** Bellarmine's letter to Foscarini and Galileo's Letter to the Grand Duchess Christina, both 1615 (read in the lesson's own translations from the Italian or pre-1930 English ones). (a) State each writer's answer to the question when a passage of Scripture about the heavens should be read other than literally, and locate the exact point where they part: what each requires before reinterpretation. (b) Historians weigh the causes of the 1633 condemnation differently: the Copernican question, Galileo's handling of Urban VIII in the *Dialogue*, and the Counter-Reformation politics of interpreting Scripture. Name the crux and the documents that bear on it. (c) Evaluate the claim "Galileo was jailed for teaching that the earth moves, and the Church rehabilitated him in 1992" against the record (7.3). (d) The Chinese rites ruling was reversed in 1939, and Copernican works left the Index in stages between 1758 and 1835. In 150 words, name the most important difference between how the two reversals came about. Parts (a)–(b) are exegetical; (c)–(d) are evaluative.

### Module 8: Revolution and the Roman Question (1750-1914)

### Module 9: The twentieth century and after (1914-present)

## Notes

- **Lesson count.** The roadmap estimated ~28; the syllabus runs 30, the ceiling allowed for this course. Two thousand years across nine eras does not fit in fewer without cramming: every event on the steering list needs a lesson with room for its historiographical dispute, since practising that dispute is the point. Merges already made to hold the line: Constantine gets one lesson; Leo I joins Chalcedon; the iron century joins the Frankish papacy; the crusades are one lesson; Boniface VIII joins Innocent III; the eve of the Reformation joins Luther. If a lesson overflows when built, split it and trim elsewhere rather than grow past ~31.
- **The primacy thread.** `ecclesiology-and-mariology` takes this course as a prerequisite mainly for the historical record of Roman primacy. Lessons 1.2, 2.2, 3.3, 4.1–4.2, 5.1, 5.4 and 8.2 each carry an explicit "what this shows, what it doesn't" paragraph on it, and the reference card should gather them into one timeline. The theological argument over what the record proves stays with `ecclesiology-and-mariology` and `apologetics-catholic-claims`, where Orthodox and Protestant readings are stated at their strongest.
- **Honesty rule for scandal lessons.** Where the historical record is hard (the Rhineland massacres, the Albigensian Crusade, *Ad extirpanda*, the Mortara case, the abuse crisis), lessons give the facts first, then the historians' debate, and only then the Church's own later acknowledgement (the Day of Pardon, 2000; John Paul II on 1204 in Athens, 2001; *We Remember*, 1998). An acknowledgement never substitutes for the facts, and no lesson softens a number that the evidence supports. Where popular numbers are inflated (Inquisition executions, the Galileo myth), the correction is made with the same care.
- **Ownership collisions resolved.** `fundamental-theology`'s syllabus cedes "councils, the Galileo affair, the modernist crisis as events" here and gives the history of *Pastor Aeternus* to `ecclesiology-and-mariology`; 8.2 therefore treats Vatican I as an event (convocation, factions, the minority's departure, the war) and leaves the text's drafting and meaning there. No built lessons in the library cover this course's topics.
- **Bridges.** The Lateran IV canons on Jews and the 1096 massacres return in `apologetics-foundations` (Christianity in dialogue with Judaism); medieval usury and the Templars' finance belong to the debt thread (`history-of-debt`, `theology-of-debt`); the indulgence controversy of 6.1 is the treasury of merit (`theology-of-debt`) meeting church politics; Westphalia (6.2) is where `international-relations` starts the state system; the Galileo affair (7.3) is the test case `fundamental-theology` uses for faith and reason.
- **Date discipline.** Every date in a lesson is checked against the ODCC or a standard history before publishing. Where the date itself is disputed (the Jerusalem meeting, 1 Clement, Ignatius, the destruction of the Serapeum, Benedict's Rule), the lesson gives a range and says so.
