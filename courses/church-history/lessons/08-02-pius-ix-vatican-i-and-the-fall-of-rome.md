# Church History · Lesson 8.2: Pius IX, Vatican I, and the fall of Rome

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 8: Revolution and the Roman Question · Builds on: [8.1 Enlightenment, Revolution, and Napoleon](08-01-enlightenment-revolution-and-napoleon.md), [5.4 Avignon, the Great Schism, and the councils](05-04-avignon-the-great-schism-and-the-councils.md) · Unlocks: [8.3 Leo XIII and the modernist crisis](08-03-leo-xiii-and-the-modernist-crisis.md), [9.1 The Church and the totalitarian states](09-01-the-church-and-the-totalitarian-states.md)

## Why this matters

On 18 July 1870 a council in St Peter's defined the pope's primacy and infallibility. Two months later, on 20 September, Italian troops breached the walls of Rome at Porta Pia and ended a papal state that had existed for more than a thousand years. The same pontificate saw the papacy's spiritual claims defined at their fullest and its temporal power abolished. Pius IX reigned 31 years (1846–1878), the longest reign of any pope whose dates are secure.

## The idea

Two tendencies divided nineteenth-century Catholics. [Gallicanism](../reference.md#gallicanism), whose classic statement was the French clergy's Four Articles of 1682, held that a general council stood above the pope, that papal definitions needed the Church's consent, and that kings were independent of Rome in temporal affairs. [Ultramontanism](../reference.md#ultramontanism) ("beyond the mountains", looking to Rome across the Alps) answered that after the Revolution only a strong, centralized papacy could protect the Church from the state. Its manifesto was Joseph de Maistre's *Du Pape* (1819). Napoleon's treatment of the Church ([8.1](08-01-enlightenment-revolution-and-napoleon.md)) made the ultramontane case for many bishops. John O'Malley's *Vatican I* (2018) reads the council as that movement's victory.

Rome also policed theology on both sides of the faith-and-reason question. Gregory XVI's *Mirari vos* (August 1832) condemned the liberal programme of Lamennais's journal *L'Avenir*, though without naming him. In September 1835 his brief *Dum acerbissimas* condemned the works of Georg Hermes, a Bonn theologian who tried to build faith on reason. The Strasbourg priest Louis Bautain, accused of the opposite fault, had to sign propositions affirming that reason can prove God's existence (8 September 1840). Anton Günther's works went on the Index on 8 January 1857. Vatican I's *Dei Filius* later settled the doctrine; that belongs to [`fundamental-theology` 2.3](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/02-03-fideism-and-rationalism.md).

The shape of the lesson is a trade no one planned. The pope lost his state, and the spiritual power of Rome over the Church grew.

## Source

The minority's letter to Pius IX, Rome, 17 July 1870, the day before the final vote (Latin text printed by "Quirinus", *Letters from Rome on the Council*, 1870; the lesson's own translation):

> In the general congregation held on the 13th of this month we cast our votes on the schema of the first dogmatic constitution on the Church of Christ. … Since that time nothing at all has happened to change our judgment; on the contrary, many things, and very grave ones, have confirmed us in it. We therefore declare that we renew and confirm the votes we have given. Confirming our votes by this writing, we have resolved to be absent from the public session to be held on the 18th. For the filial piety and reverence which only lately brought our envoys to Your Holiness's feet do not allow us, in a cause touching Your Holiness's person so closely, to say *non placet* openly and to our father's face. … We therefore return without delay to our flocks.

The signers do not withdraw their objection. They repeat it in writing, give reverence as their reason for staying away, and close by professing faith and obedience to the pope. Whether the letter is evidence of a free council or of a cornered minority is P3's question.

## The record

1. **The liberal pope (1846–48).** Elected 16 June 1846, Pius IX granted an amnesty to political prisoners (16 July 1846) and a constitution for the Papal States (14 March 1848). In an allocution of 29 April 1848 he refused to make war on Catholic Austria for Italian unity, and nationalist enthusiasm turned against him.
2. **Revolution and exile (1848–50).** His minister Pellegrino Rossi was stabbed to death on 15 November 1848. On 24 November Pius fled in disguise to Gaeta, in the Kingdom of the Two Sicilies. A Roman Republic was proclaimed on 9 February 1849 and abolished his temporal power. French troops took the city that summer, and Pius returned on 12 April 1850, no longer a liberal. A French garrison guarded Rome for most of the next twenty years.
3. **Doctrine as event.** On 8 December 1854, in *Ineffabilis Deus*, Pius defined the Immaculate Conception after consulting the bishops by letter, a pope defining a dogma without a council (content: [`ecclesiology-and-mariology`](../../ecclesiology-and-mariology/syllabus.md); level: solemnly defined, rung 1 on [`fundamental-theology` 4.4](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-04-the-ladder-of-doctrinal-authority.md)'s ladder). On 8 December 1864 came the encyclical *Quanta cura* with the [Syllabus of Errors](../reference.md#syllabus-of-errors), a list of eighty propositions censured in earlier papal documents. Bishop Félix Dupanloup of Orléans published a moderating reading in 1865. Boss problem 8 takes up the text.
4. **The [Mortara case](../reference.md#mortara-case) (1858).** Facts first. In June 1858 papal police in Bologna, acting for the local inquisitor, Pier Gaetano Feletti, took six-year-old Edgardo Mortara from his Jewish parents. A Catholic servant, Anna Morisi, said she had baptized him secretly during an infant illness. Church law held that a baptized child must be raised a Catholic, and papal law already forbade Jews to employ Christian servants. Pius refused every appeal, from the family, from Rome's Jewish community, from Moses Montefiore (in Rome in 1859), and from a storm of protest in Europe and America, and raised the boy under his own patronage. Bologna left papal rule in 1859, and in April 1860 its new authorities tried Feletti, who was acquitted as having acted under the government of the day. Edgardo became a priest of the Canons Regular of the Lateran and died in Belgium in 1940. The debate is Example 2.
5. **The shrinking state (1859–70).** In 1859–60 the Romagna, the Marches and Umbria went to Piedmont, after the papal army's defeat at Castelfidardo (18 September 1860). Only Rome and its surrounding region remained.
6. **The council.** Vatican I opened on 8 December 1869; about 700 bishops and prelates attended its first public session. *Dei Filius* passed on 24 April 1870. Infallibility, not in the original agenda, was added on the majority's petition, and Pius made no secret that he wanted it. In the [test vote of 13 July](../reference.md#vatican-i-votes-of-july-1870) 601 fathers voted: 451 *placet*, 88 *non placet*, 62 *placet iuxta modum* (yes, with amendments). The majority had already closed the general debate in June while dozens were still waiting to speak, and a minority deputation's last appeal to Pius for changes failed. Some 55 to 60 of the minority signed the Source letter and left. On 18 July, 535 present, *Pastor Aeternus* passed 533 to 2. The definition itself, primacy of jurisdiction and the irreformability of *ex cathedra* definitions, is [`ecclesiology-and-mariology`](../../ecclesiology-and-mariology/syllabus.md)'s; its conditions are [`fundamental-theology` 4.2](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-02-infallibility-and-its-conditions.md)'s.
7. **Porta Pia.** France declared war on Prussia on 19 July and recalled its garrison in early August. After Sedan (2 September) Italian troops entered Rome on 20 September 1870, and a plebiscite on 2 October ratified annexation. On 20 October Pius suspended the council indefinitely. It never reconvened.
8. **The prisoner in the Vatican.** The [Law of Guarantees](../reference.md#law-of-guarantees) (13 May 1871) offered the pope sovereign honours, use of the Vatican palaces and an annual endowment of about 3.2 million lire. Pius rejected it in *Ubi nos* (15 May 1871) and never again left the Vatican. The Roman Question stayed open until the Lateran Treaty of 1929 ([9.1](09-01-the-church-and-the-totalitarian-states.md)).
9. **Refusals.** In Munich the historian Ignaz von Döllinger refused the decrees and was excommunicated in April 1871. His followers formed the [Old Catholics](../reference.md#old-catholics) (first congress, Munich, September 1871; bishop Joseph Hubert Reinkens consecrated at Rotterdam by the Jansenist bishop of Deventer, 11 August 1873), though Döllinger himself never joined them. In the new German Empire, Bismarck's [Kulturkampf](../reference.md#kulturkampf) (1871–78) expelled the Jesuits (1872) and put church appointments under state control in Prussia's May Laws (1873). Bishops were imprisoned or exiled, and the laws were eased only after Pius died on 7 February 1878.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean: why refuse the Law of Guarantees?).** The offer looked generous: honours, a guard, extraterritorial palaces, an income. Run the four questions from [1.1](01-01-the-first-generation-and-its-sources.md). Who granted it? The Italian parliament, by ordinary statute. What could that parliament do next year? Amend or repeal it. So the pope's independence would rest on the goodwill of the state that had just conquered him, and every bishop in the world would deal with a pope whose freedom was an Italian government's gift. Pius's *Ubi nos* argued exactly this: no guarantee granted by another sovereign could secure the pope's freedom. The refusal cost income and freedom of movement; it kept the Roman Question open until 1929 settled it by treaty, between sovereigns.

**Example 2 (hard: whose testimony settles the Mortara case?).** David Kertzer's *The Kidnapping of Edgardo Mortara* (1997), from the archives of the Bologna inquisition, the Vatican and the family, tells a seizure under canon law that shocked liberal Europe. It also hardened opinion, including Napoleon III's, against papal rule just as that rule was failing. Defenders of Pius IX point to Edgardo himself: a priest who defended the pope for life, whose memoir Vittorio Messori published in Italian in 2005 (English 2017). The evidence strains in two places. First, the witness: an adult formed from the age of six in papal custody is a real source for what he came to believe, and a poor one for whether the removal was just. Second, the text: in 2018 Kertzer showed that the Italian edition departed from Mortara's Spanish manuscript, adding praise of Feletti and dropping hostile remarks about Jews. The publishers said the changes were minor and promised corrections. The Church has made no statement on the case. When Pius IX was beatified on 3 September 2000, members of the Mortara family and Jewish groups protested, citing it.

## Primacy: what this shows, what it doesn't

1870 is the culmination of the thread. A council of about 700 bishops and prelates from every continent defined the pope's universal jurisdiction and infallibility, and within a few years every bishop of the minority had accepted the decrees. No earlier era produces so explicit a conciliar statement of Roman primacy. What the record also shows is how the minority argued, and their case was largely historical. Bishop Karl Joseph Hefele, the historian of the councils, pressed the case of Pope Honorius, condemned by the Sixth Council (681). Others appealed to Constance ([5.4](05-04-avignon-the-great-schism-and-the-councils.md)), and asked for infallibility tied to the witness of the churches. Many, Dupanloup among them, thought a definition possibly true but inopportune. August Hasler (*How the Pope Became Infallible*, English 1981) read the pressure on them as making the council unfree. Klaus Schatz (*Vaticanum I*, 1992–94) defends the participants' freedom. Orthodox theologians treat 1870 as the sharpest statement of the claim they reject, and many Protestant historians see it as centralization completed. Catholic historians (Schatz, O'Malley) tell it as a development whose balance, the bishops, came only at Vatican II. What it proves belongs to [`ecclesiology-and-mariology`](../../ecclesiology-and-mariology/syllabus.md) and [`apologetics-catholic-claims`](../../apologetics-catholic-claims/syllabus.md).

## Watch out

- **"Vatican I made every papal word infallible."** The definition is narrow, and its conditions are [`fundamental-theology` 4.2](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-02-infallibility-and-its-conditions.md)'s. Overstating it is an exegetical error.
- **"533 to 2 means near-unanimity."** It is the vote of those present on 18 July. The test vote, with the minority present, was 451 to 88, with 62 conditional.
- **The council did not "end" in 1870.** It was suspended on 20 October 1870 and never formally closed.
- **Old Catholics are not Döllinger's church.** He inspired them but never joined; their bishops came through Utrecht.

## One-liner

> In 1870 Pius IX lost Rome and won the council: the papacy's temporal power ended two months after its spiritual authority was defined at its fullest.

**P1 (🟢) *(Formal (a)–(b) · Exegetical (c).)*** Use the lesson's figures for 13 and 18 July 1870.
(a) On 13 July, give each of the three responses as a percentage of those voting (one decimal), and the percentage who did *not* give a simple *placet*.
(b) On 18 July, what percentage voted *placet*, and by how many did attendance fall between the two votes?
(c) Take as given: the full text of the minority's letter says about 70 fathers in Rome abstained on 13 July, and some 55 to 60 bishops of the minority left before the 18th. In two sentences or fewer, say why the fall in attendance from (b) cannot be read as the number who walked out, and what the 533 to 2 result does not measure.

**P2 (🟡) *(Exegetical.)*** An invented museum placard:

> "In 1858 the Pope's police seized six-year-old Edgardo Mortara from his Jewish family in Bologna after a servant claimed she had secretly baptized him. International outrage forced Pius IX to back down, and when Bologna joined Italy in 1859 the boy was returned to his parents. The inquisitor responsible was convicted, and the Vatican formally apologized in 2000."

Identify every factual error, correct each from the record, and name the assumption about the case's outcome that the errors share. 150 words or fewer.

**P3 (🔴, optional) *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)*** Use the Source letter. (a) State the reasons the signers give for staying away on 18 July, and what they say about their earlier votes. Two sentences. (b) Hasler holds that pressure made the council unfree; Schatz defends the participants' freedom. Evaluate what the minority's departure shows about the question, any verdict. 150 words or fewer for (b).

**P1** *(Formal (a)–(b) · Exegetical (c).)*

(a) Total voting $451 + 88 + 62 = 601$. *Placet* $451/601 = 75.04\ldots \approx$ **75.0 percent**; *non placet* $88/601 = 14.64\ldots \approx$ **14.6 percent**; *placet iuxta modum* $62/601 = 10.32\ldots \approx$ **10.3 percent** (check: 99.9 after rounding, 100 exactly). Not a simple *placet*: $(88 + 62)/601 = 150/601 = 24.96\ldots \approx$ **25.0 percent**. (Computed in Python.)

(b) $533/535 = 0.99626\ldots \approx$ **99.6 percent** *placet*. Attendance fell by $601 - 535 =$ **66**.

**Must hit, strict (c):** the 66 is a *net* figure. It equals departures minus arrivals, and some who had not voted on 13 July (among the letter's roughly 70 abstainers, and Italian bishops recalled for the session, as the hostile correspondent Quirinus reports) voted on the 18th. So it cannot count the minority's departures, and the 533 to 2 result cannot be read as the council's opinion with the minority present.
**Wrong turns:** dividing by 774 (everyone who attended at some point) or by the roughly 1,050 entitled to attend. Reading 25 percent as "a quarter opposed the definition": the 62 conditional votes were yes votes that asked for amendments. Treating 66 as the number who walked out.
**Model answer (c):** The fall of 66 is net: some bishops left, and others who had not voted on 13 July took their seats, so it undercounts the departures. It measures who was in the hall on the 18th, not what the council thought on the 13th.

**P2** *(Exegetical.)*

**Must hit, strict:** four errors. (1) Pius did not back down; he refused every appeal and raised the boy under his patronage. (2) Edgardo was not returned in 1859. He stayed in Rome, left after 1870, became a priest and died in Belgium in 1940. (3) Feletti was tried in 1860 and **acquitted**, as having acted under the government of the day. (4) There was no apology in 2000. That year Pius IX was **beatified**, and the Mortara family and Jewish groups protested; the Church has made no statement on the case. The shared assumption is that the story ended in restitution: that outrage and the change of government undid the seizure. In fact the seizure stood.
**Wrong turns:** "correcting" the true parts (the seizure was in Bologna in 1858, he was six, and the claim was a secret baptism by a servant). Adding a moral verdict on the seizure, which the question does not ask for.
**Model answer:** Pius did not back down; he refused all appeals and kept Edgardo. The boy was never returned: he grew up in Rome, became a priest, and died in Belgium in 1940. Feletti was tried in 1860 and acquitted as having acted for the government of the time. There was no apology in 2000; that was the year Pius IX was beatified, and Jewish groups and Mortara's family protested. The placard assumes the story ended in restitution, as if outrage and Bologna's change of ruler reversed the seizure. The seizure stood.

**P3** *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** they stay away out of filial piety and reverence, unwilling to say *non placet* to the pope's face in a cause touching his person, and they must return to their flocks. They say nothing has changed their view, and they formally renew and confirm the votes of 13 July.
**Must hit, any verdict (b):** separate *freedom to vote* from *freedom from pressure*. Use the letter's own evidence: the signers voted *non placet* on 13 July and renew it in writing, and they give reverence, not threat, as their reason for leaving. Weigh the pressure the letter does not record: Pius's own advocacy of the definition, the refusal of their envoys' last requests, and the closing of debate. Say what would move the question: evidence of sanctions for opposing votes, or the minority bishops' private correspondence.
**Wrong turns:** treating departure as proof of coercion without evidence of a threat, or 533 to 2 as proof of consent. Deciding the definition's validity, which is [`ecclesiology-and-mariology`](../../ecclesiology-and-mariology/syllabus.md)'s question.
**Model answer (b), one of several:** The departure shows a minority that could vote freely and did: 88 said *non placet* on 13 July, and the letter renews that vote in writing. Nothing in it claims they were forbidden to vote again; their stated reason is reverence. That favours Schatz on the narrow question of the vote. Hasler's point is about pressure, not prohibition. The pope was a party to the question, lobbied for it, and turned down the minority deputation's last appeal, and the letter itself says reverence kept them from saying *non placet* "to our father's face". So the departure shows a council free to vote but not neutral in atmosphere. Whether that reaches "unfree" depends on evidence of sanctions, which the letter does not supply.

## Flashback

**From Lesson [7.3](07-03-the-galileo-affair.md) (The Galileo affair):** *(Exegetical.)* Four acts of the Galileo affair: (i) the opinion of eleven consultors of the Holy Office, 24 February 1616; (ii) the decree of the Congregation of the Index, 5 March 1616; (iii) the sentence read at Santa Maria sopra Minerva, 22 June 1633; (iv) the Holy Office's permission of 1822. (a) For each, say who issued it, what kind of act it was (advice, disciplinary decree, judicial sentence or permission), and in a phrase what it said about the Copernican doctrine or about Galileo. (b) Test the claim "In 1616 the Church declared the earth's motion a heresy" against (i) and (ii). Two sentences for (b).

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):** (i) The Holy Office's theological consultors; advice, never published. They judged the sun's immobility absurd in philosophy and formally heretical, and the earth's motion absurd and at least erroneous in faith. (ii) The Congregation of the Index, with Paul V's approval; a disciplinary decree. It called the doctrine false and wholly contrary to Scripture, suspended Copernicus's *De revolutionibus* until corrected, prohibited Foscarini's book and banned others teaching the same; Galileo was not named. (iii) The cardinals of the Holy Office sitting as judges (seven of ten signed); a judicial sentence. It found Galileo vehemently suspected of heresy, required his abjuration, banned the *Dialogue* and imposed formal imprisonment. (iv) The Holy Office, with Pius VII's approval; a permission. It allowed books teaching the earth's motion generally, after Settele's case of 1820.
**Must hit, strict (b):** the claim fails twice. The only 1616 text to use "formally heretical" was the unpublished consultors' opinion, and it attached that word to the sun's immobility, calling the earth's motion only "at least erroneous in faith". The published decree said "false" and "contrary to Scripture", not heresy.
**Wrong turns:** treating (i) as the Church's public act; calling (iii) a conviction for heresy (vehement suspicion is the middle of three grades); saying (iv) took Copernicus off the Index (that came with the 1835 edition); calling any of the four a council or a papal definition.
**Model answer:** (a) (i) Holy Office consultors, unpublished advice: the sun's immobility formally heretical, the earth's motion at least erroneous in faith. (ii) The Index, a decree Paul V approved: the doctrine false and contrary to Scripture, Copernicus suspended until corrected, Foscarini banned. (iii) The Holy Office cardinals as judges, a sentence: Galileo vehemently suspected of heresy, made to abjure, the *Dialogue* banned. (iv) The Holy Office with Pius VII's approval, a permission: such books allowed generally. (b) The word "heretical" appears only in the unpublished advice, and there it is applied to the sun standing still, while the earth's motion is called erroneous in faith. The decree that was published called the doctrine false and contrary to Scripture, which is a grave censure but not a declaration of heresy.
