# Church History · Lesson 9.2: The Second Vatican Council

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 9: The twentieth century and after · Builds on: [8.2 Pius IX, Vatican I, and the fall of Rome](08-02-pius-ix-vatican-i-and-the-fall-of-rome.md), [9.1 The Church and the totalitarian states](09-01-the-church-and-the-totalitarian-states.md) · Unlocks: [9.3 After the council](09-03-after-the-council.md)

## Why this matters

On 25 January 1959, three months after his election, John XXIII told a small group of cardinals at St Paul Outside the Walls that he meant to call an ecumenical council. Almost nobody had asked for one, and many in the Curia thought the last council, Vatican I, had made councils unnecessary. Six years later more than two thousand bishops had approved sixteen documents that changed the language of Catholic worship and the Church's stated relation to other Christians, to Jews and to the modern state. Everything argued about in [9.3](09-03-after-the-council.md) presupposes this lesson: what happened in St Peter's between 1962 and 1965, who made it happen, and how much of the outcome was decided by procedure rather than by argument.

## The idea

Keep apart three things that popular accounts run together.

**The texts** are what the council produced: sixteen documents, whose teaching belongs to the dogmatic courses. One fact about their weight matters here. Vatican II defined no new dogma. Its constitutions carry the weight of what they restate, which is often very high, and its other teaching sits mostly at the level of authentic ordinary magisterium; how to read that weight is [`fundamental-theology` 4.5](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-05-reading-a-documents-weight.md).

**The event** is the four autumn sessions: votes, commissions, petitions and crises. A large majority wanted renewal. A small, organised minority resisted. Both sides used the rules of procedure as weapons, and several turning points were procedural.

**The pope** stood above both. John XXIII set the tone in his opening address of 11 October 1962: he dismissed the "prophets of doom", distinguished the substance of the faith from the way it is expressed, and asked for the medicine of mercy rather than condemnation. Paul VI, elected in 1963, protected the majority's work and repeatedly intervened to bring the minority along.

John O'Malley's *What Happened at Vatican II* (2008), the standard one-volume history, argues that under all the specific quarrels lay three [issues under the issues](../reference.md#issues-under-the-issues). How may the Church change? How should the centre (pope and Curia) relate to the periphery (the bishops)? And in what *style* should the Church speak? His distinctive claim is the third: the council gave up the juridical, condemnatory genre of earlier councils for a rhetoric of invitation and praise.

## Source

Syllabus of Errors (1864), propositions 77–79, from the Latin. Every numbered item in the Syllabus is a proposition Pius IX **condemned**, so the Church's position is that each is false. The translation is this lesson's own.

> 77. In this our age it is no longer expedient that the Catholic religion be held as the only religion of the State, all other forms of worship whatever being excluded.
> 78. Hence it has been laudably provided by law in certain regions of Catholic name that persons immigrating there may have the public exercise of their own worship.
> 79. For it is false that the civil liberty of every form of worship, and likewise the full power granted to all of manifesting openly and publicly any opinions and thoughts whatever, leads more easily to the corruption of the morals and minds of peoples and to the spread of the plague of indifferentism.

Read 79 slowly. It is a double negative: the error is to *deny* that liberty of worship corrupts morals. This is the text the council's minority brought to the floor in 1964–65 against the draft on religious liberty. Whether the council's answer developed or reversed it is a doctrinal question, set in [`fundamental-theology`](../../fundamental-theology/syllabus.md) (boss problem 5) and [`catholic-social-teaching`](../../catholic-social-teaching/syllabus.md) 5.2.

## The record

1. **Preparation (1959–1962).** An antepreparatory commission (May 1959) canvassed the world's bishops. Ten preparatory commissions, most chaired by curial cardinals, drafted the texts ("schemas"). The Theological Commission under Cardinal Alfredo Ottaviani, head of the Holy Office, wrote the doctrinal ones. The apostolic constitution *Humanae Salutis* formally convoked the council on 25 December 1961.
2. **The first session (11 October–8 December 1962).** About 2,400 bishops processed into St Peter's. Two days later, at the first working meeting, Cardinal Achille Liénart of Lille moved to postpone the election of the ten conciliar commissions so that national hierarchies could propose candidates; Cardinal Josef Frings of Cologne seconded. The elections were held on 16 October from lists that the bishops' conferences drew up (Example 1).
3. **Liturgy first.** The preparatory schema on the liturgy, the one draft the reformers liked, was debated first; in November only 46 fathers voted against accepting it as the basis for discussion.
4. **The schema on revelation (20–21 November 1962).** Ottaviani's *De fontibus revelationis* ("on the sources of revelation") taught two sources, Scripture and Tradition; that disputed question belongs to [`fundamental-theology` 3.4](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/03-04-tradition.md). Asked whether discussion should be interrupted, a clear majority voted yes, but short of the two-thirds the rules required (P1). The next day John XXIII withdrew the schema and sent it to a mixed commission chaired by Ottaviani and by Cardinal Augustin Bea of the Secretariat for Christian Unity. It returned, rewritten, as *Dei Verbum* (1965).
5. **A new pope (1963).** John XXIII died on 3 June 1963. Giovanni Battista Montini, archbishop of Milan, was elected on 21 June as Paul VI and at once confirmed that the council would continue. The second session (29 September–4 December 1963) ended with the first promulgations: *Sacrosanctum Concilium* on the liturgy (2,147 to 4) and *Inter Mirifica*.
6. **The minority organises.** In October 1963 the [Coetus Internationalis Patrum](../reference.md#coetus-internationalis-patrum) (International Group of Fathers) took shape, founded by Archbishop Geraldo de Proença Sigaud of Diamantina; its steering committee included Archbishop Marcel Lefebvre and Bishop Luigi Carli. Its letters drew signatures in the hundreds, against majorities of two thousand, but it knew the rules well.
7. **Black Week (November 1964).** Near the end of the third session (14 September–21 November 1964), Paul VI had a [Nota explicativa praevia](../reference.md#nota-explicativa-praevia) attached to chapter 3 of the constitution on the Church, communicated on 16 November. It stated that episcopal collegiality never acts without its head and does not diminish papal primacy. On 19 November Cardinal Eugène Tisserant announced that the vote on religious liberty was postponed to the next session, after Coetus members appealed to the rules; a petition to the pope from the majority, with 441 signatures, failed to reverse it. Paul also required nineteen late changes to the decree on ecumenism. The Dutch coined the name [Black Week](../reference.md#black-week). On 21 November *Lumen Gentium* passed 2,151 to 5.
8. **Religious liberty and the Jews (1965).** Both drafts came from Bea's Secretariat, and both had been fought over since 1962. The text on the Jews went back to John XXIII's meeting with the French Jewish historian Jules Isaac (13 June 1960). It was dropped from the 1962 agenda after an Israeli official was named an unofficial observer (the "Wardi affair"). Arab governments protested it throughout. It was folded into a declaration on non-Christian religions, and the word "deicide" was removed, though the text still denied that the Passion could be charged against all Jews. *Nostra Aetate* passed on 28 October 1965 by 2,221 to 88. *Dignitatis Humanae*, largely shaped by the American Jesuit John Courtney Murray, went through six drafts. It passed on 7 December 1965 by 2,308 to 70.
9. **The close.** Also on 7 December, a joint declaration of Paul VI and Patriarch Athenagoras I was read simultaneously at the council and in Istanbul, consigning the excommunications of 1054 to oblivion ([4.2](04-02-east-and-west-the-long-estrangement.md)). The council closed on 8 December 1965.

## The sixteen documents

| Genre | Document | Promulgated |
|---|---|---|
| Constitution (4) | *Sacrosanctum Concilium*: liturgy | 4 Dec 1963 |
| | *Lumen Gentium*: the Church (dogmatic) | 21 Nov 1964 |
| | *Dei Verbum*: revelation (dogmatic) | 18 Nov 1965 |
| | *Gaudium et Spes*: the Church in the modern world (pastoral) | 7 Dec 1965 |
| Decree (9) | *Inter Mirifica*: media | 4 Dec 1963 |
| | *Orientalium Ecclesiarum*, *Unitatis Redintegratio*: Eastern Catholic churches, ecumenism | 21 Nov 1964 |
| | *Christus Dominus*, *Perfectae Caritatis*, *Optatam Totius*: bishops, religious life, priestly formation | 28 Oct 1965 |
| | *Apostolicam Actuositatem*: the laity | 18 Nov 1965 |
| | *Ad Gentes*, *Presbyterorum Ordinis*: missions, priests | 7 Dec 1965 |
| Declaration (3) | *Gravissimum Educationis*, *Nostra Aetate*: education, non-Christian religions | 28 Oct 1965 |
| | *Dignitatis Humanae*: religious liberty | 7 Dec 1965 |

Notice the rhythm: none after the first session, two after the second, three after the third, and eleven in the fourth session's last six weeks. The hardest texts were the last to be finished.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean: why a procedural motion mattered).** On 13 October 1962 the fathers were handed ballots for sixteen elected members of each of ten commissions, together with a booklet listing everyone who had sat on the preparatory commissions. Most bishops knew almost nobody outside their own country, so that list was the obvious one to copy, and the Curia's drafters would likely have been elected to revise their own drafts. Liénart's motion bought three days. National conferences met and exchanged lists, and the northern European hierarchies built a cross-national slate. On the figures of the journalist Ralph Wiltgen, 79 of the slate's 109 candidates were elected, nearly half of all elected seats. No doctrine was debated. But commissions revised texts between sessions, and the vote on commission membership shaped every text that followed.

**Example 2 (hard: using a partisan source).** Those figures come from Wiltgen's *The Rhine Flows into the Tiber* (1967). Wiltgen, a Divine Word priest, ran a news service at the council with thousands of subscribers and close access to council fathers. His book supplies tallies, letters and backroom detail found nowhere else in English, and much of it can be checked against the official acts. But its organising thesis is itself an interpretation: that a ["European alliance"](../reference.md#european-alliance) of bishops from the Rhine countries planned and won a campaign against the Curia. The book counts its chapters as "victories", and it is sympathetic in places to the Coetus, whose unfavourable press it regrets. It has since been reissued by traditionalist publishers. So use it for what an insider could document, and treat its framing as one reading.

O'Malley's narrative, like most recent histories, speaks instead of a majority and a minority: the organised northern bishops led a body of opinion that already existed. The best evidence for that is early, before any papal pressure: in November 1962 only 46 fathers opposed taking the liturgy schema as the basis, and 1,368 wanted the revelation schema stopped. The minority's reply concerns the later, lopsided final votes (2,147 to 4, 2,151 to 5): they followed concessions and papal pressure for near-unanimity, so they measure consent to finished texts, not the balance of opinion at the start. The crux is what a vote in a council measures, and neither book settles it alone.

## Watch out

- **"Vatican II changed doctrine" and "Vatican II was only pastoral" are both overstatements.** It defined no dogma, but its dogmatic constitutions restate teaching at high weight ([`fundamental-theology` 4.5](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-05-reading-a-documents-weight.md)). Assign levels there, not here.
- **The revelation schema was not voted down.** The motion to interrupt it *failed* under the rules; the pope removed the schema.
- **Paul VI was nobody's rubber stamp.** Black Week was his doing, and so was the push to near-unanimity.
- **The 1965 declaration did not end the schism.** It says itself that the gesture is not enough to end the differences. It was a joint act of pope and patriarch, read at the council, not a conciliar decree.

## One-liner

> Vatican II was a majority for renewal, a minority that knew the rules, and a pope who needed both. What it *said* is in sixteen texts; what it *was* is in the votes.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢)** On 20 November 1962 the fathers voted on the question "Should the discussion of the schema on the sources of revelation be interrupted?" Wiltgen reports 2,209 ballots: 1,368 yes, 822 no, 19 invalid. The rules required a two-thirds majority.
(a) *(Formal)* Compute the number of yes votes needed and the shortfall, first taking two-thirds of all ballots cast, then two-thirds of valid ballots only. Give the yes share of all ballots to one decimal place.
(b) *(Exegetical)* In two sentences: what did the vote technically decide, and what happened the next day?

**P2 (🟡)** *Quanta Cura* (1864), section 3, in this lesson's translation from the Latin. Pius IX condemns the opinion, which Gregory XVI had called a madness:

> that liberty of conscience and of worship is each man's own right, which ought to be proclaimed and asserted by law in every rightly constituted society; and that citizens have a right to every kind of liberty, to be restrained by no authority, whether ecclesiastical or civil, by which they may openly and publicly manifest and declare any of their ideas whatever, by voice, by print, or in any other way.

(a) *(Exegetical)* List the distinct claims the condemned opinion joins together, and quote the words that mark each.
(b) *(Exegetical)* In 1965 the Coetus argued that *Dignitatis Humanae* asserted what this sentence condemned; the drafters answered that it did not. *Dignitatis Humanae* (paraphrased) declares a right to immunity from coercion in religious matters, grounded in the dignity of the person, to be recognised in civil law, limited by just public order (sections 2 and 7), and says it leaves untouched the traditional teaching on the moral duty toward the true religion (section 1). Name the single interpretive premise the two sides disagree about, and one piece of evidence from this lesson that the Coetus reading can use. 150 words or fewer in total.

**P3 (🔴, optional)** *(Exegetical)* An invented documentary voice-over:

> "On the very first working morning of the council, the bishops of the Rhine seized power. They threw out every text the Roman Curia had written and wrote a new Church from scratch. Paul VI, their ally, signed whatever they sent him — and on the council's last day, the council itself ended the thousand-year schism with the Orthodox."

Name the source whose framing the script inherits, and correct three distinct factual errors from the lesson's record. 150 words or fewer.

**P1** *(Formal (a) · Exegetical (b))*

(a) All ballots: $\tfrac{2}{3} \times 2209 = 1472.67$, so **1,473** yes votes were needed; shortfall $1473 - 1368 =$ **105**. Valid ballots only: $2209 - 19 = 2190$, and $\tfrac{2}{3} \times 2190 =$ **1,460** exactly; shortfall $1460 - 1368 =$ **92**. Yes share of all ballots: $1368 / 2209 = 0.6193$, i.e. **61.9 percent**. (Computed in Python.) Published accounts give different shortfalls partly for this reason, and some print the yes figure as 1,360.

**Must hit, strict (b):** the motion to interrupt failed, so under the rules debate on the schema was to continue; the next day John XXIII withdrew the schema and sent it to a mixed commission of the Theological Commission and Bea's Secretariat for revision.
**Wrong turns:** saying the council "rejected" the schema by vote; saying the minority won the outcome as well as the vote; forgetting that 1,473 is the next whole vote above 1,472.67.
**Model answer:** (b) Technically nothing was rejected: the motion to interrupt fell short of two-thirds, so discussion should have gone on. On 21 November John XXIII took the schema off the floor himself and gave it to a joint commission under Ottaviani and Bea, which in effect gave the majority what the rules had denied it.

**P2** *(Exegetical (a) · Exegetical (b))*

**Must hit, strict (a):** three claims. (1) Liberty of conscience and worship is a personal right: "each man's own right". (2) It ought to be established in law everywhere: "proclaimed and asserted by law in every rightly constituted society". (3) Citizens have an unlimited liberty of public expression: "every kind of liberty, to be restrained by no authority, whether ecclesiastical or civil".
**Must hit, strict (b):** the premise is whether the condemnation targets the *civil right as such* (claims 1–2, which *Dignitatis Humanae* 2 appears to assert) or the *package*: a right conceived as unlimited ("restrained by no authority") and, in context, grounded in indifferentism. *Dignitatis Humanae* limits the right by public order (7) and grounds it in the duty to seek truth, not in indifferentism. Evidence for the Coetus: Syllabus 77–78 (the Source) condemn even a *limited* grant of public worship to immigrants, which is harder to read as aimed only at unlimited liberty.
**Wrong turns:** deciding whether *Dignitatis Humanae* is a development or a reversal (not asked; that belongs to `fundamental-theology` and `catholic-social-teaching`); misreading Syllabus 79's double negative as Pius affirming liberty.
**Model answer:** (a) Personal right ("each man's own right"); legal establishment everywhere ("proclaimed and asserted by law in every rightly constituted society"); unlimited public expression ("restrained by no authority"). (b) They disagree about which element is essential to what was condemned. For the Coetus, claims one and two are the target, and *Dignitatis Humanae* 2 asserts exactly those. For the drafters, what was condemned was a liberty "restrained by no authority" and resting on indifferentism, while the council's right is limited by public order and rests on the duty to seek truth. The Coetus can cite Syllabus 78, which condemns praise of even limited public worship for immigrants.

---

**P3** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict:** the framing is Wiltgen's "Rhine flows into the Tiber" story of a European alliance. Any three corrections: (1) 13 October 1962 produced only a *postponement*; the alliance's influence came through elections on 16 October, where on Wiltgen's own figures its slate won about half the elected seats, not "power". (2) Not every curial text was thrown out: the liturgy schema was accepted as the basis with only 46 against and became *Sacrosanctum Concilium*; even the revelation schema was not voted down but withdrawn by John XXIII. (3) Paul VI was no rubber stamp: in Black Week he imposed the *Nota praevia* and nineteen changes to the ecumenism decree, and let the postponement of the religious-liberty vote stand. (4) The 1054 excommunications were lifted on 7 December, the day before the close, by a joint declaration of pope and patriarch rather than a conciliar act, and the text says it does not end the differences.
**Wrong turns:** calling Wiltgen hostile to the alliance, or treating the framing as simply false (his tallies are usable); counting "first morning" and "seized power" as two errors when they are the same point.
**Model answer:** The script inherits Wiltgen's picture of a Rhine alliance capturing the council. First, 13 October brought only a postponement of elections; the alliance's slate then won about half the elected commission seats, a strong position but not seized power. Second, the Curia's liturgy schema was accepted as the basis with just 46 against and became *Sacrosanctum Concilium*, and the revelation schema was withdrawn by the pope, not voted out. Third, Paul VI imposed the *Nota praevia* and nineteen changes to the ecumenism decree during Black Week. And the lifting of the 1054 excommunications on 7 December was a joint act of pope and patriarch, which says itself that it does not end the schism.

## Flashback

**From Lesson [8.3](08-03-leo-xiii-and-the-modernist-crisis.md) (Leo XIII and the modernist crisis):** *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)* A popular claim: "Leo XIII was a liberal pope, and Pius X reversed him." (a) List two acts of Leo XIII that opened the Church to the modern world and two that set limits on that opening, with the year of each. One line each. (b) Evaluate the claim. Any verdict, 100 words or fewer.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):** openings, any two: *Au milieu des sollicitudes* and the ralliement (1892), telling French Catholics to accept the Republic; *Rerum Novarum* on industrial workers (1891); the Vatican archives opened to researchers (1881); *Providentissimus Deus* encouraging Catholic biblical study (1893); the negotiated end of the Kulturkampf (peace laws of 1886–87). Limits, any two: *Testem benevolentiae* against "Americanism" (1899); *Providentissimus Deus*'s teaching that inspiration covers the whole of Scripture and excludes error (1893); the Pontifical Biblical Commission (1902), meant to supervise as well as promote; his retention of the Syllabus's principles throughout. Credit for noting that *Providentissimus Deus* belongs on both lists.
**Must hit, any verdict (b):** (1) separate *posture* (engagement versus defence) from *doctrine* (what each pope taught); (2) use Leo's own brakes as evidence against "liberal"; (3) say what Pius X added that Leo had not done: the system-condemnation of *Lamentabili* and *Pascendi* (1907), the councils of vigilance, the oath of 1910, and his blessing of Benigni's network's aims; (4) give a verdict on "reversed".
**Wrong turns:** calling the ralliement an endorsement of liberal political theory (it accepted a regime, not a theory of the state); treating *Rerum Novarum* as evidence of theological liberalism; forgetting that the brakes of 1893–1902 were Leo's.
**Model answer (b), one of several:** Neither half holds as stated. Leo was not a liberal: he kept the Syllabus's principles, taught strict inerrancy and condemned Americanism. What he changed was posture, engaging governments and scholarship instead of only refusing. Pius X did not reverse Leo's doctrine; he reversed the posture, turning Leo's brakes into a system of condemnation and surveillance: *Pascendi*, vigilance councils, the oath. "Leo engaged and Pius defended, on the same doctrine" fits the record better. Evidence that Leo had planned a wider opening, cut short by his death, would push toward the popular claim.
