# Church History · Lesson 6.3: The English Reformations

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 6: The Reformations · Builds on: [6.2 Reformations beyond Wittenberg](06-02-reformations-beyond-wittenberg.md) · Unlocks: [6.4 Trent and Catholic reform](06-04-trent-and-catholic-reform.md), [6.5 The Spanish and Roman Inquisitions](06-05-the-spanish-and-roman-inquisitions.md)

## Why this matters

England changed its official religion four times in twenty-five years, and each change was made by a monarch and ratified by Parliament. Both sides killed for religion in those years, and each gave its killing a different name: Mary's government burned Protestants as heretics, and Elizabeth's hanged Catholic priests as traitors. Then, from the 1960s, historians fought over what ordinary English people made of it all. That fight is one of the best-documented disputes in the historiography of the Reformation, and it turns on kinds of evidence you can name. This lesson gives you the events, the killing on both sides, and the dispute.

## The idea

Keep two questions apart.

**What did the Crown do?** This is the easy one, because it is in the statute book. Henry VIII broke with Rome in 1534 without becoming Protestant. Edward VI's regime made the Church Protestant (1547–53). Mary I restored Catholicism and reconciled England to Rome (1553–58). Elizabeth I set up a Protestant settlement in 1559 that lasted. Each ruler used the same instrument, the [royal supremacy](../reference.md#royal-supremacy), which put the Crown where the pope had been.

**What did the people believe?** This is the hard one, because belief leaves only indirect traces. Did England turn Protestant quickly because it wanted to, or slowly because it was made to? Christopher Haigh's title, *English Reformations* (1993), makes the point in its plural. What happened in England was not one reformation but a series of political acts, started, reversed and restarted, that a population had to live through.

A third thread runs through both: **loyalty**. Once the monarch heads the Church, religious dissent looks like disloyalty to the Crown, and a papal act against the monarch looks like a call to rebellion. That is how an argument over the Mass ended on the gallows at Tyburn.

## Source

The Act of Supremacy (26 Henry VIII c. 1, November 1534), from Gee and Hardy, *Documents Illustrative of English Church History* (1896), public domain:

> Albeit the king's majesty justly and rightfully is and ought to be the supreme head of the Church of England, and so is recognized by the clergy of this realm in their Convocations, yet nevertheless for corroboration and confirmation thereof ... be it enacted by authority of this present Parliament, that the king our sovereign lord, his heirs and successors, kings of this realm, shall be taken, accepted, and reputed the only supreme head in earth of the Church of England, called Anglicana Ecclesia ...

Read the first word. *Albeit* ("although") means the act does not claim to **make** the king head of the Church. It says he already is, and it only "confirms" that. The claim is that the supremacy was an ancient right of the Crown that Rome had usurped, not a new invention. The act also gives the king power to "visit" and to correct "errors, heresies, abuses." That is jurisdiction: the power to inspect, discipline and reform. It is not a power to ordain or to say Mass. Compare Elizabeth's act of 1559. Her oath calls her the "only supreme governor" of the realm in spiritual as well as temporal things. "Head" was dropped, partly because many balked at a woman as head of the Church.

## The record

1. **The "great matter" (1527–34).** Henry sought an annulment of his marriage to Catherine of Aragon, arguing from Leviticus that marrying his brother's widow had been invalid. Clement VII, under pressure from Catherine's nephew Charles V after the sack of Rome (1527), would not grant it. The Act in Restraint of Appeals (1533) barred appeals to Rome. Cranmer then pronounced the marriage void in May 1533. Clement ruled the marriage valid in March 1534. The Act of Supremacy followed that November.
2. **Fisher and More (1535).** Treason now covered denying the king's titles. John Fisher, bishop of Rochester, was beheaded on 22 June 1535, and Thomas More on 6 July. Both were canonized in 1935. During More's own chancellorship, six people were burned for heresy, with his approval.
3. **The monasteries (1536–40).** Cromwell's visitors collected reports of monastic vice in 1535–36; they knew such findings were wanted. An act of 1536 dissolved houses with incomes under 200 pounds a year. The larger houses followed by "surrender" and a second act (1539). The abbots of Glastonbury, Reading and Colchester were executed in 1539. Waltham, the last abbey, fell on 23 March 1540. Over 800 houses closed in all ([dissolution of the monasteries](../reference.md#dissolution-of-the-monasteries)).
4. **The Pilgrimage of Grace (October 1536).** Risings in Lincolnshire and then Yorkshire under Robert Aske gathered perhaps 30,000 men. Their demands included saving the abbeys, and they put monks back into some that had been suppressed. Henry promised pardon, then used a fresh rising in 1537 as the reason to execute about 216 people. On 30 July 1540 three Protestants were burned and three Catholics hanged on the same day. Henry's Church was neither Roman nor Protestant.
5. **Edward VI (1547–53).** The chantries were dissolved, images removed, and the Prayer Books of 1549 and 1552 imposed. The Prayer Book Rebellion in Devon and Cornwall (1549) was crushed at the cost of thousands of lives.
6. **Mary I (1553–58).** Cardinal Pole absolved the realm and reconciled it to Rome in November 1554. From February 1555 to November 1558 about 280–290 Protestants were burned for heresy (the usual counts run 283–287), Cranmer among them (March 1556). John Foxe's *Acts and Monuments* (1563) made them the founding martyrs of Protestant England.
7. **Elizabeth I (1558–1603).** The [Elizabethan settlement](../reference.md#elizabethan-settlement) of 1559 passed a new Act of Supremacy and an Act of Uniformity restoring a Protestant prayer book. Of Mary's bishops, only Kitchin of Llandaff took the oath. The Northern Rising of November 1569 failed. The queen demanded at least 700 executions, and several hundred were carried out.
8. **The bull and its aftermath (1570–1603).** Pius V's *[Regnans in Excelsis](../reference.md#regnans-in-excelsis)* (25 February 1570) excommunicated Elizabeth, declared her deposed and absolved her subjects of their allegiance. It was issued after the rising had already failed. John Felton posted it in London in May 1570 and was executed that August. Parliament answered in 1571, and again in 1581 (20 pounds a month for [recusancy](../reference.md#recusancy)) and 1585 (any priest ordained by Rome's authority since 1559 who stayed in England more than forty days was a traitor). Seminary priests trained at Douai (founded 1568) arrived in the 1570s, and Jesuits from 1580. Cuthbert Mayne was executed in 1577 and Edmund Campion in 1581. On the usual count, about 189 Catholics were executed under Elizabeth: some 126 priests and 63 lay people, most after 1581. The Forty Martyrs of England and Wales were canonized in 1970.

## Map of positions

The dispute over belief, set out as [fast versus slow Reformation](../reference.md#fast-and-slow-reformation):

| Position | Speed and direction | Where Protestantism came from | Key evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A. G. Dickens**, *The English Reformation* (1964) | Fast, and welcomed from below | Lollardy, anticlericalism, Lutheran books; well rooted by 1553 in the south-east | Heresy trials; Protestant wording in the preambles of wills |
| **J. J. Scarisbrick**, *The Reformation and the English People* (1984) | Slow; most people did not want it | The Crown | Lay giving to guilds, chantries and church fabric up to the break |
| **Christopher Haigh**, *English Reformations* (1993) | Slow, and imposed from above | Politics: a series of reversible acts of state | Visitation records, and regional studies such as his work on Lancashire |
| **Eamon Duffy**, *The Stripping of the Altars* (1992); *The Voices of Morebath* (2001) | Slow, and a violent break with a vigorous religion | The Crown, obeyed with grief | Churchwardens' accounts, primers, rood screens, bequests |
| **Ethan Shagan**, *Popular Politics and the English Reformation* (2003) | Neither simply welcomed nor simply imposed | Negotiated: people collaborated for their own ends | Local disputes and purchases of church goods and lands |

**Morebath** shows why the revisionists' evidence persuades. Sir Christopher Trychay, vicar of this small Devon parish from 1520 to 1574, kept its accounts in his own chatty voice. Duffy reads them as a parish that spent money on its saint, Sidwell, and on requiem vestments. It hid rather than destroyed its goods under Edward, sent five men to the rebel camp outside Exeter in 1549, and welcomed Mary's restoration. Then it conformed under Elizabeth. The critics reply that one articulate vicar is a single parish, and that a church that conformed so quickly shows how shallow its attachment was. Most historians now accept that the Reformation came slowly in most regions. The dispute that remains is about why people conformed, and what conformity meant.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean: the deaths of 30 July 1540).** Three Protestant preachers were burned at Smithfield under the Six Articles, and three priests were hanged for denying the supremacy. Apply the lesson's two questions. The Crown's religion was defined by the supremacy, not by a confession, so dissent in *either* direction was punishable. The Protestants were killed as heretics under a statute that upheld Catholic doctrine, and the Catholics as traitors under a statute about the king's title. The day is the clearest single proof that "Henry made England Protestant" is wrong. What Henry imposed was royal control of the Church.

**Example 2 (hard: was Campion executed for religion or for treason?).** The government's case is at its strongest after 1570. The bull had told English subjects not to obey their queen. William Allen and Robert Persons, leaders of the English Catholic exiles, later backed invasion plans. From 1585 England was at war with Spain. So, the argument ran, a priest acting on Rome's orders was a possible agent of an enemy power. A 1580 explanation attributed to Gregory XIII let Catholics obey the queen only as things stood, which did not remove the deposing claim. The Catholic case is at its strongest on the facts of the trials. Campion was convicted on a charge of plotting abroad that historians generally regard as fabricated. Priests were questioned with the "bloody question" (whom they would support if the pope invaded), which tried a hypothetical and not an act. And after 1585, simply being a priest ordained since 1559 and staying in England was treason. Where the evidence strains: the loyalty problem was real, and it was created by the bull. But the statutes turned the priest's office into proof of treason. Both descriptions fit different parts of the record. Whether the pope may depose rulers was a claim of the medieval canonists and was never defined by a council; its theological standing belongs to [`ecclesiology-and-mariology`](../../ecclesiology-and-mariology/syllabus.md).

## Watch out

- **"Henry VIII made England Protestant."** Henry repudiated the pope, not the Mass. He kept Catholic doctrine in the Six Articles (1539) and burned people who denied it. Protestant doctrine came under Edward.
- **The two persecutions are not the same act under different names, and not wholly different either.** Mary's victims died under heresy law, and most of Elizabeth's under treason statutes that made the priesthood itself treasonable. Count both, and name each charge accurately.
- **Don't read the bull backwards into 1569.** *Regnans in Excelsis* came after the Northern Rising had failed. It did not start the rising; it followed it and made the loyalty question far worse afterwards.
- **Scope.** The doctrinal disputes (the Mass, justification, the royal supremacy as theology) are argued in [`apologetics-catholic-claims`](../../apologetics-catholic-claims/syllabus.md). Liturgy as doctrine belongs to [`sacraments-and-liturgy`](../../sacraments-and-liturgy/syllabus.md).

## One-liner

> England's Reformations were acts of state, reversed and reimposed; the historians' fight is over how fast the people followed, and the martyrs of both sides died on the fault line between conscience and allegiance.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢) *(Exegetical.)*** From *Regnans in Excelsis* (25 February 1570). Translated for this lesson from the Latin text of 1570:

> From the fullness of apostolic power we declare the aforesaid Elizabeth a heretic and a favourer of heretics, and those who adhere to her in the aforesaid matters to have incurred the sentence of anathema and to be cut off from the unity of the body of Christ. Further, we declare her deprived of her pretended right to the aforesaid kingdom, and of all dominion, dignity and privilege whatsoever; and likewise the nobles, subjects and peoples of the said kingdom, and all others who have sworn to her in any way, absolved forever from that oath and from every duty of lordship, fealty and obedience. We command and forbid each and all of the nobles, subjects, peoples and others aforesaid to dare to obey her, or her admonitions, mandates and laws. Those who do otherwise we bind with the like sentence of anathema.

(a) The passage contains a spiritual sentence and a political one. Quote the words that carry each. (b) Name the choice the final two sentences put to an English Catholic, and the words Elizabeth's government could cite to call a Catholic priest a traitor rather than a heretic. Two sentences per part.

**P2 (🟡) *(Formal (a) · Evaluative (b).)*** Take as given the usual counts: about **284** Protestants burned under Mary and about **189** Catholics executed under Elizabeth. Mary reigned 5.3 years (July 1553 to November 1558), but the burnings ran for only 3.8 of them (February 1555 to November 1558). Elizabeth reigned 44.3 years. The first seminary priest was executed in November 1577, which leaves about 25.3 years to the end of her reign.
(a) Compute executions per year for Mary over her reign and over the burning years. Compute Elizabeth's rate over her reign and from 1577. Give the ratio of Mary's rate to Elizabeth's for the pairing that gives the largest ratio and for the pairing that gives the smallest. Round to one decimal place.
(b) A popular book says: "The numbers prove Mary was the persecutor and Elizabeth the tolerant queen." In 150 words or fewer, say what the comparison does show and what it does not, reaching any verdict you like.

**P3 (🔴, optional) *(Exegetical.)*** An invented tour-guide script, read at the ruins of a Yorkshire abbey:

> "By the 1530s these monks were a scandal, and the king's inspectors wrote it all down: idleness, vice, fake relics. So Henry closed them, as any Protestant reformer would. And the abbots went quietly. They signed the surrender, took their pensions, and nobody in the North lifted a finger."

Name three historical claims the script depends on. For each, say what in the record tells against it, or complicates it. 150 words or fewer.

<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical.)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** the spiritual sentence is the declaration that she is "a heretic and a favourer of heretics" who, with her adherents, has incurred "the sentence of anathema" and is "cut off from the unity of the body of Christ". The political sentence is that she is "deprived of her pretended right to the aforesaid kingdom" and that her subjects are "absolved forever from that oath and from every duty of lordship, fealty and obedience". The political sentence is the one that goes beyond excommunication.
**Must hit, strict (b):** the final two sentences forbid obedience to "her ... mandates and laws" on pain of anathema. So a Catholic must either obey the queen and fall under the Church's sentence, or obey the pope and be disloyal to the Crown. The government could point to the absolution from "fealty and obedience" and the command not to "dare to obey her". On those words, a priest who brought or stood by the bull's authority was, in law, withdrawing subjects from their allegiance.
**Wrong turns:** treating the excommunication alone as the source of the treason charge (popes had excommunicated rulers without deposing them); saying the bull launched the Northern Rising (it came after); saying the bull called for her assassination (it does not).
**Model answer:** (a) The spiritual sentence is "a heretic and a favourer of heretics ... incurred the sentence of anathema ... cut off from the unity of the body of Christ." The political one is "deprived of her pretended right to the aforesaid kingdom" and the subjects "absolved forever ... from every duty of lordship, fealty and obedience." (b) Obey the queen and incur anathema, or obey the pope and deny the Crown obedience. The government cited "absolved ... from every duty of ... fealty and obedience" and "forbid ... to dare to obey her" to argue that a priest acting on Rome's authority was undoing allegiance, which is treason.

**P2** *(Formal (a) · Evaluative (b).)*

(a) Computed in Python:
- Mary over her reign: $284 / 5.3 = 53.6$ a year. Over the burning years: $284 / 3.8 = 74.7$ a year.
- Elizabeth over her reign: $189 / 44.3 = 4.27$ a year. From 1577: $189 / 25.3 = 7.47$ a year.
- Largest ratio (Mary's burning years against Elizabeth's whole reign): $74.7 / 4.27 = $ **17.5**.
- Smallest ratio (Mary's whole reign against Elizabeth from 1577): $53.6 / 7.47 = $ **7.2**.

Every pairing leaves Mary's rate several times Elizabeth's, and the answer moves by more than a factor of two depending on which years you choose to count.

**Must hit, any verdict (b):** (i) state what is robust: on any pairing, Mary's government killed for religion at a far higher annual rate. (ii) Name at least two things the comparison omits or distorts. These include: the charge (heresy against treason statutes that made priesthood itself treason); the choice of denominator (shown in (a)); the several hundred executed after the Northern Rising and the Catholics who died in prison, both outside the 189; fines, imprisonment and exclusion, which a death count does not measure; the war with Spain after 1585. (iii) Say what "tolerant" would have to mean for the claim to hold, and whether the evidence supports that.
**Wrong turns:** treating the 189 as the whole Elizabethan cost; treating "executed for treason" as settling that no one died for religion (or the reverse); comparing raw totals without any time base.
**Model answer, one of several:** The comparison shows something real: however you pick the years, Mary's regime killed for religion seven to seventeen times faster. It does not show that Elizabeth was tolerant. Her statutes made a priest's mere presence in England treason, and she enforced recusancy by fines and prison. The 189 also leaves out several hundred rebels hanged in 1569–70 and those who died in custody. The names matter, since the government insisted it punished treason, and the "bloody question" shows how far that could stretch. My verdict: "persecutor" fits both reigns, and the numbers measure intensity rather than principle.

**P3** *(Exegetical.)*

**Must hit, strict:** any three of the following, each with its counter-evidence:
(1) *The visitors' reports prove the monks' corruption.* The visitors knew such findings were wanted and put the worst construction on what they heard. The 1536 act also used an income threshold (under 200 pounds), not a moral test.
(2) *Henry dissolved the houses as a Protestant reformer.* Henry kept Catholic doctrine (Six Articles, 1539) and burned Protestants. The dissolution followed from the royal supremacy and the Crown's need for money. It was not a Protestant programme.
(3) *The abbots went quietly.* The abbots of Glastonbury, Reading and Colchester were executed in 1539, and abbots were among those executed after the Pilgrimage.
(4) *The North did nothing.* The Pilgrimage of Grace (October 1536) gathered perhaps 30,000 men, demanded that the abbeys be saved, and put monks back into some suppressed houses. About 216 people were executed afterwards.
**Wrong turns:** saying the visitors fabricated everything (the evidence is exaggeration and selection, not wholesale invention); turning the answer into the fast-versus-slow debate about belief, which the script does not raise.
**Model answer:** The script depends on three claims. First, that the visitors' reports prove corruption. But Cromwell's men knew such findings were wanted and put the worst construction on what they heard, and the 1536 act dissolved houses by income, not by conduct. Second, that Henry acted as a Protestant. He kept Catholic doctrine in the Six Articles and burned Protestants. The dissolution followed from the supremacy and his need for money. Third, that the North accepted it quietly. The Pilgrimage of Grace, perhaps 30,000 strong, demanded that the abbeys be saved and restored some monks, and about 216 people were executed after it. Three abbots were hanged in 1539.

## Flashback

**From Lesson [6.2](06-02-reformations-beyond-wittenberg.md) (Reformations beyond Wittenberg):** *(Exegetical.)* Apply the Peace of Augsburg (1555) to three cases in the 1560s. (i) A Catholic prince-bishop turns Lutheran and wants to keep his see, its lands, and his subjects' obedience. (ii) The Elector Palatine adopts the Reformed faith and issues the Heidelberg Catechism (1563). (iii) A Lutheran burgher lives in an imperial city where Catholics and Lutherans already worship side by side, and the Catholic council wants to end Lutheran worship. For each, say what the peace gives or denies and which provision decides it. Then say which case shows most plainly that *cuius regio, eius religio* overstates the treaty. Two sentences per case, one for the last part.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict:** (i) The **ecclesiastical reservation** (article 18): a bishop who converts must give up his see, so the church lands stay Catholic and do not follow him. He may change his own religion but not take the territory with him. (ii) **Article 17** excludes all who do not belong to the two named religions. The Reformed claimed the protection of the Augsburg Confession, but Catholics and Lutherans argued for ninety years that it did not cover them. So the Palatinate's standing was contested, and that gap was closed only when Westphalia admitted the Reformed in 1648. (iii) The peace required imperial cities that were already mixed to **keep both confessions**, so the council may not suppress Lutheran worship. **Last part:** (iii), or (i), since in each the ruler does *not* decide the religion. Either answer earns credit with the reason stated.
**Wrong turns:** giving the burgher only article 24's right to emigrate, which is the answer for a subject in a single-confession territory, not a mixed city; saying the peace protected Calvinists outright; treating *cuius regio* as a phrase in the treaty.
**Model answer:** (i) The ecclesiastical reservation of article 18 lets the bishop change his own faith but requires him to give up his see, so its lands stay Catholic. (ii) Article 17 excludes anyone outside the two named religions, and Catholics and Lutherans read that as shutting out the Reformed. The Palatinate's Calvinism therefore stood outside the peace's clear protection until Westphalia admitted the Reformed in 1648. (iii) Imperial cities that were already mixed had to keep both confessions, so the council cannot abolish Lutheran worship. The mixed city shows the formula's overstatement most plainly, since there the ruling council may not impose its own religion at all.
