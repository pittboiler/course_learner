# Church History · Lesson 4.1: The reform papacy and the investiture contest

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 4: Reform, schism, and crusade · Builds on: [3.3 Popes, Franks, and emperors](03-03-popes-franks-and-emperors.md) · Unlocks: [4.2 East and West: the long estrangement](04-02-east-and-west-the-long-estrangement.md), [5.1 Papal monarchy: Innocent III to Boniface VIII](05-01-papal-monarchy-innocent-iii-to-boniface-viii.md)

## Why this matters

In 1046 a German king crossed the Alps, found three men claiming to be pope, and removed all of them. Thirty years later a pope declared that same king's son deposed and released his subjects from their oaths. Between those two events the relation between the Latin Church and the lay powers was turned upside down, and it never went back. The fight that followed, the "investiture contest," ended in a compromise in 1122. It left behind a papacy that governed the Western Church through law, legates and councils. The Innocent III and Boniface VIII of [5.1](05-01-papal-monarchy-innocent-iii-to-boniface-viii.md) are unthinkable without it.

## The idea

Around 1000 a bishop was two things at once. He was a pastor, consecrated to teach and give the sacraments. He was also a great lord: in the German kingdom, bishops held counties, tolls and markets, and owed the king troops and counsel. Kings chose them. The king handed the new bishop a **ring and a staff**, the symbols of his spiritual office. This was [lay investiture](../reference.md#lay-investiture). To a king it was plain sense: his greatest landholders could not be strangers. Rulers anointed at their coronation did not think of themselves as mere laymen.

The reformers saw two diseases. **[Simony](../reference.md#simony)**, named for Simon Magus in Acts 8, was buying or selling a spiritual office. **[Nicolaitism](../reference.md#nicolaitism)** was clerical marriage and concubinage. At first the reformers wanted only a clean clergy. They came to think the root was the system itself: as long as laymen handed out churches, churches would be bought and sold. Their slogan became *libertas ecclesiae*, the freedom of the Church.

The paradox of the story is that the reform began with an emperor. Henry III was the reformers' patron. His son Henry IV became their enemy.

## Source

From the *[Dictatus Papae](../reference.md#dictatus-papae)*, entered in Gregory VII's register in March 1075. The numbers are the propositions' own. Translation: E. F. Henderson, *Select Historical Documents of the Middle Ages* (1910 printing), public domain.

> 3. That he alone can depose or reinstate bishops.
> 12. That it may be permitted to him to depose emperors.
> 16. That no synod shall be called a general one without his order.
> 19. That he himself may be judged by no one.
> 22. That the Roman church has never erred; nor will it err to all eternity, the Scripture bearing witness.
> 27. That he may absolve subjects from their fealty to wicked men.

Read it as a historian first: 27 bare headings, no argument, no addressee. It sits in the register between letters of 3 and 4 March 1075, and it was never sent to anyone or published. Some think Gregory dictated it as an agenda. Others think it was a list of headings for a canon-law collection, since Cardinal Deusdedit's collection (1086/7) matches it closely. It shows what Gregory's curia thought the pope could do, not what anyone outside accepted. Proposition 22 speaks of the Roman *church* never erring. It is not a definition of papal infallibility.

## The record

1. **Cluny, 910.** Duke William of Aquitaine founded a monastery and gave it to the apostles Peter and Paul, that is, to Rome, forbidding any lord or bishop to interfere. Popes later exempted it from the local bishop (998, extended 1024). *In words:* a model of a church answerable only to Rome existed a century and a half before Gregory.
2. **Sutri, December 1046.** Henry III had three rival claimants set aside (Sylvester III, Gregory VI, Benedict IX) and installed his own bishop of Bamberg as Clement II, who crowned him emperor on Christmas Day. A young Roman cleric, Hildebrand, followed the deposed Gregory VI into exile.
3. **Leo IX, 1049–54.** Another of Henry's German appointees. He travelled and held councils across Italy, France and Germany. At Reims in October 1049 he made bishops declare in public whether they had paid for their sees. *In words:* the papacy began to reach bishops directly, in person.
4. **The [papal election decree of 1059](../reference.md#papal-election-decree-of-1059).** At the Lateran in April 1059, Nicholas II gave the choice of pope to the cardinal bishops. The rest of the clergy and the people were to assent, and the emperor kept an undefined right of his own. *In words:* the next pope would not be made at Sutri.
5. **Gregory VII, 1073.** Hildebrand was acclaimed pope by the Roman crowd on 22 April 1073, which was hard to square with the 1059 procedure. His Lenten synod of February 1075 may have forbidden lay investiture. A Milanese chronicler, Arnulf, says it did. The synod's own record in the register is silent, and some historians hold that the ban concerned only the disputed see of Milan. The first surviving general decree is from November 1078.
6. **The break, 1075–76.** Henry named his own archbishop of Milan against the reformers' candidate, and chose bishops for Fermo and Spoleto. Gregory's letter of December 1075 rebuked him for this and for keeping excommunicated councillors. At Worms on 24 January 1076 Henry and his bishops renounced Gregory. In February Gregory's synod excommunicated Henry, withdrew from him the rule of Germany and Italy, and absolved his subjects from their oaths.
7. **[Canossa](../reference.md#canossa), January 1077.** In October 1076 the German princes gave Henry a year to be absolved. He crossed the Alps in winter and waited outside Matilda of Tuscany's castle from 25 January. He was absolved on the 28th. In March the rebel princes elected Rudolf of Rheinfelden king anyway.
8. **War and exile, 1080–85.** Gregory excommunicated Henry again in March 1080. In June a synod at Brixen declared Gregory's election invalid and chose Archbishop Wibert of Ravenna as "Clement III." Henry took Rome in March 1084, and Clement crowned him emperor. Gregory's Norman allies drove Henry out and sacked the city. Gregory left with them and died at Salerno on 25 May 1085. His last words are reported, by his supporters, as a reworking of Psalm 45: "I have loved justice and hated iniquity; therefore I die in exile." They reach us through partisan accounts such as Paul of Bernried's *Life* (c. 1128). The attribution is traditional, and the exact wording cannot be checked.
9. **Settlement, 1111–1122.** In 1111 Paschal II offered that the German bishops give up their royal lands if the king gave up investiture. The plan collapsed. Henry V seized the pope and forced a grant of investiture, which a Roman synod repudiated the next year. England had reached a working bargain in 1107. The **[Concordat of Worms](../reference.md#concordat-of-worms)** (23 September 1122) settled the German question, and the First Lateran Council confirmed it in 1123.

### Primacy: what this shows, what it doesn't

**Shows:** between 1049 and 1122 the bishop of Rome claimed supreme jurisdiction in the Latin Church and largely exercised it. His legates presided over councils and deposed bishops. His decree governed papal elections. He passed sentence on a king, and a large party of the king's own subjects acted on it. **Doesn't show:** that the claims were accepted, even in the West, as settled. Henry's bishops rejected Gregory at Worms and Brixen, an antipope held Rome, and the deposition worked only where German princes wanted it to. Nor does it speak for the East, which had no part in the contest. The readings divide. Catholic historians stress that the reformers cited older texts, among them Gelasius and earlier canon collections (proposition 23 names Pope Symmachus and Ennodius of Pavia). Some of those texts, however, were the ninth-century Pseudo-Isidorian forgeries. Aristeides Papadakis, with John Meyendorff (*The Christian East and the Rise of the Papacy*, 1994), writing from the Orthodox side, treats the reform papacy's ecclesiology as the real novelty behind the estrangement of [4.2](04-02-east-and-west-the-long-estrangement.md). Reformation polemic made "Hildebrand" the very type of papal usurpation. What the record proves theologically is argued in [`ecclesiology-and-mariology`](../../ecclesiology-and-mariology/syllabus.md) 4.2.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean: reading the Concordat).** Two short privileges, papal and imperial, each give something up. Henry V, in Henderson's translation, renounces "all investiture through ring and staff" and grants "canonical election and free consecration." Calixtus II grants that German elections take place in the emperor's presence, and that in a disputed election the emperor, with the advice of the metropolitan and the other bishops of the province, may back the side with the better right. The bishop-elect receives his *regalia*, the lands and rights held from the crown, *per sceptrum*, by the sceptre. (Henderson renders *sceptrum* as "lance"; the Latin says sceptre.) In Germany this happens before consecration. In Italy and Burgundy it comes within six months after. So the symbols were split: ring and staff, which signify the spiritual office, went to the Church, and the sceptre, which signifies temporal lordship, stayed with the king. The timing kept a real German veto, since a man the emperor refused the regalia would find it hard to be consecrated. Both sides could claim a win; that is why it held.

**Example 2 (hard: what happened at Canossa?).** The popular picture is of a king humbled in the snow, and Bismarck gave it its modern form in the Reichstag on 14 May 1872, when he promised that Germany would not "go to Canossa" in the Kulturkampf. The main source is Gregory's own letter of 28 January 1077 to the German princes, the king's enemies. He writes that Henry, "having laid aside all the belongings of royalty, wretchedly, with bare feet and clad in wool, he continued for three days to stand before the gate of the castle." So the penance is not legend; Gregory reports it himself.

The strain is over what it meant. The letter is addressed to princes who had wanted a verdict against Henry, and it defends Gregory for giving absolution instead. It stresses that he is bound to the king by nothing more than hopes held out, and that the main question is still far from settled. Absolution restored Henry to communion and removed the princes' deadline. It did not in terms restore his kingdom. On that reading Henry gained the most: the tribunal the princes had planned in Germany never sat. Johannes Fried (*Canossa: Entlarvung einer Legende*, 2012) goes further. He argues that king and pope concluded a negotiated peace, and that the humiliation is a later construction. Critics including Gerd Althoff and Stefan Weinfurter rejected his reading of the sources. The penance and absolution are secure; whether they were a defeat depends on what Henry went there to get.

## Watch out

- **"Investiture" was the flashpoint, not the whole quarrel.** The 1075–76 break came over Milan, excommunicated councillors and Gregory's claim to judge a king. The first certain general ban on lay investiture is from 1078. Two readings frame the whole: Harold Berman (*Law and Revolution*, 1983) calls it a "[papal revolution](../reference.md#papal-revolution)" that created the first modern Western legal system, the Church's canon law, and by contrast the secular state. Gerd Tellenbach (*Libertas*, 1936) reads it as a religious struggle for "right order in the world," with investiture a symptom.
- **The *Dictatus Papae* is not a decree.** It is an unpublished list. Quote it as evidence of what the curia thought, not as a law anyone obeyed.
- **Worms did not end royal influence over bishops.** It changed the symbols and the order of steps. Kings went on shaping appointments for centuries.

## One-liner

> The reformers set out to free the Church from lay hands and ended by claiming to judge kings; Worms split the symbols and left the power to be fought over.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢) *(Exegetical.)*** Use the six propositions quoted in the Source. (a) Sort them into claims about the pope's power *within the Church* and claims about his power *over lay rulers*. (b) Name the proposition(s) Gregory acted on in his sentence of February 1076, and say why a strict reader might hesitate to cite proposition 12 for that sentence. Two sentences or fewer per part.

**P2 (🟡) *(Exegetical.)*** An invented museum placard beside a copy of the Concordat of Worms:

> "Here the emperor surrendered. After fifty years of struggle, Henry V gave up the investiture of bishops, and from 1122 the Church alone chose its leaders, free at last of any king's say."

Name two things in the Concordat's actual terms that the placard leaves out, and say which single word in it is most wrong and why. 150 words or fewer.

**P3 (🔴) *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)*** A popular claim: "The investiture contest began when Gregory VII banned lay investiture in 1075 and Henry IV refused to obey." (a) State what the evidence for a 1075 ban is, and what the first securely documented general ban is. (b) Evaluate the claim as an account of how the conflict *began*, any verdict. 150 words or fewer in total.

<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical.)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** within the Church: 3 (deposing bishops), 16 (general synods), 19 (judged by no one), 22 (the Roman church has never erred). Over lay rulers: 12 (deposing emperors) and 27 (absolving subjects from fealty). An answer that puts 19 in both groups, since it also puts the pope beyond any king's judgment, is acceptable if it says so.
**Must hit, strict (b):** 27 directly: the February 1076 sentence absolves all Christians from their oaths to Henry. Deposition is covered in substance by 12. The hesitation: in 1076 Henry was king, not emperor. He was crowned emperor only in 1084, and by the antipope Clement III. So 12, which names emperors, covers him only by extension.
**Wrong turns:** putting 22 under papal infallibility (it speaks of the Roman church, and the Dictatus defines nothing); saying the Dictatus was cited in the 1076 sentence (it was an unpublished register entry; the sentence appeals to Peter's power of binding and loosing).
**Model answer:** (a) Within the Church: 3, 16, 19, 22. Over lay rulers: 12 and 27. (b) The sentence carries out 27, absolving Henry's subjects, and in effect 12, taking his rule from him. But Henry was only king in 1076, not yet emperor, so 12 fits him only by extension.

**P2** *(Exegetical.)*

**Must hit, strict:** at least two of: (i) German elections were held *in the emperor's presence*; (ii) in a disputed election the emperor, with the metropolitan and the provincial bishops, could back one side; (iii) the elect received the regalia from the emperor by the sceptre, *before* consecration in Germany (within six months after in Italy and Burgundy), which gave him a practical veto. What Henry gave up was investiture *with ring and staff*, the spiritual symbols, not all say in appointments. Most-wrong word: **"alone"** (or "surrendered," if argued as a compromise mislabelled as surrender); either is accepted with the reason that the settlement was mutual and left the king a role.
**Wrong turns:** saying the pope conceded investiture with ring and staff (that was the extorted grant of 1111, repudiated in 1112); treating "fifty years" as the error (1075/76 to 1122 is roughly right).
**Model answer:** The placard omits that elections in Germany took place in the emperor's presence, with his right to back one side in a disputed election, and that every bishop-elect still received his lands and rights from the emperor by the sceptre, in Germany before he could be consecrated. Henry V renounced investiture with ring and staff, the symbols of the spiritual office, not his say in who became bishop. The most wrong word is "alone": the Concordat divided the process between Church and crown, and the German timing left the emperor an effective veto.

---

**P3** *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** the 1075 ban rests on the Milanese chronicler Arnulf's report of the Lenten synod (February 1075). The synod's record in Gregory's register does not mention it, and some historians read it as a measure about Milan only. The first securely documented general decree is November 1078 (renewed March 1080).
**Must hit, any verdict (b):** (i) weigh the chronology of the break: Henry's appointments to Milan, Fermo and Spoleto, and his excommunicated councillors (the December 1075 letter), then Worms (January 1076) and the deposition (February 1076); (ii) say what the break was over, investiture specifically or the wider question of who judges whom; (iii) concede what is true in the claim, since royal appointment of bishops was at the centre of Milan.
**Wrong turns:** citing the *Dictatus Papae* as the 1075 ban (it says nothing of investiture); dating Canossa as the start.
**Model answer (one of several):** (a) Only Arnulf of Milan reports a 1075 ban. The register's record of the synod is silent, and it may have concerned Milan alone. The first secure general decree is November 1078. (b) Partly true. The break did begin over royal appointments, Milan above all, so investiture in the broad sense was at the heart of it. But what Henry "refused" in 1075 was Gregory's judgment on particular appointments and on his excommunicated advisers, not a general law we can show he received. The escalation at Worms and in February 1076 was about whether pope or king could depose the other, and the general ban came after the break, not before it.

## Flashback

**From Lesson [3.3](03-03-popes-franks-and-emperors.md) (Popes, Franks, and emperors):** *(Exegetical.)* Two readings of Nicholas I (858–867). Klaus Schatz, a Catholic historian, concedes that forgeries such as the Pseudo-Isidorean Decretals were used in the service of papal claims, but holds that the claims were older than the forgeries. John Meyendorff, an Orthodox historian, reads Nicholas's claims as a break with the first millennium's conciliar ordering of the sees. (a) Name two things Nicholas did that both readings accept he did. (b) Name the single question on which they divide, and one kind of evidence that would push it each way. (c) Constantinople declared Nicholas deposed in 867. Why does that fact not settle the dispute? Two sentences per part.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):** any two of: he overruled Lothar II's attempt to put away his wife; he deposed the archbishops of Cologne and Trier who had backed Lothar; he reversed Hincmar of Reims on appeal; he refused to recognize Photius as patriarch of Constantinople.
**Must hit, strict (b):** the crux is whether the jurisdiction Nicholas exercised, judging archbishops on appeal and a patriarch's election, **continued earlier Roman claims and practice or was new**. Toward continuity: earlier genuine texts making the same claim, such as Leo I's sermons on Peter's care living on in his see, or Gelasius's letter of 494 placing Rome's see "above all others"; or proof that Nicholas acted without the forgeries. Toward novelty: proof that Nicholas knew and used Pseudo-Isidore (historians dispute it), or first-millennium cases where the East settled its sees by councils without accepting a Roman verdict.
**Must hit, strict (c):** both sides accept that it happened. It shows the claim was **not accepted** in Constantinople, which is a question about reception, while the dispute is about whether the claim itself was old or new.
**Wrong turns:** assuming that proof of forgery use settles the question (Schatz already concedes the forgeries served the claims; his point is that the claims predate them); turning the crux into a theological question about what primacy proves, which belongs to `ecclesiology-and-mariology`; offering the 867 deposition as evidence for novelty (see c).
**Model answer:** (a) He deposed the archbishops of Cologne and Trier for backing Lothar II's divorce, and he refused to recognize Photius as patriarch. (b) They divide on whether judging archbishops and a patriarch was an old Roman prerogative or a ninth-century novelty. Genuine earlier claims like Leo's and Gelasius's push toward continuity, while showing that Nicholas's case rested on Pseudo-Isidore, or that the East had never accepted such judgments, pushes toward novelty. (c) Both historians accept the deposition. It shows that Constantinople rejected the claim, not whether the claim was new.

</details>
