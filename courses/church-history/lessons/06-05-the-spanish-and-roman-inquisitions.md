# Church History · Lesson 6.5: The Spanish and Roman Inquisitions

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 6: The Reformations · Builds on: [5.3 Heresy, inquisition, and the Jews](05-03-heresy-inquisition-and-the-jews.md), [6.4 Trent and Catholic reform](06-04-trent-and-catholic-reform.md) · Unlocks: [7.1 Conquest and conscience in the Americas](07-01-conquest-and-conscience-in-the-americas.md), [7.3 The Galileo affair](07-03-the-galileo-affair.md)

## Why this matters

"The Inquisition killed millions" is one of the most repeated sentences about the Catholic past. The opposite slogan, that it was a fair court that hardly killed anyone, is almost as common. Both can now be tested, because for a century and a half the Spanish tribunals sent regular case reports to Madrid, and historians have counted them. The count settles some questions and leaves others open, and a historian's job is to know which are which. This lesson tells what the early modern inquisitions were, sets out the numbers with their error bars, and marks where the real disagreement now lies.

## The idea

There was never one Inquisition. The papal inquisitors of [5.3](05-03-heresy-inquisition-and-the-jews.md) were commissioned friars working under local bishops and lords. The early modern tribunals were standing institutions with archives, salaried staff and networks of lay informers, and they differed by who controlled them:

- **The Spanish Inquisition** (bull of Sixtus IV, 1 November 1478; first inquisitors named in 1480) was a **crown tribunal with papal sanction**. The bull let Ferdinand and Isabella appoint the inquisitors, and its governing council, the Suprema, was created in 1483 with members the crown named. See [Spanish Inquisition](../reference.md#spanish-inquisition).
- **The Portuguese Inquisition** (1536) copied the Spanish model, and in 1560 opened a tribunal at Goa that lasted until 1812.
- **The Roman Inquisition** (Paul III's *Licet ab initio*, 21 July 1542) was a **papal congregation of cardinals** supervising local tribunals across Italy. See [Roman Inquisition](../reference.md#roman-inquisition).

Two facts do most of the work in this lesson. First, an inquisition had jurisdiction only over the **baptized**. It tried Christians suspected of heresy, not Jews or Muslims as such. Second, it did not execute anyone itself. It "relaxed" the condemned to the secular arm, which burned them. The distinction mattered to canon lawyers. It made no difference to the person at the stake, and this lesson does not treat it as an excuse.

## Source

Henry Charles Lea, *A History of the Inquisition of Spain*, vol. 4 (1907), p. 517. Lea was a Philadelphia publisher and the greatest archival historian of the Inquisition in his century, and no friend of it:

> There is no question that the number of these has been greatly exaggerated in popular belief, an exaggeration to which Llorente has largely contributed by his absurd method of computation, on an arbitrary assumption of a certain annual average for each tribunal in successive periods. It is impossible now to reconstruct the statistics of the Inquisition, especially during its early activity, but some general conclusions can be formed from the details accessible as to a few tribunals. The burnings without doubt were numerous during the first few years, through the unregulated ardor of inquisitors, little versed in the canon law, who seem to have condemned right and left, on flimsy evidence…

Read it for what it establishes. The most hostile major historian of the Spanish Inquisition rejected the inflated totals *in 1907*. The correction of the numbers is not a modern Catholic apologetic. And in the same breath Lea insists on the burnings of the first years. Both halves stand.

## The record

**1. The conversos.** After the massacres of 1391 and decades of pressure, Castile and Aragon held many thousands of Jewish converts and their descendants, the **[conversos](../reference.md#conversos)**. Some kept Jewish practices in secret. How many did so is still disputed. Many prospered, and they were resented. In 1449 rebels in Toledo issued the first statute barring people of Jewish descent from office, and Nicholas V condemned it. **[Limpieza de sangre](../reference.md#limpieza-de-sangre)** ("purity of blood") statutes spread anyway through cathedral chapters, orders and colleges in the sixteenth century. *In words:* a Church that taught that baptism made one people let its institutions sort Christians by ancestry.

**2. The founding terror, 1480–1530.** The first *[auto de fe](../reference.md#auto-de-fe)* was held in Seville on 6 February 1481, and six people were burned. Tomás de Torquemada was named inquisitor general in 1483 and served until his death in 1498. Hernando del Pulgar, Isabella's own secretary, put the number burned by about 1490 at 2,000. An inscription placed on the Seville tribunal's castle in 1524 claimed more than 20,000 abjurations there and nearly 1,000 burnings. Property was confiscated at arrest. Torture was lawful when guilt was judged half-proven, and was bound by rules: a physician attended, and a confession made under torture had to be ratified afterwards. Punishments short of death were heavy too: prison, the penitential garment (*sanbenito*), later hung in the parish church with the wearer's name, and loss of office for descendants.

**3. Expulsions by the crown.** Ferdinand and Isabella expelled the unbaptized Jews by the decree of 31 March 1492. Estimates of how many left range widely and are disputed. Philip III expelled the Moriscos, Christians of Muslim descent, between 1609 and 1614: roughly 300,000 people on the older estimates. Both were acts of the crown, not sentences of the tribunal. But the Inquisition's first targets had been baptized Jews, and Torquemada is reported to have urged the 1492 expulsion.

**4. New targets.** In 1559–62 autos de fe at Valladolid and Seville destroyed Spain's small Protestant circles. After that the Spanish tribunals spent most of their time on Moriscos, blasphemy, bigamy, "propositions" (careless talk about the faith) and clerical misconduct, and death sentences became rare.

**5. Witches.** At Logroño in November 1610 six accused witches were burned in person and five in effigy. The junior inquisitor Alonso de Salazar Frías then spent 1611 investigating and reported that he could find no proof that any act of witchcraft had occurred. The Suprema's instructions of 1614 adopted his rules of evidence, and the Spanish Inquisition effectively stopped executing witches. Europe's witch trials, which killed an estimated 40,000–60,000 people (Levack), were overwhelmingly the work of local and secular courts, Protestant and Catholic alike.

**6. Rome.** The Roman Inquisition broke the Italian evangelical circles of the 1540s–60s, enforced the Index (the first papal Index, 1559; a Congregation of the Index from 1571), sent Giordano Bruno to the stake in Rome on 17 February 1600, and tried Galileo ([7.3](07-03-the-galileo-affair.md)). Andrea Del Col estimates roughly 1,250 death sentences out of some 51,000–75,000 cases in Italy after 1542.

**7. The end.** Joseph Bonaparte suppressed the Spanish Inquisition in 1808 and the Cortes of Cádiz did so in 1813. Ferdinand VII restored it in 1814, and it was suspended again in 1820. The last execution for heresy in Spain, of the schoolteacher Cayetano Ripoll on 26 July 1826, was ordered by a diocesan "junta of faith" that stood in for the tribunal. A decree of 15 July 1834 abolished the Inquisition for good. The Roman congregation survived as the Holy Office (renamed 1908) and is today's Dicastery for the Doctrine of the Faith.

## Map of estimates

| Who | When | Executions in person (Spain) | Basis |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Llorente**, secretary of the Inquisition at Madrid 1789–91 | 1817–18 | 31,912 (plus 17,659 in effigy, 291,450 penanced), 1481–1808 | An assumed annual average per tribunal, multiplied out |
| **Lea** | 1906–07 | Declined to give a total; showed Llorente's guesses far above surviving records | Tribunal-by-tribunal archives |
| **Henningsen and Contreras** | 1986 | 826 in person, 778 in effigy, in 44,674 recorded cases, 1540–1700 (about 1.8 percent in person) | The tribunals' annual reports to the Suprema; incomplete, and Henningsen later estimated about 87,000 trials in the period |
| **Kamen; Monter** | 1990s | About 2,000 before 1530; around 3,000 in all (Monter 2,750) | Local studies for the early years, plus the report series |
| **Pérez; Dedieu** | 1987–2005 | Fewer than 10,000; most of them before about 1530 | Estimated total trials (125,000 to 200,000) and early execution rates |
| **Del Col** | 2010 | About 12,000 (about 10,500 before 1530) | Assumes 15 percent of early trials ended in execution |

See [Inquisition death toll estimates](../reference.md#inquisition-death-toll-estimates). Read the table in two halves. After 1540 the estimates agree closely, because they rest on the same reports. Before 1530 they diverge by a factor of five, because the records of the founding decades are largely lost. **Every serious estimate is in the thousands.** None is in the hundreds of thousands, and "millions" has never had any documentary basis.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean): testing "the Inquisition burned millions."** Take the highest scholarly estimate, about 12,000 over three and a half centuries. "Millions" overshoots it by a factor of more than 80, and it overshoots even Llorente's inflated 31,912 by a factor of about 30. The claim fails on every source. Now say what survives the correction: several thousand people burned for their beliefs or their ancestors' beliefs, most of them conversos, most in the first fifty years; far more imprisoned, impoverished and shamed; a whole minority made suspect by descent. Correcting the number removes a falsehood. It leaves a grave record.

**Example 2 (hard): is 1.8 percent a small number?** Henningsen and Contreras's rate invites the reply that the Inquisition was lenient, and Kamen points out that the secular courts of Antwerp alone executed 103 heretics in 1557–62, more than Spain's tribunals executed in the same years. But three things strain the inference. The rate comes from 1540–1700, *after* the killing years, so it cannot be applied back to 1480–1530 (Del Col uses 15 percent there). The denominator is mostly blasphemy, bigamy and loose talk; among suspected judaizers in the bad decades the share sent to the stake was far higher. And execution was not the tribunal's main instrument. Confiscation, secrecy, anonymous denunciation and inherited infamy did their work on the thousands who were never burned. "Low by the standards of early modern justice" and "a grave injustice" can both be true. The number alone decides neither.

## Watch out

- **"The Inquisition" is three institutions, plus the medieval one.** A claim about Rome (Bruno, Galileo) is not evidence about Spain, and a claim about Spain is not evidence about the papal inquisitors of 1230.
- **The Inquisition did not try Jews for being Jews.** It tried baptized Christians. The expulsions of 1492 and 1609–14 were royal decrees. Putting them on the tribunal's account is an error of fact; leaving the tribunal out of their story is an error too.
- **The witch-hunt was not mainly inquisitorial.** The Spanish Inquisition was among the most sceptical courts in Europe after 1614. The mass burnings happened elsewhere, chiefly in secular courts of the Holy Roman Empire.
- **The Kamen–Lea dispute is not about body counts.** Lea already rejected Llorente. They disagree about causes and effects: see [Kamen vs Lea](../reference.md#kamen-vs-lea) and P3.
- **Don't read the Day of Pardon back as the historians' verdict.** On 12 March 2000 John Paul II asked forgiveness for the use of violence "in the service of the truth." A symposium of historians the Vatican convened in October 1998 came first; its proceedings (ed. Agostino Borromeo) were published in 2004, and the pope's letter presenting them says that asking pardon required knowing the facts first. The acknowledgement comes after the record, not in place of it.

## One-liner

> The Inquisition killed thousands, not millions, and mostly in its first fifty years; the number was corrected by its fiercest historian, and the correction leaves the conversos' story as grave as it was.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢) *(Formal.)*** Lea (vol. 4, p. 524) compares surviving records of relaxations in person with the figures Llorente's method produces for the same tribunals and years: Toledo 1483–1501, records 297, Llorente 666; Saragossa 1485–1502, records 124, Llorente 584; Valladolid 1485–92, records 50, Llorente 424.
(a) For each tribunal, by what factor does Llorente's figure exceed the record? What is the factor for the three combined?
(b) Henningsen and Contreras found 826 executions in person in 44,674 recorded cases. Give the rate as a percentage to two decimals, and the number of executions it implies if it held across Henningsen's later estimate of 87,269 trials in the same period.

**P2 (🟡) *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)*** An invented tour-guide script:

> "These are the cells of the Holy Office. From 1478 the Pope's inquisitors hunted down Spain's Jews, and over three centuries they burned more than a million people: heretics, Jews and, above all, witches. Only Napoleon's army closed the Inquisition for good, in 1808."

(a) Name four factual errors and correct each in one line. (b) A defender of the guide replies: "The exact number doesn't matter; the moral point stands." In 150 words or fewer, any verdict, say what correcting the number changes and what it does not.

**P3 (🔴, optional) *(Exegetical.)*** Lea and Kamen accept roughly the same scale of executions after 1540, yet Kamen called his book a "revision." In 150 words or fewer, name the crux between them and the kind of evidence that would move it.

<details>
<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Formal.)*

(a) Toledo: $666/297 = 2.24$. Saragossa: $584/124 = 4.71$. Valladolid: $424/50 = 8.48$. Combined: records $297 + 124 + 50 = 471$; Llorente $666 + 584 + 424 = 1{,}674$; factor $1674/471 = 3.55$. So Llorente runs between about 2 and 8.5 times the record, and about 3.5 times overall. (Computed in Python.)

(b) $826/44{,}674 = 0.01849$, so **1.85 percent**. Applied to 87,269 trials: $0.01849 \times 87{,}269 \approx$ **1,614 executions** in 1540–1700. This is the figure Del Col's estimate of about 1,600 for the period rests on.

**Wrong turns:** dividing the record by Llorente's figure (a factor below 1 answers a different question); adding the 778 effigies to the in-person rate; applying 1.85 percent to the years before 1530, when the rate was far higher.

---

**P2** *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** any four of: (1) not "the Pope's inquisitors": a crown tribunal whose inquisitors the monarchs named under Sixtus IV's 1478 bull; (2) not "Spain's Jews": it tried baptized Christians, mainly conversos suspected of judaizing, while unbaptized Jews were expelled by royal decree in 1492; (3) not "more than a million": scholarly estimates run from under 3,000 to about 12,000, and even Llorente claimed 31,912; (4) not "above all witches": it burned few witches (six in person at Logroño, 1610) and after 1614 effectively none; (5) not "for good in 1808": suppressed 1808 and 1813, restored 1814, finally abolished 15 July 1834.
**Must hit, any verdict (b):** say what the number changes (scale, comparison with other courts and the witch trials, the plausibility of the Black Legend) and what it leaves standing (coercion of conscience, the conversos' fate, confiscation and torture, inherited infamy), and then judge whether the guide's "moral point" survives in the form he stated it.
**Wrong turns:** "the Inquisition executed no Jews" (it burned many conversos accused of judaizing); treating Llorente's figure as a modern estimate; arguing in (b) that because the number is low there is no moral point, or that because there is a moral point the number is irrelevant, without weighing the two.
**Model answer (b), one of several:** The number matters because the guide's moral point depends on it: "more than a million" puts the Inquisition beside the great massacres of history, and the evidence puts it at several thousand executions, most before 1530. Correcting that removes a comparison the record does not support and a staple of anti-Spanish and anti-Catholic polemic. It does not remove the rest. Several thousand people burned, most of them baptized Jews and their descendants, thousands more ruined by confiscation and shame, and a whole minority made suspect by ancestry are grave wrongs at any scale, and in 2000 the Church asked pardon for violence used in the service of truth. So the moral point stands, but only in a corrected form: a real persecution, not an imagined holocaust.

---

**P3** *(Exegetical.)*

**Must hit, strict:** the crux is not the death toll but the Inquisition's **causes and effects**. Lea read it as the instrument of a pervasive clerical intolerance that deformed Spanish religion and intellectual life and contributed to Spain's decline; Kamen reads it as driven as much by crown politics and Old-Christian social conflict as by religion, with little reach into most Spaniards' lives, and not a cause of decline. Evidence that bears on it: how far the tribunals' presence reached (familiares, rural contact, case density by region), and measured effects on literacy, book output and scholarship where the tribunals were stronger or weaker (e.g. recent quantitative studies comparing municipalities by inquisitorial activity).
**Wrong turns:** saying Kamen lowered Lea's numbers (Lea had already rejected Llorente); making the crux Protestant against Catholic bias rather than a claim evidence could test; naming a verdict instead of a crux.
**Model answer:** They agree roughly on how many people the tribunals killed after 1540. They disagree about what the Inquisition was and what it did to Spain. For Lea it was the engine of an intolerance that shaped Spanish religion and contributed to national decline. For Kamen it served the crown and Old-Christian society, touched most Spaniards only lightly, and did not cause decline. Evidence that would move the question is regional and comparative: how dense the tribunal's network was, and whether places where it was more active show lower literacy, book production or scholarship than places where it was weaker.

</details>

## Flashback

**From Lesson [5.3](05-03-heresy-inquisition-and-the-jews.md) (Heresy, inquisition, and the Jews):** *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)* Test a popular claim. A common defence runs: "The medieval Church never executed a single heretic. The inquisitors only judged; the state did the burning." (a) In two sentences, state what in this claim is accurate about thirteenth-century procedure. (b) Does that accuracy settle the question of the Church's responsibility for the burnings? 100 words or fewer, any verdict.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):** inquisitors did not carry out death sentences. The obstinate and the relapsed were "relinquished" to the secular arm, and lay authorities burned them; under *Ad extirpanda* (1252) lay officials, not friars, also applied torture. (An answer may add that execution was the exception: most of those convicted received penances or prison.)
**Must hit, any verdict (b):** (1) grant that the distinction between judging and executing was real in canon law, not a later excuse; (2) weigh against it at least two of: the inquisitor handed people over knowing what the lay authorities would do; Lateran IV (canon 3) threatened rulers who failed to purge heresy with the loss of their lands, so the secular arm acted under Church pressure; *Ad extirpanda* was a papal licence for the torture lay officials applied; the Albigensian Crusade was called by a pope, and the killing at Béziers was done by an army whose spiritual leader was a papal legate; (3) say whether "never executed" and "not responsible" are the same claim.
**Wrong turns:** denying that the procedure separated judging from executing; saying friars lit the fires or applied the torture; answering with the Spanish Inquisition's figures, which belong to a later institution.
**Model answer (b), one of several:** No. The claim is right about who carried out the sentence and wrong about what follows from it. The distinction was real to canonists, but the inquisitor relinquished the relapsed knowing the result. Lateran IV had threatened any ruler who failed to purge heresy with the loss of his lands, and Innocent IV's *Ad extirpanda* licensed the torture lay officials applied. The Church designed the procedure and pressed rulers to enforce it. The formal distinction assigns the act to laymen; it does not remove the Church's share of responsibility for the outcome.

</details>
