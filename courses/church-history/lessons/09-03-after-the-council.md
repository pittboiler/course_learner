# Church History · Lesson 9.3: After the council

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 9: The twentieth century and after · Builds on: [9.2 The Second Vatican Council](09-02-the-second-vatican-council.md), [9.1 The Church and the totalitarian states](09-01-the-church-and-the-totalitarian-states.md) · Unlocks: the dogmatic courses, starting with [`ecclesiology-and-mariology`](../../ecclesiology-and-mariology/syllabus.md) 5.4

## Why this matters

The council closed on 8 December 1965, and the sixty years since have been one long argument about what it was. Traditionalists who say it broke with the past and progressives who say it began something its texts only half-say agree that there was a rupture. Two popes spent their pontificates denying it. The same decades brought the modern papacy's most celebrated political moment, Poland in 1979–89, and the Church's gravest modern scandal, the sexual abuse of minors by clergy and its concealment by bishops. For the abuse crisis this lesson keeps the course's order: facts first, then the debate, then the Church's acknowledgement.

## The idea

Three lines run in parallel.

**Reception.** A council produces texts; then the Church has to live them. The worst fights came over two things the council did not settle itself: contraception, which Paul VI reserved to himself, and the liturgy, where the council set principles and a commission wrote the new books.

**The papacy on the world stage.** John Paul II (1978–2005) drew crowds that in Poland became a political fact.

**The abuse crisis.** Across countries the pattern recurs. Clerics abused children. Superiors learned of it and chose transfer, therapy or silence over removal and report. Decades later the facts came out through journalists, civil commissions and grand juries, not the Church's own processes, so the main sources for the crisis are civil documents.

## Source

Pius X, motu proprio *Tra le sollecitudini* on sacred music (22 November 1903), standard English version (public domain):

> We deem it necessary to provide before anything else for the sanctity and dignity of the temple, in which the faithful assemble for no other object than that of acquiring this spirit from its foremost and indispensable font, which is the active participation in the most holy mysteries and in the public and solemn prayer of the Church.

And from section 7:

> The language proper to the Roman Church is Latin. Hence it is forbidden to sing anything whatever in the vernacular in solemn liturgical functions.

The first sentence brings the liturgical movement's watchword, "active participation," into papal teaching; Vatican II made "full, conscious, and active participation" the aim of its liturgical reform (*Sacrosanctum Concilium* 14, 1963, paraphrased). The second is a rule the post-conciliar reform set aside. So both parties after 1969 could cite the same pope: reformers his principle, and Marcel Lefebvre, who named his Society after him, his rules. That is the continuity debate in miniature: was Latin chant the *principle*, or only the *means* Pius X chose for it in 1903?

## What happened

1. **[*Humanae Vitae*](../reference.md#humanae-vitae) (25 July 1968).** The majority of a papal commission on birth control had favoured change; its reports reached Paul VI in June 1966 and leaked in April 1967. He reaffirmed the exclusion of contraception anyway (the teaching itself: [`moral-theology`](../../moral-theology/syllabus.md)). On 30 July Charles Curran and colleagues at the Catholic University of America issued a public dissent, eventually signed by hundreds of theologians. The Canadian bishops' Winnipeg Statement (27 September) counselled Catholics who found the teaching impossible to accept. *In words:* the first great test of how a papal teaching would be received after the council. Its weight: an encyclical is at least authentic ordinary magisterium, owed religious submission ([`fundamental-theology` 4.3](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-03-religious-submission.md)). John Ford and Germain Grisez (1978) argued that the teaching is infallible through the ordinary universal magisterium. That is disputed among Catholic theologians, and this course does not settle it.
2. **The new Mass and Lefebvre.** Paul VI's *Missale Romanum* (3 April 1969) brought the new Order of Mass into force on 30 November 1969; the full missal followed in 1970. Lefebvre founded the Society of St Pius X at Écône in 1970. On 30 June 1988 he consecrated four bishops without papal mandate. A decree of the Congregation for Bishops (1 July) and John Paul II's *Ecclesia Dei* (2 July) declared that the [Econe consecrations](../reference.md#econe-consecrations) incurred automatic excommunication. Benedict XVI freed the older missal (*Summorum Pontificum*, 2007) and had the four bishops' excommunications remitted (21 January 2009). Francis restricted the older missal again (*Traditionis custodes*, 2021). *In words:* the liturgy became the flag of the continuity question.
3. **John Paul II and communism.** Karol Wojtyła, elected on 16 October 1978, was the first non-Italian pope since 1522. He visited Poland on 2–10 June 1979. At Mass in Warsaw on 2 June he prayed that the Spirit would renew the face of the earth, adding "of this land." The Gdańsk strikes of August 1980 produced Solidarity, which had some ten million members within a year. Martial law followed in December 1981. The semi-free elections of 4 June 1989 ended communist rule in Poland; the Berlin Wall fell that November. In 1992 Mikhail Gorbachev wrote that the changes in Eastern Europe would not have been possible without this pope (paraphrased). How much causal weight the pope carries is disputed (P3).
4. **The [Day of Pardon](../reference.md#day-of-pardon) (12 March 2000).** John Paul II led seven confessions of sins by the Church's members, among them sins "in the service of truth" and against the Jewish people.
5. **Benedict XVI, Francis, Leo XIV.** Benedict, elected 19 April 2005, announced his resignation on 11 February 2013, effective 28 February: the first papal resignation since Gregory XII in 1415. Francis, elected 13 March 2013, died on 21 April 2025. On 8 May 2025 the conclave elected Robert Prevost, an Augustinian long a missionary and bishop in Peru, as Leo XIV, the first pope from the United States.

## The abuse crisis: facts, debate, acknowledgement

**The facts.** On 6 January 2002 the *Boston Globe* reported that John Geoghan, accused of abusing more than 130 children over three decades, had been moved from parish to parish by an archdiocese that knew. Cardinal Bernard Law resigned on 13 December 2002. The US bishops commissioned the John Jay College of Criminal Justice to count the cases ([John Jay reports](../reference.md#john-jay-reports)). *Nature and Scope* (2004) found allegations, not withdrawn or known to be false, against 4,392 priests who served in 1950–2002: about 4 percent of the 109,694 priests the dioceses reported. 10,667 people had made allegations; 81 percent of victims were male. Incidents rose from the mid-1960s and peaked in the 1970s, and two-thirds of the allegations had been made only since 1993.

In Ireland, the Ryan Report (20 May 2009) found sexual abuse "endemic" in boys' industrial schools run by religious orders, mostly in 1936–70. The Murphy Report on Dublin (26 November 2009) concluded that the archdiocese had put secrecy, the avoidance of scandal and its own reputation ahead of children's welfare. The Cloyne Report (13 July 2011) found the diocese had ignored the Irish bishops' own 1996 child-protection guidelines until 2008. In 2018 a Pennsylvania grand jury (14 August) named 301 priests and more than 1,000 identifiable victims in six dioceses over seven decades. Theodore McCarrick, former archbishop of Washington, was removed from ministry that June and dismissed from the clerical state in February 2019; the Vatican's own report (November 2020) traced how he rose despite years of warnings.

**The failure, stated plainly.** The crime was the abusers'. The institutional failure was the bishops', and it was a failure of choice, not of ignorance: known abusers were transferred, treated and returned to ministry, settlements kept confidential, police not told.

**The debate over causes.** John Jay's second study, *Causes and Context* (2011), named no single cause. It tied the rise to the social disruption of the 1960s and 1970s and to thin seminary formation. It argued that celibacy and an all-male priesthood, constant across the rise and the fall, cannot explain the curve, and it attributed the preponderance of boys among victims in large part to opportunity. Critics answered from opposite sides. William Donohue of the Catholic League argued that the report explained away homosexual behaviour, given 81 percent male victims. A. W. Richard Sipe, a former priest turned psychotherapist, argued that mandatory celibacy sustained a culture of secrecy that shielded abuse. Francis named clericalism as the root of the concealment (Letter to the People of God, 2018). Each fits part of the record; none has settled the question.

**The acknowledgements.** John Paul II told the US cardinals in April 2002 that there was no place in the priesthood for those who would harm the young (paraphrased). Benedict XVI met victims in Washington (2008) and wrote to the Catholics of Ireland (19 March 2010). Francis admitted "grave errors" over Chile, whose bishops all offered their resignations on 18 May 2018, and summoned the heads of the world's bishops' conferences in February 2019. His [*Vos estis lux mundi*](../reference.md#vos-estis-lux-mundi) (7 May 2019; made permanent in 2023) obliged clergy and religious to report abuse and its cover-up and set procedures for investigating bishops.

## Map of positions: continuity or rupture?

| Position | Where is "the council" found? | Rupture? | Key evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| **The [Bologna school](../reference.md#bologna-school)** (Giuseppe Alberigo, ed., *History of Vatican II*, 5 vols, 1995–2001) | In the **event**: the assembly's experience and drive to renewal, which the compromise texts only partly record | A real, welcome novelty; "rupture" is mostly their critics' word | Diaries, drafts, votes; the overturned preparatory schemas |
| **Benedict XVI**, address to the Curia, 22 December 2005 ([hermeneutic of reform](../reference.md#hermeneutic-of-reform)); Agostino Marchetto (2005) | In the **texts** as promulgated, read within the tradition | No: against a "hermeneutic of discontinuity and rupture" he sets reform, renewal in the continuity of the one Church; discontinuity only in contingent applications | The final texts; his religious-liberty example |
| **John O'Malley** (*What Happened at Vatican II*, 2008) | Texts *and* event, read through a new **style** | In style, within doctrinal continuity | Vocabulary of invitation and dialogue in place of anathema |
| **Lefebvre's Society** | In the texts, whose novelties it rejects | Yes, and deplored | Religious liberty, ecumenism, the new Mass |

The Society and Bologna both see a rupture and judge it oppositely; Benedict denies it against both.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean: the Murphy Report as a source).** Apply the [four questions for a source](../reference.md#four-questions-for-a-source). *Author:* a statutory commission of investigation chaired by a judge, able to compel documents. *Date:* 2009, covering 1975–2004. *Audience:* the Irish government and public. *Purpose:* not to decide each allegation, but how Church and State authorities *handled* complaints. So it can establish one archdiocese's pattern of handling: files kept, police not told, priests moved. It cannot establish national rates, since it studied a sample of 46 of the 102 priests within its remit, nor causes. Its evidence is the archdiocese's own files.

**Example 2 (hard: was the abuse crisis post-conciliar?).** The claim comes from opposite directions: the council's aftermath loosened discipline, or pre-conciliar formation and secrecy produced both abuse and concealment. The John Jay curve rises from the mid-1960s, which fits "post-conciliar." But 44 percent of the accused were ordained before 1960, under the old seminary regime; the Irish industrial-school abuse was mostly before 1970; and the habit of secrecy is older than both. The same years hold the council, the sexual revolution and the wider rise in deviance John Jay cites, and the data cannot separate them. Where the evidence strains: "after the council" is a date, and a date is not a cause. Separating the causes needs comparable data from other institutions, which John Jay noted does not exist.

## Watch out

- **"Allegations" is not "findings."** The 4,392 are priests with allegations not withdrawn or known to be false, not priests convicted. The count is also a floor, since allegations kept arriving after 2002. Both corrections apply at once.
- **The Day of Pardon was not a response to the abuse crisis.** It came two years before Boston; its seventh confession prayed for minors who were victims of abuse only in general terms.
- **"Vatican II abolished Latin" is wrong.** The council kept Latin and widened the vernacular; the 1970 missal is itself a Latin book.
- **Don't overstate *Humanae Vitae*'s level either way.** "Infallible" asserts a disputed thesis as settled; "only an opinion" ignores the religious submission owed to an encyclical.

## One-liner

> After 1965 the Church argued over whether it had continued itself or broken with itself, while its bishops' concealment of abuse, exposed by outsiders, did more damage than any liturgical quarrel.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢) *(Formal (a)–(b) · Exegetical (c).)*** Figures from John Jay's *Nature and Scope* (2004): 4,392 priests with allegations out of 109,694 priests reported by dioceses (1950–2002); using a second count (CARA) for 1960–2002, 4,127 accused out of 94,607 priests; 10,667 people made allegations; 56 percent of accused priests were alleged to have abused one victim.
(a) Compute both percentages of priests accused, to one decimal place.
(b) Compute the mean number of accusers per accused priest, and say in one sentence why the typical accused priest had fewer.
(c) An invented museum placard reads: "Between 1950 and 2002, one American priest in twenty-five abused a child." Say in two sentences what the 4 percent actually measures, and give one way the placard overstates and one way it may understate.

**P2 (🟡) *(Exegetical.)*** The Bologna school and Benedict XVI's 2005 address disagree about whether Vatican II was a new beginning. (a) Name the single premise, about where "the council" is to be found, that one side must deny. (b) Say what kind of evidence each side treats as decisive, and what finding would embarrass each. 150 words or fewer in total.

**P3 (🔴, optional) *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)*** An invented documentary voice-over:

> "In June 1979 a Polish pope stood in Warsaw and told his people to rise up. Ten years later the Iron Curtain was gone. Communism in Europe was brought down by John Paul II."

(a) Identify one accurate fact, one misdescription of the source, and the causal leap. (b) How much weight does the pope's role deserve? Any verdict, 150 words or fewer.

<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Formal (a)–(b) · Exegetical (c).)*

(a) $4{,}392 / 109{,}694 = 0.04004\ldots$, so **4.0 percent**. $4{,}127 / 94{,}607 = 0.04362\ldots$, so **4.4 percent** to one decimal place. (The report prints 4.3 percent, truncating.) Computed in Python.

(b) $10{,}667 / 4{,}392 = 2.43$ accusers per accused priest on average. The distribution is skewed: most accused priests (56 percent) had one accuser, while a small group had many. The report finds that 149 priests with more than ten allegations accounted for about a quarter of all allegations, which pulls the mean above the typical case.

**Must hit, strict (c):** the 4 percent is the share of priests *against whom allegations were made* and recorded in diocesan and religious files (not withdrawn or known to be false), as reported to the researchers by 2003. It is not a count of proven abusers. **Overstates:** "abused" turns an allegation into a finding. **May understate:** the count only includes allegations made by 2002–03, and reporting is often decades late (two-thirds came after 1993), so further allegations were expected. It also depends on dioceses' own files.
**Wrong turns:** "correcting" the number itself (one in twenty-five *is* 4 percent); treating the low conviction count as proof of innocence, when statutes of limitation had often expired; naming only one direction of error.
**Model answer (c):** The 4 percent is the share of priests serving between 1950 and 2002 with allegations on file that had not been withdrawn or shown false, as dioceses reported by 2003. The placard overstates by calling allegations abuse, and it may understate, because the count stops in 2002 while victims often reported decades later.

**P2** *(Exegetical.)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** whether the council is found in its **promulgated texts** read within the tradition (Benedict) or also in the **event**: the assembly's experience and dynamic, of which the texts are compromises (Bologna). Benedict must deny that the "spirit" or event can override the texts; Alberigo's school must deny that the texts alone exhaust the council.
**Must hit, strict (b):** Bologna treats the process as decisive: diaries, drafts, votes, the rejection of the preparatory schemas. Benedict treats the final texts and their continuity with earlier teaching as decisive. Bologna is embarrassed by texts whose plain sense repeats earlier teaching, where no compromise explains the wording. Benedict's reading is embarrassed by a point of doctrine (not a contingent application) that the texts reverse.
**Wrong turns:** making the crux "progressive vs conservative" (a verdict, not a premise); saying Benedict denies all change (he allows discontinuity in contingent applications); saying Bologna ignores the texts.
**Model answer:** (a) Where the council is: in its texts, or in the event those texts only partly record. Benedict must deny that the event can outrank the texts; Bologna must deny that the texts are the whole council. (b) Bologna reads the history of the sessions, meaning drafts, diaries and the overturned schemas, as the key to the council's direction. Benedict reads the final texts against earlier teaching. A text that plainly repeats earlier teaching where nothing forced a compromise embarrasses Bologna. A reversal at the level of principle, not application, embarrasses Benedict.

---

**P3** *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** the fact: John Paul II visited Poland in June 1979 and said Mass in Warsaw on 2 June, and communist rule in Poland and the Wall both ended in 1989. The misdescription: the homily did not call for a rising. It was a prayer that the Spirit renew the face of the earth, "of this land": religious language with political resonance. The leap: from sequence (1979 then 1989) to sole cause, skipping Solidarity, martial law, economic collapse and Gorbachev's refusal to use force.
**Must hit, any verdict (b):** name at least two rival causal factors; use evidence from both sides (e.g. Gorbachev's 1992 tribute, or Weigel's *The Final Revolution*, 1992, for the pope; Kotkin's *Uncivil Society*, 2009, for the regimes' implosion, which still treats Poland's Solidarity as the real exception); distinguish Poland from the rest of the bloc; say what evidence would change the weighting.
**Wrong turns:** "the pope had nothing to do with it" (as unsupported as sole cause); treating Gorbachev's tribute as proof rather than as one actor's testimony; forgetting that Solidarity was a trade union with its own leaders and aims.
**Model answer (b), one of several:** The strongest case for the pope is Polish: the 1979 crowds showed Poles their own numbers, and Solidarity formed fourteen months later with the Church as its sheltering institution. Gorbachev himself called the pope indispensable. But the bloc fell in 1989 because Soviet power would not intervene and the regimes were bankrupt; Kotkin argues that the establishments imploded, with Poland the one place where organized society really forced the pace. So I'd weight the pope as a major cause in Poland and a contributing one elsewhere. Evidence that would move me: archival proof that Moscow's restraint was shaped by fear of Polish Catholic resistance.

## Flashback

**From Lesson [9.1](09-01-the-church-and-the-totalitarian-states.md) (The Church and the totalitarian states):** *(Exegetical.)* Germany, 1933. (a) Put in order, with day and month: the Centre Party dissolves itself; the Reichskonkordat is ratified; the Enabling Act passes with the Centre's votes; the Reichskonkordat is signed by Cardinal Pacelli and Vice-Chancellor von Papen; the German bishops withdraw their earlier warnings against National Socialism. Then say in one sentence which causal claim the order alone rules out. (b) Klaus Scholder and Konrad Repgen disagree about the Centre's vote of March 1933. Name the crux, and one kind of evidence that would push toward each side. 100 words or fewer for (b).

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):** Enabling Act (23 March) → bishops withdraw their warnings (28 March) → Centre dissolves (5 July) → concordat signed (20 July) → ratified (10 September). The treaty came after the Enabling Act and after the Centre's dissolution, so the signed concordat cannot have caused either. Its Article 32, barring clergy from party politics, confirmed a withdrawal that had already happened.
**Must hit, strict (b):** the crux is whether the prospect of a concordat, in negotiations before 23 March, motivated the Centre's vote. Scholder argued there was such a link; Repgen held that the Centre voted on its own political reckoning. Evidence toward Scholder: documents showing concordat talks or assurances passing to Centre leaders before the vote. Evidence toward Repgen: records of the party's deliberations giving other grounds for its vote, with no reference to a treaty. The state of the question: later work in the Vatican files (Brechenmacher) has not proved the link.
**Wrong turns:** treating the order of events as settling the Scholder–Repgen question (it rules out the signed treaty as cause, not the negotiations); saying the archives have disproved the link (they have not proved it, which is different); saying the concordat "made Hitler legitimate" or "saved the German Church", both causal claims the lesson flags.
**Model answer:** (a) 23 March, Enabling Act; 28 March, bishops' warnings withdrawn; 5 July, Centre dissolves; 20 July, signing; 10 September, ratification. The signed treaty cannot have caused either the Enabling Act or the Centre's end, both of which came first. (b) The crux is motive: was the Centre's yes in March bought with the prospect of a concordat (Scholder), or did the party vote on its own reckoning (Repgen)? Papers showing concordat assurances reaching Centre leaders before 23 March would favour Scholder. Party records giving other reasons, with no mention of a treaty, would favour Repgen. The Vatican files have not proved the link.

## Closing the course

Ten dates carry the story: 312, 451, 800, 1054, 1122, 1517, 1545, 1789, 1870, 1965. Each marks a change in the Church's relation to power. First came an emperor's patronage, then a council's contest with an imperial see and a pope crowning an emperor. Then a breach with the East, a settlement with the emperor, and a revolt against Rome's authority. Then a council's answer, a revolution that nearly destroyed the Church, the loss of the pope's temporal power, and a council that met the modern world without a throne behind it. After 1965 the question is no longer who protects the Church, but whether it can be trusted: by its own members, by the victims it failed, and by historians who read its record.
