# Church History · Lesson 5.4: Avignon, the Great Schism, and the councils

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 5: Papal monarchy and its crisis · Builds on: [5.1 Papal monarchy](05-01-papal-monarchy-innocent-iii-to-boniface-viii.md), [5.3 Heresy, inquisition, and the Jews](05-03-heresy-inquisition-and-the-jews.md) · Unlocks: [6.1 The late medieval Church and Luther's protest](06-01-the-late-medieval-church-and-luthers-protest.md)

## Why this matters

In 1302 Boniface VIII told the world that every human creature must be subject to the Roman pontiff ([5.1](05-01-papal-monarchy-innocent-iii-to-boniface-viii.md)). A century later there were three Roman pontiffs, each excommunicating the others. Saints backed rival popes, and so did whole kingdoms. What ended it was not a pope but a council, which deposed two claimants, accepted the resignation of a third, and elected a fourth. This is the hardest test the papacy ever faced, and how it was passed still shapes arguments about who governs the Church.

## The idea

Three linked crises, each growing out of the one before.

**Avignon (1309–1377).** After Anagni, the papacy settled on the Rhône, in a town that belonged to the count of Provence and sat next to the papal Comtat Venaissin. The seven Avignon popes were all from southern France, and so were most of their cardinals. They built one of the most efficient governments in Europe. To Italians it looked like exile. Petrarch called Avignon "the Babylon of the West," and the name "Babylonian captivity" stuck to the whole [Avignon papacy](../reference.md#avignon-papacy).

**The schism (1378–1417).** The return to Rome produced a disputed election. The same cardinals chose two popes five months apart, and Christendom split along political lines.

**The councils (1409–1449).** The only body that could judge between claimants was a general council. Its success raised the question the papacy had never had to answer: if a council can make a pope, is it above one?

## Source

Petrarch, letter to a friend, written between 1340 and 1353 (tr. J. H. Robinson, *Readings in European History*, 1904):

> Now I am living in France, in the Babylon of the West. … Here reign the successors of the poor fishermen of Galilee; they have strangely forgotten their origin.

Read what kind of evidence this is. Petrarch was a humanist who wanted the papacy back in Rome, and the charge is moral and rhetorical. It does not claim the popes took orders from the king of France. Later readers heard "captivity," meaning subjection to France. The modern revision starts from that gap. Guillaume Mollat's *Les Papes d'Avignon* (1912), built on the papal registers, described popes who ran their own policy, spent heavily on reconquering the Papal States in Italy, and kept planning a return, and an administration whose centralizing and fiscal reach, not its subservience, was the real novelty.

## The record

1. **Clement V (1305–1314).** Bertrand de Got, archbishop of Bordeaux, was elected at Perugia on 5 June 1305 after an eleven-month deadlock. He was crowned at Lyon, never went to Rome, and took up residence at Avignon in March 1309. He was under heavy pressure from Philip IV, who was demanding a posthumous trial of Boniface VIII.
2. **The Templars.** Philip had every Templar in France arrested on 13 October 1307, and confessions to heresy and obscenity were extracted under torture. Clement first protested, then took over the process. His cardinals heard the leaders at Chinon in August 1308. A parchment recording that hearing, rediscovered in the Vatican archives in 2001, appears to record their absolution, though its weight is argued. At the Council of Vienne (1311–12), most of the council's commission held that the order should be allowed a defence and that the evidence would not support a condemnation. With Philip at Vienne pressing him, Clement suppressed it anyway with the bull [*Vox in excelso*](../reference.md#vox-in-excelso) (announced 22 March 1312). He did so "by apostolic provision," not by judicial sentence, and he gave its property to the Hospitallers. The grand master, Jacques de Molay, retracted his confession and was burned at Paris in March 1314 on the king's order.
3. **The Avignon government.** John XXII (1316–34) and his successors centralized appointments. They reserved ever more benefices to the pope and collected annates (a first year's revenue) and other dues through the Apostolic Camera. Clement VI bought Avignon outright in 1348. Urban V went back to Rome in 1367 but returned in 1370.
4. **The return.** Catherine of Siena went to Avignon in June 1376 to urge Gregory XI to go to Rome. He entered Rome on 17 January 1377 and died there on 27 March 1378.
5. **The [disputed election of 1378](../reference.md#disputed-election-of-1378).** Sixteen cardinals, most of them French, entered conclave on 7 April with a Roman crowd outside demanding a Roman or at least an Italian pope. On 8 April they elected Bartolomeo Prignano, archbishop of Bari, as Urban VI. When the crowd broke in, they briefly presented an elderly Roman cardinal as pope to calm it. In the following weeks they enthroned and crowned Urban, asked him for favours, and announced his election to the princes. Urban turned out harsh and abusive toward them. By summer they had withdrawn to Anagni, and in August they declared the election void as made under fear. On 20 September at Fondi they elected Robert of Geneva as Clement VII, who was in Avignon by 1379.
6. **Two obediences** (figure). Deadlock followed for thirty years, with rival popes succeeding their own: Boniface IX, Innocent VII and Gregory XII at Rome, and Benedict XIII (Pedro de Luna) at Avignon.
7. **Pisa (1409).** Cardinals from both sides called a council, which opened on 25 March. It deposed both popes on 5 June and elected Alexander V on 26 June. Neither deposed pope yielded, so there were now three. John XXIII (Baldassare Cossa) succeeded Alexander in 1410.
8. **[Constance](../reference.md#council-of-constance) (1414–18).** John XXIII called the council under pressure from King Sigismund, and it voted by "nations." John promised to resign, then fled on 20 March 1415. On 6 April the council issued [*Haec Sancta*](../reference.md#haec-sancta). It deposed John on 29 May. On 4 July Gregory XII had his envoy formally reconvoke the council and then resigned. Benedict was deposed on 26 July 1417. [*Frequens*](../reference.md#frequens) (9 October 1417) required councils after five years, then seven, then every ten. An electoral body of the cardinals plus thirty deputies of the nations chose Oddo Colonna as Martin V on 11 November 1417.
9. **Hus.** Jan Hus came to Constance under a safe-conduct from Sigismund. He was arrested on 28 November 1414, before Sigismund arrived, and condemned and burned on 6 July 1415. At its session of 23 September 1415 the council decreed that safe-conducts from princes do not stop a competent judge from trying heresy.
10. **Basel and Florence.** Martin V convoked Basel under *Frequens* in 1431, and it quarrelled with Eugenius IV. Eugenius moved the council to Ferrara (1438) and then [Florence](../reference.md#council-of-florence), where union with the Greeks was proclaimed on 6 July 1439 (*Laetentur Caeli*; its fate is in [4.2](04-02-east-and-west-the-long-estrangement.md)). The rump at Basel deposed Eugenius and elected Felix V (1439). He abdicated in 1449, and the rump dissolved.

Read the boxes as a map of alliances, not of theology. The Hundred Years' War runs straight through the figure: England was Roman, so Scotland, England's enemy, was Avignonese. Rulers chose, and their clergy and people followed. The university theologians of the time mostly sided with their own country.

## Primacy: what this shows, what it doesn't

**The evidence.** For forty years no party proposed doing without a pope, and every expedient aimed at producing one undoubted pope. Constance ended the schism by a method that preserved the Roman line: Gregory XII reconvoked the council before resigning. **What it doesn't settle.** Whether that reconvocation was the legal hinge, or a courtesy to a pope who by then had few followers, is exactly what is disputed. Conciliarists, and later Orthodox and Protestant readers, see in Constance proof that the Church, gathered in council, stood above its head when the head failed. Catholic historians in the ultramontane line read it as an emergency tribunal filling a doubtful vacancy. The theory is in [`history-of-political-thought` 2.5](../../history-of-political-thought/lessons/02-05-marsilius-and-conciliarism.md), and what the record proves about primacy is argued in [`ecclesiology-and-mariology`](../../ecclesiology-and-mariology/syllabus.md) 4.2.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean): how Constance got one pope out of three.** Each claimant was removed by a different route, and the council was careful about which route it used for whom. John XXIII had convoked the council and then fled from it, so the council judged him by its own authority, citing *Haec Sancta*, and deposed him. John submitted. Gregory XII was allowed to reconvoke the council in his own name before his envoy resigned for him, which gave the Roman line a dignified exit and, on the Roman reading, the lawful one. Benedict XIII refused everything. Once the Spanish kingdoms abandoned him (Treaty of Narbonne, December 1415), he was tried and deposed. Only then did the council elect, through a hybrid body built to bind every former obedience. The method was *remove every doubt, then elect*.

**Example 2 (hard): which pope was the real one?** For contemporaries, the 1378 election was a question of fact with the witnesses split: the same cardinals testified first one way and then the other. The modern official list counts the Roman line (Urban VI to Gregory XII) and lists the Avignon and Pisan claimants as antipopes. The evidence strains in two places. First, the list's own history: the *Annuario Pontificio* still printed Alexander V and John XXIII as popes in the early 1940s. When Angelo Roncalli took the name John XXIII in 1958, that settled Cossa's status in practice. Yet the later Alexander, elected in 1492, had numbered himself VI, counting the Pisan pope. Second, the verdict rests on a judgement about fear, which no document settles. Noël Valois, author of a four-volume study of France and the schism, at first judged the question beyond history's power to decide and later inclined to the Roman side. So the list records a settled tradition built on a strong but not conclusive historical case. Treat it that way, not as proof of the facts of April 1378.

## Watch out

- **Avignon was not France, and the popes were not simply the king's men.** The city belonged to Provence until Clement VI bought it. Clement V's conduct over the Templars is the strongest evidence of French pressure; whether it typifies the whole period is the question Boss 5 leaves to you.
- **Don't call the Chinon parchment an acquittal of the order.** It records the absolution of named leaders who confessed and asked pardon. It did not stop the suppression of 1312.
- **Don't assign *Haec Sancta* a doctrinal level.** Its standing is disputed: a standing rule, an emergency measure, or not validly enacted because only John XXIII's obedience was present in April 1415. Say that it is disputed and point to [`fundamental-theology` 4.5](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-05-reading-a-documents-weight.md) and `ecclesiology-and-mariology`.
- **[Conciliarism](../reference.md#conciliarism) was not anti-papal.** Constance wanted a pope, elected one, and burned Hus.

## One-liner

> The papacy that claimed every soul went to Avignon, split in two, then in three, and was put back together by a council that left behind the question of who was above whom.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢) *(Exegetical.)*** *Haec Sancta* (6 April 1415), tr. J. H. Robinson, *Translations and Reprints*, vol. III no. 6:

> It first declares that this same council, legitimately assembled in the Holy Ghost, forming a general council and representing the Catholic Church militant, has its power immediately from Christ, and every one, whatever his state or position, even if it be the Papal dignity itself, is bound to obey it in all those things which pertain to the faith and the healing of the said schism, and to the general reformation of the Church of God, in head and members. It further declares that any one … even if it be the Papal, who shall contumaciously refuse to obey the mandates … issued by this holy council, or by any other general council, legitimately summoned, which concern … the above mentioned objects, shall … be suitably punished.

An invented museum placard reads:

> "In 1415 the Council of Constance declared that a council stands above the pope in every matter of Church life. The decree was an emergency measure for that crisis alone and said nothing of future councils. Martin V confirmed it, and it has been binding Catholic dogma ever since."

Identify three claims in the placard that the passage or the lesson does not support, and give the words or facts that show it. 150 words or fewer.

**P2 (🟡) *(Exegetical.)*** In 1378 the Urbanist case said the election of 8 April was valid. The Clementist case, made by the cardinals at Anagni and Fondi, said it was void as made under fear. (a) Name the single premise about the cardinals' conduct between 8 April and their departure from Rome that each side must hold. (b) Name one kind of evidence that would move a neutral historian either way. Two sentences per part.

**P3 (🔴) *(Evaluative.)*** Test the popular claim "Hus was lured to Constance by a promise of safety and then burned in breach of it." Say what in it the record supports, what it blurs, and reach a verdict. 150 words or fewer.

<details>
<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical.)*

**Must hit, strict:** any three of: (1) **"every matter"**: the text limits the duty of obedience to matters of "the faith and the healing of the said schism, and … the general reformation of the Church … in head and members." (2) **"said nothing of future councils"**: the second clause extends the penalty to disobedience toward "any other general council, legitimately summoned." Whether the council meant a *standing* rule is the disputed question, but the placard's statement about the text is false. (3) **"binding Catholic dogma"**: the decree's standing is disputed, and assigning it a doctrinal level is an overstatement (fundamental-theology's ladder; ecclesiology-and-mariology 4.2). (4) **"Martin V confirmed it"**: stated flatly, this goes beyond the record. His approval of what the council did "in a conciliar way" is ambiguous and is itself part of the dispute. "Emergency measure" alone is one of the rival readings, so it counts as an error only if the answer says the placard presents it as settled.
**Wrong turns:** treating the placard's "emergency measure" as plainly true or plainly false; saying the text claims authority over the pope in nothing at all.
**Model answer:** First, the text gives the council authority only in matters of faith, ending the schism, and general reform "in head and members," not every matter. Second, the decree does speak beyond Constance: it threatens anyone who disobeys "any other general council, legitimately summoned." Whether that was meant as a lasting rule is disputed, but the text names future councils. Third, "binding dogma ever since" assigns a doctrinal level the record does not support. The decree's standing is disputed: it may have been a standing rule, an emergency act, or invalidly enacted by one obedience. Martin V's approval of what was done "conciliariter" does not clearly cover it.

---

**P2** *(Exegetical.)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** Urbanist: the cardinals' acts after the vote were free, so the repeated vote, the enthronement, the coronation of 18 April, the petitions for favours and the letters to princes ratified the election even if the vote itself was pressured. Clementist: the fear lasted as long as they were in Rome, so none of those acts was free, and only once outside the city (Anagni) could they speak freely.
**Must hit, strict (b):** evidence of what cardinals did or wrote when they were beyond the crowd's reach. Examples: private letters, and whether the cardinals who left Rome in early summer went on treating Urban as pope before the August declaration. The six cardinals at Avignon, who recognized Urban on their colleagues' report, show how the election was first described. Also evidence about timing and motive: did the reversal follow Urban's abuse of the cardinals rather than any new danger?
**Wrong turns:** making Urban's harsh character the crux (a valid pope does not become invalid by being unpleasant, and both sides knew it); arguing only about the noise on 7–8 April while ignoring the weeks after.
**Model answer:** (a) Urbanists must hold that the cardinals were free after the vote, so months of acknowledgement ratified it. Clementists must hold that fear persisted until they left Rome, so none of it counts. (b) Letters and acts from cardinals once outside the crowd's reach, for instance at Anagni before August, would test the fear claim. So would evidence that the reversal tracked Urban's conduct rather than any threat.

---

**P3** *(Evaluative.)*

**Must hit, any verdict:** separate the **arrest** (28 November 1414, before Sigismund arrived, which Bohemians at once called a breach) from the **execution** (6 July 1415, after trial). Say who granted the safe-conduct (Sigismund, a secular ruler) and who judged (the council). State the rival reading of what the safe-conduct covered: a guarantee of travel against unlawful violence, or protection from judgment. Note that Sigismund acquiesced by January 1415 and that the council's decree denying that safe-conducts bar heresy trials came *after* the burning (23 September 1415). A verdict either way passes if these moves are made. Mentioning John Paul II's regret (1999) is acceptable only after the facts, not in place of them.
**Wrong turns:** treating "lured" as established intent (the record shows Hus came willingly, hoping to persuade the council); saying the pope issued the safe-conduct; citing the September 1415 decree as the ground on which Hus was tried.
**Model answer (one of several):** The core is true. Hus travelled under Sigismund's safe-conduct, was imprisoned within weeks, and was burned. But the claim blurs two acts. The arrest in November 1414 came before Sigismund reached Constance, and Bohemians protested it at once as a breach. The execution followed a heresy trial, and defenders held the safe-conduct guaranteed lawful passage, not immunity from a church court. That the council formally stated this only in September 1415, after the burning, weakens the defence. "Lured" also overstates: Hus came willingly, expecting to argue his case. Verdict: a breach of the safe-conduct's evident purpose, which contemporaries themselves recognized, but not a premeditated trap.

## Flashback

**From Lesson [5.2](05-02-friars-and-universities.md) (Friars and universities):** *(Exegetical.)* In each of these acts a pope overruled someone: *Quo elongati* (1230), *Parens scientiarum* (1231), *Quasi lignum vitae* (1255), *Ad conditorem canonum* (1322). (a) For each, name who was overruled and, in a phrase, on what. (b) Two of the four overrode an earlier pope's arrangement. Name them and the earlier arrangement each set aside, then say in one sentence what the pair shows about papal privilege. One or two sentences per act in (a); three sentences for (b).

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):** *Quo elongati* (Gregory IX): overruled **Francis's *Testament***, declared not binding on the order, and let friars use a go-between (*nuntius*) to handle money. *Parens scientiarum* (Gregory IX): overruled the **bishop's chancellor** and backed the masters against the town and crown after the 1229 strike. The chancellor had to license qualified candidates, and the masters could make statutes and suspend lectures. *Quasi lignum vitae* (Alexander IV): overruled the **secular masters' guild** and ordered the friars readmitted to the Paris corporation. *Ad conditorem canonum* (John XXII): overruled the **Franciscan order's legal arrangement** by renouncing papal ownership of its goods.
**Must hit, strict (b):** *Quasi lignum vitae* set aside **Innocent IV's curbs** on the friars' pastoral privileges (late 1254). *Ad conditorem* set aside **Nicholas III's *Exiit qui seminat*** (1279) arrangement, under which the Holy See owned the friars' goods so that they had use without ownership. What the pair shows: a privilege that rested on a pope's grant could be withdrawn or reversed by the next pope, so both the friars' place in the university and their form of poverty depended on papal favour.
**Wrong turns:** saying *Quo elongati* overruled a pope (it overruled the founder); treating *Parens scientiarum* as founding the university; naming *Cum inter nonnullos* (1323) as the act that ended papal ownership (it condemned a claim about Christ and the apostles; *Ad conditorem* renounced the ownership).
**Model answer:** (a) *Quo elongati* overruled Francis, declaring his *Testament* not binding and allowing a *nuntius* to handle money. *Parens scientiarum* overruled the bishop's chancellor, who now had to license qualified masters, and confirmed the guild's right to strike and make statutes. *Quasi lignum vitae* overruled the secular masters and forced them to readmit the friars. *Ad conditorem* overruled the order's own arrangement by giving up papal ownership of Franciscan goods. (b) *Quasi lignum vitae* reversed Innocent IV's curbs of 1254, and *Ad conditorem* dismantled *Exiit*'s papal ownership of 1279. Both show that what one pope granted, another could take back.

</details>
