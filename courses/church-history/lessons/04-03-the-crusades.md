# Church History · Lesson 4.3: The crusades

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 4: Reform, schism, and crusade · Builds on: [4.1 The reform papacy and the investiture contest](04-01-the-reform-papacy-and-the-investiture-contest.md), [4.2 East and West: the long estrangement](04-02-east-and-west-the-long-estrangement.md) · Unlocks: [5.1 Papal monarchy: Innocent III to Boniface VIII](05-01-papal-monarchy-innocent-iii-to-boniface-viii.md), [5.3 Heresy, inquisition, and the Jews](05-03-heresy-inquisition-and-the-jews.md)

## Why this matters

In November 1095 a reform pope asked the knights of France to march two thousand miles and take Jerusalem, and promised them that the journey would count as penance. Four years later they took it and killed a large part of its population. On the way, some of the bands that answered the call destroyed the Jewish communities of the Rhineland. The crusades are where the reform papacy of [4.1](04-01-the-reform-papacy-and-the-investiture-contest.md) turned its moral authority into an army, and they are still argued over in op-eds, sermons and speeches. Most of what is said there depends on three questions: what the Church actually offered, why men went, and how to read the sources for what they did.

## The idea

A crusade was an **armed pilgrimage**. The participants spoke of the "journey" or the "pilgrimage"; the word "crusade" came into use only later. A man took a vow and sewed a cloth cross onto his clothes. In return the Church gave him a spiritual reward and legal protection for his family and property while he was gone. Two things were new in 1095. A pope, not a king, summoned the army. And fighting was offered as an act of *penance*, work that makes satisfaction for sin, instead of something that needed penance afterwards.

That combination explains both the enthusiasm and the horror. A penitential war could draw tens of thousands of people who were not professional soldiers and who had often sold land to go. A war fought as satisfaction for sin, against "the enemies of Christ", could also be turned by some of those people against the nearest non-Christians, who were Jews at home.

## Source

Canon 2 of the Council of Clermont (1095). No official record of the council survives. The canon comes down in the notes of participants, among them Bishop Lambert of Arras. Latin (public domain), then the lesson's own translation:

> *Quicumque pro sola devotione, non pro honoris vel pecunie adeptione, ad liberandam ecclesiam Dei Hierusalem profectus fuerit, iter illud pro omni penitentia ei reputetur.*
>
> Whoever, out of devotion alone and not to gain honour or money, sets out for Jerusalem to liberate the Church of God, let that journey be counted to him in place of all penance.

Read it with the [four questions for a source](../reference.md#four-questions-for-a-source). It is a disciplinary canon of a regional council, not a doctrinal definition. Three features decide what it offers. The reward attaches to the *journey*, not to killing. It is **conditional on intention**: devotion alone, not honour or money. And what it remits is *penance*, the satisfaction a confessor imposed for sins already confessed. It is not a promise of heaven. This is the [crusade indulgence](../reference.md#crusade-indulgence) in its earliest surviving form. Its later theology belongs to [`sacraments-and-liturgy`](../../sacraments-and-liturgy/syllabus.md), and the treasury of merit behind it to [`theology-of-debt`](../../theology-of-debt/syllabus.md).

## The record

1. **The appeal (March 1095).** Envoys of the Byzantine emperor Alexios I Komnenos asked Urban II's council at Piacenza for help against the Seljuk Turks. *In words:* the trigger was a request from the East, four decades after the Humbert–Cerularius quarrel of 1054 ([4.2](04-02-east-and-west-the-long-estrangement.md)). Alexios probably had troops he could command in mind, not a mass pilgrimage.
2. **Clermont (27 November 1095).** Urban preached the expedition at the close of the council. Every surviving version of the speech, by Fulcher of Chartres, Robert the Monk and others, was written *after* Jerusalem fell in 1099. *In words:* we have what writers who knew the outcome thought Urban should have said, not a transcript.
3. **The Rhineland (May–June 1096).** Crusading bands and townsmen attacked Jewish communities before the main armies left. At Speyer (3 May) about a dozen were killed. Bishop John sheltered the rest and punished some of the attackers. At Worms (18 and 25 May) the Hebrew chronicles count about 800 dead. At Mainz (27 May), bands under Count Emicho broke into Archbishop Ruthard's palace, where the Jews had taken refuge. The Hebrew chronicle counts about 1,100 dead, and Albert of Aachen about 700. Many Jews killed their own families rather than be forcibly baptized. The [Rhineland massacres](../reference.md#rhineland-massacres) are known from three Hebrew chronicles and from Christian writers. *In words:* the pope did not call for it, and several bishops tried to stop it. But it was done by crusaders, in the language of the crusade. Estimates of the total start at about two thousand, and the figures cannot be checked closely.
4. **Jerusalem (15 July 1099).** After a three-year march by way of Constantinople and Antioch, the armies stormed the city and killed much of its Muslim and Jewish population (Example 2). Four crusader states were set up between 1098 and 1109.
5. **The military orders.** The Hospitallers received papal protection in 1113. The Templars were founded about 1119–1120 and given a rule at the Council of Troyes in 1129. The [military orders](../reference.md#military-orders) were professed religious who fought. *In words:* the permanent garrison of the Latin East was monastic.
6. **Hattin (4 July 1187).** Saladin destroyed the army of the kingdom of Jerusalem, and the city surrendered on 2 October. Residents who could pay a ransom left; many who could not were enslaved. The Third Crusade (1189–1192) regained the coast but not Jerusalem.
7. **Diversion and end.** In 1204 the Fourth Crusade sacked Constantinople ([4.2](04-02-east-and-west-the-long-estrangement.md)). From 1209 crusades were also declared against heretics in the West ([5.3](05-03-heresy-inquisition-and-the-jews.md)). Acre, the last major Latin city, fell to the Mamluks on 18 May 1291.

**Why they went.** The [crusader motives debate](../reference.md#crusader-motives-debate) has three positions. An older **materialist** reading, associated with Georges Duby's essay on aristocratic "youth" (1964; in English in *The Chivalrous Society*, 1977), saw landless younger sons looking for estates and plunder. **Jonathan Riley-Smith** (*The First Crusaders, 1095–1131*, 1997) went through the charters in which departing knights sold or pledged land. He found that crusading was ruinously expensive, several years' income for a knight. It ran in families, and it drew heads of households as well as younger sons. Its motive, he argued, was penitential piety. **Christopher Tyerman** (*The Invention of the Crusades*, 1998; *God's War*, 2006) is more skeptical of any single motive. He stresses lordship, kinship, money and social pressure alongside devotion, and argues that the "crusade" as a defined institution took shape gradually after 1095 rather than all at once at Clermont.

**Afterwards.** On the [Day of Pardon](../reference.md#day-of-pardon) (12 March 2000) John Paul II led confessions of sins "committed in the service of truth" and of sins against the people of Israel. The crusades were not named. In Athens on 4 May 2001 he named the sack of 1204 and expressed Catholics' "deep regret" that Latin Christians had done it.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean: what the canon promised, and what the chroniclers heard).** Fulcher of Chartres, who went on the crusade and began writing soon after 1100, has Urban promise that "all who die by the way, whether by land or by sea, or in battle against the pagans, shall have immediate remission of sins" (Thatcher's translation, 1905). Set it beside the canon. The canon remits *penance* for a *journey* made with pure intention. Fulcher's Urban remits *sins* for those who *die*, and he says nothing about intention. Run the four questions. Fulcher wrote after the victory, for readers who had lost men, and the promise to the dead reads like consolation. The canon is the council's own legislation. The clean verdict is that the canon is the better evidence for what Urban offered. Fulcher is the better evidence for what crusaders came to believe they had been offered. Both are real, and the gap between them is itself a historical fact.

**Example 2 (hard: counting the dead in Jerusalem).** Here the sources strain. The *Gesta Francorum* says the killing in the Temple area was so great that the crusaders waded in blood to their ankles. Raymond of Aguilers, a chaplain in the army, says men "rode in blood up to their knees and bridle reins", and calls it God's just judgment (Krey's translation, 1921). Fulcher reports almost ten thousand killed in the Temple alone. Ibn al-Athir, writing in the early thirteenth century, gives seventy thousand.

Now apply source criticism. Raymond's image echoes Revelation 14:20, where blood flows from the winepress of God's wrath "up to the horses' bridles". It is a biblical way of saying *judgment*, not a depth measurement. Seventy thousand is probably more than the city's whole population, and the figure was written more than a century later. Benjamin Kedar's study of the massacre in Western historiography (*Crusades* 3, 2004) brought in a different witness: Abu Bakr ibn al-Arabi, a scholar from al-Andalus who had lived in Jerusalem shortly before the siege. Ibn al-Arabi reports about three thousand killed in the al-Aqsa mosque. Kedar also argued the other way. The sources stress killing and say little about captives held for ransom or sold, so the massacre was more thorough than was usual when a medieval town was stormed.

So the evidence cuts both ways. The large numbers are inflated, and the massacre was real and exceptional. A correction that reduces seventy thousand to a few thousand is not a defence of what happened.

## Watch out

- **The indulgence was not "salvation for killing."** The canon attaches remission of penance to the journey, conditional on intention. The opposite overcorrection is just as wrong: preachers and chroniclers did promise more, including remission of sins and a martyr's death, and many crusaders believed them (Example 1).
- **The Rhineland massacres were neither papal policy nor "just a mob."** No papal text ordered them, and bishops at Speyer, Worms, Mainz and Cologne tried to shelter Jews. But the attackers were crusaders, and the protection often failed. The Hebrew chronicle says Ruthard's men fled first and that he himself fled.
- **Don't let the acknowledgement stand in for the facts.** The Day of Pardon confessed sins in general categories. The crusades are a case historians place under those categories, but the text does not name them.

## One-liner

> The Church offered a penitential journey with conditions attached. Men heard a promise of salvation and marched on Jerusalem, and some of them killed Jews in Mainz along the way; each part of that has its own evidence and needs its own reading.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢) *(Exegetical.)*** Albert of Aachen on Mainz, 1096 (Krey's translation, 1921). Albert wrote in Aachen from returned crusaders' reports and never went east.

> "The Jews of this city, knowing of the slaughter of their brethren, and that they themselves could not escape the hands of so many, fled in hope of safety to Bishop Rothard. They put an infinite treasure in his guard and trust … He placed the Jews in the very spacious hall of his own house, away from the sight of Count Emico and his followers … [Later:] So the hand of the Lord is believed to have been against the pilgrim who had sinned by excessive impurity and fornication, and who had slaughtered the exiled Jews through greed of money, rather than for the sake of God's justice, although the Jews were opposed to Christ. The Lord is a just judge and orders no one unwillingly, or under compulsion, to come under the yoke of the Catholic faith."

(a) On what grounds does Albert condemn the killers? Name the words that show each ground. (b) Name one thing the passage cannot establish about the archbishop, and the source in the lesson that complicates it. Two sentences per part.

**P2 (🟡) *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)*** A popular claim: "The crusaders were promised salvation for killing Muslims." (a) Using canon 2 of Clermont (the Source), state three ways the canon's terms differ from the claim. (b) Say how much of the claim survives once Fulcher's version (Example 1) is taken into account, and what kind of evidence it survives *as*. 150 words or fewer for (b).

**P3 (🔴, optional) *(Exegetical.)*** An invented documentary voice-over:

> "Eyewitnesses agree: when the crusaders took Jerusalem, the blood ran ankle-deep, and Muslim chroniclers of the day counted seventy thousand dead. Modern historians have shown those numbers were wildly exaggerated. The massacre, in other words, was a medieval legend."

Identify three distinct errors in how the voice-over handles its sources, and name the lesson's evidence against each. 150 words or fewer.

<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical.)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** two grounds. **Motive:** they killed "through greed of money, rather than for the sake of God's justice." **Coercion:** God "orders no one unwillingly, or under compulsion, to come under the yoke of the Catholic faith." This condemns forced conversion, the choice of baptism or death. Full credit also notes the limit on Albert's sympathy: he condemns the crusaders *although the Jews were opposed to Christ*. His objection is to greed and compulsion, not to the view of Jews as enemies of the faith. Mentioning "impurity and fornication" as part of the charge is acceptable.
**Must hit, strict (b):** the passage cannot establish that the archbishop's protection was sincere or effective. Albert reports only that he took the treasure and hid the Jews, and he was writing at a distance from reports. The Hebrew chronicle complicates it: the archbishop's men fled first, Ruthard fled too, and he was later accused of taking plunder. An answer that says instead that the passage cannot confirm the death toll (Albert's roughly 700 against the Hebrew chronicle's 1,100) earns partial credit, because that is about the victims, not the archbishop.
**Wrong turns:** reading Albert as a modern advocate of tolerance; treating "that excellent Bishop" as proof that the protection worked.
**Model answer:** (a) Albert condemns the killers for greed, since they acted "through greed of money, rather than for the sake of God's justice", and for compulsion, since God forces no one "unwillingly, or under compulsion" into the faith. He does this while still calling the Jews "opposed to Christ", so he objects to their motive and to forced baptism, not to the hostility itself. (b) It cannot show that Ruthard's protection was sincere or that it held, since Albert only reports the deposit and the hiding place. The Hebrew chronicle says the archbishop's men fled first and that he fled as well.

---

**P2** *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** any three of these: the reward attaches to the **journey** to Jerusalem, not to killing; it is **conditional on intention** ("devotion alone", not honour or money); it remits **penance**, the satisfaction for confessed sins, not sin as such and not a promise of salvation; it is aimed at **liberating the Church of God**, not at killing Muslims as such.
**Must hit, any verdict (b):** state what Fulcher's version adds: remission of *sins* for those who *die* on the way or in battle, with no condition of intention. Place it as a text written after 1099 by a participant. Then say what the claim survives as. Either side has to make this distinction: it is weak evidence of Urban's legislation and good evidence of what crusaders believed. A verdict either way passes if both halves are addressed.
**Wrong turns:** declaring the claim simply false, which ignores Fulcher and what the crusaders believed; declaring it simply true on Fulcher's word, which treats a post-1099 account as the council's text; comparing Fulcher with Robert the Monk (not asked).
**Model answer (b), one of several:** Some of it survives, but as a fact about belief, not law. Fulcher, writing after 1099, has Urban grant "immediate remission of sins" to those who die on the way or in battle, and he drops the condition of pure intention. That is weak evidence for what the council enacted, which is better known from the canon. It is good evidence that crusaders and their families understood death on crusade as saving. So "promised salvation" is too strong for the Church's legislation, and roughly right for what crusaders thought they had been promised. "For killing" fails on both readings, since even Fulcher ties the reward to dying on the journey, not to killing.

---

**P3** *(Exegetical.)*

**Must hit, strict:** three errors, each with its evidence. (i) **"Eyewitnesses agree … ankle-deep" treats a trope as a measurement.** The witnesses do not agree: the *Gesta* says ankles and Raymond says knees and bridle reins, which echoes Revelation 14:20. The image signals judgment, not depth. (ii) **"Chroniclers of the day counted seventy thousand"** is wrong on date. Ibn al-Athir wrote more than a century later. The witness closest in time that Kedar brought forward, Ibn al-Arabi, gives about three thousand in al-Aqsa. (iii) **"Therefore a legend"** does not follow. Deflating the numbers does not remove the event. Kedar argues the massacre was more thorough than usual, with little evidence of captives taken, and every Latin eyewitness reports mass killing.
**Wrong turns:** attacking only the conclusion and missing the two source errors; replacing seventy thousand with a new precise total; reading Raymond's "just judgment" as an exaggeration of the numbers rather than a theological framing.
**Model answer:** First, the eyewitnesses do not agree on "ankle-deep". The *Gesta* says ankles, but Raymond says knees and bridle reins, echoing Revelation 14:20. That is a biblical image of judgment, not a measurement. Second, seventy thousand does not come from a chronicler "of the day". It comes from Ibn al-Athir, writing over a century later, while Ibn al-Arabi, who had lived in the city shortly before, gives about three thousand in al-Aqsa. Third, showing that the numbers are inflated does not make the massacre a legend. The Latin eyewitnesses themselves describe mass killing, and Kedar argues the slaughter was unusually thorough for a stormed town.

## Flashback

**From Lesson [4.1](04-01-the-reform-papacy-and-the-investiture-contest.md) (The reform papacy and the investiture contest):** *(Exegetical.)* Henry IV's letter to Gregory VII from the assembly at Worms, 24 January 1076 (trans. E. F. Henderson, *Select Historical Documents of the Middle Ages*, 1910). It is addressed to "Hildebrand, at present not pope but false monk." Henry describes himself as one:

> "who—as the tradition of the holy Fathers teaches, declaring that I am not to be deposed for any crime unless, which God forbid, I should have strayed from the faith—am subject to the judgment of God alone."

(a) What does Henry claim about who may judge him, and what one exception does he allow? Quote the words for each. (b) "Not pope": what fact about Gregory's accession in 1073 gave this charge a footing, and when did an assembly of Henry's party formally act on it? (c) Was the quarrel of 1075–76, as the lesson reconstructs it, a case that fits Henry's exception? Name the grievances, and say what Gregory's sentence of February 1076 did. Two sentences per part.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):** the claim: a king is "subject to the judgment of God alone," so no earthly judge, the pope included, may depose him. The exception: "unless … I should have strayed from the faith," that is, heresy.
**Must hit, strict (b):** Gregory was acclaimed pope by the Roman crowd on 22 April 1073, which was hard to square with the 1059 election decree giving the choice to the cardinal bishops. The synod of Brixen (June 1080) declared his election invalid and chose Wibert of Ravenna as "Clement III."
**Must hit, strict (c):** no. The grievances were Henry's appointments at Milan, Fermo and Spoleto and his keeping of excommunicated councillors: disobedience, not straying from the faith. Gregory's synod nonetheless excommunicated Henry, withdrew from him the rule of Germany and Italy, and absolved his subjects from their oaths, claiming a power to judge a king that Henry's formula denied.
**Wrong turns:** reading the exception as a concession that the pope may judge kings in general (it is confined to heresy, and the passage does not say who judges that); dating Brixen to 1076 (Worms renounced Gregory in 1076; Brixen chose an antipope in 1080); answering (c) with a general ban on lay investiture broken by Henry, when no such ban is securely documented before November 1078.
**Model answer:** (a) Henry says he is "subject to the judgment of God alone," so no pope may depose him. His one exception is if he "should have strayed from the faith." (b) Gregory had been acclaimed by the Roman crowd in 1073, not chosen by the cardinal bishops as the 1059 decree required. The synod of Brixen acted on this in 1080, declaring his election invalid and choosing Wibert of Ravenna as Clement III. (c) No: the quarrel was over Milan, Fermo, Spoleto and excommunicated advisers, which is disobedience, not heresy. Gregory excommunicated Henry, took away his rule and freed his subjects from their oaths anyway, so the two men disagreed about the grounds on which a king could be judged at all.

</details>
