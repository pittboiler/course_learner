# Church History · Lesson 7.1: Conquest and conscience in the Americas

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 7: A Church in new worlds · Builds on: [6.4 Trent and Catholic reform](06-04-trent-and-catholic-reform.md), [6.5 The Spanish and Roman Inquisitions](06-05-the-spanish-and-roman-inquisitions.md) · Unlocks: [7.2 Accommodation in Asia](07-02-accommodation-in-asia.md)

## Why this matters

Within a century of 1492 the native population of the Americas fell, on most estimates, by something like nine-tenths. The Church was part of the conquest that brought this about: a pope's bull gave Castile its title, clergy held forced native labourers, and religious orders later owned African slaves. From the same Church came the conquest's sharpest critics. Friars refused absolution to colonists, a pope declared the Indians free, and a former colonist turned Dominican argued before the emperor's council that the wars were unjust. Popular accounts keep one half. A historian holds both, and explains how one institution produced them.

## The idea

Two facts explain most of what follows.

**First, the Church in the Indies was the crown's Church.** Through the ***[patronato real](../reference.md#patronato-real)*** (royal patronage), Rome granted the Castilian monarchs the tithes of the Indies (1501) and the right to nominate every bishop and benefice (Julius II, *Universalis ecclesiae regimini*, 1508). No church could be built without royal consent, and the crown claimed that papal letters for the Indies needed its approval before they could circulate. The price was that the king paid for, and was answerable for, the mission. So the fight over native peoples was fought mainly *inside Spain*, in the king's councils, rather than between Rome and Madrid.

**Second, the critics' lever was the royal conscience.** Castile's title to the Indies rested on a papal grant justified by the duty to convert. A friar who showed that conquest and forced labour obstructed conversion, and were mortal sins, was attacking the crown's title, not its manners. So confession became a political weapon, and theologians were summoned to judge wars.

The result was a pattern repeated for fifty years: **denunciation, then law, then evasion.** Friars exposed abuses; the crown legislated; colonists evaded or revolted; the law was softened. The labour institution at the centre of it was the ***[encomienda](../reference.md#encomienda)***: a grant of native people to a Spaniard, who took their tribute and labour and was supposed in return to protect them and have them instructed in the faith. Legally it was not slavery, and in practice on the islands it often worked like it.

## Source

Antonio Montesinos, Dominican, preaching in Santo Domingo on the fourth Sunday of Advent (21 December) 1511. No text survives from him; this is Las Casas's report, written decades later (*Historia de las Indias*, book 3, ch. 4). The lesson's own translation from the Spanish:

> "This voice [says] that you are all in mortal sin, and live and die in it, because of the cruelty and tyranny you use with these innocent peoples. Tell me, by what right and with what justice do you hold these Indians in such cruel and horrible servitude? By what authority have you made such detestable wars on these peoples, who were living in their own lands, gentle and peaceful? ... Are these not men? Do they not have rational souls? Are you not bound to love them as yourselves?"

Read it as a source. The author is not the preacher but a witness (Las Casas was on the island, and still an *encomendero*), writing decades later as the movement's chief advocate, so the words are his reconstruction. What the passage can establish is the *form* of the argument the Dominicans used: a question of **right** ("by what right," "by what authority"), not only of cruelty. Three grounds are stacked: the wars were unjust, the servitude is unjust, and the Indians are fellow humans owed the love of neighbour. That form reappears in every later round.

## What happened

1. **Title (1493–94).** Alexander VI's *[Inter caetera](../reference.md#inter-caetera)* (dated 4 May 1493) granted Castile the lands beyond a line 100 leagues west of the Azores and Cape Verde islands, with the duty of converting their peoples. Castile and Portugal moved the line to 370 leagues by treaty at Tordesillas (7 June 1494). *In words:* the Spanish title rested on a papal grant tied to evangelization, which is why evangelization later became the critics' ground.
2. **Denunciation and first law (1511–13).** Montesinos's sermons (1511) were followed by the Laws of Burgos (27 December 1512). They regulated the encomienda (hours, food, instruction, limits on beating) rather than abolishing it. The ***[requerimiento](../reference.md#requerimiento)***, drafted in 1513 by the jurist Palacios Rubios, was to be read to native peoples before any attack: it demanded that they acknowledge the Church and the pope's grant to the Spanish monarchs, and it declared that any war which followed was their fault. *In words:* the first response to a question of justice was a procedure, and one that made conquest legal.
3. **Las Casas (1514).** A priest and *encomendero* in Cuba, Bartolomé de las Casas (1484–1566) was preparing a sermon in 1514 when he read Ecclesiasticus 34 on offerings made from goods taken unjustly. He gave up his Indians and spent fifty years as their advocate. He became a Dominican (1522–23) and bishop of Chiapas (consecrated 1544).
4. **Rome speaks (1537).** Paul III's ***[Sublimis Deus](../reference.md#sublimis-deus)*** (2 June 1537) declared the Indians "truly men," not to be deprived of liberty or property, nor enslaved. A companion brief, *Pastorale officium*, threatened excommunication for doing so. In 1538, after Charles V protested that it infringed his patronage, Paul III annulled the brief. Whether *Sublimis Deus* fell with it is disputed.
5. **The New Laws (20 November 1542).** They banned new Indian slavery and ordered that encomiendas lapse at the holder's death. In Peru the encomenderos rose under Gonzalo Pizarro, and the viceroy was killed in 1546. In 1545 the crown had already revoked the inheritance clause. *In words:* the most radical law of the century lasted about three years in its central provision.
6. **Valladolid (1550–51).** Charles V ordered conquests suspended in 1550 while a junta of theologians and jurists heard Juan Ginés de Sepúlveda, who argued from Aristotle's natural slavery that war to subject the Indians was just, and Las Casas, who denied every premise. The junta issued **no formal verdict** (see the [Valladolid debate](../reference.md#valladolid-debate)). Las Casas's *Brevísima relación de la destrucción de las Indias*, written in 1542, was printed at Seville in 1552.

Behind all of this ran the **demographic collapse**, and its numbers are among the most disputed in historical demography. Estimates for the whole hemisphere in 1492 run from about 8 million (Kroeber) to over 100 million (Dobyns). Denevan's widely used synthesis (1992) gives roughly 43–65 million. Cook and Borah put central Mexico alone at about 25 million in 1519 and near one million by 1605. Their high starting figure is contested, but the scale of the fall is not. Most deaths came from diseases to which the population had no immunity: smallpox reached Hispaniola in 1518–19 and Mexico in 1520, and later epidemics followed. Disease, though, is not the whole story. Livi-Bacci ("Return to Hispaniola," 2003) argues that the Taíno of Hispaniola were near extinction *before* smallpox arrived. The causes he identifies are forced labour in the gold mines, war, famine, the breakup of households, and falling births.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean: what the Laws of Burgos show).** Test the popular claim "Spain was the first empire to legislate for the rights of conquered peoples." The Laws of Burgos are a royal statute of 1512, answering the Dominicans' complaint. They *can* establish that the crown accepted the Indians' treatment as its legal responsibility, and what it had to forbid is evidence of what was happening. They *cannot* establish that the rules were kept. Montesinos's successors and Las Casas said they were not, and the Taíno population kept falling. The laws also presuppose the encomienda: they regulate forced labour and do not question it. The claim survives in a narrow form: Spain legislated early and seriously, which shows the debate was real, not that protection happened.

**Example 2 (hard: the conscience that stopped at Africa).** The critics' logic was universal: "Are these not men?" Yet it did not reach Africans for decades. In memorials of 1516–18, Las Casas himself proposed that colonists be allowed to import African slaves so that they would release their Indians. A licence for 4,000 was granted and, on his own account, sold on to Genoese merchants. Later, in the *Historia* (book 3, ch. 129), he wrote of himself in the third person (the lesson's translation): he "soon afterwards found himself repentant, judging himself guilty through inadvertence," once he saw that "the captivity of the blacks was as unjust as that of the Indians." His repentance changed little. The Atlantic trade grew, and Church institutions took part in it. Jesuit sugar and wine estates in coastal Peru, for example, ran on enslaved African labour (Cushner, *Lords of the Land*, 1980). The counterpoint is Pedro Claver (1580–1654). At Cartagena, the chief slave port of Spanish America, he met the ships for nearly forty years and signed his final vows (1622) "slave of the Ethiopians forever." Even here the evidence strains: his interpreters were themselves enslaved Africans owned by the Jesuit college. Claver's work fits both readings, conscience and complicity together.

**The legends.** Las Casas's *Brevísima* was translated and reprinted across Protestant Europe, above all in the Netherlands during the revolt against Spain, and de Bry's engravings (1598) made its atrocities into images. Julián Juderías popularized the name *la leyenda negra* ([Black Legend](../reference.md#black-legend)) in 1914 for the anti-Spanish caricature that resulted. The term was used before him (Emilia Pardo Bazán, 1899). Lewis Hanke's *The Spanish Struggle for Justice in the Conquest of America* (1949) answered with the debates themselves: no other empire argued so publicly about whether its conquest was just. Benjamin Keen replied (1969, 1971) that this risked a "White Legend," judging Spain by its theorists instead of its practice. The irony: the Black Legend's key text was written by a Spanish friar, for the Spanish king.

**Only then, the acknowledgement.** In Bolivia in 2015, Francis asked forgiveness for the Church's offences and for crimes against native peoples in the conquest, adding that many clergy had opposed "the logic of the sword." In 2023 a joint note of two Vatican dicasteries repudiated the "doctrine of discovery." It said that *Inter caetera* and the earlier Portuguese bulls were never part of the Church's teaching and had been used by colonial powers to justify immoral acts.

## Watch out

- **"The Church" is not one actor here.** Rome, a crown-nominated bishop, a Dominican convent and a cleric holding an encomienda could take opposite sides in the same year. Name the actor before you assign praise or blame.
- **Law is not practice.** Burgos, *Sublimis Deus* and the New Laws are strong evidence of what was argued and weak evidence of what happened. The 1545 revocation and the 1538 annulment are part of the record too.
- **"Mostly disease" is not "no one's fault."** Disease killed most of the dead, but Hispaniola's collapse began before smallpox, and war, forced labour and famine made epidemics deadlier everywhere. The numbers are uncertain; the direction and scale are not.
- **Don't assign a doctrinal weight.** *Sublimis Deus* says "we define and declare." Don't read the phrase as settling the document's weight: what note it carries is a question for [`fundamental-theology` 4.4](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-04-the-ladder-of-doctrinal-authority.md), not this course.

## One-liner

> In the Americas the Church both licensed the conquest and put it on trial, and the laws that trial produced were written in Spain and broken in the Indies.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢) *(Exegetical.)*** From Paul III's *Sublimis Deus*, in F. A. MacNutt's translation (*Bartholomew de Las Casas*, 1909):

> "... [the devil's agents] have not hesitated to publish abroad that the Indians of the West and the South, and other people of whom We have recent knowledge should be treated as dumb brutes created for our service, pretending that they are incapable of receiving the catholic faith. We ... consider, however, that the Indians are truly men and that they are not only capable of understanding the catholic faith but, according to our information, they desire exceedingly to receive it. ... the said Indians and all other people who may later be discovered by Christians, are by no means to be deprived of their liberty or the possession of their property, even though they be outside the faith of Jesus Christ; ... nor should they be in any way enslaved; should the contrary happen, it shall be null and of no effect."

(a) What claim does the bull reject, and what reason does it give for rejecting it? (b) Does the passage, read strictly, forbid the encomienda? Name the words that decide your answer. (c) The Latin is dated *quarto nonas Junii*, which MacNutt renders "the fourth of June." Give the correct date, with the reckoning. Two sentences per part.

**P2 (🟡) *(Exegetical.)*** An invented museum placard:

> "In 1537 the Pope forbade the enslavement of the Indians, and Spain obeyed: the Laws of Burgos and the New Laws protected native peoples throughout the century. The terrible loss of life was caused by disease, which no one could have prevented. The 'Black Legend' of Spanish cruelty was invented by Protestant enemies of Spain."

Name three claims in the placard that the record in this lesson contradicts or complicates, with the specific evidence for each, and say what the placard gets right. 150 words or fewer.

**P3 (🔴, optional) *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)*** (a) Hanke and Keen agree on most of the facts. Name the crux between them: the premise about how to judge a colonial regime that one accepts and the other denies. (b) Evaluate the claim "In the sixteenth-century Americas, the Church was the conscience of the conquest." 150 words or fewer for (b).

<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical.)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** it rejects the claim that the Indians are "dumb brutes" made for Spanish service and *incapable of receiving the faith*. The reason is that they are "truly men": they can understand the faith and, by the pope's information, want it. (Credit for noting that capacity for the faith is the hinge: the error is framed as an obstacle to preaching.)
**Must hit, strict (b):** not by name. The operative words forbid depriving the Indians of "liberty" and "property" and forbid enslaving them. The encomienda was legally a grant of tribute and labour, not ownership of persons, so the text reaches it only if forced encomienda labour counts as loss of liberty. That is a question of application which the words leave open. "Null and of no effect" makes any enslavement legally void, not merely sinful.
**Must hit, strict (c):** 2 June 1537. The Nones of June fall on the 5th, and Roman dates count inclusively backwards: 5, 4, 3, 2, so "the fourth day before the Nones" is 2 June.
**Wrong turns:** (a) saying the bull rejects conquest as such, which it does not mention. (b) Answering "yes, it abolished the encomienda," which confuses the bull with the New Laws of 1542. (c) Counting exclusively, which gives 1 June, or accepting "4 June."
**Model answer:** (a) It rejects the view that the Indians are like beasts, made to serve and unable to receive the faith, because they are "truly men" capable of the faith and eager for it. (b) Not expressly: it forbids taking their "liberty" and "property" and enslaving them, while the encomienda was legally neither ownership nor slavery. Whether forced encomienda labour violates "liberty" is left to application. (c) 2 June 1537: the Nones of June are the 5th, and counting inclusively four days back gives the 2nd.

---

**P2** *(Exegetical.)*

**Must hit, strict:** any three of the following, each with its evidence.
(i) *"Spain obeyed"*: after Charles V objected, Paul III annulled the enforcing brief *Pastorale officium* in 1538, and under the patronato the crown controlled which papal letters circulated.
(ii) *"The laws protected"*: Burgos regulated the encomienda rather than ending it. The New Laws' inheritance clause was revoked in 1545 after resistance and, in Peru, a revolt.
(iii) *"Disease, which no one could have prevented"*: most deaths were from disease, but Livi-Bacci argues that Hispaniola's Taíno were near extinction before smallpox (1518), with forced labour, war and famine as causes.
(iv) *"Invented by Protestants"*: Protestants spread and exaggerated it, but its central text was the Spanish Dominican Las Casas's *Brevísima*, written for the Spanish king.
What it gets right: *Sublimis Deus* did forbid enslavement; the laws were real and early; disease was the largest killer; Protestant propaganda did exploit the story.
**Wrong turns:** calling the whole placard false (the brief asks what it gets right); counting "disease" as simply wrong rather than incomplete; confusing the 1512 and 1542 laws.
**Model answer:** First, "Spain obeyed": Paul III annulled the enforcing brief in 1538 at Charles V's insistence, and the patronato let the crown screen papal letters. Second, "the laws protected": Burgos regulated forced labour and left it in place, and the New Laws' inheritance clause was withdrawn in 1545 after the Peruvian encomenderos revolted. Third, "no one could have prevented": disease killed most victims, but Hispaniola's population was collapsing before smallpox arrived, from forced labour, war and famine. The placard is right that the bull condemned enslavement and that disease was the largest cause of death. Protestant printers did spread the Black Legend, but its core text came from a Spanish friar.

---

**P3** *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** the crux is **what the character of a colonial regime is to be judged by.** Hanke takes the debates, laws and reformers as telling evidence of what Spanish colonialism was, and as setting it apart from other empires. Keen denies that these outweigh practice and outcomes: he looks at enforcement, labour and mortality, and charges Hanke with a "White Legend." Evidence that would move it: enforcement records (how far the Laws of Burgos and New Laws changed labour and tribute on the ground), and comparisons with empires that did not hold such debates.
**Must hit, any verdict (b):** (1) say which "Church" is meant (friars, bishops, Rome, clergy who held encomiendas, religious orders as slaveholders), since the claim may be true of one and false of another; (2) weigh evidence on both sides, at least one item from each: Montesinos, Las Casas, *Sublimis Deus* and Valladolid for the claim; *Inter caetera* as the title for conquest, the requerimiento, clerical encomenderos, and institutional slaveholding against it; (3) face the Africa problem: a conscience that did not extend to enslaved Africans for decades; (4) state a verdict and what would change it.
**Wrong turns:** treating the 2015 apology or the 2023 note as evidence about the sixteenth century; letting Claver stand for the whole Church, or the encomenderos stand for it; deciding (b) by counting famous names.
**Model answer (b), one of several:** Only if "Church" means its reforming friars. Montesinos, Las Casas and the Dominicans forced the crown to legislate, and Rome's *Sublimis Deus* backed them. But the same Church gave the conquest its title (*Inter caetera*) and its legal ritual (the requerimiento, which demanded submission to the Church). It lived under a patronage that let the crown silence Rome's strongest brief within a year, and its clergy and orders held encomiendas and later African slaves. A conscience is more than a voice in the room. The friars' protest changed law and rarely changed practice, and until late it stopped at Africa: Las Casas himself proposed African slaves before repenting. So "a conscience within the conquest" fits the record better than "the conscience of the conquest." Enforcement studies showing that the New Laws did change labour on the ground would push me toward the stronger claim.

## Flashback

**From Lesson [6.4](06-04-trent-and-catholic-reform.md) (Trent and Catholic reform):** *(Exegetical.)* Diagnose. An invented documentary voice-over:

> "In 1540 the pope launched the Jesuits as the shock troops of the Counter-Reformation, created for one purpose: to stop Luther. Within a generation Jesuits were running Europe's dioceses as the resident bishops Trent demanded. No wonder historians have always called the whole movement simply 'the Counter-Reformation'."

Identify three claims the record contradicts or complicates, and give the correction for each. 100 words or fewer.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict:** any three of the following, each with its correction:
(1) *Purpose.* Paul III approved the Society in 1540 for missions, preaching, the sacraments and works of charity; schools and Germany came later. On O'Malley's reading, fighting Protestantism was incidental to their program until about the mid-1550s and marginal in most of their missions.
(2) *Bishops.* Trent's instruments of reform were bishops and parish priests, and the early Jesuits refused those offices. They served diocesan reform through teaching instead, as in the seminary opened in Borromeo's Milan in 1564, staffed by Jesuits.
(3) *The name.* Historians have not agreed. Jedin (1946) set "Catholic Reform", a self-renewal with late-medieval roots, beside "Counter-Reformation", a self-defence; O'Malley (2000) argued that both labels distort and proposed "early modern Catholicism". Bossy and the confessionalization historians offered further frames.
(4) *"Launched."* The pope approved an order formed by Ignatius of Loyola and his companions; he did not create it.
**Wrong turns:** turning the answer into an argument over whether Trent was a reaction to Protestantism, when the script's claims are about the Jesuits and the label; saying the Jesuits never engaged Protestantism (they turned to Germany later); saying Jedin rejected the term "Counter-Reformation" (he kept it, alongside "Catholic Reform").
**Model answer:** First, the Jesuits were not founded to stop Luther: Paul III approved them in 1540 for missions, preaching, sacraments and charity, and O'Malley finds anti-Protestant work incidental to them until the mid-1550s. Second, they did not become Trent's resident bishops. They refused episcopal and parish office, the very instruments of Trent's reform, and served it through schools and seminaries. Third, historians have not agreed on the name: Jedin paired "Catholic Reform" with "Counter-Reformation" in 1946, and O'Malley proposed "early modern Catholicism" in 2000.
