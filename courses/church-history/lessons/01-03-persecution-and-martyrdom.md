# Church History · Lesson 1.3: Persecution and martyrdom

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 1: The apostolic Church to Constantine · Builds on: [1.1 The first generation and its sources](01-01-the-first-generation-and-its-sources.md), [1.2 Bishops, canon, and the parting of the ways](01-02-bishops-canon-and-the-parting-of-the-ways.md) · Unlocks: [1.4 Constantine](01-04-constantine.md)

## Why this matters

The Church remembers its first three centuries as the age of the martyrs, and that memory shaped everything after: the cult of the saints, the calendar, the churches built over graves, and a lasting habit of reading history as persecution and endurance. The memory is true, but it is not a chronology. Persecution came in bursts, with long quiet stretches between them. The number of dead is far smaller than later legend claimed. And historians still disagree about *why* Rome punished Christians at all. This lesson gives you the dates, the numbers as far as they can be known, and the argument over the cause.

## The idea

Rome had no law against Christianity until the middle of the third century. Christians were exposed to danger all the time, but action against them was rare. Someone had to accuse them before a provincial governor, and the governor then used his broad discretion (*cognitio*, trial at the magistrate's own judgment). A Christian in one city might die while another a few hundred miles away lived untroubled for decades. That is the **local and sporadic** phase, from Nero in 64 to about 250.

Then the state itself took the initiative. Decius (250) ordered everyone to sacrifice, Valerian (257–258) struck at clergy and property, and Diocletian and his colleagues launched the **Great Persecution** (303). That last one ended in the East only with Galerius's deathbed edict (311), and fully only in 313.

Across both phases the test was the same. Would you offer sacrifice to the gods, or for the emperor? Refusal is what killed people. What historians argue about is what Rome thought the refusal *meant*.

## Source

*The Passion of Perpetua and Felicity* 6 (chapter 2.2 in the *ANF* division), trial before the procurator Hilarianus at Carthage, 203 (tr. R. E. Wallis, *Ante-Nicene Fathers* vol. 3, 1885). Perpetua, about twenty-two and nursing an infant, writes this part in the first person:

> And Hilarianus the procurator … said, 'Spare the grey hairs of your father, spare the infancy of your boy, offer sacrifice for the well-being of the emperors.' And I replied, 'I will not do so.' Hilarianus said, 'Are you a Christian?' And I replied, 'I am a Christian.'

Read the order of events. The magistrate asks for sacrifice *first*, as a way out, and appeals to family. Only after she refuses does he put the question of the name. The name is the charge, and the sacrifice is the test that could dissolve it. Both halves of the historians' dispute are in these four lines. A compiler, possibly Tertullian, framed the prison diary with an introduction and the account of the deaths in the arena.

## The record

The chronology, with what each episode was:

1. **64, Nero.** Christians in Rome are blamed for the fire (Tacitus, *Annals* 15.44; see [1.1](01-01-the-first-generation-and-its-sources.md)). Local, and a scapegoating rather than a policy.
2. **c. 112, Pliny and Trajan** (*Letters* 10.96–97). The governor of Bithynia-Pontus asks the emperor how to handle accused Christians. Trajan's answer: do not hunt them, reject anonymous accusations, punish the convicted who persist, and pardon anyone who sacrifices. *In words:* being a Christian is punishable, but the state will not go looking. This rescript set the pattern for over a century.
3. **177, Lyons and Vienne; 203, Perpetua at Carthage.** Local outbreaks, often driven by crowds. Tertullian (*Apology* 40, c. 197) describes the mood. The people blame every flood, drought or plague on the Christians: "straightway the cry is, Away with the Christians to the lion!" (tr. S. Thelwall, *ANF* 3). Septimius Severus's supposed edict of 202 against converts rests only on the unreliable *Historia Augusta*.
4. **235, Maximinus Thrax.** Action against church leaders. Bishop Pontian of Rome and Hippolytus are exiled to Sardinia.
5. **250–251, Decius.** After his accession Decius ordered every inhabitant to sacrifice before local commissioners and obtain a certificate, a [libellus](../reference.md#libellus). The edict dates to late 249 or early 250 and is disputed. Bishop Fabian of Rome died in January 250. The order lapsed with Decius's death in June 251. *In words:* for the first time the state came to the Christian, not the other way round.
6. **257–258, Valerian.** A first edict ordered clergy to sacrifice and banned Christian assemblies and cemeteries. A second, in 258, ordered clergy executed and stripped Christians of senatorial and equestrian rank and property. Sixtus II of Rome died in August 258 and Cyprian of Carthage on 14 September 258. After Valerian's capture by the Persians, Gallienus restored church property (260).
7. **260–303, the "Little Peace."** More than forty years without general persecution. Churches were built, and one stood in view of the palace at Nicomedia.
8. **303–304, the [Great Persecution](../reference.md#great-persecution).** On 23 February 303 the Nicomedia church was destroyed. Four edicts followed: scriptures surrendered and churches demolished, clergy arrested, clergy freed if they sacrificed, then (early 304) universal sacrifice on pain of death.
9. **305–313, uneven end.** In the West enforcement faded early (Constantius; Constantine from 306; Maxentius tolerated Christians from 306). In the East it ran on. On 30 April 311 the dying Galerius issued the [edict of Galerius](../reference.md#edict-of-galerius) at Nicomedia, ending it. Maximinus Daia soon renewed persecution in his territories, and it ended only with his defeat in 313.

**The lapsed.** Decius's order produced mass apostasy, and a crisis after it. The [lapsi](../reference.md#lapsi) fell into kinds: those who sacrificed (*sacrificati*), those who offered incense (*thurificati*), and those who bought or wangled a certificate without sacrificing (*libellatici*). Some confessors, Christians who had suffered and survived, issued letters of peace readmitting them. Cyprian, bishop of Carthage, who had gone into hiding, insisted the bishops decide. A council at Carthage in 251 graded penance by the seriousness of the lapse. At Rome, Novatian refused readmission to those who had sacrificed and went into schism against Cornelius. The dispute over who may readmit the fallen, and on what terms, returns after 303 as the Donatist schism of [1.4](01-04-constantine.md).

## Map of positions

Why were Christians punished? The classic exchange ran in *Past and Present* (1963–64), and the [de Ste Croix vs Sherwin-White](../reference.md#de-ste-croix-vs-sherwin-white) crux still frames the question.

| | G. E. M. de Ste. Croix | A. N. Sherwin-White |
|---|---|---|
| **Charge** | The name itself (*nomen Christianum*): being a Christian was enough. | Obstinacy ([contumacia](../reference.md#contumacia)): refusing a magistrate's order to sacrifice turned a religious oddity into defiance of Roman authority. |
| **Real motive** | Religious. Christians' refusal of the gods ("atheism") endangered the [pax deorum](../reference.md#pax-deorum), the peace between Rome and its gods. Disasters were read as divine anger, and crowds demanded action. | Administrative and legal. Governors dealt with trouble in their province, and a public refusal to obey was intolerable in itself. |
| **Leans on** | Trajan's "they must be punished" for the name alone. Pardon for sacrificers, which shows the offence was the religion, not crimes. Tertullian's crowds. | Pliny's own stated ground: "contumacious and inflexible obstinacy certainly deserved correction" (tr. Melmoth, rev. Bosanquet). The repeated questioning. |
| **Weak point** | Explains why accusers came forward, less well why governors convicted. | Explains the courtroom, less well why anyone was accused in the first place. |

In words: de Ste. Croix answers "why did Romans want Christians punished?" and Sherwin-White answers "why did a governor feel entitled to execute one?" Both read the same procedure: accusation, the question of the name, the offer of sacrifice. The crux is whether refusing to sacrifice was the *ground* of punishment or only the test that revealed the real offence.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean: what a libellus can prove).** About forty-six Decian certificates survive on papyrus from Egypt, most from the Fayum village of Theadelphia, nearly all dated June–July 250. Each follows a formula. The declarant says they have always sacrificed to the gods and have now sacrificed again in the commissioners' presence, and the officials sign that they saw it. One is issued to Aurelia Ammonous, a priestess of the crocodile god Petesouchos. What does the evidence establish? The order reached villages, was run through paperwork with named witnesses and deadlines, and applied to pagans too, even a priestess. What does it not establish? None of the surviving certificates mentions Christians. So they cannot tell us how many Christians complied, bribed their way out, or died. For that we depend on Christian sources: Cyprian's letters and *On the Lapsed*, and Dionysius of Alexandria as quoted by Eusebius. Genre decides what a source can answer.

**Example 2 (hard: how many died?).** Older piety spoke of whole legions martyred, and popular writing still speaks of hundreds of thousands. The evidence is thinner. Eusebius, an eyewitness in Caesarea, named the martyrs of his province in *The Martyrs of Palestine*: 83 by one modern count, 92 by Gibbon's, depending on the recension and what counts. Gibbon (*Decline and Fall*, ch. 16) treated Palestine as a sixteenth of the East and extrapolated about 1,500 deaths there. Adding the West, where the persecution was short, he reached "somewhat less than two thousand" for the Great Persecution. W. H. C. Frend (*The Rise of Christianity*, 1984) put it at roughly 3,000–3,500. For Decius and Valerian no one can do more than list the known names.

The estimate strains at every joint. Was Palestine typical? Eusebius also claims to have seen, in the Thebaid of Upper Egypt, days on which dozens and once about a hundred were killed (*Church History* 8.9), so Egypt was probably hit harder. Eusebius's presence may make it better recorded, not worse hit. Were those who died in prison or the mines counted? Did governors like Maximinus Daia's officials kill more than the milder ones? Each assumption can move the total by a factor of two or three. None gets it anywhere near six figures. The honest statement: **thousands, probably low thousands, for the Great Persecution; the whole period's total is unknown**. That is a real atrocity, and it needs no inflation.

## Watch out

- **"Persecuted" is not "always persecuted."** Legal exposure was constant from Trajan on; state-initiated persecution covers roughly 250–251, 257–260 and 303–313. Keep the two apart.
- **The catacombs were cemeteries, not hideouts.** Christians buried their dead there openly; Valerian's edict *banned* access to them precisely because they were known places.
- **Galerius's edict is not the "Edict of Milan."** 311 is Galerius's grudging toleration. The 313 Constantine–Licinius agreement is [1.4](01-04-constantine.md)'s subject.
- **Martyr numbers cut both ways.** Correcting "hundreds of thousands" to "thousands" corrects legend. It does not erase the executions, tortures, mine sentences and confiscations the sources document.

## One-liner

> Rome killed Christians in bursts, not continuously, and in thousands, not millions; what it thought it was punishing, the name or the defiance, is still the historians' argument.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢) *(Exegetical.)*** Galerius's edict of 311, as copied by Lactantius, *On the Deaths of the Persecutors* 34 (tr. W. Fletcher, *ANF* 7, 1886):

> After the publication of our edict, ordaining the Christians to betake themselves to the observance of the ancient institutions, many of them were subdued through the fear of danger, and moreover many of them were exposed to jeopardy; nevertheless, because great numbers still persist in their opinions, and because we have perceived that at present they neither pay reverence and due adoration to the gods, nor yet worship their own God, therefore we, from our wonted clemency in bestowing pardon on all, have judged it fit to extend our indulgence to those men, and to permit them again to be Christians, and to establish the places of their religious assemblies; yet so as that they offend not against good order. […] Wherefore it will be the duty of the Christians, in consequence of this our toleration, to pray to their God for our welfare…

(a) What two reasons does the edict give for ending the persecution? (b) What does it grant, on what condition, and what does it ask in return? (c) Which words show it is not an admission that the persecution was wrong? Two sentences or fewer per part.

**P2 (🟡) *(Exegetical (a) · Formal (b))*** An **invented** museum placard:

> "Under Diocletian the Roman state set out to exterminate the Church. Over 300,000 believers were executed in a decade, a slaughter recorded by the Church historian Eusebius, who witnessed it."

(a) Name the two claims in the placard's second sentence that the lesson's evidence contradicts, and give the evidence against each. 100 words or fewer. (b) Gibbon's base figure is 92 martyrs named for Palestine. Compute Gibbon's extrapolation for the East, treating Palestine as one-sixteenth. Then compute the factor by which Palestine's 92 must be multiplied to reach the placard's 300,000, and the factor by which Frend's upper estimate falls short of it.

**P3 (🔴, optional) *(Evaluative.)*** Test the popular claim "Decius's edict of 250 was an anti-Christian law" against the evidence of this lesson. Say what the libelli show, what the Christian sources show, and what the edict's lost text leaves open, then give a verdict. 150 words or fewer.

<details>
<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical.)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** (1) persecution had not worked, since "great numbers still persist in their opinions"; (2) it had produced people who worshipped neither the gods nor "their own God", so it left them with no worship at all. The second reason is the distinctive one: an irreligious subject was worse for the state than a Christian one.
**Must hit, strict (b):** it permits them "again to be Christians" and to rebuild their meeting places, on condition that they "offend not against good order", with details left to a further mandate to magistrates. In return Christians must pray to their God for the emperors' welfare, the public's and their own.
**Must hit, strict (c):** "from our wonted clemency in bestowing pardon" and "extend our indulgence": the language of pardon, which presupposes an offence. The unquoted opening of the chapter also calls Christianity "wilfulness and folly", and credit goes for citing it.
**Wrong turns:** reading the edict as religious freedom in a modern sense; confusing it with the 313 settlement of [1.4](01-04-constantine.md); forgetting that the text survives through Lactantius, a Christian author hostile to Galerius, writing c. 315. (The copy is generally trusted because Eusebius gives a Greek version that agrees in substance.)
**Model answer:** (a) The persecution failed, because most Christians held firm, and it left many honouring neither the gods nor their own God. (b) It lets them be Christians again and rebuild their assembly places, provided they keep good order, and it asks them to pray to their God for the emperors and the state. (c) "Clemency", "pardon" and "indulgence" frame the toleration as mercy toward offenders, not a confession of wrong.

---

**P2** *(Exegetical (a) · Formal (b).)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** (1) the number: scholarly estimates run from Gibbon's under 2,000 to Frend's 3,000–3,500, so 300,000 is off by roughly two orders of magnitude; (2) that Eusebius recorded it: he gives no empire-wide total. His *Martyrs of Palestine* names under a hundred, and his reports of mass executions in the Thebaid are counts of single days, not a total. The placard borrows his eyewitness authority for a figure he never gives. Credit for noting that "exterminate the Church" fits the fourth edict (304) better than the first ones, which targeted buildings, scriptures and clergy.
**Formal (b):** Gibbon: $92 \times 16 = 1{,}472$, which is his "about fifteen hundred" for the East. Placard: $300{,}000 / 92 \approx 3{,}261$, so Palestine's toll must be multiplied about 3,260-fold. Frend's upper figure: $300{,}000 / 3{,}500 \approx 85.7$, so the placard is about 86 times Frend's estimate. (Computed in Python.)
**Wrong turns:** treating Gibbon's figure as a count rather than an extrapolation; objecting to "a decade", which fits the East (303–313); "correcting" the placard to trivial numbers.
**Model answer:** (a) First, 300,000 exceeds the scholarly estimates (Gibbon under 2,000, Frend 3,000–3,500) by about a hundredfold. Second, Eusebius recorded no such figure. He gives no empire-wide total, and his *Martyrs of Palestine* names under a hundred; even his worst Egyptian days run to dozens. (b) $92 \times 16 = 1{,}472$; $300{,}000/92 \approx 3{,}261$; $300{,}000/3{,}500 \approx 86$.

---

**P3** *(Evaluative.)*

**Must hit, any verdict:** (1) the libelli: the order bound everyone, including a pagan priestess, and the certificates never mention Christians; (2) the Christian sources (Cyprian, Dionysius in Eusebius) and events: bishops were arrested, and some died (Fabian, January 250), and mass lapse followed, so Christians bore the effect because only they could not comply; (3) the edict's text is lost, so its *intent* is inferred, which is the gap Rives's reading ("The Decree of Decius and the Religion of Empire", *JRS* 1999) exploits. He reads it as a new empire-wide act of religious unity, whatever its toll on Christians. (4) a one-line verdict that says whether it judges the edict by its wording, its target or its effect.
**Wrong turns:** treating the libelli as Christian certificates; assuming the edict's wording survives; arguing that because pagans also sacrificed, no Christian was persecuted.
**Model answer (one of several):** The libelli show a universal order. Pagans, even a priestess of Petesouchos, had to sacrifice and prove it, and no certificate names Christians. The Christian sources show where the weight fell. Fabian of Rome died a martyr, bishops fled or were seized, and Carthage and Rome were left with crowds of lapsed. Because the text of the decree is lost, we cannot read Decius's intent off it; Rives argues the aim was a new, empire-wide religious unity, not Christians as such. Verdict: in form it was not an anti-Christian law but a loyalty sacrifice for all. In effect it was the first empire-wide persecution, since Christians were the people whose faith forbade them to comply. Whether Decius intended that outcome is the open question.

</details>

## Flashback

**From Lesson [1.1](01-01-the-first-generation-and-its-sources.md) (The first generation and its sources):** *(Exegetical.)* Two reports about the Jerusalem church in the 60s:

- **A.** Josephus, a Jewish historian writing in Rome, says in his *Antiquities* (completed 93–94) that in 62 the high priest Ananus used the gap between two Roman governors to have James, "the brother of Jesus, who was called Christ," stoned. Moderate citizens protested and Ananus was deposed.
- **B.** Eusebius, a Christian bishop writing in the early fourth century, says the Jerusalem Christians were warned by an oracle to leave for Pella, across the Jordan, before the war with Rome (66–70).

(a) For each report, give its two dates (event and writing) and the approximate gap. (b) Which is the stronger evidence for its core claim, and why? Use the author's position and interest, not only the gap. (c) What would you need to know before trusting the weaker report more? Two sentences per part.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):** A: event 62, written 93–94, a gap of about thirty years. B: event before or during 66–70, written in the early fourth century, a gap of well over two centuries.
**Must hit, strict (b):** A is stronger. It is closer to the event, and Josephus is a non-Christian with no stake in James; the mention is incidental to a story about a high priest's overreach. B is very late, comes from a Christian writer with an interest in the mother church's survival, and rests on an oracle.
**Must hit, strict (c):** a reason to think the late report preserves an early fact: evidence that Eusebius drew on an earlier source, or independent corroboration. Lateness alone makes it unproven, not false. Historians divide on whether Pella is memory or legend.
**Wrong turns:** judging by the gap alone and ignoring interest (1.1's rule: weigh proximity and interest separately); declaring the Pella story false because it is late ("later" is not "false"); treating Josephus as a Christian witness.
**Model answer:** (a) Josephus writes in 93–94 about an event of 62, about thirty years on. Eusebius writes in the early 300s about a flight before or during 66–70, well over two centuries on. (b) Josephus is the stronger witness. He is much closer in time, and as a non-Christian he has no reason to invent James or his standing; James comes up only in a story about Ananus. Eusebius is late, has a Christian interest in the Jerusalem church being providentially spared, and reports an oracle. (c) I would need a reason to think Eusebius was passing on something early, such as a named earlier source or independent corroboration. Without that the story is unproven, not disproved.

</details>
