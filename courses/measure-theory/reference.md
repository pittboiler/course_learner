# Measure Theory · Reference Card

> Open book. This card is meant to be open while you work — including during
> quizzes. Nothing here is something you're expected to recall cold.

The whole subject is one reordering: **measure the sets first, integrate
second.** Module 1 builds the measuring machine (σ-algebras, outer measure,
Carathéodory, Lebesgue measure) and shows it cannot cover every set. Module 2
builds the integral on top of it and buys the three theorems that let limits pass
through an integral. Module 3 turns the integrable functions into complete normed
spaces. Module 4 integrates over products, differentiates one measure against
another, and hands the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus back. Most of what you'll
want mid-problem is a *hypothesis* — which theorem needs finite measure, which
needs a dominator, which needs σ-finiteness — so the tables below lead with those.

## Definitions

### σ-algebra

A bookkeeping system of "answerable questions": a family of subsets you can
complement and pool **countably** many at a time without falling out of it.
Countable — not finite — is the load-bearing word, because limits are countable
operations.

$$X\in\mathcal{A};\qquad E\in\mathcal{A}\Rightarrow E^c\in\mathcal{A};\qquad E_1,E_2,\dots\in\mathcal{A}\Rightarrow\bigcup_{n=1}^\infty E_n\in\mathcal{A}$$

*Introduced:* [1.2](lessons/01-02-sigma-algebras.md)

### Algebra of sets

The same three axioms with **finite** unions only. Every σ-algebra is an algebra;
the converse fails, and the gap is exactly what limits need. The finite/cofinite
family on $\mathbb{N}$ is an algebra that misses the even numbers.

*Introduced:* [1.2](lessons/01-02-sigma-algebras.md)

### Generated σ-algebra

The smallest σ-algebra containing a chosen family $\mathcal{E}$ — obtained by
intersecting every σ-algebra big enough to hold $\mathcal{E}$ (an arbitrary
intersection of σ-algebras is again one, so this is legal).

$$\sigma(\mathcal{E})=\bigcap\{\mathcal{A}:\mathcal{A}\text{ a }\sigma\text{-algebra},\ \mathcal{E}\subseteq\mathcal{A}\}$$

*Introduced:* [1.2](lessons/01-02-sigma-algebras.md)

### Borel σ-algebra

Everything you can build from the open sets by countably many complements,
unions, and intersections — essentially every set you can *describe*. Much bigger
than the intervals, strictly smaller than the power set.

$$\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})=\sigma(\{\text{open sets}\})=\sigma(\{(a,b)\})=\sigma(\{(a,\infty)\})=\sigma(\{(q,\infty):q\in\mathbb{Q}\})$$

*Introduced:* [1.2](lessons/01-02-sigma-algebras.md)

### Measurable space

A pair $(X,\mathcal{A})$ — the arena of answerable questions, with **no measure
chosen yet**. "Measurable set" here means only "belongs to $\mathcal{A}$."

*Introduced:* [1.2](lessons/01-02-sigma-algebras.md)

### Measure

A way of weighing sets that never double-counts and never loses mass. Two axioms;
everything else about size is a theorem.

$$\mu:\mathcal{A}\to[0,\infty],\qquad \mu(\varnothing)=0,\qquad \mu\Big(\biguplus_{n=1}^\infty E_n\Big)=\sum_{n=1}^\infty\mu(E_n)$$

*Introduced:* [1.3](lessons/01-03-measures-properties.md)

### Null set

A measurable set of measure zero. It is a statement about **weight, not
cardinality** — $\mathbb{Q}$ is dense, infinite, and Lebesgue-null.

*Introduced:* [1.3](lessons/01-03-measures-properties.md)

### Complete measure

Every subset of a null set is itself measurable (hence null). Monotonicity
already makes *measurable* subsets of a null set null; completeness demands the
σ-algebra be fat enough to contain the rest too. Lebesgue measure is complete;
its restriction to the Borel sets is not.

*Introduced:* [1.3](lessons/01-03-measures-properties.md)

### σ-finite

The space is a countable union of finite-measure pieces: $X=\bigcup_n X_n$ with
$\mu(X_n)<\infty$. Lebesgue measure is σ-finite; counting measure on an
uncountable set is **not**. This is the hypothesis that makes product measures
and Radon–Nikodym work.

*Introduced:* [4.1](lessons/04-01-product-measures.md)

### Outer measure

The cheapest total length (volume) of any countable cover by intervals (boxes).
Defined for **every** subset, with no measurability hypothesis — and that
generosity is why it is only *sub*additive, never additive.

$$m^*(E)=\inf\Big\{\sum_{k=1}^\infty \ell(I_k)\ :\ E\subseteq\bigcup_{k=1}^\infty I_k\Big\}$$

*Introduced:* [1.4](lessons/01-04-outer-measure-caratheodory.md)

### Carathéodory measurability

$E$ is measurable when it slices **every** test set additively — no outer measure
leaks at its boundary. The test set $A$ is external and arbitrary; that
universality is the whole strength.

$$m^*(A)=m^*(A\cap E)+m^*(A\setminus E)\qquad\text{for every }A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^n$$

*Introduced:* [1.4](lessons/01-04-outer-measure-caratheodory.md)

### Lebesgue measure

The outer measure with its domain cut down to where it behaves. No new
construction — only a restriction.

$$\lambda:=m^*\big|_{\mathcal{L}},\qquad \mathcal{L}=\{\text{Carathéodory-measurable sets}\}$$

*Introduced:* [1.5](lessons/01-05-lebesgue-measure-rn.md)

### Regularity

A measurable set is "almost open" from outside and "almost compact" from inside —
squeezed between two friendly sets that differ from it by as little measure as
you like.

$$\lambda(E)=\inf\{\lambda(U):U\supseteq E\text{ open}\}=\sup\{\lambda(K):K\subseteq E\text{ compact}\}$$

*Introduced:* [1.5](lessons/01-05-lebesgue-measure-rn.md)

### Vitali set

Pick exactly one point from each equivalence class of the rational-difference
relation $x\sim y\iff x-y\in\mathbb{Q}$ on $[0,1]$. Its countably many rational
translates are disjoint and sandwiched between $[0,1]$ and $[-1,2]$, so no length
survives both translation invariance and countable additivity. Existence requires
the Axiom of Choice; you can never exhibit one.

*Introduced:* [1.6](lessons/01-06-non-measurable-set.md)

### Measurable function

Every measurable question about the output pulls back to a measurable question
about the input. Depends only on the σ-algebra, never on the measure.

$$f^{-1}(B)\in\mathcal{A}\ \text{ for every }B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \{f>a\}\in\mathcal{A}\ \text{ for every }a$$

*Introduced:* [2.1](lessons/02-01-measurable-functions.md)

### Almost everywhere

A property holds $\mu$-a.e. if the set where it fails sits inside a $\mu$-null
set. This is the equivalence relation whose classes are the elements of $L^p$.

*Introduced:* [2.1](lessons/02-01-measurable-functions.md)

### Simple function

A finite measurable staircase: finitely many flat heights, each over a
*measurable* set. Finiteness of the range alone is not enough — the level sets
must be in $\mathcal{M}$.

$$s=\sum_{i=1}^n a_i\chi_{E_i},\qquad E_i=s^{-1}(\{a_i\})\in\mathcal{M}\ \text{ (canonical form: }a_i\text{ distinct and nonzero)}$$

*Introduced:* [2.2](lessons/02-02-simple-functions-integral.md)

### Integral of a nonnegative function

The most area you can trap underneath $f$ with a simple staircase. A supremum,
not a maximum — for unbounded $f$ no single simple minorant attains it.

$$\int_X f\,d\mu=\sup\Big\{\int_X s\,d\mu\ :\ s\text{ simple},\ 0\le s\le f\Big\},\qquad \int_X s\,d\mu=\sum_i a_i\,\mu(E_i)$$

*Introduced:* [2.2](lessons/02-02-simple-functions-integral.md)

### Positive and negative parts

Split $f$ into the piece above the axis and the size of the piece below it. Both
are nonnegative and measurable.

$$f^+=\max(f,0),\quad f^-=\max(-f,0),\qquad f=f^+-f^-,\quad \lvert f\rvert=f^++f^-$$

*Introduced:* [2.3](lessons/02-03-general-lebesgue-integral.md)

### Integrable ($L^1$)

The *total unsigned* area is finite. Lebesgue never bargains with
$\infty-\infty$, so there is no conditional convergence here — this
**absoluteness** is the sharpest break from the improper Riemann integral, and it
is what buys completeness.

$$f\in L^1(\mu)\iff \int_X\lvert f\rvert\,d\mu<\infty \iff \int f^+<\infty\text{ and }\int f^-<\infty$$

*Introduced:* [2.3](lessons/02-03-general-lebesgue-integral.md)

### Convergence in measure

For every tolerance, the *set* of points still off by more than that tolerance
shrinks to zero size. A global, set-sized promise — a rogue region may misbehave
as long as it shrinks.

$$\mu\big(\{\lvert f_n-f\rvert>\varepsilon\}\big)\to 0\qquad\text{for every }\varepsilon>0$$

*Introduced:* [3.1](lessons/03-01-modes-of-convergence.md)

### Convergence in $L^p$

The total accumulated $p$-th-power error vanishes. Weighs *both* how big the
error is and how much room it occupies. For $p=1$ this is "in mean"; for $p=2$,
"in mean square."

$$\lVert f_n-f\rVert_p^p=\int_X\lvert f_n-f\rvert^p\,d\mu\to 0$$

*Introduced:* [3.1](lessons/03-01-modes-of-convergence.md)

### Almost uniform convergence

Convergence is uniform once you delete a set of measure smaller than any $\delta$
you name in advance. This is the mode Egorov produces; you generally **cannot**
take $\delta=0$.

*Introduced:* [3.2](lessons/03-02-egorov-lusin.md)

### $L^p$ space

Measurable functions with finite $p$-norm, taken **modulo a.e. equality** — the
quotient is what makes $\lVert\cdot\rVert_p$ a genuine norm instead of a
seminorm.

$$\lVert f\rVert_p=\Big(\int_X\lvert f\rvert^p\,d\mu\Big)^{1/p}\ (1\le p<\infty),\qquad \lVert f\rVert_\infty=\operatorname{ess\,sup}\lvert f\rvert$$

*Introduced:* [3.3](lessons/03-03-lp-holder-minkowski.md)

### Essential supremum

The smallest ceiling $\lvert f\rvert$ respects once you're allowed to ignore a
null set — the sup that doesn't care about measure-zero spikes.

$$\operatorname{ess\,sup}\lvert f\rvert=\inf\{M\ge0:\ \mu(\{\lvert f\rvert>M\})=0\}$$

*Introduced:* [3.3](lessons/03-03-lp-holder-minkowski.md)

### Conjugate exponents

The duality that runs all of $L^p$ theory. Forget the constraint and Hölder is
simply false.

$$\frac1p+\frac1q=1,\qquad q=\frac{p}{p-1},\qquad (p-1)q=p,\qquad (p,q)=(1,\infty)\text{ and }(2,2)\text{ allowed}$$

*Introduced:* [3.3](lessons/03-03-lp-holder-minkowski.md)

### Banach space

A normed vector space with no missing limit points: every Cauchy sequence
converges to something still inside the space. $L^2$, whose norm comes from an
inner product, is a **Hilbert** space.

*Introduced:* [3.4](lessons/03-04-completeness-riesz-fischer.md)

### Product σ-algebra

The smallest σ-algebra containing every measurable rectangle — the product
analogue of "Borel sets are generated by the intervals." Equivalently, the
coarsest σ-algebra making both coordinate projections measurable.

$$\mathcal{A}\otimes\mathcal{B}=\sigma\big(\{A\times B: A\in\mathcal{A},\ B\in\mathcal{B}\}\big)$$

*Introduced:* [4.1](lessons/04-01-product-measures.md)

### Section

The slice of a set (or function) at a fixed coordinate. Sectioning commutes with
complements and countable unions, which is why sections of measurable sets are
measurable.

$$E_x=\{y:(x,y)\in E\},\qquad E^y=\{x:(x,y)\in E\},\qquad f_x(y)=f(x,y)$$

*Introduced:* [4.1](lessons/04-01-product-measures.md)

### Product measure

Size a set by integrating the length of its slices. For σ-finite factors the two
slicing orders agree, and this is the **unique** measure giving each rectangle
$\mu(A)\nu(B)$.

$$(\mu\times\nu)(E)=\int_X\nu(E_x)\,d\mu(x)=\int_Y\mu(E^y)\,d\nu(y)$$

*Introduced:* [4.1](lessons/04-01-product-measures.md)

### Signed measure

A measure with the nonnegativity axiom deleted, and **at most one** of the two
infinite values allowed (convention: $\nu>-\infty$) — otherwise additivity would
produce $\infty-\infty$. The archetype is $\nu(E)=\int_E f\,d\mu$.

*Introduced:* [4.3](lessons/04-03-signed-measures-decomposition.md)

### Positive set, negative set, null set (for a signed measure)

A *hereditary* property: $P$ is positive when **every** measurable subset of $P$
has $\nu\ge0$, not merely $\nu(P)\ge0$. Negative and null are defined the same
way with $\le0$ and $=0$.

*Introduced:* [4.3](lessons/04-03-signed-measures-decomposition.md)

### Hahn decomposition

Every signed measure cleaves the space into an all-nonnegative region and an
all-nonpositive region. The *sets* are unique only up to a $\nu$-null shuffle;
the measures they produce are unique outright.

$$X=P\uplus N,\qquad P\text{ positive},\ N\text{ negative}$$

*Introduced:* [4.3](lessons/04-03-signed-measures-decomposition.md)

### Jordan decomposition and total variation

Read the positive charge off $P$ and the magnitude of the negative charge off
$N$; subtract to rebuild $\nu$, add to get the honest nonnegative measure that
tracks charge regardless of sign.

$$\nu^+(E)=\nu(E\cap P),\quad \nu^-(E)=-\nu(E\cap N),\qquad \nu=\nu^+-\nu^-,\quad \lvert\nu\rvert=\nu^++\nu^-$$

*Introduced:* [4.3](lessons/04-03-signed-measures-decomposition.md)

### Mutual singularity

Each measure lives entirely on its own set and is invisible to the other. The
Jordan parts are automatically mutually singular, since they sit on $P$ and $N$.

$$\alpha\perp\beta\iff X=A\uplus B\ \text{ with }\ \alpha(B)=0,\ \beta(A)=0$$

*Introduced:* [4.3](lessons/04-03-signed-measures-decomposition.md)

### Absolute continuity (of measures)

Wherever $\mu$ is blind, $\nu$ must be blind too. This is obviously *necessary*
for $\nu$ to be an integral against $\mu$; Radon–Nikodym says it is also
sufficient.

$$\nu\ll\mu\iff\big(\mu(E)=0\Rightarrow\nu(E)=0\big)$$

*Introduced:* [4.4](lessons/04-04-radon-nikodym.md)

### Radon–Nikodym derivative

The density that turns $\nu$ into an integral against $\mu$. Defined **only up to
$\mu$-a.e. equality** — it has no value at a single point.

$$\nu(E)=\int_E \frac{d\nu}{d\mu}\,d\mu\qquad\text{for all }E\in\mathcal{M}$$

*Introduced:* [4.4](lessons/04-04-radon-nikodym.md)

### Absolutely continuous function

The function-level cousin of $\nu\ll\mu$: a finite total length of intervals
forces a finite total rise. It is exactly the condition under which you can
integrate the derivative and get the function back.

$$\sum_k (b_k-a_k)<\delta\ \Longrightarrow\ \sum_k\lvert F(b_k)-F(a_k)\rvert<\varepsilon\quad\text{(finite disjoint intervals)}$$

*Introduced:* [4.5](lessons/04-05-lebesgue-differentiation.md)

### Hardy–Littlewood maximal function

One function dominating every averaging window at once — the worst-case average
of $\lvert f\rvert$ over all balls centered at $x$.

$$Mf(x)=\sup_{r>0}\frac{1}{\lambda(B_r(x))}\int_{B_r(x)}\lvert f\rvert\,d\lambda$$

*Introduced:* [4.5](lessons/04-05-lebesgue-differentiation.md)

### Lebesgue point

Not just "the average of $f$ converges to $f(x)$" but "the average *distance*
from $f(x)$ vanishes" — $f$ is genuinely close to $f(x)$ on most of the ball, not
close only through cancellation. Almost every point is one.

$$\lim_{r\to0^+}\frac{1}{\lambda(B_r(x))}\int_{B_r(x)}\lvert f(y)-f(x)\rvert\,d\lambda(y)=0$$

*Introduced:* [4.5](lessons/04-05-lebesgue-differentiation.md)

## Formulas and rules

### Consequences of the measure axioms

Everything below is a *theorem* from "empty weighs nothing" plus "disjoint
countable piles add."

| Property | Statement | Hypothesis |
|---|---|---|
| Monotonicity | $E\subseteq F\Rightarrow\mu(E)\le\mu(F)$ | none |
| Subtraction | $\mu(F\setminus E)=\mu(F)-\mu(E)$ for $E\subseteq F$ | $\mu(E)<\infty$ |
| Finite subadditivity | $\mu(E\cup F)\le\mu(E)+\mu(F)$ | none |
| Countable subadditivity | $\mu\big(\bigcup_n E_n\big)\le\sum_n\mu(E_n)$ | none |
| Inclusion–exclusion | $\mu(E\cup F)=\mu(E)+\mu(F)-\mu(E\cap F)$ | $\mu(E\cap F)<\infty$ |
| **Continuity from below** | $E_n\uparrow E\Rightarrow\mu(E_n)\to\mu(E)$ | **none** |
| **Continuity from above** | $E_n\downarrow E\Rightarrow\mu(E_n)\to\mu(E)$ | **$\mu(E_1)<\infty$** |

Continuity from below *is* countable additivity reorganized (and, given finite
additivity, is equivalent to it). Continuity from above needs the finite cap:
$E_n=[n,\infty)$ has $E_n\downarrow\varnothing$ but $\lambda(E_n)=\infty$ forever.
Two standard tricks recur in every proof here: **disjointification**
($B_n=E_n\setminus(E_1\cup\cdots\cup E_{n-1})$) and the **$\varepsilon/2^k$
budget** (give the $k$-th set an allowance $\varepsilon 2^{-k}$; the total error
is $\varepsilon$).

*From* [1.3](lessons/01-03-measures-properties.md), [1.4](lessons/01-04-outer-measure-caratheodory.md)

### The standard measures

| Measure | Definition | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Counting measure | $\mu(E)=\#E$ (or $\infty$) | integration becomes summation; $\ell^p=L^p(\text{counting})$; **not σ-finite** on an uncountable set |
| Dirac $\delta_x$ | $1$ if $x\in E$, else $0$ | a probability measure; the standard non-$\ll\lambda$ example |
| Lebesgue $\lambda$ on $\mathbb{R}^n$ | $m^*$ restricted to $\mathcal{L}$ | translation-invariant, regular, complete, σ-finite |
| Measure with density | $\nu(E)=\int_E f\,d\mu$, $f\ge0$ | always a measure (term-by-term integration); always $\nu\ll\mu$ |
| Countable–cocountable | $\mu(E)=0$ if $E$ countable, $1$ if $E^c$ countable | lives on the countable/cocountable σ-algebra |

*From* [1.3](lessons/01-03-measures-properties.md), [2.4](lessons/02-04-monotone-convergence-fatou.md), [4.4](lessons/04-04-radon-nikodym.md)

### Constructing a measure — the Carathéodory recipe

1. Define a crude set function on a simple family (length of intervals, volume of
   boxes) — nothing in dispute.
2. Extend to **all** sets by infimum over countable covers: this is $m^*$, which
   is monotone, countably subadditive, and agrees with length on intervals (the
   last uses Heine–Borel), but is **not** additive.
3. Keep only the sets that split every test set additively. That collection
   $\mathcal{M}$ is a σ-algebra containing the intervals, and $m^*|_{\mathcal{M}}$
   is a **complete**, countably additive measure.

**Always prove measurability one-sided.** Subadditivity gives
$m^*(A)\le m^*(A\cap E)+m^*(A\setminus E)$ free; you only ever check "$\ge$".

Properties of Lebesgue measure that fall out: translation invariance
($\lambda(E+x)=\lambda(E)$, from box volumes being translation-invariant), outer
and inner regularity, $\mathcal{B}\subsetneq\mathcal{L}$, and
$\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{B}$ completed (every $E\in\mathcal{L}$ sits inside a
$G_\delta$ set $G$ with $\lambda(G\setminus E)=0$).

*From* [1.4](lessons/01-04-outer-measure-caratheodory.md), [1.5](lessons/01-05-lebesgue-measure-rn.md)

### Building measurable functions

| To show | Check | Why it's enough |
|---|---|---|
| $f$ measurable | $\{f>a\}\in\mathcal{A}$ for all $a$ | rays generate $\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})$; preimages respect complements and countable unions |
| $f$ measurable (cheaper) | $\{f>q\}\in\mathcal{A}$ for **rational** $q$ | rational rays already generate $\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})$ |
| Same, other families | $\{f\ge a\}$, $\{f<a\}$, or $\{f\le a\}$ — any one family works | complements and $\{f\ge a\}=\bigcap_n\{f>a-\tfrac1n\}$ |

Closed under: $cf$, $f+g$, $fg$, $\lvert f\rvert$, $\max$, $\min$,
$\varphi\circ f$ for continuous $\varphi$, and — the payoff Riemann never had —
$\sup_n f_n$, $\inf_n f_n$, $\limsup_n f_n$, $\liminf_n f_n$, and pointwise
limits. The engine is one identity:

$$\Big\{\sup_n f_n>a\Big\}=\bigcup_{n}\{f_n>a\},\qquad \Big\{\inf_n f_n<a\Big\}=\bigcup_n\{f_n<a\}$$

Both need **strict** inequality; with $\ge$ the identity is false (a sup can equal
$a$ without any term reaching it). On a **complete** measure space, $f=g$ a.e.
with $g$ measurable makes $f$ measurable, and a.e. limits of measurable functions
are measurable.

*From* [2.1](lessons/02-01-measurable-functions.md)

### The integral in three stages

$$\text{simple }s=\sum a_i\chi_{E_i}:\quad \int s\,d\mu=\sum_i a_i\mu(E_i)\qquad(\text{convention } 0\cdot\infty=0)$$
$$f\ge0:\quad \int f\,d\mu=\sup\Big\{\int s\,d\mu: s\text{ simple},\ 0\le s\le f\Big\}$$
$$f\text{ signed}:\quad \int f\,d\mu=\int f^+\,d\mu-\int f^-\,d\mu\quad(\text{undefined iff both are }\infty)$$

**Simple-function approximation.** For measurable $f\ge0$ there are simple
$0\le s_1\le s_2\le\cdots\le f$ with $s_n\uparrow f$ pointwise, uniformly wherever
$f$ is bounded — round $f$ down to a multiple of $2^{-n}$ and cap at $n$:

$$s_n(x)=\min\Big(\frac{\lfloor 2^n f(x)\rfloor}{2^n},\ n\Big)$$

**Properties.** Linearity and monotonicity on $L^1$; the triangle inequality
$\lvert\int f\rvert\le\int\lvert f\rvert$, hence
$\lvert\int f-\int g\rvert\le\int\lvert f-g\rvert$; $\int_E f\,d\mu=0$ whenever
$\mu(E)=0$; and $\int\lvert f\rvert\,d\mu=0\iff f=0$ a.e.

*From* [2.2](lessons/02-02-simple-functions-integral.md), [2.3](lessons/02-03-general-lebesgue-integral.md)

### The three convergence theorems — exact hypotheses

The point of the whole module. Read the **Hypotheses** column as a checklist; the
last column is what happens when you skip an item.

| Theorem | Hypotheses (all needed) | Conclusion | Drop one → this breaks it |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Monotone convergence (MCT)** | $f_n$ measurable; $0\le f_1\le f_2\le\cdots$; $f_n\uparrow f$ pointwise (a.e. is fine) | $\int f_n\uparrow\int f$; both sides may be $+\infty$ | drop *increasing*: $f_n=n\chi_{(0,1/n)}$ has $\int f_n=1$, $\int\lim f_n=0$. Reverse to *decreasing*: $h_n=\chi_{[n,\infty)}\downarrow0$ with $\int h_n=\infty$ — the downward MCT needs $\int h_1<\infty$ |
| **Fatou's lemma** | $f_n\ge0$ measurable. **No** monotonicity, **no** convergence, **no** dominator | $\int\liminf_n f_n\le\liminf_n\int f_n$ | drop $f_n\ge0$: $f_n=-\chi_{[n,n+1]}$ gives $0\le-1$, false. The direction never flips — *liminf loses mass* |
| **Dominated convergence (DCT)** | $f_n$ measurable; $f_n\to f$ a.e.; **one** $g\in L^1$, independent of $n$, with $\lvert f_n\rvert\le g$ a.e. for every $n$ | $f\in L^1$, $\int f_n\to\int f$, **and** $\int\lvert f_n-f\rvert\to0$ | drop the dominator: $n\chi_{(0,1/n)}$ again — any envelope obeys $g(x)\ge\lfloor 1/x\rfloor$, so $\int g=\infty$ |
| **Bounded convergence** | $\mu(X)<\infty$; $\lvert f_n\rvert\le M$ a *constant*; $f_n\to f$ a.e. | $\int f_n\to\int f$ | drop $\mu(X)<\infty$: $f_n=\tfrac1n\chi_{[0,n]}$ has $\lvert f_n\rvert\le1$, $f_n\to0$, $\int f_n=1$ — the constant $1$ is not integrable on $\mathbb{R}$ |

**Term-by-term integration (MCT corollary).** For $g_n\ge0$ measurable,
$\int\sum_n g_n\,d\mu=\sum_n\int g_n\,d\mu$, **unconditionally** — nonnegativity
means there is nothing to cancel. Do *not* use it on a series with sign changes;
that needs DCT.

**MCT and DCT are complementary, not nested.** MCT permits $f_n\uparrow\infty$
(which DCT forbids); DCT permits non-monotone, sign-changing sequences (which MCT
forbids). Fatou is the common parent: DCT is proved by applying Fatou twice, to
$g+f_n\ge0$ and $g-f_n\ge0$.

**Beyond DCT.** Domination is sufficient but not necessary for
$\int\lvert f_n-f\rvert\to0$. On a finite-measure space the sharp condition is
*uniform integrability* plus convergence in measure (Vitali); DCT is the
checkable special case where one fixed $g$ supplies uniform integrability free.

*From* [2.4](lessons/02-04-monotone-convergence-fatou.md), [2.5](lessons/02-05-dominated-convergence.md)

### Continuity and differentiation under the integral sign

Both are DCT corollaries, and in both the **dominator is the hypothesis**, not a
formality. Let $F(t)=\int_X f(x,t)\,d\mu(x)$.

| Want | Need |
|---|---|
| $F$ continuous at $t_0$ | $t\mapsto f(x,t)$ continuous at $t_0$ for a.e. $x$, and $\lvert f(x,t)\rvert\le g(x)$ with $g\in L^1$ for all $t$ near $t_0$ |
| $F'(t)=\int_X\partial_t f(x,t)\,d\mu(x)$ | $\partial_t f(x,t)$ exists for a.e. $x$, and $\lvert\partial_t f(x,t)\rvert\le g(x)$ with $g\in L^1$, for **all** $t$ in the interval |

The mean value theorem converts the difference quotient into $\partial_t f$ at
some interior point, which is why one dominator for the *derivative* suffices.

### Riemann vs. Lebesgue

The comparison the whole course exists to make.

| | Riemann | Lebesgue |
|---|---|---|
| What gets chopped | the **domain**, into vertical strips | the **range**, into horizontal layers |
| Prerequisite | none beyond partitions | you must be able to measure $\{f>a\}$ first |
| Scope | bounded $f$ on a bounded $[a,b]$ | any measurable $f$ on any measure space |
| Integrability test | discontinuity set has measure zero (**Lebesgue's criterion**) | $\int\lvert f\rvert\,d\mu<\infty$ |
| $\mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{Q}\cap[0,1]}$ | not integrable ($\overline{\int}=1$, $\underline{\int}=0$) | integrable, integral $0$ |
| Closed under pointwise limits | **no** — the class itself escapes | yes (measurability survives countable limits) |
| Limit–integral interchange | only via uniform convergence | MCT, Fatou, DCT |
| Completeness under $\lVert\cdot\rVert_1$ | **incomplete** (fat-Cantor sequence escapes) | complete — Riesz–Fischer |
| Conditional convergence | improper Riemann accepts $\int_1^\infty\frac{\sin x}{x}dx$ | rejects it: $\int_1^\infty\frac{\lvert\sin x\rvert}{x}dx=\infty$, so the integral is undefined |

**The two are compatible, not rivals.** If $f$ is bounded on $[a,b]$ and Riemann
integrable, it is Lebesgue integrable with the *same value*. The only gap runs the
other way: conditionally convergent improper Riemann integrals (and improper
integrals over unbounded domains generally) have no Lebesgue counterpart, because
Lebesgue measures $f^+$ and $f^-$ separately and never negotiates.

*From* [1.1](lessons/01-01-where-riemann-fails.md), [2.3](lessons/02-03-general-lebesgue-integral.md), [3.4](lessons/03-04-completeness-riesz-fischer.md)

### Modes of convergence — the implication map

| From | To | Holds? | Cost / obstruction |
|---|---|---|---|
| $L^p$ | in measure | **always** | Chebyshev |
| a.e. | in measure | only if $\mu(X)<\infty$ | escaping bump $\chi_{[n,n+1]}$ on $\mathbb{R}$ |
| a.e. | almost uniform | only if $\mu(X)<\infty$ | Egorov; same counterexample |
| almost uniform | a.e. **and** in measure | always | — |
| in measure | a.e. | **subsequence only** | Borel–Cantelli extraction; typewriter kills the full sequence |
| $L^p$ | a.e. | never in general | typewriter (converges at *no* point) |
| a.e. | $L^p$ | never in general | needs a dominator — this is exactly DCT |
| in measure | $L^p$ | never in general | moving spike $n\chi_{(0,1/n)}$ |
| $L^1$ | $L^2$ | never in general | $n\chi_{(0,1/n^2)}$: $\lVert\cdot\rVert_1\to0$ but $\lVert\cdot\rVert_2=1$ |

**Chebyshev / Markov**, the engine of the first row:

$$\mu(\{g\ge t\})\le\frac1t\int_X g\,d\mu\ (g\ge0)\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \mu\big(\{\lvert f_n-f\rvert>\varepsilon\}\big)\le\frac{\lVert f_n-f\rVert_p^p}{\varepsilon^p}$$

**On a probability space** ($\mu(X)=1<\infty$) every finite-measure caveat above
is automatically satisfied — which is why "almost sure implies in probability"
needs no hypothesis.

*From* [3.1](lessons/03-01-modes-of-convergence.md), [3.2](lessons/03-02-egorov-lusin.md)

### Littlewood's three principles

The organizing slogan of Module 3: *nearly* always means "off a set of
arbitrarily small measure."

| Principle | Theorem | Hypothesis |
|---|---|---|
| Every measurable set is nearly a finite union of intervals | regularity of $\lambda$ | — |
| Every measurable function is nearly continuous | **Lusin**: compact $K$ with $m([a,b]\setminus K)<\delta$ and $f\vert_K$ continuous | $f$ finite a.e. |
| Every a.e.-convergent sequence is nearly uniformly convergent | **Egorov**: $\mu(E)<\delta$ with $f_n\to f$ uniformly off $E$ | $\mu(X)<\infty$ |

Lusin's conclusion is about the **restriction** $f\vert_K$, not about continuity
of $f$ at points of $K$ in the ambient space. Combined with Tietze extension,
Lusin gives: the measurable functions on $[0,1]$ are exactly the a.e. limits of
continuous ones — and hence continuous functions are dense in $L^p$.

*From* [3.2](lessons/03-02-egorov-lusin.md), [1.5](lessons/01-05-lebesgue-measure-rn.md)

### The $L^p$ inequality chain

One convexity fact gives everything else.

$$\textbf{Young:}\quad ab\le\frac{a^p}{p}+\frac{b^q}{q}\quad(a,b\ge0),\qquad\text{equality}\iff a^p=b^q$$
$$\textbf{Hölder:}\quad \int_X\lvert fg\rvert\,d\mu\le\lVert f\rVert_p\,\lVert g\rVert_q\qquad\Big(\tfrac1p+\tfrac1q=1\Big)$$
$$\textbf{Minkowski:}\quad \lVert f+g\rVert_p\le\lVert f\rVert_p+\lVert g\rVert_p\qquad(1\le p<\infty)$$
$$\textbf{Cauchy–Schwarz:}\quad \int_X\lvert fg\rvert\,d\mu\le\lVert f\rVert_2\,\lVert g\rVert_2\qquad(\text{Hölder at }p=q=2)$$

Hölder is proved by normalizing to $\lVert F\rVert_p=\lVert G\rVert_q=1$ and
integrating Young pointwise. Minkowski is proved by splitting
$\lvert f+g\rvert^p=\lvert f+g\rvert\cdot\lvert f+g\rvert^{p-1}$ and applying
Hölder to each half — the exponent bookkeeping $(p-1)q=p$ is conjugacy doing its
job. **Equality in Hölder** holds iff $\alpha\lvert f\rvert^p=\beta\lvert g\rvert^q$
a.e. for some constants not both zero.

**Interpolation.** For $p\le r\le q$ with
$\tfrac1r=\tfrac\theta p+\tfrac{1-\theta}{q}$,

$$\lVert f\rVert_r\le\lVert f\rVert_p^{\,\theta}\,\lVert f\rVert_q^{\,1-\theta},\qquad\text{hence}\qquad L^p\cap L^q\subseteq L^r\ \text{ on any measure space.}$$

*From* [3.3](lessons/03-03-lp-holder-minkowski.md)

### $L^p$ inclusions — which way do they run?

There is no universal answer; **the total measure decides the direction.** This
is the single most-missed fact in Module 3, so read the middle column first.

| Setting | Inclusion | Estimate | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| $\mu(X)<\infty$ (in particular a probability space) | $L^q\subseteq L^p$ for $1\le p<q\le\infty$ — **bigger exponent is the smaller space** | $\lVert f\rVert_p\le\mu(X)^{\frac1p-\frac1q}\lVert f\rVert_q$; on a probability space the prefactor is $1$, so $\lVert f\rVert_p\le\lVert f\rVert_q$ | Hölder on $\lvert f\rvert^p\cdot1$ with the pair $\tfrac qp,\ \tfrac{q}{q-p}$ — the constant function is integrable only because $\mu(X)<\infty$ |
| $\mu(X)=\infty$ (e.g. $\lambda$ on $\mathbb{R}$) | **no inclusion in either direction** | — | there are two independent ways to fail: a local spike and a fat tail |
| counting measure on $\mathbb{N}$, i.e. $\ell^p$ | $\ell^p\subseteq\ell^q$ for $1\le p<q\le\infty$ — **bigger exponent is the bigger space**, the exact reverse | $\lVert a\rVert_q\le\lVert a\rVert_p$ | $\sum\lvert a_n\rvert^p<\infty$ forces $\lvert a_n\rvert\to0$, so eventually $\lvert a_n\rvert\le1$ and $\lvert a_n\rvert^q\le\lvert a_n\rvert^p$ |

**The two witnesses on $\mathbb{R}$ that kill both inclusions:**

| Function | In | Not in | What it exploits |
|---|---|---|---|
| $f(x)=x^{-1/2}\mathbf{1}_{(0,1)}$ | $L^1$ | $L^2$ | a **local spike** — squaring makes a non-integrable singularity |
| $f(x)=x^{-1}\mathbf{1}_{[1,\infty)}$ | $L^p$ for every $p>1$ | $L^1$ | a **fat tail** — larger $p$ tolerates fatter tails; $\lVert f\rVert_p=(p-1)^{-1/p}$ |

Mnemonic: **finite measure punishes height, counting measure punishes width.** On
a finite space there is no room for a tail, so only spikes can spoil a norm and
the larger exponent is the stronger demand. Under counting measure there are no
small sets, so height is already bounded and only decay at infinity matters — the
larger exponent is the weaker demand.

A useful special case: on $[0,1]$, $x^{-\alpha}\in L^p\iff \alpha p<1$; on
$[1,\infty)$, $x^{-\alpha}\in L^p\iff \alpha p>1$.

*From* [3.3](lessons/03-03-lp-holder-minkowski.md), [1.3](lessons/01-03-measures-properties.md)

### Completeness: Riesz–Fischer and how to use it

**Theorem.** $L^p(\mu)$ is a Banach space for every $1\le p\le\infty$; $L^2$ is a
Hilbert space.

**The criterion that makes the proof mechanical.** A normed space is complete
**iff** every absolutely summable series converges: $\sum_n\lVert f_n\rVert<\infty$
implies the partial sums converge in the space. The hypothesis is stronger than
"Cauchy," and it hands you a free dominating function $G=\sum_n\lvert f_n\rvert$.
The proof then runs each Module 2 tool exactly once:

$$\underbrace{\lVert G_N\rVert_p\le\sum_{n\le N}\lVert f_n\rVert_p\le M}_{\text{Minkowski}}\ \Longrightarrow\ \underbrace{\int G^p\le M^p<\infty}_{\text{MCT}}\ \Longrightarrow\ G<\infty\text{ a.e.}\ \Longrightarrow\ \underbrace{\lVert f-s_N\rVert_p\to0}_{\text{DCT with dominator }G^p}$$

For $p=\infty$ the flavor is different: pool the countably many null sets where
the essential-sup bounds fail, and off that single null set the sequence is
*uniformly* Cauchy.

**Free companion fact.** If $f_n\to f$ in $L^p$ ($p<\infty$) then **some
subsequence** converges to $f$ a.e. Never the whole sequence — the typewriter.

**Dense subspaces (the reason completeness is useful).** For $1\le p<\infty$:
simple functions with $\mu(E_k)<\infty$ are dense in $L^p(\mu)$; for Lebesgue
measure, step functions and continuous compactly-supported functions are dense
too. Standard move: **prove it on the dense class, extend by limits** — legal
precisely because the limits stay in $L^p$.

For $0<p<1$ the triangle inequality fails, $\lVert\cdot\rVert_p$ is not a norm,
and this whole scaffolding collapses.

*From* [3.4](lessons/03-04-completeness-riesz-fischer.md)

### Fubini–Tonelli — the working recipe

Both theorems require **σ-finite** factors.

| Theorem | Hypothesis on $f$ | Conclusion |
|---|---|---|
| **Tonelli** | $f\ge0$, product-measurable. Nothing else | the double integral and both iterated integrals are the same element of $[0,\infty]$ — the common value may be $+\infty$ |
| **Fubini** | $f\in L^1(\mu\times\nu)$ | a.e. slice is integrable, the iterated integrands are integrable, and all three integrals agree |

$$\underbrace{\int_X\!\Big(\int_Y\lvert f\rvert\,d\nu\Big)d\mu<\infty}_{\text{Tonelli check on }\lvert f\rvert}\ \Longrightarrow\ \underbrace{\int_X\!\Big(\int_Y f\,d\nu\Big)d\mu=\int_Y\!\Big(\int_X f\,d\mu\Big)d\nu}_{\text{Fubini interchange on }f}$$

Run Tonelli on $\lvert f\rvert$ in whichever order is easier; if it is finite you
have earned Fubini. If it is infinite, **stop** — the two orders may genuinely
disagree.

**Layer cake** (a Tonelli application, and the standard way to convert an
integral into a tail estimate): for $f\ge0$ measurable and $1\le p<\infty$,

$$\int_X \lvert f\rvert^p\,d\mu=p\int_0^\infty t^{p-1}\,\mu\big(\{\lvert f\rvert>t\}\big)\,dt$$

*From* [4.2](lessons/04-02-fubini-tonelli.md), [4.1](lessons/04-01-product-measures.md)

### Radon–Nikodym and Lebesgue decomposition

**Theorem.** $\mu,\nu$ σ-finite and $\nu\ll\mu$ $\Rightarrow$ there is a
measurable $f\ge0$, unique up to $\mu$-a.e. equality, with
$\nu(E)=\int_E f\,d\mu$ for every $E$. Write $f=\frac{d\nu}{d\mu}$.

| Rule | Statement |
|---|---|
| Change of measure | $\displaystyle\int_X g\,d\nu=\int_X g\,\frac{d\nu}{d\mu}\,d\mu$ for $g\ge0$ or $g\in L^1(\nu)$ |
| Linearity | $\dfrac{d(\nu_1+\nu_2)}{d\mu}=\dfrac{d\nu_1}{d\mu}+\dfrac{d\nu_2}{d\mu}$ |
| Chain rule | $\nu\ll\mu\ll\rho\Rightarrow\dfrac{d\nu}{d\rho}=\dfrac{d\nu}{d\mu}\cdot\dfrac{d\mu}{d\rho}$ |
| Reciprocal | $\nu\ll\mu$ **and** $\mu\ll\nu\Rightarrow\dfrac{d\mu}{d\nu}=\Big(\dfrac{d\nu}{d\mu}\Big)^{-1}$ |
| ε–δ form | for **finite** $\nu$: $\nu\ll\mu\iff\big(\forall\varepsilon\ \exists\delta:\ \mu(E)<\delta\Rightarrow\nu(E)<\varepsilon\big)$ |

**Lebesgue decomposition.** For σ-finite $\mu,\nu$ there is a unique splitting

$$\nu=\nu_{ac}+\nu_s,\qquad \nu_{ac}\ll\mu,\qquad \nu_s\perp\mu.$$

For a *signed* $\nu\ll\mu$, apply the theorem to $\nu^+$ and $\nu^-$ separately
and subtract the densities. Also useful:
$\lvert\nu(E)\rvert\le\lvert\nu\rvert(E)$, usually strictly; when
$\nu(E)=\int_E f\,d\mu$ one has $\lvert\nu\rvert(E)=\int_E\lvert f\rvert\,d\mu$
and $\lVert\nu\rVert=\lVert f\rVert_1$; and $\lvert\nu\rvert(E)$ equals the sup of
$\sum_j\lvert\nu(E_j)\rvert$ over finite measurable partitions of $E$.

*From* [4.4](lessons/04-04-radon-nikodym.md), [4.3](lessons/04-03-signed-measures-decomposition.md)

### Differentiation and the FTC, Lebesgue form

You cannot *evaluate* an $L^1$ function — only *average* it.

$$A_r f(x)=\frac{1}{\lambda(B_r(x))}\int_{B_r(x)}f\,d\lambda$$

| Result | Statement |
|---|---|
| **Vitali covering (finite form)** | from any finite family of balls, a disjoint subfamily whose triples cover the union; tripling costs a factor $3^n$ in measure |
| **Maximal inequality (weak $(1,1)$)** | $\lambda(\{Mf>\alpha\})\le\dfrac{3^n}{\alpha}\lVert f\rVert_1$ — and this is sharp: $Mf\notin L^1$ unless $f\equiv0$ |
| **Lebesgue differentiation** | $A_r f(x)\to f(x)$ as $r\to0^+$ for a.e. $x$, for $f\in L^1_{\mathrm{loc}}$ |
| **Lebesgue points** | almost every $x$ satisfies the stronger $A_r\lvert f-f(x)\rvert(x)\to0$ |
| **FTC, easy half** | $F(x)=\int_a^x f\,d\lambda$ with $f\in L^1$ is absolutely continuous, and $F'=f$ a.e. |
| **FTC, deep half** | $F$ is absolutely continuous $\iff$ $F'$ exists a.e., $F'\in L^1$, and $F(x)=F(a)+\int_a^x F'\,d\lambda$ |
| **Bridge to 4.4** | $F$ absolutely continuous $\iff dF\ll\lambda$, and then $F'=\frac{dF}{d\lambda}$ $\lambda$-a.e. |

The proof template is worth remembering on its own: approximate $f$ in $L^1$ by a
continuous $g$ (where averaging is trivial), and control the error $f-g$ at *all
radii simultaneously* with $M(f-g)$, bounded by the maximal inequality.

*From* [4.5](lessons/04-05-lebesgue-differentiation.md)

### The Cantor set and its two relatives

Used repeatedly and constructed nowhere else on this card's courses, so here it
is once.

- **Cantor set $C$.** Delete the open middle third of $[0,1]$, then of each
  remaining interval, forever. At stage $n$ you hold $2^n$ closed intervals of
  length $3^{-n}$. $C$ is compact, uncountable, has empty interior, and
  $\lambda(C)=\lim (2/3)^n=0$ — an uncountable null set.
- **Fat Cantor set $K$.** Same construction, but shrink what you delete so the
  total deleted length is only $\tfrac12$. Still closed with empty interior, but
  now $\lambda(K)=\tfrac12>0$ — so $\mathbf{1}_K$ is discontinuous on a set of
  positive measure and is not Riemann integrable ([1.1](lessons/01-01-where-riemann-fails.md)).
- **Cantor function $F$.** Continuous, nondecreasing, $F(0)=0$, $F(1)=1$, constant
  on every deleted interval, and increasing by exactly $2^{-n}$ across each of the
  $2^n$ stage-$n$ intervals. Hence $F'=0$ a.e. while $F$ climbs by $1$: the
  induced measure $\mu_C$ is *continuous but singular*, $\mu_C\perp\lambda$
  ([4.5](lessons/04-05-lebesgue-differentiation.md)).

## Pitfalls

### σ-algebras and measurability

- Finite closure only makes an *algebra*; analysis is about limits, and only **countable** closure survives them. *([1.2](lessons/01-02-sigma-algebras.md))*
- In a measurable space $(X,\mathcal{A})$, "measurable set" means "belongs to $\mathcal{A}$" — no size has been assigned yet. Measure is a separate object. *([1.2](lessons/01-02-sigma-algebras.md))*
- $\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})$ is neither "just the intervals" nor all of $\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})$; you name it by generators and never enumerate it. *([1.2](lessons/01-02-sigma-algebras.md))*
- $\mathcal{L}\ne\mathcal{B}$: $\mathcal{L}$ is strictly larger, being $\mathcal{B}$ completed by the subsets of null sets. They agree only *up to null sets*. *([1.5](lessons/01-05-lebesgue-measure-rn.md))*
- Measurability of $f$ depends on the **σ-algebra only**, never on $\mu$; the measure enters only through "almost everywhere." *([2.1](lessons/02-01-measurable-functions.md))*
- Testing every Borel preimage is never how it's done — check the rays $\{f>a\}$ (rational $a$ suffices). *([2.1](lessons/02-01-measurable-functions.md))*
- The sup/inf identities need **strict** inequalities: $\{\sup_n f_n\ge a\}=\bigcup_n\{f_n\ge a\}$ is false ($f_n=a-\tfrac1n$). *([2.1](lessons/02-01-measurable-functions.md))*
- "$f=g$ a.e. and $g$ measurable $\Rightarrow$ $f$ measurable" needs the measure to be **complete**. Lebesgue measure is; state the hypothesis abstractly. *([2.1](lessons/02-01-measurable-functions.md))*
- "Finitely many values" does not make a function simple — each level set must be measurable. *([2.2](lessons/02-02-simple-functions-integral.md))*

### Measure, outer measure, and null sets

- Countable additivity is genuinely stronger than finite additivity; never prove an infinite-pile identity from the finite version. *([1.3](lessons/01-03-measures-properties.md))*
- Continuity from **above** needs $\mu(E_1)<\infty$; from below it needs nothing. The asymmetry is that the proof subtracts, and $\infty-\infty$ is meaningless. *([1.3](lessons/01-03-measures-properties.md), [1.4](lessons/01-04-outer-measure-caratheodory.md))*
- Measure zero is about weight, not cardinality or topology: $\mathbb{Q}$ is dense, infinite, and null; the Cantor set is uncountable and null. *([1.3](lessons/01-03-measures-properties.md), [1.4](lessons/01-04-outer-measure-caratheodory.md))*
- $m^*$ is **not** a measure — only monotone and countably subadditive. Additivity is bought by restricting to $\mathcal{M}$; that restriction is the entire Carathéodory theorem. *([1.4](lessons/01-04-outer-measure-caratheodory.md))*
- Never prove both inequalities in the Carathéodory criterion — the "$\le$" is free from subadditivity. *([1.4](lessons/01-04-outer-measure-caratheodory.md))*
- The test set $A$ in the criterion is **external and arbitrary**; checking $A=E$ or $A\subseteq E$ proves nothing. *([1.4](lessons/01-04-outer-measure-caratheodory.md))*
- Outer regularity approximates from outside by an **open** set, not by the closure: $\overline{\mathbb{Q}\cap[0,1]}=[0,1]$ has measure $1$, not $0$. Compact sets are the *inner* approximants. *([1.5](lessons/01-05-lebesgue-measure-rn.md))*
- The Vitali set is not pathological because it is dense or infinite — $\mathbb{Q}$ is both and is measurable. It is pathological because it meets each coset once, which requires a non-constructive choice. *([1.6](lessons/01-06-non-measurable-set.md))*
- Finite additivity would **not** have sufficed to kill the Vitali set: a finitely additive translation-invariant length on all of $2^{\mathbb{R}}$ does exist in dimension $1$. The contradiction lives in the infinite sum. *([1.6](lessons/01-06-non-measurable-set.md))*

### Integration

- $f^++f^-=\lvert f\rvert$; it is $f^+-f^-$ that equals $f$. Mixing these flips signs. *([2.3](lessons/02-03-general-lebesgue-integral.md))*
- "The integral exists" and "$f$ is integrable" are different: $\int f$ can be a well-defined $+\infty$ while $f\notin L^1$. Everything downstream (linearity, DCT, $L^p$) needs the stronger condition. *([2.3](lessons/02-03-general-lebesgue-integral.md))*
- A convergent improper Riemann integral is **not** automatically a Lebesgue integral — conditional cancellation is a Riemann-limit phenomenon. *([2.3](lessons/02-03-general-lebesgue-integral.md), [2.4](lessons/02-04-monotone-convergence-fatou.md))*
- $(f+g)^+\ne f^++g^+$; the additivity proof routes around it via the nonnegative rearrangement $h^++f^-+g^-=h^-+f^++g^+$. *([2.3](lessons/02-03-general-lebesgue-integral.md))*
- The integral of $f\ge0$ is a **supremum, not a maximum** — for unbounded $f$ no single simple minorant attains it. *([2.2](lessons/02-02-simple-functions-integral.md))*
- Dyadic approximation is uniform only where $f$ is bounded; on $\{f=\infty\}$ you only get $s_n=n\uparrow\infty$. *([2.2](lessons/02-02-simple-functions-integral.md))*
- Keep the convention $0\cdot\infty=0$, or a height-zero slab over an infinite set wrongly reads as $\infty$. *([2.2](lessons/02-02-simple-functions-integral.md))*

### The convergence theorems

- Fatou's direction is fixed, with $\int\liminf$ on the **small** side. *Liminf loses mass.* If you "prove" the reverse, you dropped mass somewhere. *([2.4](lessons/02-04-monotone-convergence-fatou.md))*
- MCT is for **increasing** nonnegative sequences. The decreasing version is false without $\int h_1<\infty$. *([2.4](lessons/02-04-monotone-convergence-fatou.md))*
- Term-by-term integration is unconditional **only** because the terms are nonnegative; a signed series needs DCT. *([2.4](lessons/02-04-monotone-convergence-fatou.md))*
- DCT needs **one fixed** $g\in L^1$ dominating the whole sequence; a bound $\lvert f_n\rvert\le g_n$ varying with $n$ is worthless. *([2.5](lessons/02-05-dominated-convergence.md))*
- A uniform *constant* bound is a dominator only on a **finite**-measure space. *([2.5](lessons/02-05-dominated-convergence.md))*
- You may not differentiate under the integral just because the derivative exists pointwise — exhibit the dominating derivative. *([2.5](lessons/02-05-dominated-convergence.md))*
- MCT and DCT do not imply each other: MCT allows $f_n\uparrow\infty$, DCT allows sign changes and non-monotonicity. *([2.5](lessons/02-05-dominated-convergence.md))*
- All of these hold with the hypotheses only **a.e.** — a null set never affects an integral. *([2.4](lessons/02-04-monotone-convergence-fatou.md))*

### Modes of convergence, Egorov, Lusin

- $L^p$ convergence gives you a convergent **subsequence** a.e., never the full sequence. *([3.1](lessons/03-01-modes-of-convergence.md), [3.4](lessons/03-04-completeness-riesz-fischer.md))*
- "a.e. beats in measure" only on finite measure; on $\mathbb{R}$ the bad set can flee instead of shrinking. *([3.1](lessons/03-01-modes-of-convergence.md), [3.2](lessons/03-02-egorov-lusin.md))*
- "In $L^p$" is a *family* of inequivalent modes, not one condition — $L^1$ convergence does not give $L^2$ convergence. Chebyshev relates each $L^p$ to *in measure*, never to another $L^p$. *([3.1](lessons/03-01-modes-of-convergence.md))*
- Egorov's finite-measure hypothesis is not cosmetic — continuity from above needs a finite-measure set to start from. *([3.2](lessons/03-02-egorov-lusin.md))*
- Egorov's $\delta$ can be made tiny but never zero: there is no single null set off which $x^n\to0$ uniformly. *([3.2](lessons/03-02-egorov-lusin.md))*
- Lusin gives continuity of the **restriction** $f\vert_K$, not continuity of $f$ at the points of $K$ in the ambient interval. *([3.2](lessons/03-02-egorov-lusin.md))*

### $L^p$ spaces

- $\lVert\cdot\rVert_p$ is only a *seminorm* on functions: $\lVert f\rVert_p=0$ forces $f=0$ **a.e.** Elements of $L^p$ are equivalence classes, and the limit produced by Riesz–Fischer is pinned down only a.e. *([3.3](lessons/03-03-lp-holder-minkowski.md), [3.4](lessons/03-04-completeness-riesz-fischer.md))*
- Hölder is not "Cauchy–Schwarz with other letters" — the hypothesis is conjugacy $\tfrac1p+\tfrac1q=1$, and it includes the endpoint pair $(1,\infty)$. *([3.3](lessons/03-03-lp-holder-minkowski.md))*
- You cannot get Minkowski by raising $\lvert f+g\rvert\le\lvert f\rvert+\lvert g\rvert$ to the $p$-th power; the split $\lvert f+g\rvert^p=\lvert f+g\rvert\cdot\lvert f+g\rvert^{p-1}$ plus Hölder is what closes it. *([3.3](lessons/03-03-lp-holder-minkowski.md))*
- $L^p$ inclusions have **no** universal direction: bigger $p$ is smaller on finite measure, bigger on counting measure, and incomparable on $\mathbb{R}$. Check the total measure before quoting one. *([3.3](lessons/03-03-lp-holder-minkowski.md))*
- The absolutely-summable criterion rests on Minkowski; for $0<p<1$ there is no triangle inequality and the argument collapses. *([3.4](lessons/03-04-completeness-riesz-fischer.md))*

### Products, signed measures, differentiation

- Sections of a product-measurable set are measurable — but a set with all sections measurable need not be product-measurable, and $\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{R}^m)\otimes\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{R}^n)$ is strictly smaller than $\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{R}^{m+n})$. Build the product first, complete second. *([4.1](lessons/04-01-product-measures.md))*
- σ-finiteness is load-bearing for both the product measure and Tonelli — the diagonal against counting measure gives $1\ne0$ for a nonnegative function. *([4.1](lessons/04-01-product-measures.md), [4.2](lessons/04-02-fubini-tonelli.md))*
- Tonelli needs **no** integrability check for $f\ge0$; running one *is* an application of Tonelli. Only Fubini charges the $\int\lvert f\rvert<\infty$ price, and only that check certifies a swap. *([4.2](lessons/04-02-fubini-tonelli.md))*
- "Positive set" means every measurable **subset** has $\nu\ge0$, not $\nu(P)\ge0$. *([4.3](lessons/04-03-signed-measures-decomposition.md))*
- $\lvert\nu\rvert(E)\ne\lvert\nu(E)\rvert$: total variation is a measure that refuses to let charge cancel. *([4.3](lessons/04-03-signed-measures-decomposition.md))*
- Hahn is unique only up to null sets; Jordan ($\nu^\pm$, $\lvert\nu\rvert$) is unique outright. *([4.3](lessons/04-03-signed-measures-decomposition.md))*
- $\ll$ and $\perp$ are opposite extremes — don't conflate them, and $\nu\ll\mu$ never implies $\mu\ll\nu$ (the reciprocal rule needs both). *([4.3](lessons/04-03-signed-measures-decomposition.md), [4.4](lessons/04-04-radon-nikodym.md))*
- The ε–δ form of absolute continuity is a **finite-$\nu$** statement; with $\frac{d\nu}{d\lambda}=1/x$ on $(0,1)$ no $\delta$ controls $\nu$. *([4.4](lessons/04-04-radon-nikodym.md))*
- A Radon–Nikodym derivative has no value at a point — "evaluating a pdf" is a convenient fiction; only its integrals over sets mean anything. *([4.4](lessons/04-04-radon-nikodym.md))*
- LDT is "almost everywhere," never "everywhere": at a jump the averages converge to the mean of the one-sided limits. *([4.5](lessons/04-05-lebesgue-differentiation.md))*
- $Mf\notin L^1$ for any $f\not\equiv0$ — weak $(1,1)$ is the sharp endpoint bound, not a stepping stone to a strong one. *([4.5](lessons/04-05-lebesgue-differentiation.md))*
- "Differentiable a.e. with $F'\in L^1$" does **not** give the FTC; the Cantor function clears that bar and still loses its entire rise. Only absolute continuity forces equality. *([4.5](lessons/04-05-lebesgue-differentiation.md))*
