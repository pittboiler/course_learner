# Philosophy of Science — Syllabus

> Philosophy · Tier 2 · ~26 lessons · Prereqs: [`epistemology`](../epistemology/syllabus.md), [`prob-stat-refresher`](../prob-stat-refresher/syllabus.md) · Roadmap id: `philosophy-of-science`

## Goal

This course owns the philosophy of scientific reasoning for the whole library: what it is for evidence to support a theory, whether theories can be refuted, how science changes, what explanation and laws are for, and whether we should believe what our best theories say about things we cannot see. It starts where [`epistemology`](../epistemology/syllabus.md) stops. That course left Hume's problem open (4.2–4.3), named grue without solving it, and built credences and conditionalization (Module 5). This one turns those tools into confirmation theory: the new riddle, Bayesian confirmation measures, the ravens and old evidence. It then follows the twentieth-century argument from Popper through Duhem, Kuhn, Lakatos and Feyerabend to the demarcation problem. The statistics wars get a module of their own, because Jacob already runs hypothesis tests in [`econometrics`](../econometrics/syllabus.md) and regularized models in [`statistical-learning`](../statistical-learning/syllabus.md), and this is where he asks what a p-value, a stopping rule or a prior actually licenses. It closes with explanation, laws and reduction, and the realism debate, both of which `philosophy-of-physics` builds on. You will finish able to compute and compare confirmation measures, say what Popper, Kuhn and Lakatos each conclude about the same historical episode, run the D-N, causal and unification accounts on a hard case, and state the no-miracles argument and the pessimistic induction at full strength. The course takes no side between Bayesians and error statisticians, realists and anti-realists, or Popperians and Kuhnians. It deliberately skips the philosophy of particular sciences (quantum interpretations, spacetime and probability in statistical mechanics go to `philosophy-of-physics`; what economic models explain goes to [`philosophy-of-economics`](../philosophy-of-economics/syllabus.md)), the metaphysics of laws and causation (owned by [`metaphysics`](../metaphysics/syllabus.md)), the sociology of scientific knowledge as a research program, and the history of science as history.

## How practice works in this course

Problems follow [HUMANITIES-BUILD-BRIEF.md](../../HUMANITIES-BUILD-BRIEF.md): every part declares its kind, and every solution is a rubric (must-hit moves, wrong turns) plus a model answer. Here that plays out in four ways:

- **Formal parts are common and graded as math.** Modules 1 and 3 and one part of Boss 5 are computations: confirmation measures that disagree in ranking, likelihood ratios for the ravens, p-values under two stopping rules, the positive predictive value of a significant result, the base rate the no-miracles argument needs. Every number in a lesson or a key is computed twice, once by hand and once in closed form, before it ships.
- **Exegetical parts are strict.** What Popper's criterion says about an auxiliary hypothesis, what Lakatos calls a progressive problem shift, which flagpole explanation the D-N model admits, what constructive empiricism says one should believe about electrons. These have right answers.
- **Evaluative parts are verdict-neutral.** No rubric rewards concluding that Bayesianism beats error statistics, that realism is true, that Kuhn made science irrational, or that values have no place in science. Must-hit moves are ones either side has to make: state the view precisely, run it on the case, name the premise the objection attacks, and say what the reply costs.
- **Archetypes.** Apply-to-a-hard-case (a historical episode run through three methodologies) and find-the-crux are the workhorses. Counterexample carries the explanation module (flagpoles, hexed salt). **Diagnose** uses invented press releases and op-eds that misread a p-value or smuggle in a demarcation criterion. Close reading uses public-domain texts only: Aristotle, *Posterior Analytics* (Oxford translation); Newton's rules of reasoning from *Principia* Book III (Motte, 1729); Whewell, *The Philosophy of the Inductive Sciences*; Duhem, *La Théorie physique* (1906, in the lesson's own translation); Poincaré, *Science and Hypothesis* (English translation, 1905). Modern authors (Hempel, Goodman, Popper, Kuhn, Lakatos, Feyerabend, Salmon, van Fraassen, Laudan, Worrall, Mayo, Longino) are paraphrased and cited, never quoted at length.

## Dangerous Checklist

When you finish, you can:

- [ ] State Hempel's conditions of adequacy and the hypothetico-deductive account, and show how each generates the ravens paradox or the tacking problem
- [ ] Explain the new riddle of induction precisely, and say what entrenchment, natural kinds and a Bayesian prior each offer as a way to privilege "green" over "grue"
- [ ] Compute the difference, ratio and likelihood-ratio measures of confirmation on a case, and build a pair of cases on which they rank the evidence differently
- [ ] Give the Bayesian treatment of the ravens with numbers, and say how it depends on how the evidence was sampled
- [ ] State the problem of old evidence as an equation, and say what the counterfactual and logical-learning responses each change
- [ ] Say why a simpler curve can be expected to predict better on the Akaike account and on the Bayesian Occam account, and where the two part ways
- [ ] Run Popper's falsificationism, the Duhem–Quine thesis, Kuhn's account of anomaly and crisis, and Lakatos's methodology of research programmes on the same historical episode, and give each one's verdict
- [ ] Compute p-values for the same data under two stopping rules, say what the likelihood principle implies about the difference, and state the frequentist reply
- [ ] Compute the positive predictive value of a significant result from a base rate, a significance level and a power, and say what it shows about the replication crisis and what severe testing proposes instead
- [ ] Reconstruct the argument from inductive risk that values enter the acceptance of hypotheses, and state the strongest reply
- [ ] Run the deductive-nomological, causal-mechanical, interventionist and unification accounts of explanation on the flagpole and on a ceteris paribus economic law
- [ ] State the no-miracles argument and the pessimistic induction in their strongest forms, show the base-rate objection with numbers, and place selective realism, structural realism and constructive empiricism against both

## Modules

### Module 1: Induction and confirmation

What it is for evidence to support a hypothesis: first as a logical relation, which breaks on grue and the ravens, then as probability raising, which turns out to be several different things.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1.1 | From Hume to a logic of confirmation | State what a qualitative theory of confirmation must deliver, and find where the obvious candidates break | Hume's problem assumed (from [`epistemology`](../epistemology/syllabus.md) 4.2–4.3), instance confirmation and Nicod's criterion, Hempel's equivalence and special consequence conditions, hypothetico-deductivism, the tacking (irrelevant conjunction) problem |
| 1.2 | The new riddle of induction | Say exactly what grue shows and assess the main answers to it | grue and bleen, projectibility, lawlike vs accidental generalizations, entrenchment (Goodman), natural kinds, grue as a problem of priors (from [`epistemology`](../epistemology/syllabus.md) 5.4), no free lunch as its formal cousin ([`statistical-learning`](../statistical-learning/syllabus.md) 1.4) |
| 1.3 | Confirmation as probability raising | Compute confirmation measures and show that the choice among them matters | incremental vs absolute confirmation, the difference measure, the log-ratio measure, the log-likelihood-ratio measure (from method 2.4), measure sensitivity (Fitelson), confirmation vs acceptance |
| 1.4 | The ravens paradox | Reconstruct the paradox and assess the Bayesian solution with numbers | Hempel's paradox and his own bullet-biting reply, the Bayesian solution (Hosiasson-Lindenbaum), relative population sizes, dependence on the sampling procedure, Good's case in which a black raven disconfirms |
| 1.5 | The problem of old evidence | State why known evidence cannot confirm on the standard account, and weigh the repairs | Glymour's problem, Mercury's perihelion and general relativity, the counterfactual solution (Howson), learning that a theory entails the evidence (Garber, Jeffrey, Niiniluoto), the problem of new theories |
| 1.6 | Simplicity and curve-fitting | Explain why a simpler hypothesis might be expected to predict better, on two rival accounts | Occam's razor as a methodological rule, the curve-fitting problem, overfitting ([`statistical-learning`](../statistical-learning/syllabus.md) 2.1, [`machine-learning`](../machine-learning/syllabus.md) 4.1), Akaike's criterion (Forster and Sober), the Bayesian Occam factor and marginal likelihood, simplicity as a prior (from [`statistical-learning`](../statistical-learning/syllabus.md) 2.5) |

**Boss problem 1:** (a) Formal: in case A, a hypothesis moves from prior 0.1 to posterior 0.2 on its evidence; in case B, a second hypothesis moves from 0.5 to 0.7 on its evidence. Compute the difference, log-ratio and log-likelihood-ratio measures for each, and show that two of them rank B above A while the third ranks A above B. (b) Formal: a world holds 100 ravens and 1,000,000 non-black non-ravens. H says all the ravens are black; the rival says exactly 10 of the 100 are white, everything else unchanged. Compute the likelihood ratio for H of (i) sampling a raven at random and finding it black and (ii) sampling a non-black object at random and finding it a non-raven, and say what the pair of numbers does to Hempel's paradox. (c) Formal: show that if the evidence already has probability 1, every measure from (a) returns zero, and say which lesson-1.5 repair drops which assumption of that derivation. (d) In 300–500 words: does the Bayesian treatment in (b) dissolve the ravens paradox, or does its dependence on the sampling procedure concede that the paradox was never about the logic of confirmation? Any verdict, if the answer engages Good's case and says whether the Bayesian solution also handles grue. Parts (a)–(c) are formal; (d) is evaluative.

### Module 2: Falsification, paradigms, and research programmes

The argument that ran from Popper to Feyerabend over whether science has a method, whether theories can be refuted, and whether theory change is rational, ending with the question the whole argument started from: what separates science from pseudoscience.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2.1 | Popper: conjectures and refutations | State falsificationism as a response to Hume and as a demarcation criterion, and test it on its own examples | the modus tollens asymmetry, falsifiability, bold conjectures, corroboration vs confirmation, Popper's contrast between Einstein's risky prediction and the theories of Marx, Freud and Adler, the problem of probabilistic hypotheses |
| 2.2 | Duhem, Quine, and underdetermination | Show why no experiment refutes a hypothesis in isolation, and separate the weak thesis from the strong one | auxiliary hypotheses, Duhem against crucial experiments, Quine's web of belief, Uranus and Neptune vs Mercury and Vulcan, contrastive underdetermination, empirically equivalent theories and the Laudan–Leplin objections |
| 2.3 | Kuhn: normal science and revolutions | Describe science the way *Structure* does, and say what that description is meant to show | paradigm as disciplinary matrix and as exemplar, normal science as puzzle-solving, anomaly and crisis, revolution and the gestalt switch, theory-ladenness of observation (Hanson) |
| 2.4 | Kuhn: incommensurability and theory choice | Say whether revolutions are rational, and state Kuhn's later answer at full strength | semantic and methodological incommensurability, the five values (accuracy, consistency, scope, simplicity, fruitfulness) as shared but differently weighted, choice without an algorithm, the charge of relativism and Kuhn's reply |
| 2.5 | Lakatos and Feyerabend | Apply the methodology of research programmes to a case, and state Feyerabend's challenge to any methodology | hard core, protective belt, positive and negative heuristic, progressive vs degenerating problem shifts, novel prediction; Feyerabend's methodological anarchism, counterinduction, his reading of Galileo's tower argument (the history cited to [`church-history`](../church-history/syllabus.md) 7.3) |
| 2.6 | Demarcation after Popper | Assess whether there is a line between science and pseudoscience, and what drawing one requires | Laudan's argument that the demarcation problem is dead, the Arkansas creation-science trial (*McLean v. Arkansas*, 1982) and the criteria given there, cluster and family-resemblance accounts, pseudoscience vs bad science vs non-science |

**Boss problem 2:** (a) Close-read Newton's fourth rule of reasoning in *Principia* Book III (Motte, 1729): what does it tell the investigator to do with a proposition gathered by induction when a contrary hypothesis is merely imagined, and when contrary phenomena occur? Name the words that mark the difference. (b) Uranus's observed orbit departed from Newtonian prediction, and Le Verrier posited an unseen planet, found in 1846; Mercury's perihelion also departed, and a posited inner planet, Vulcan, was never found, the discrepancy later being accounted for by general relativity. Say what naive falsificationism implies about Newtonian gravitation in each case, and how the Duhem–Quine thesis complicates that verdict. (c) Classify each auxiliary move as a progressive or a degenerating problem shift on Lakatos's criteria, saying at what date the verdict could first have been given. (d) In 300–500 words: can Popper, Kuhn or Lakatos say, without hindsight, when it became rational to stop blaming the auxiliaries in the Mercury case? Any verdict, if each of the three is stated at the strength its defenders would recognize and Feyerabend's objection to the whole question is answered. Parts (a)–(c) are exegetical; (d) is evaluative.

### Module 3: Statistical evidence and the values of science

What the tools of working science actually license: two schools of statistical inference and the likelihood principle that divides them, the replication crisis read as a problem in the theory of evidence, and whether values belong inside the acceptance of a hypothesis.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3.1 | Two schools of statistical inference | Say what a p-value and a confidence level mean on the frequentist view, and how Fisher and Neyman–Pearson disagreed | probability as long-run frequency vs degree of belief, Fisher's significance test and the p-value as evidence, Neyman–Pearson error rates and inductive behaviour, the hybrid taught in textbooks, the reading of a p-value assumed from [`prob-stat-refresher`](../prob-stat-refresher/syllabus.md) 4.3 and [`econometrics`](../econometrics/syllabus.md) 2.3 |
| 3.2 | The likelihood principle | Show that two experiments with proportional likelihoods give different p-values, and say which school must care | the law of likelihood (Hacking, Royall), the likelihood principle and Birnbaum's argument (stated, not proved), stopping rules and optional stopping, Bayes factors, Lindley's paradox, observation selection effects in general (Eddington's net; the fine-tuning application cited to [`philosophy-of-religion`](../philosophy-of-religion/syllabus.md) 3.2) |
| 3.3 | Replication, error statistics, and severe testing | Compute how often a significant finding is true, and compare the Bayesian and error-statistical diagnoses of the replication crisis | positive predictive value from base rate, power and significance level (Ioannidis), forking paths and specification search ([`econometrics`](../econometrics/syllabus.md) 2.7), pre-registration, proposals to lower the significance threshold, Mayo's severity requirement as a descendant of Popper |
| 3.4 | Values and inductive risk | Reconstruct the argument that accepting a hypothesis requires a value judgment, and state the replies | the value-free ideal, epistemic vs non-epistemic values, Rudner's argument from inductive risk, Jeffrey's reply that scientists assign probabilities rather than accept, direct vs indirect roles for values (Douglas), Weber's value freedom cited to [`social-theory`](../social-theory/syllabus.md) 1.3 |
| 3.5 | Objectivity | Separate the senses of "objective," and assess social accounts of how a community secures it | procedural, convergent and interactive objectivity, Longino's critical contextual empiricism and its four norms for criticism, feminist standpoint epistemology, the division of cognitive labour (Kitcher), objectivity as a property of communities vs of individuals |

**Boss problem 3:** (a) Formal: a coin is flipped and lands 9 heads and 3 tails. Compute the one-sided p-value against fairness if the design was "flip 12 times" (299/4096, about 0.073) and if it was "flip until the third tail" (67/2048, about 0.033); show the two likelihood functions are proportional, and state what the likelihood principle implies about the difference. (b) Formal: in a field where 10% of tested hypotheses are true, tests run at a significance level of 0.05. Compute the probability that a significant result is true when power is 0.8, and again when power is 0.35. (c) Say how a Fisherian, a Neyman–Pearson frequentist, a likelihoodist, a subjective Bayesian and an error statistician in Mayo's sense each treat the stopping rule in (a), with a one-line reason each. (d) An agency must decide whether a solvent causes cancer at workplace exposure levels, and its scientists must choose the evidential threshold for saying so. In 300–500 words: does the choice of threshold make the science value-laden in a way that threatens its objectivity? Any verdict, if Rudner's argument and Jeffrey's reply are both stated and the answer uses at least one sense of objectivity from 3.5. Parts (a)–(b) are formal; (c) is exegetical; (d) is evaluative.

### Module 4: Explanation, laws, and reduction

What makes an answer to a why-question scientific: four accounts of explanation, what laws do inside science, and whether the special sciences reduce to physics.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4.1 | The deductive-nomological model | State explanation as argument from laws, and build the counterexamples that sank it | the D-N and inductive-statistical models (Hempel and Oppenheim), explanation–prediction symmetry, the flagpole and its shadow, the barometer, hexed salt and the man taking birth-control pills, paresis and the high-probability requirement |
| 4.2 | Causal explanation | Run the statistical-relevance, causal-mechanical and interventionist accounts on the D-N counterexamples | statistical relevance (Salmon), causal processes and mark transmission, mechanisms (Machamer, Darden and Craver), Woodward's interventionism and invariance, the bridge to potential outcomes ([`econometrics`](../econometrics/syllabus.md) 3.1), the analyses of causation each presupposes cited to [`metaphysics`](../metaphysics/syllabus.md) 4.1–4.3 |
| 4.3 | Unification and the pragmatics of explanation | Say what unification buys that causal accounts don't, and what van Fraassen says an explanation is | Whewell's consilience of inductions, Friedman's and Kitcher's unification accounts, argument patterns, Newton and Darwin as unifiers, van Fraassen's why-questions (topic, contrast class, relevance relation), explanatory pluralism |
| 4.4 | Laws in the sciences | Say what laws do in scientific practice, and whether biology and economics have any | counterfactual support, lawlike vs accidental generalizations (the gold sphere and the uranium sphere), ceteris paribus laws (tendency laws in economics cited to [`philosophy-of-economics`](../philosophy-of-economics/syllabus.md) 6.1), the evolutionary contingency thesis (Beatty), pragmatic accounts of laws (Mitchell), which theory from [`metaphysics`](../metaphysics/syllabus.md) 4.3 fits the practice best |
| 4.5 | Reduction and emergence | State what it would take for one science to reduce to another, and assess the case for the autonomy of the special sciences | Nagel's bridge-law reduction, multiple realizability (Fodor's special sciences), the autonomy of higher-level explanation, weak vs strong emergence, individualism vs holism in social science cited to [`social-theory`](../social-theory/syllabus.md) 1.1–1.2, the mental case cited to [`philosophy-of-mind`](../philosophy-of-mind/syllabus.md) 2.2 |

**Boss problem 4:** (a) Close-read the passage of Aristotle's *Posterior Analytics* I.13 (Oxford translation) that contrasts two syllogisms about the planets, one inferring that they are near because they do not twinkle and one inferring that they do not twinkle because they are near. Which does Aristotle say gives the reasoned fact, and which words mark the distinction? (b) Put both syllogisms in D-N form and show that the model counts both as explanations; name the flagpole case as the same defect. (c) Give the interventionist verdict on both directions of the flagpole case, stating the intervention that separates them. (d) The law of demand holds only ceteris paribus, and markets are multiply realized by very different physical arrangements. In 300–500 words: is the law of demand a law, and can it explain on the D-N, interventionist and unification accounts? Any verdict, if the answer says what the ceteris paribus clause does to each account and whether multiple realizability makes economics autonomous from the sciences below it. Parts (a)–(c) are exegetical; (d) is evaluative.

### Module 5: Scientific realism

Whether the success of science is a reason to believe what its theories say about the unobservable, and what the history of discarded theories does to that reason. `philosophy-of-physics` starts from this module.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5.1 | Realism and the no-miracles argument | Separate the theses realism bundles together, and state the no-miracles argument and the base-rate objection with numbers | metaphysical, semantic and epistemic realism, the observable–unobservable distinction, the no-miracles argument (Putnam, Boyd) as a meta-level inference to the best explanation (from method 2.1), the base-rate fallacy objection (Magnus and Callender), the prior it would take to rescue the argument |
| 5.2 | The pessimistic induction and selective realism | State Laudan's historical argument and assess the realist strategy of saving only the parts that worked | Laudan's list (the ether, caloric, phlogiston), the pessimistic meta-induction reconstructed as an argument, divide et impera (Kitcher's working vs presuppositional posits, Psillos), Stanford's problem of unconceived alternatives, underdetermination recalled from 2.2 |
| 5.3 | Structural realism | State what structural realism says survives theory change, and press the objection that threatens to empty it | Worrall's argument from Fresnel's equations surviving into Maxwell's theory, Poincaré as its forerunner, epistemic vs ontic structural realism (Ladyman, French), the Newman objection, the handoff to `philosophy-of-physics` for particles and fields |
| 5.4 | Constructive empiricism and entity realism | State van Fraassen's alternative to realism and the two strongest challenges to where it draws its line | empirical adequacy, acceptance vs belief, the observable as what unaided humans could observe, Maxwell's continuum argument, instrumentalism distinguished (Friedman's essay cited to [`philosophy-of-economics`](../philosophy-of-economics/syllabus.md) 6.2), Hacking on microscopes and entity realism through manipulation, the natural ontological attitude (Fine) named |

**Boss problem 5:** (a) Close-read the passage of Poincaré's *Science and Hypothesis* (English translation, 1905) on the fate of Fresnel's theory of light after Maxwell: what does Poincaré say was abandoned, what does he say survived, and which words carry the claim that survived? (b) Formal: suppose the probability that a theory is empirically successful is 0.95 if it is approximately true and 0.05 if it is not, and only 1% of candidate theories are approximately true. Compute the probability that a successful theory is approximately true, then find the smallest base rate at which that probability reaches one half, and say what the result shows about the no-miracles argument as usually stated. (c) Take the caloric theory of heat, which appears on Laudan's list. Say what a selective realist must show about its successes to defuse the example, and what Stanford's unconceived-alternatives argument adds that Laudan's list alone does not. (d) In 300–500 words: after the pessimistic induction, which of standard realism, structural realism and constructive empiricism gives the most defensible attitude toward electrons? Any verdict, if each view states what it says we should believe about electrons, the answer engages both the no-miracles argument and the base-rate objection, and the chosen view's hardest objection (the Newman objection, the arbitrariness of the observable line, or the list itself) is met. Part (a) is exegetical; (b) is formal; (c) is exegetical; (d) is evaluative.

The steering adds three topics beyond the roadmap blurb that each need real space: the statistics wars (three lessons, because the frequentist schools, the likelihood principle and the replication crisis are separate arguments), values and objectivity (two lessons), and laws and reduction (two lessons, the second ceded here by [`social-theory`](../social-theory/syllabus.md)). Kuhn takes two lessons so that incommensurability and theory choice are not a footnote to the description of revolutions. Nothing is crammed: Lakatos and Feyerabend share 2.5 because Feyerabend's challenge is taught as a reply to Lakatos's methodology.
- **Verdict-neutral, including about statistics.** Module 3 is where neutrality is hardest, because Jacob's built courses are written in frequentist practice. Lessons present Fisher, Neyman–Pearson, likelihoodism, subjective Bayesianism and error statistics each at the strength its defenders would recognize, and may report accurately where working statisticians sit (for example, that the American Statistical Association issued a statement on p-values warning against common misreadings) without arguing for a school in the lesson's voice.
- **Ownership checks (2026-09-15).** A grep of every built lesson and syllabus found no coverage of Popper, Kuhn's philosophy of science, Lakatos, Feyerabend, Duhem–Quine, the ravens, the pessimistic induction, structural realism in the philosophy-of-science sense, demarcation, or the likelihood principle outside [`philosophy-of-religion`](../philosophy-of-religion/syllabus.md) 3.1, whose own Notes hand the likelihood principle and observation selection effects here as general confirmation theory. The built statistics coverage is cited rather than re-taught: [`prob-stat-refresher`](../prob-stat-refresher/syllabus.md) 4.3 already teaches the correct reading of a p-value, [`econometrics`](../econometrics/syllabus.md) 2.7 multiple testing and pre-registration, [`statistical-learning`](../statistical-learning/syllabus.md) 1.4 the no-free-lunch theorem (which [`epistemology`](../epistemology/syllabus.md) also cites as a bridge to Hume) and 2.5 regularization as a prior. The laws split follows [`metaphysics`](../metaphysics/syllabus.md)'s own table: its 4.3 owns the theories of laws, and 4.4 here owns laws in scientific practice.
- **For `philosophy-of-physics`.** That course lists this one as a prerequisite and should cite, not re-teach: underdetermination and empirical equivalence (2.2), explanation and unification (4.1–4.3), laws in practice (4.4), and the whole realism module, especially structural realism in general (5.3), before taking ontic structural realism into quantum field theory and spacetime. Probability in statistical mechanics and the interpretation of probability in physics are that course's; 3.1 here covers frequency vs degree of belief only as the root of the statistics dispute.
