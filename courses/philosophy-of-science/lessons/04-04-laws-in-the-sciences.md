# Philosophy of Science · Lesson 4.4: Laws in the sciences

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 4: Explanation, laws, and reduction · Builds on: [1.2](01-02-the-new-riddle-of-induction.md), [4.1](04-01-the-deductive-nomological-model.md), [4.2](04-02-causal-explanation.md), [`metaphysics` 4.3](../../metaphysics/lessons/04-03-powers-dispositions-and-laws.md) · Unlocks: [4.5](04-05-reduction-and-emergence.md)

## Why this matters

Your courses have handed you Newton's law of gravitation, Mendel's law of segregation, the law of demand and the law of large numbers: a claim stated without exceptions, a rule that evolved and has exceptions, a rule with a named exception in Giffen goods, and a theorem. Mill noticed: "It is the custom in science, wherever regularity of any kind can be traced, to call the general proposition which expresses the nature of that regularity, a law" (*A System of Logic*, III.iv.1, eighth edition, 1882). That would be harmless if [4.1](04-01-the-deductive-nomological-model.md)'s D-N model did not put a law in every explanation. Whether biology and economics have laws decides whether, on that model, they explain anything.

## The idea

Two true statements about one lift. Everyone in it is wearing a coat. Everyone in it is pulled toward the floor. Ask what would have happened had a coatless courier stepped in: the first fails, the second doesn't.

Traditionally, a law does three jobs an accident can't. It [supports counterfactuals](../reference.md#counterfactual-support), it can explain its instances, and those instances license **projection** to unexamined cases. That is the [lawlike and accidental](../reference.md#lawlike-and-accidental-generalizations) split of [1.2](01-02-the-new-riddle-of-induction.md).

What a law *is* belongs to [`metaphysics` 4.3](../../metaphysics/lessons/04-03-powers-dispositions-and-laws.md): the best-system account (laws describe), necessitation $N(F,G)$ (laws govern), dispositional essentialism (laws fall out of what properties are). That lesson also scored the [gold and uranium spheres](../reference.md#gold-and-uranium-spheres) on all three. So this lesson takes a fresh pair and a different question: what do the sciences ask of laws, do biology and economics have any, and which of those theories fits the practice?

The awkward fact is that outside fundamental physics, the generalizations doing those jobs have exceptions. So they get a hedge, *ceteris paribus*, other things equal, and the question becomes whether anything is left.

## The argument

A composite case that only fundamental physics has laws. Earman and Roberts press P2 and P3, Beatty P4; no one author holds all of it.

**P1. A law supports counterfactuals, can explain, and licenses projection.** Goodman (*Fact, Fiction, and Forecast*, 1955) held that only lawlike statements are confirmed by their instances. Two counterexamples press it. Once a fair coin's first nine flips land heads, "all ten land heads" has risen from $1/1024$ to $1/2$, with the tenth flip still even (Dretske, 1977). If fifty men in a room say they are third sons, expecting the next to say so is reasonable (Jackson and Pargetter, 1980), against [1.2](01-02-the-new-riddle-of-induction.md)'s example. Armstrong and Dretske keep a stronger link: projection to the unexamined runs through a law inferred as the best explanation of the instances. Sober and van Fraassen reply that with the right background beliefs almost anything can be confirmed. *In words:* a law is what you can lean on; how tightly that ties to confirmation is open.

**P2. Stated without a hedge, generalizations outside physics are false.** Mendel's first law meets genes that bias their own transmission; the law of demand meets Giffen goods. Nancy Cartwright (*How the Laws of Physics Lie*, 1983) extends this to physics: Snell's law holds only in isotropic media, and laws read as statements of fact are false, while amended to be true they cover few cases. Earman and Roberts reject that extension. Idealization is the general form of this ([`metaphysics` 1.2](../../metaphysics/lessons/01-02-abstract-objects.md) deferred it here; [`philosophy-of-economics` 6.1](../../philosophy-of-economics/lessons/06-01-idealization-and-isolation.md) works it through for models). *In words:* remove the hedge and the claim is false.

**P3. With a hedge, the claim is false or empty.** A [ceteris paribus](../reference.md#ceteris-paribus-laws) clause is *comparative* if other factors are held fixed and *exclusive* if disturbing factors are absent (Gerhard Schurz, 2002; Cartwright's "other things being right"). Make the exclusion a finite list and the law is strict and probably false. Leave it open, "unless something interferes", and it says that any $A$ is a $B$ unless it isn't, for properties $A$ and $B$. This [falsity-or-triviality dilemma](../reference.md#falsity-or-triviality-dilemma) is Marc Lange's statement (1993) of a problem he credits to Hempel's 1988 paper on provisos. John Earman and John Roberts ("Ceteris Paribus, There Is No Problem of Provisos", 1999; with Smith, 2002) take the hard line: fundamental physics, if true, has proviso-free laws, and hedged sentences lack clear content and cannot be tested. *In words:* the hedge that saves the claim empties it.

**P4. In biology the generalizations are also contingent.** John Beatty's [evolutionary contingency thesis](../reference.md#evolutionary-contingency-thesis) (1995): generalizations about the living world are either just mathematics, physics or chemistry, or distinctively biological, and then they describe contingent outcomes of evolution. Weakly, because the conditions that made a trait common can change. Strongly, because the same conditions and selection pressures can yield functionally equivalent alternatives. Nothing in nature necessitates them. *In words:* replay evolution and the rule differs.

**∴ C. Outside fundamental physics there are generalizations that hold within limits, and no laws.**

**Three replies.**

*Give the hedge content.* Paul Pietroski and Georges Rey (1995) call a ceteris paribus law [non-vacuous](../reference.md#independent-confirmability-condition) when every failure is explained by a factor confirmable independently of that failure, and the law explains something. Earman and Roberts answer that a hedged "all spherical bodies conduct electricity" passes, since molecular structure explains each failure. Lange (*Natural Laws in Scientific Practice*, 2000) ties the hedge to a discipline's policy about which interferers matter, not to excuses added afterwards. Mill's [tendency laws](../reference.md#tendency-laws) and Cartwright's capacities remove the exemption: the law states what a cause contributes in every case, outweighed or not ([`philosophy-of-economics` 6.1](../../philosophy-of-economics/lessons/06-01-idealization-and-isolation.md)). Peter Lipton (1999) objects that specifying what triggers a tendency brings the clause back.

*Deny that contingency disqualifies.* Elliott Sober ("Two Outbreaks of Lawlessness in Recent Philosophy of Biology", 1997): if historical conditions $I$ made "if $F$ then $G$" true, the law is the conditional (if $I$ obtains, then thereafter if $F$ then $G$), which need not be contingent. He leaves open that a law may be a priori. Sandra Mitchell ([pragmatic laws](../reference.md#pragmatic-laws), 1997; *Biological Complexity and Integrative Pluralism*, 2003) answers that physics is contingent too: Fourier's law of heat conduction presumes a universe out of thermal equilibrium. What separates laws from accidents is how stable the conditions are and how strongly they determine the result, and generalizations vary continuously on both.

*Drop the word.* James Woodward and Christopher Hitchcock (2003) replace laws with generalizations [invariant](../reference.md#invariance) under interventions over some range ([4.2](04-02-causal-explanation.md)), allowing that this may be read as reconceiving lawhood.

**A hard case: the law of demand.** Compensated, it is a theorem; uncompensated, a Giffen good is an exception ([`grad-micro` 2.4](../../grad-micro/lessons/02-04-slutsky-equation-comparative-statics.md)). The ordinary statement hedges comparatively (income, tastes, other prices fixed) and exclusively (no rationing, no panic buying). On Mill's reading ([`philosophy-of-economics` 6.1](../../philosophy-of-economics/lessons/06-01-idealization-and-isolation.md)) the substitution effect is a tendency present in every case, outweighed in Giffen cases; on a ceteris paribus reading those cases are exempted. Whether it is a law, and whether it explains, is Boss 4(d).

**Which theory of laws fits the practice?**

- *Best system.* A [better best system](../reference.md#better-best-system) (Jonathan Cohen and Craig Callender, 2009) gives each science its own vocabulary, so Mendel's law can be biology's. John Roberts (2008) replies that nobody weighs simplicity against strength among rival true systems. Armstrong's charge that Humean laws cannot explain their instances gets Barry Loewer's reply (2012): the mosaic grounds the laws metaphysically, and the laws explain the mosaic scientifically, so no circle arises.
- *Necessitation.* It gives projection a direct route through $N(F,G)$. But most generalizations biologists and economists use are then laws only derivatively, if at all.
- *Dispositional essentialism.* Masking makes hedged laws the expected case. But its fundamental laws are necessary and Beatty's generalizations are not, so biology's rules come out as arrangements of powers.

On Mitchell's pragmatic approach the question is misposed: her target is a single test for lawhood, and each of the three offers one.

**Where the argument is weakest.** P1, which packs three jobs into one category with one line. The strongest objection, Mitchell's, is that the jobs come apart: Mendel's law explains and projects across a wide but bounded range while failing the necessity test, so a definition only fundamental physics can meet is a stipulation that leaves biology's success unexplained. The strongest reply is that if lawhood comes in degrees it stops marking a difference in what nature does, and "law" becomes a compliment. Mitchell concedes that physics and biology may still occupy distinct regions of her space. The strict side concedes that most working science then explains and projects without laws, which puts the burden on [4.1](04-01-the-deductive-nomological-model.md)'s requirement rather than on biology.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean case: [Popper's moa](../reference.md#poppers-moa), with a partner).** In *The Logic of Scientific Discovery* (1959, appendix \*x), Karl Popper supposes that the moa, an extinct New Zealand bird, could live sixty years or more in good conditions, but that a virus saw to it that no moa ever reached fifty. Then "all moas die before fifty" is true of every moa, and it is no law. Add a partner, this lesson's and not Popper's: "all moas die before five hundred", supposing physiology forbids a moa that old.

| Generalization | Had the virus been absent | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| Die before fifty | some would have passed fifty | accidental |
| Die before five hundred | none would have reached it | lawlike |

Same form, same subject, both true. Every dead moa is an instance of both, so the record cannot separate them: the separating is done by what we believe about moa bodies, the [1.2](01-02-the-new-riddle-of-induction.md) situation again.

**Example 2 (hard case: Mendel's law, contingent and still explaining).** A pea hybrid for seed colour is crossed with a green-seeded recessive, and about half the seeds are green. Why? Because each gamete of the hybrid carries one of its two alleles with equal chance ([`genetics` 1.1](../../genetics/lessons/01-01-mendels-laws-probability.md)). Had the hybrid set more seeds, those too would have split evenly, so the counterfactual holds. But fair segregation is itself a genetic trait under selection, segregation distorters exist, and on another evolutionary history the ratio could differ.

- *Beatty:* nothing necessitates the even split, so it is no law. Nothing in the thesis denies that it explains this cross.
- *The D-N model:* R2 requires a law, so either this is no D-N explanation or "law" must loosen.
- *Sober:* the law is the conditional from the historical conditions to the rule.
- *Woodward and Hitchcock:* it explains because it is invariant across the crosses at issue.
- *Mitchell:* it functions as a law over a known range, and the range is what you must know before relying on it.

Everyone agrees about the biology. They part over whether "law" requires necessity; Sober reports agreeing with Beatty that there are no empirical laws of evolution, and differing on what a law may be. Whether that makes the dispute verbal ([`philosophical-method` 3.2](../../philosophical-method/lessons/03-02-distinctions-and-verbal-disputes.md)) is where the accounts stop: Beatty holds that the word marks a real difference in what nature necessitates, and Mitchell denies that the difference is one of kind.

## Watch out

- **You might think a ceteris paribus law and a tendency law are the same, but actually** the first exempts cases and the second says the cause operates in every case, even when outweighed. They disagree about a Giffen good.
- **You might think Beatty's "contingent" is the metaphysician's, but actually** the best-system and necessitation accounts already make the laws of physics metaphysically contingent. Beatty means contingent on evolutionary history, which is how he keeps those laws and denies biology's.
- **You might think Mitchell calls every useful generalization a law, but actually** she rejects extending the title by decree as firmly as the all-or-nothing norm.
- **You might think ceteris paribus means "usually", but actually** Earman, Roberts, Pietroski and Rey all treat it as a condition; reading it as high probability is a rival proposal (Schurz's normality theory).

## One-liner

> Science asks laws to support counterfactuals, explain and project; outside physics, hedged or evolved generalizations do those jobs, so the live question is whether "law" should follow the jobs or the necessity.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢) *(Exegetical.)*** Say whether each statement is lawlike or accidental, name the test you used, and say in one clause what might mislead a quick reader. One line each. Take "lawlike" as [1.2](01-02-the-new-riddle-of-induction.md) did: a law if true.

(a) All twelve people who walked on the Moon in the Apollo programme were born before 1936.

(b) Near the Earth's surface, a freely falling body accelerates at about 9.8 metres per second squared.

(c) A body on which no force acts keeps a constant velocity.

(d) In a vacuum, heavier bodies fall faster than lighter ones.

**P2 (🟡) *(Evaluative (a), (c); Exegetical (b). Counterexample.)*** Take "ceteris paribus, plants given more nitrogen grow taller."

(a) Write a ceteris paribus clause that makes the sentence impossible to falsify. One sentence.

(b) State Pietroski and Rey's condition on a non-vacuous ceteris paribus law, and say what it requires of a fertilized plant that does not grow taller. Two sentences.

(c) Earman and Roberts say the condition lets through a hedged "all spherical bodies conduct electricity". Build your own case of the same kind, and say what such cases show the condition is missing. Two sentences in all.

**P3 (🔴) *(Evaluative. Apply to a hard case.)*** Are there laws of biology? Use Hardy–Weinberg as your test case ([`evolution-ecology` 1.3](../../evolution-ecology/lessons/01-03-hardy-weinberg-testable-null.md)): in a large, randomly mating population with Mendelian inheritance and no selection, mutation or migration, genotype frequencies reach $p^2$, $2pq$ and $q^2$ after one generation, where $p$ and $q$ are the allele frequencies. Say what you require of a law, apply it, and state the cost of your verdict. Any verdict; 150 words or fewer.

<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict:**

- (a) Accidental, by the counterfactual test: had the programme flown an astronaut born in 1940, he would have walked on the Moon. What misleads is a perfect record.
- (b) Lawlike, though derived from gravitation together with the Earth's mass and radius. Counterfactual test: had this stone been dropped, it would have accelerated so. What misleads is that it names a place.
- (c) Lawlike, by the counterfactual test. What misleads is that no actual body satisfies the antecedent, so the instance test has nothing to run on.
- (d) Lawlike, and false. Lawlike means a law if true, and if true this would support counterfactuals about falling bodies. What misleads is its falsity.

**Wrong turns:** treating "names a place" or "has no instances" as marks of an accident. Calling (d) accidental because it is false: truth is a different property. Using "true of every case" as the test, which (a) passes.

**Model answer:** (a) Accidental, counterfactual test; the record is perfect, but a younger astronaut would have counted. (b) Lawlike, counterfactual test; naming the Earth does not make it an accident. (c) Lawlike, counterfactual test; no body is free of all force, so instances cannot test it. (d) Lawlike, law-if-true test; being false is beside the point.

---

**P2** *(Evaluative (a), (c); Exegetical (b))*

**Must hit, any verdict (a):** a clause every failure satisfies, such as "unless they do not" or "unless anything at all prevents it".

**Must hit, strict (b):**

- The condition: the generalization is non-vacuous when $A$ and $B$ are otherwise nomological, every $A$ that is not $B$ has its failure explained by a factor confirmable independently of that failure, and the law explains something.
- So the plant that does not grow needs an explanation (drought, salinity, disease) detectable apart from its failure to grow.

**Must hit, any verdict (c):**

- A generalization whose antecedent is irrelevant to its consequent, every failure of which has an independently confirmable explanation.
- The diagnosis: the condition polices the exceptions and never the link between antecedent and consequent. Accept a statement in terms of causal relevance, invariance or normality.

**Accept (c):** a defender who presses the third clause, "explains something", provided the answer says what explaining would then add.

**Wrong turns:** (a) listing named interferers, which gives the other horn, a strict and probably false claim. (b) Treating "explained by some factor" as enough, when the factor must be confirmable apart from the failure. (c) Using a relevant antecedent, such as "metals conduct electricity".

**Model answer (b):** Pietroski and Rey require that the predicates be otherwise nomological, that every failure be explained by an independently confirmable factor, and that the law explain something, so the stunted plant must show drought, salinity or disease detectable apart from its stuntedness.

**Model answer (a), (c), one of several:** (a) "Ceteris paribus" means "unless anything at all stops them", so every failure is covered in advance. (c) "Ceteris paribus, all bodies over a kilogram are magnetic": composition, confirmable independently, explains every failure. Such cases show the condition constrains the exceptions and not the connection between antecedent and consequent.

---

**P3** *(Evaluative, any verdict)*

**Must hit, any verdict:**

- A stated criterion of law.
- Locate the contingency: as a conditional, Hardy–Weinberg is provable mathematics; its empirical premise, Mendelian inheritance, is on Beatty's account an evolved and breakable outcome.
- Engage one reply and name its cost. Sober: laws may be a priori, so empirical content moves to the antecedent. Mitchell: laws come in degrees, so "law" no longer marks a kind. Strict denial: biology explains without laws, so [4.1](04-01-the-deductive-nomological-model.md)'s R2 goes.

**Wrong turns:** treating departures from equilibrium in real populations as settling it, when they are how biologists detect evolution. "It is a priori, so not a law", without addressing whether laws must be empirical, which Sober denies. A verdict with no stated criterion.

**Model answer, one of several:** If a law must be an empirical generalization that nature necessitates, Hardy–Weinberg is not one. As a conditional it is arithmetic, and its empirical premise, Mendelian inheritance, is on Beatty's account an evolved and breakable outcome. Sober keeps laws by allowing a priori ones, at the price that the empirical content moves to the antecedent. Mitchell fits what biologists do: they rely on Hardy–Weinberg over a known range and treat departures as signals. But then "law" grades rather than divides, and nothing says how stable is stable enough. I conclude that biology has no laws in the strict sense, and that this matters less than it seems, because it explains without them. The cost is Hempel's R2.

## Flashback

**From Lesson [4.2](04-02-causal-explanation.md) (Causal explanation):** *(Formal (a) · Exegetical (b).)* A bookshop chain's weekly records (invented, written for this problem), with $P$ as relative frequency. $Z$: holiday week, $P(Z)=0.2$. $X$: a discount runs, $P(X\mid Z)=0.8$, $P(X\mid\neg Z)=0.2$. $Y$: sales target met, with $P(Y\mid X,Z)=0.9$, $P(Y\mid\neg X,Z)=0.7$, $P(Y\mid X,\neg Z)=0.4$, $P(Y\mid\neg X,\neg Z)=0.2$.

(a) Compute the naive comparison $P(Y\mid X)-P(Y\mid\neg X)$. Then, taking the graph $Z\to X$, $Z\to Y$, $X\to Y$, compute $P(Y\mid\mathrm{do}(X{=}1))-P(Y\mid\mathrm{do}(X{=}0))$ and name what the gap between the two answers is.

(b) The discount is announced on blue flyers. An analyst says tracing each flyer from shop to till will show which feature of the flyer sold the books. What can that tracing show, what does Hitchcock (1995) say it cannot, and how would Woodward's test separate price from colour? One sentence each.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

(a) First the weights: $P(X)=0.2(0.8)+0.8(0.2)=0.32$, so $P(Z\mid X)=0.16/0.32=\tfrac{1}{2}$ and $P(Z\mid\neg X)=0.04/0.68=\tfrac{1}{17}$. Then

$$P(Y\mid X)=\tfrac{1}{2}(0.9)+\tfrac{1}{2}(0.4)=\tfrac{13}{20},\qquad P(Y\mid\neg X)=\tfrac{1}{17}(0.7)+\tfrac{16}{17}(0.2)=\tfrac{39}{170},$$

and the naive comparison is $\tfrac{13}{20}-\tfrac{39}{170}=\tfrac{143}{340}\approx 0.421$.

Intervening replaces the line for $X$, so $Z$ keeps its own distribution:

$$P(Y\mid\mathrm{do}(X{=}1))=0.9(0.2)+0.4(0.8)=0.50,\qquad P(Y\mid\mathrm{do}(X{=}0))=0.7(0.2)+0.2(0.8)=0.30.$$

The effect is $0.20$. The gap, $\tfrac{143}{340}-\tfrac{1}{5}=\tfrac{15}{68}\approx 0.221$, is selection term: discounts cluster in holiday weeks, which meet the target more often anyway.

**Must hit, strict (b):**

- Tracing shows a [causal process](../reference.md#causal-processes-and-mark-transmission) from shop to till, so it shows where influence can travel.

- Hitchcock: the flyer carries its price and its blueness alike, so the network cannot say which property matters.

- Woodward: intervene on colour with price fixed, and on price with colour fixed. Whichever changes $Y$ is the difference-maker.

**Wrong turns:** using $P(Y\mid X)$ for $P(Y\mid\mathrm{do}(X{=}1))$. Expecting $Z$ to screen off $X$, as pressure screened off the needle: here $P(Y\mid X,Z)=0.9$ and $P(Y\mid Z)=0.86$, so $X$ still matters within the cell. Saying the process account gives the wrong answer, when it gives none on this question.

**Model answer (b):** Tracing shows that a causal process connects shop and till, so influence could travel that way. Hitchcock says the same flyer carries the price and the colour alike, so the network cannot tell which property mattered. Woodward would change the colour with the price held fixed, and the price with the colour held fixed, and see which one moves $Y$.
