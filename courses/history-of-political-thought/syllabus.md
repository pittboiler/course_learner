# History of Political Thought — Syllabus

> Politics & Society · Tier 1 · ~27 lessons · Prereqs: none (the reconstruction tools of [`philosophical-method`](../philosophical-method/syllabus.md) help but are introduced where used) · Roadmap id: `history-of-political-thought`

## Goal

Read the great political texts from Plato to Marx and Tocqueville as moves in real arguments: what each thinker was answering, what they actually argued, and what they changed for everyone after them. You will finish able to close-read a passage of the *Republic*, *Leviathan* or *Democracy in America* and say which interpretation its words support, reconstruct a thinker's central argument in their own terms, find the premise two thinkers really disagree about, and spot which old idea a modern political argument is borrowing, usually without knowing it. The course owns the texts, context and full thought of each thinker, including the social-contract theorists, whose authority argument [`political-philosophy`](../political-philosophy/syllabus.md) reconstructs analytically. It deliberately skips twentieth-century theory (owned by political-philosophy), non-Western political thought (Confucian, Islamic and Indian traditions deserve their own course, not a token lesson), the Reformation resistance theorists, Bodin, Grotius, Hume, Kant, Paine, Constant, Bentham and Nietzsche as political writers, and any attempt to crown a winner.

## How practice works in this course

Problems follow [HUMANITIES-BUILD-BRIEF.md](../../HUMANITIES-BUILD-BRIEF.md), verdict-neutral: Plato and the democrat, Burke and Wollstonecraft, Marx and Tocqueville are each presented at the strength their best readers would recognize, and no rubric rewards siding with any of them. Four archetypes carry the course:

- **Close reading** is the workhorse: a public-domain passage of 150 words or fewer, and the question of which interpretation its words support. Graded strictly as exegesis.
- **Reconstruct**: a thinker's argument in numbered premises, in *their* terms (Hobbes's laws of nature are theorems of self-preservation, not moral duties in the modern sense), with the weakest premise flagged.
- **Find the crux**: two thinkers answering the same question (Aristotle and Plato on who should rule, Locke and Rousseau on property), and the single premise one must deny.
- **Diagnose**: an invented op-ed, speech or policy memo, and the question of whose idea it is borrowing and what it has dropped from the original. Invented material only; no invented words are ever attributed to a real person.

Evaluative parts (who has the better of an exchange, whether an idea survives its context) appear in most lessons and every boss problem, graded on the moves, never the verdict. A recurring exegetical discipline is **anachronism**: marking where a modern reader imports a later meaning (Aristotle's *politeia* is not "constitution" in the American sense; Machiavelli's *virtù* is not virtue).

**Texts.** All close-reading passages come from public-domain translations: Plato and Aristotle (Jowett), Cicero (Yonge), Augustine's *City of God* (Dods, in the *Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers*), Aquinas's *Summa* (English Dominican Province), Machiavelli (Marriott for *The Prince*, Thomson for the *Discourses*), Montesquieu (Nugent), Rousseau (Cole), Hegel's *Philosophy of Right* (Dyde), the *Communist Manifesto* (Moore) and *Eighteenth Brumaire* (De Leon), Tocqueville (Reeve); Hobbes, Locke, *The Federalist*, Burke, Wollstonecraft and Mill in the original English. Where no public-domain English translation is available (Marsilius's *Defensor Pacis*, Aquinas's *De Regno*, several of Marx's shorter writings), lessons paraphrase and cite, and those texts are never used for close reading.

## Dangerous Checklist

When you finish, you can:

- [ ] Close-read a short passage from any text in the course and defend an interpretation by pointing to the words that decide it
- [ ] Say what question a canonical text was answering in its own time, and name one way a modern reading distorts it
- [ ] Reconstruct Plato's case against democracy and Aristotle's summation argument for the many, and name the crux between them
- [ ] Explain how Augustine and Aquinas each relate political order to justice, and what Marsilius and the conciliarists did to the medieval theory of authority
- [ ] Contrast Machiavelli's two books, and say whether *The Prince* and the *Discourses* are one teaching or two
- [ ] Reconstruct Hobbes's route from mechanistic psychology to an absolute sovereign, including why he spends half of *Leviathan* on scripture and the church
- [ ] Explain Locke's labor theory of property and its provisos, and Rousseau's rival story of property as the origin of inequality
- [ ] Trace the separation of powers from Montesquieu to *Federalist* 51, and say what the extended-republic argument of *Federalist* 10 changes about the small-republic tradition
- [ ] State Burke's case against abstract rights and Wollstonecraft's case for rights grounded in reason and virtue, each at full strength
- [ ] Explain what Hegel meant by freedom realized in the state, what Marx kept of Hegel and what he reversed, and what Marx thought the state is for
- [ ] Explain Tocqueville's equality of conditions, tyranny of the majority and soft despotism, and why Mill thought representative government had to cultivate active citizens
- [ ] Take a modern political argument and diagnose which thinker's idea it is borrowing, and what it has left behind

## Modules

### Module 1: The Greek polis

Plato and Aristotle on the question the whole tradition inherits: what is a political community for, and who should rule it?

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1.1 | Plato's *Republic*: justice in the city and the soul | Read the *Republic* as an answer to Thrasymachus, and learn to read an old text in its context | Thrasymachus's challenge, the city-soul analogy, the three classes, justice as each part doing its own work; reading in context vs as a perennial answer, **close reading** |
| 1.2 | Plato against democracy, and the second-best city | Reconstruct Plato's case for rule by knowledge and see what the *Laws* concedes | the ship of state, philosopher-rulers, the decline of regimes (Book VIII), rulers as servants of the law in the *Laws*, the mixed constitution |
| 1.3 | Aristotle: the city exists by nature | Reconstruct the argument that humans are political animals and see Aristotle's critique of Plato | the polis by nature, household and city, natural slavery and its critics then and now, the critique of the *Republic*'s communism |
| 1.4 | Aristotle: constitutions, citizens and the polity | Classify regimes Aristotle's way and reconstruct his case for rule by the many | six regimes (correct vs deviant), the citizen as ruling and being ruled, the summation argument, polity and the middle class, *politeia* vs "constitution" |

**Boss problem 1:** Take the ship-of-state passage from *Republic* Book VI and the summation passage from *Politics* III.11, in which Aristotle argues that the many, taken together, may judge better than the few best (both Jowett). (a) Close-read each passage and reconstruct its argument in numbered premises. (b) Name the crux: the single premise about political knowledge that one side must deny. (c) Diagnose an invented op-ed proposing that votes be weighted by a civic-knowledge test: which of the two arguments does it borrow, and what has it dropped from the original? (d) In 300-500 words, assess whether Aristotle's argument answers Plato's or talks past it, any verdict, if the crux from (b) is used. Parts (a)-(c) are exegetical; (d) is evaluative.

### Module 2: Rome and Christendom

From the Roman republic's claim to embody natural law, through Augustine's demotion of every earthly city, to the medieval fight over who holds authority and why.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2.1 | Cicero: the commonwealth and the law of nature | Reconstruct Cicero's definition of a *res publica* and his case for natural law | *res publica* as the affair of a people, the mixed constitution (from Polybius), true law as right reason, the Stoic inheritance |
| 2.2 | Augustine: the two cities | Read *City of God* as a reply to pagan blame for the sack of Rome, and reconstruct its political argument | the earthly and heavenly cities, *libido dominandi*, kingdoms without justice as great robberies (Book IV), the redefinition of a people (Book XIX), the peace of Babylon |
| 2.3 | Aquinas on law | Reconstruct the definition of law and the fourfold scheme, and say when a human law binds | the definition of law (ST I-II q.90), eternal, natural, human and divine law, derivation of human law, unjust laws and conscience, why human law does not forbid every vice |
| 2.4 | Aquinas on kingship, tyranny and property | See how Aquinas turns Aristotle's politics into a Christian theory of rule | monarchy and the mixed regime, tyranny and the limits of resistance, the common good, property and extreme necessity; Aquinas as political theorist, not metaphysician |
| 2.5 | Pope, emperor and council: Marsilius and conciliarism | Trace how the fight over supreme authority produced theories of consent and representation | the two swords, *Unam Sanctam* (1302), Marsilius's *Defensor Pacis* (1324) and the human legislator, the Council of Constance and the council over the pope |

**Boss problem 2:** Take the robber-band passage from *City of God* Book IV and Aquinas's article on whether human law binds in conscience (ST I-II q.96 a.4), which draws on Augustine. (a) Close-read the Augustine passage: does it claim that a state without justice is not a state at all, or only that it is no better than a gang? Point to the words that decide it, and check your reading against Augustine's later redefinition of a people in Book XIX. (b) Reconstruct Aquinas's conditions for a law's binding force in numbered premises. (c) Name the crux between Augustine's Book XIX and Aquinas on whether political authority requires justice or only a shared peace. (d) Steelman the view that Aquinas has quietly abandoned Augustine's realism about earthly power, then reply as a reader who thinks the two are continuous, 150 words each. Parts (a)-(c) are exegetical; (d) is evaluative.

### Module 3: Machiavelli and Hobbes: the state without a common good

A Florentine and an English answer to the collapse of shared authority: rule as an art of necessity, and a sovereign built from fear and consent.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3.1 | Machiavelli's *Prince*: *virtù* and *fortuna* | Read *The Prince* against the mirror-for-princes genre it overturns | Florence and the Medici, *virtù* vs Christian and classical virtue, better feared than loved, appearing good, *fortuna*, the closing call to redeem Italy |
| 3.2 | Machiavelli's *Discourses*: the republic of conflict | See the republican Machiavelli and decide how the two books fit together | class conflict as the source of Roman liberty, the armed citizen, civil religion, return to first principles, one teaching or two |
| 3.3 | Hobbes I: matter, motion and the state of nature | Rebuild the state of nature from Hobbes's own premises about mind and science | the English Civil War, mechanistic psychology, equality and the three causes of quarrel (ch. 13), the right and laws of nature as theorems, the geometry model of political science |
| 3.4 | Hobbes II: the sovereign, the church and the Kingdom of Darkness | Reconstruct authorization and absolute sovereignty, and see why Hobbes rewrites Christian politics | persons and authorization (ch. 16), the rights of sovereigns, the true liberty of subjects (ch. 21), the Christian commonwealth, the sovereign over the church, the papacy as the ghost of Rome (ch. 47) |

**Boss problem 3:** Take *The Prince* chapter 18 (on whether princes should keep faith) and *Leviathan* chapter 21 (the true liberty of subjects). (a) Close-read the Hobbes passage: what liberties does a subject keep against the sovereign, and on what ground? (b) Reconstruct each thinker's account of why a ruler may break ordinary moral rules, in numbered premises, and find the crux: is necessity a feature of the ruler's situation or built into the institution of sovereignty? (c) Diagnose an invented memo arguing that a head of government needs unreviewable emergency powers: is it borrowing Machiavelli, Hobbes, or both, and what would each object to in it? (d) In 300-500 words, assess whether Hobbes's authorization theory tames Machiavelli or systematizes him, any verdict, if (a) and (b) are used. Parts (a)-(c) are exegetical; (d) is evaluative.

### Module 4: Consent, property and the general will

Locke and Rousseau, read as full thinkers and not only as contract theorists: one builds limited government and toleration on property and consent, the other diagnoses property as the root of inequality and seeks freedom in collective self-rule.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4.1 | Locke I: against Filmer, and the origin of property | Read the *Second Treatise* as a reply to divine-right patriarchalism and reconstruct the labor theory of property | Filmer's patriarchalism, the state of nature under natural law, mixing labor, the spoilage and "enough and as good" provisos, money |
| 4.2 | Locke II: limited government, revolution and toleration | Reconstruct Locke's limits on government and his argument for toleration, including its exclusions | the Exclusion Crisis and 1688, consent and tacit consent, the dissolution of government, the *Letter Concerning Toleration* (1689), why atheists and Catholics were excluded |
| 4.3 | Rousseau I: the *Discourse on Inequality* | Reconstruct Rousseau's conjectural history of how inequality arose | natural man, pity and perfectibility, *amour-propre*, the first enclosure, the fraudulent social contract of the rich |
| 4.4 | Rousseau II: the *Social Contract* | Reconstruct the general will and see how it answers the *Discourse*'s problem | the total alienation, general will vs will of all, "forced to be free" (I.7), the lawgiver, civil religion, the small republic |

**Boss problem 4:** Take *Second Treatise* chapter V (Of Property) and the opening of Part II of the *Discourse on Inequality*, which begins with the first man to enclose a piece of ground (Cole). (a) Reconstruct Locke's argument from self-ownership to private property, including both provisos. (b) Close-read the Rousseau passage: is it a denial that property can be legitimate, or a claim about how it actually arose? Point to the words that decide it. (c) Name the crux between the two on what makes the first appropriation legitimate. (d) Diagnose an invented speech defending a settler's claim to unused land by the work they put into it: whose argument is it, and which proviso has it dropped? (e) Assess in 300-500 words whether Locke's provisos answer Rousseau's charge, any verdict, if (c) is used. Parts (a)-(d) are exegetical; (e) is evaluative.

### Module 5: The age of revolutions

The eighteenth century designs constitutions and argues over the French Revolution: liberty through divided power, through the extended republic, through inherited prescription, and through rights of reason extended to women.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5.1 | Montesquieu: the spirit of the laws | Reconstruct the case for separated powers and the claim that laws must fit their people | *The Spirit of the Laws* (1748), the three governments and their principles, the English constitution and the separation of powers, commerce as softening, climate and relativity |
| 5.2 | *The Federalist*: faction and the extended republic | Reconstruct *Federalist* 10 and 51 and see how they overturn the small-republic tradition | Publius (1787-88), faction and its causes, the extended republic, ambition counteracting ambition, the Anti-Federalist reply from Montesquieu and Rousseau |
| 5.3 | Burke: prescription against abstract rights | Reconstruct Burke's case against the French Revolution at full strength | *Reflections on the Revolution in France* (1790), prescription and inheritance, society as a partnership across generations, the little platoons, prudence vs metaphysical rights, Paine's reply |
| 5.4 | Wollstonecraft: rights, virtue and the education of women | Reconstruct Wollstonecraft's case against Burke and against Rousseau on women | *A Vindication of the Rights of Men* (1790) and *of Woman* (1792), reason and virtue as the ground of rights, dependence as corruption, the critique of Rousseau's Sophie |

**Boss problem 5:** A legislature proposes replacing its upper chamber with binding national referenda held by phone app. (a) Close-read *Federalist* 51's passage on ambition counteracting ambition and reconstruct its argument for divided power. (b) Say how Madison, Burke and Wollstonecraft would each diagnose the proposal, citing the idea from their texts that does the work; each diagnosis graded strictly for fidelity to the thinker. (c) Name the crux between Burke's and Wollstonecraft's views of inherited institutions. (d) In 300-500 words, assess the proposal using at least two of the three thinkers, any verdict, if each is represented at full strength. Parts (a)-(c) are exegetical; (d) is evaluative.

### Module 6: The nineteenth century: freedom, capital and democracy

After the revolutions: Hegel's claim that freedom is realized in the modern state, Tocqueville's and Mill's worry that democracy may smother it, and Marx's claim that the state is class rule to be overcome.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 6.1 | Hegel: freedom realized in the state | Reconstruct the structure of the *Philosophy of Right* and what Hegel means by the state | abstract right, morality, ethical life, family and civil society and state, the rabble and poverty, the owl of Minerva |
| 6.2 | Tocqueville I: equality of conditions | Read *Democracy in America* as a Frenchman's report on the democratic future | equality of conditions as a providential fact, the township, associations, religion and democratic mores, individualism |
| 6.3 | Tocqueville II: tyranny of the majority and soft despotism | Reconstruct Tocqueville's two dangers of democracy and the remedies he sees | tyranny of the majority over opinion, centralization, soft despotism (Vol. II, Part 4), the art of association, *The Old Regime and the Revolution* (1856) in brief |
| 6.4 | Mill: representative government | Reconstruct Mill's case for representative government as a school of active citizens | *Considerations on Representative Government* (1861), active vs passive character, Hare's proportional representation, plural voting, the open ballot, Mill's reading of Tocqueville |
| 6.5 | Marx I: from Hegel to historical materialism | See what Marx took from Hegel and reversed, and reconstruct the materialist theory of history | the critique of Hegel, political vs human emancipation, alienated labor (1844), base and superstructure (the 1859 Preface), class struggle in the *Manifesto* (1848) |
| 6.6 | Marx II: the state, revolution and the society after | Reconstruct Marx's account of what the state is and what replaces it | the state as executive committee of the bourgeoisie, Bonapartism and relative autonomy (*Eighteenth Brumaire*), the Paris Commune, dictatorship of the proletariat, "from each according to his ability" (Gotha critique) |

**Boss problem 6:** Tocqueville's soft despotism, Mill's passive citizen and Marx's state as class rule are three diagnoses of what threatens freedom in modern society. (a) Close-read Tocqueville's description of the immense tutelary power (Reeve, Vol. II, Part 4) and the *Manifesto*'s description of the modern state (Moore), and reconstruct each claim in numbered premises. (b) Name the crux between Tocqueville and Marx: what premise about the relation between the state and civil society must one of them deny? (c) Diagnose an invented op-ed that blames a decline in civic life on both welfare bureaucracy and corporate power: which strands come from Tocqueville, which from Marx, and where does it contradict itself? (d) In 300-500 words, assess which diagnosis Hegel's account of civil society and the state is better placed to answer, any verdict, if all three thinkers are represented at full strength. Parts (a)-(c) are exegetical; (d) is evaluative.

## Notes

- **The contract-theorist split.** Hobbes, Locke and Rousseau are owned here, as whole thinkers in their own contexts (Modules 3-4). [`political-philosophy`](../political-philosophy/syllabus.md) currently teaches them in its Module 1 as social-contract theorists; that module is to be rewritten to reconstruct the authority problem analytically (state-of-nature device, consent, obligation, anarchism) and link to 3.3-3.4, 4.1-4.4 here for the thinkers themselves. The same course owns Mill's harm principle (its 3.2); Lesson 6.4 here covers only *Considerations on Representative Government* and links there.
- **Cited by.** `catholic-social-teaching` cites 2.2-2.4 for Augustine and Aquinas as political theorists; `social-theory` cites 6.5-6.6 for Marx on the state and 6.2-6.3 for Tocqueville, and owns Marx as a sociologist. `constitutional-law` and `political-institutions` cite 5.1-5.2 for the theory behind separated powers and federalism. `ancient-medieval-philosophy` and `modern-philosophy` own the non-political philosophy of Plato, Aristotle, Aquinas and Hegel.
- **Bridges.** Aristotle's summation argument (1.4) is often read as a forerunner of the jury theorem in `social-choice`; *Federalist* 10's faction problem is the informal version of the collective-action and interest-group models in [`political-economy`](../political-economy/syllabus.md); Aquinas on law (2.3) is the historical root of natural-law jurisprudence in `philosophy-of-law`.
- **Why Marsilius earns a lesson.** Without 2.5 the course jumps from Aquinas's kingship to Machiavelli's prince and misses where consent and representation entered the tradition: in the fight over whether pope, emperor or council holds supreme authority. `church-history` owns Constance as an event; this lesson owns the argument.
- **Lesson count.** The roadmap estimated ~24; the syllabus runs 27 (+12.5 percent, inside tolerance). The extra three come from giving Hobbes, Locke and Rousseau two lessons each (required once political-philosophy cedes the thinkers to this course) and from splitting Marx and Tocqueville, both owned here for downstream courses, into two lessons apiece rather than cramming either into ten minutes.
- **Deliberate omissions** are listed in the Goal; if a later course needs one of them (Kant's political writings, Constant's liberty of the ancients and moderns, Bentham), it should own it rather than this course growing a lesson.
