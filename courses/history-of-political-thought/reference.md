# History of Political Thought · Reference Card

> Open book. This card is meant to be open while you work — including during
> quizzes. Nothing here is something you're expected to recall cold.

The course reads Plato to Marx and Tocqueville as a sequence of answers to one question: what is a political community for, and who should rule it? Plato gives rule to knowledge and Aristotle to a mixture anchored by the middle; Cicero ties the commonwealth to natural law, Augustine denies any earthly city true justice, Aquinas rebuilds law as reason for the common good, and Marsilius hands supreme authority to the human legislator. Machiavelli and Hobbes build the state without a shared good, Locke and Rousseau rebuild it on consent, property and the general will, and the age of revolutions argues over divided power, inherited prescription and the rights of reason. The nineteenth century asks whether the modern state realizes freedom (Hegel), smothers it (Tocqueville, Mill) or is class rule to be overcome (Marx). Every text is read first as a move in its own argument, in its own setting. The standing discipline is **anachronism**: *politeia* is not a written constitution, *polis* is not the modern state, *dikaiosyne* is not social justice, *res publica* is not a republic as opposed to a monarchy, *virtù* is not virtue, *amour-propre* is not selfishness, Hegel's "state" is not the government, Marx's "dictatorship" is not a one-man regime. Use the card mid-problem to look up a term, a position, an argument's premises, who said it where, which translation a passage comes from, and how to read a citation. Problems ask you to *use* these, not recite them.

## Positions and distinctions

### Thrasymachus on justice

Justice is whatever serves those in power, so the just person is a dupe.

"The different forms of government make laws democratical, aristocratical, tyrannical, with a view to their several interests; and these laws ... are the justice which they deliver to their subjects" (*Republic* I 338e-339a). A claim about *every* regime; justice is obedience delivered to subjects, and rulers are not bound by it. Justice is "another's good" (343c); the tyrant is happiest.

*Introduced:* [1.1](lessons/01-01-platos-republic-justice-in-city-and-soul.md)

### Glaucon on justice as a compromise

Justice is a truce the weak strike because they cannot do wrong with impunity.

The "received account" (358e-359b): justice is "a mean or compromise, between the best of all, which is to do injustice and not be punished, and the worst of all, which is to suffer injustice without the power of retaliation". The ring of Gyges tests it. Glaucon does not hold the view (358c); he states it at full strength so Socrates must defeat it. Opposite story from Thrasymachus: the weak write the law together against the strong.

*Introduced:* [1.1](lessons/01-01-platos-republic-justice-in-city-and-soul.md)

### Justice as each part doing its own work

Justice is a structure that keeps each part to its function, in the city and in the soul.

"Doing one's own business, and not being a busybody" (433a-b). A cobbler doing carpentry does little harm; a trader pushing into ruling ruins the city (434a-c). In the soul: reason rules, spirit is its ally, appetite is governed (441d-442b). Justice is the soul's health, injustice its civil war (444c-e). A claim about structure, not about who gets what share.

*Introduced:* [1.1](lessons/01-01-platos-republic-justice-in-city-and-soul.md)

### The city-soul analogy

Look for justice in the city first, as a short-sighted man reads large letters before small.

Justice is said of both person and city (368e); the city is larger, so easier to see. The match is offered as a hypothesis: "if they were the same" (368d); "if there be a difference in the individual, we will come back to the State and have another trial of the theory" (434d-435a). Pressure points: a just city from unjust citizens; whether inner order guarantees ordinary just acts.

*Introduced:* [1.1](lessons/01-01-platos-republic-justice-in-city-and-soul.md)

### Three ways to read the Republic

The frame you bring decides the reading before the words do.

**Perennial blueprint:** Popper, *The Open Society and Its Enemies* (1945): a closed caste society ruled by propaganda. **Perennial question, answered ironically:** a reading associated with Leo Strauss, *The City and Man* (1964): the city in speech shows the limits of politics. **In context:** Skinner, "Meaning and Understanding in the History of Ideas" (1969): ask what the author was *doing*; the noble lie adapts Athenian autochthony myth so that rank follows merit, not birth.

*Introduced:* [1.1](lessons/01-01-platos-republic-justice-in-city-and-soul.md)

### Rule by knowledge

Ruling is a craft, and the craft belongs to whoever knows the good.

"Until philosophers are kings, or the kings and princes of this world have the spirit and power of philosophy ... cities will never have rest from their evils" (*Republic* V 473c-d). The many cannot tell knower from flatterer (ship of state, beast-tamer). Knowledge is necessary but not sufficient: guardians have no private property or families (416d-417b), and the best rulers are reluctant (520d).

*Introduced:* [1.2](lessons/01-02-plato-against-democracy-and-the-second-best-city.md)

### The decline of regimes

Each regime is ruined by insatiable pursuit of its own good.

*Republic* VIII: aristocracy, timocracy, oligarchy, democracy, tyranny. Oligarchy chases wealth and makes a city of rich and poor (551d); democracy's "own good" is freedom, whose "insatiable desire ... and the neglect of other things" brings "a demand for tyranny" (562b-c). A typology ordered by psychology (each regime has its soul-type), not a history of Greece.

*Introduced:* [1.2](lessons/01-02-plato-against-democracy-and-the-second-best-city.md)

### Law as second best

Knowledge still outranks law, but no human mind can safely hold unaccountable power, so law rules.

"There is no law or order which is above knowledge ... But then there is no such mind anywhere, or at least not much; and therefore we must choose law and order, which are second best" (*Laws* IX 875c-d). Rulers are "servants or ministers of the law" (715c-d). *Statesman* 294a-c, 297d-e: law is like an obstinate, ignorant tyrant, yet strict rule of law is second best where the true statesman is absent. Same principle as the *Republic*, different factual premise.

*Introduced:* [1.2](lessons/01-02-plato-against-democracy-and-the-second-best-city.md)

### The mixed constitution in the Laws

Blend the two mother forms so neither subjection nor freedom runs to excess.

Monarchy and democracy are the "mother" forms (693d-e); Persia fell by too much subjection, Athens by too much freedom (Book III). Election of the council is a mean (756e). The seven titles to rule are "naturally opposed", "a fountain-head of seditions" (690a-d); wisdom is "the greatest of all", lot "quite just". The law, not the demos, is sovereign.

*Introduced:* [1.2](lessons/01-02-plato-against-democracy-and-the-second-best-city.md)

### The polis exists by nature

The city is the household's completed form, so humans are made for it.

"The state is a creation of nature, and ... man is by nature a political animal. And he who by nature and not by mere accident is without a state, is either a bad man or above humanity" (*Politics* I.2 1253a1-4). It "comes into being for the sake of life, and continues in being for the sake of the good life" (1252b27-30). Whoever cannot share in a community, or needs nothing, is "a beast or a god" (1253a27-29). "By nature" does not mean without human effort: the first founder is the greatest of benefactors.

*Introduced:* [1.3](lessons/01-03-aristotle-the-city-exists-by-nature.md)

### Priority of the city to the individual

A citizen cut off from the city is like a severed hand: a hand in name only.

The whole is prior by nature to the part; things are defined by their work and power (1253a18-29). Priority of nature or definition, not of time, so it does not contradict the growth story. Not a charter for bureaucracy: a human life is completed as a member of a self-governing community.

*Introduced:* [1.3](lessons/01-03-aristotle-the-city-exists-by-nature.md)

### Natural slavery

Aristotle held that some people are slaves by nature, and recorded the critics who denied it.

A slave is "a living possession" (1253b32); a natural slave shares in reason enough to apprehend it, not possess it (1254b20-23), and has "no deliberative faculty at all" (1260a12). I.3 reports critics: mastery is by law only, so unjust. I.6 concedes slavery by convention (war captives) may be unjust, and that nature often fails to mark free and slave. Later use: Sepúlveda invoked it at Valladolid (1550-51); Las Casas contested the application.

*Introduced:* [1.3](lessons/01-03-aristotle-the-city-exists-by-nature.md)

### Aristotle against Platonic unity

A city pushed to be one stops being a city.

*Republic* V 462a-b: the greatest good is what binds the city into one. *Politics* II.2 denies the premise "the more a city is one, the better": a city is a plurality of *different* kinds, and pushed toward unity it becomes a household, then an individual. Not individualism: the city is still prior. "That which is common to the greatest number has the least care bestowed upon it" (II.3 1261b33-35); family affection is diluted like a little wine in much water (II.4).

*Introduced:* [1.3](lessons/01-03-aristotle-the-city-exists-by-nature.md)

### Private ownership and common use

Property should be private in ownership and common in use, with generosity trained by the legislator.

*Politics* II.5, with Sparta's shared use of horses and dogs as proof it works; self-love is natural, only its excess censured. Taken over by Aquinas ([2.4](lessons/02-04-aquinas-on-kingship-tyranny-and-property.md)).

*Introduced:* [1.3](lessons/01-03-aristotle-the-city-exists-by-nature.md)

### The citizen as ruling and being ruled

Citizenship is a share in rule, so who counts as a citizen depends on the constitution.

The citizen's "special characteristic is that he shares in the administration of justice, and in offices" (III.1 1275a22-23); the definition fits a democracy's citizen best. The good citizen need not be a good man (III.4): citizen virtue is relative to the constitution, like a sailor's to the ship; the two coincide only in the best city. The free citizen knows how to rule and to obey.

*Introduced:* [1.4](lessons/01-04-aristotle-constitutions-citizens-and-the-polity.md)

### The six regimes

Who holds supreme authority, crossed with whom they rule for.

| Supreme authority | Common interest (correct) | Rulers' interest (deviant) |
|---|---|---|
| One | Kingship | Tyranny |
| Few (really: the rich, or the best) | Aristocracy | Oligarchy |
| Many (really: the poor, or those bearing arms) | Polity (*politeia*) | Democracy |

Rule over free equals exists for the ruled, like the arts; ruling citizens for yourself is despotic (III.6 1279a17-21). The governing body is the constitution (III.6 1278b8-11).

*Introduced:* [1.4](lessons/01-04-aristotle-constitutions-citizens-and-the-polity.md)

### Wealth not number

Oligarchy is rule of the rich and democracy rule of the poor, whatever the headcount.

"The real difference between democracy and oligarchy is poverty and wealth. Wherever men rule by reason of their wealth, whether they be few or many, that is an oligarchy" (III.8 1279b39-1280a3). Number is a symptom: the rich happen to be few. Election counts as oligarchic, lot as democratic.

*Introduced:* [1.4](lessons/01-04-aristotle-constitutions-citizens-and-the-polity.md)

### Partial justice of oligarch and democrat

Both sides accept proportional equality and each generalizes one real difference.

Shared principle: equality for equals, inequality for unequals. Crux: which respect matters for ruling. Oligarch: unequal wealth, so unequal in everything. Democrat: equal free birth, so equal in everything. Decider: the city exists for the good life, not trade or defence, so shares follow contribution to that, political virtue (III.9). Both are judges in their own case.

*Introduced:* [1.4](lessons/01-04-aristotle-constitutions-citizens-and-the-polity.md)

### The summation argument

The many, pooled, may judge better than the few best, because partial virtues and insights add up.

*Politics* III.11: like a feast many contribute to, the many become like one person with many hands and senses; so they should at least elect and audit officials. The many *complement* one another; not the Condorcet jury theorem's independent guesses. Aristotle says it does not hold for every multitude (1281b15-20).

*Introduced:* [1.4](lessons/01-04-aristotle-constitutions-citizens-and-the-polity.md)

### Polity and the middle class

For most cities the best attainable regime mixes the classes and weights the middle.

"Those states are likely to be well-administered, in which the middle class is large, and larger if possible than both the other classes, or at any rate than either singly; for the addition of the middle class turns the scale" (IV.11 1295b35-39). A counterweight, not a ruling bloc. Two reasons: stability, and ethics (virtue is a mean; moderate fortune listens to reason). Good mixture test (IV.9): observers can call it democracy or oligarchy, as with Sparta. "The constitution is in a figure the life of the city" (IV.11 1295a40-b1).

*Introduced:* [1.4](lessons/01-04-aristotle-constitutions-citizens-and-the-polity.md)

### Cicero on the commonwealth as the affair of a people

The state belongs to its people as an estate belongs to its owner, so a tyranny is not a bad state but none.

A people is "not every association of men, however congregated, but the association of the entire number, bound together by the compact of justice, and the communication of utility" (*On the Commonwealth* I.25 (39), Yonge; Latin *iuris consensu*, *utilitatis communione*). Tyrant, faction or mob: nothing is the people's affair (III.31-33 (43-45)); the Thirty and the Athenian mob fail alike. Rome "kept the name of a republic and long since lost the reality" (V.1 (2)). The definition gives cases at the extremes, no threshold and no procedure for who judges.

*Introduced:* [2.1](lessons/02-01-cicero-the-commonwealth-and-natural-law.md)

### The mixed constitution in Cicero

Every simple form decays, so blend royal, aristocratic and popular elements.

One, few or many are tolerable while the bond of right holds, and each slides to its corruption (king to tyrant, aristocracy to faction, people to mob; I.28-29, I.44), Polybius's *anacyclosis*. The blend gives equality enough for liberty and stability no simple form has (I.45 (69)); Rome's constitution, grown over generations rather than designed, is the best instance (I.46; Book II). A just kingship still counts as a commonwealth and ranks first among simple forms.

*Introduced:* [2.1](lessons/02-01-cicero-the-commonwealth-and-natural-law.md)

### Cicero on true law

Law is right reason in nature, the same everywhere, and no vote can repeal it.

"True law is right reason conformable to nature, universal, unchangeable, eternal, whose commands urge us to duty, and whose prohibitions restrain us from evil" (III.22 (33), preserved by Lactantius). "Neither the senate nor the people can give us any dispensation"; "not one thing at Rome, and another at Athens". Taken from the Stoics and made political: enactments against right reason no more deserve the name of law than robbers' agreements (*On the Laws* II.5 (13)). Answers Philus's revival of Carneades (155 BC): justice is convention. Says nothing about who enforces it; the penalty in Laelius's speech is inward.

*Introduced:* [2.1](lessons/02-01-cicero-the-commonwealth-and-natural-law.md)

### The two cities

Two loves make two cities, defined by final allegiance, not by institution.

"Two cities have been formed by two loves: the earthly by the love of self, even to the contempt of God; the heavenly by the love of God, even to the contempt of self" (*City of God* XIV.28, Dods). Mixed in every kingdom and in the visible Church until the Judgment. Not Rome and the Church; "contempt of self" orders loves, it does not command self-hatred. The earthly city seeking mastery is mastered by *libido dominandi* (preface to Book I).

*Introduced:* [2.2](lessons/02-02-augustine-the-two-cities.md)

### Kingdoms without justice as great robberies

Take justice away and a kingdom differs from a gang only in scale.

"Justice being taken away, then, what are kingdoms but great robberies?" (IV.4). A robber band has a leader, a pact and rules for the loot; add territory and conquest and it is a kingdom. The pirate tells Alexander he is a robber for doing with one ship what Alexander does with a fleet. The opening clause is conditional; how much follows about actual states is Boss 2.

*Introduced:* [2.2](lessons/02-02-augustine-the-two-cities.md)

### Augustine redefines a people

A people is any rational multitude united by what it loves; it is better or worse by the objects of its love.

"A people is an assemblage of reasonable beings bound together by a common agreement as to the objects of their love" (XIX.24). Offered as an alternative ("say that"), with justice dropped; it diagnoses *what kind* of people a group is. On it Rome is a people and its affairs a republic. Cicero's definition asks whether a city is just; Augustine's asks what holds it together.

*Introduced:* [2.2](lessons/02-02-augustine-the-two-cities.md)

### The peace of Babylon

Earthly peace is a real good that pilgrims use and obey, but must not love as their end.

The pilgrim city obeys the laws governing mortal life and tolerates any customs serving earthly peace, drawing the line only where they hinder worship of the one God (XIX.17); while the cities are mixed, Christians too enjoy the peace of Babylon (XIX.26).

*Introduced:* [2.2](lessons/02-02-augustine-the-two-cities.md)

### Rule of man over man in Augustine

Dominion was given over irrational creatures, not over humans; slavery is a penalty of sin.

XIX.15: the righteous of early times were shepherds of cattle, not kings of men. Slavery is unnatural, penal and to be endured (the apostle's counsel). XIX.14: rule in a just household is service. XII.27 (Dods; some editions XII.28): the race is social by nature, unsocial by corruption. **Readings:** Deane (1963): coercive government a punishment and remedy for sin; others: ordering for others' good natural, mastering penal. **Secular state readings:** Markus, *Saeculum* (1970): a neutral zone; Milbank, *Theology and Social Theory* (1990): no peace is neutral.

*Introduced:* [2.2](lessons/02-02-augustine-the-two-cities.md)

### Aquinas on the definition of law

A law is reason ordering a community to its good, made by the one responsible and made known.

"[Law] is nothing else than an ordinance of reason for the common good, made by him who has care of the community, and promulgated" (ST I-II q.90 a.4). Against Ulpian's maxim that what pleases the ruler has the force of law: "otherwise the sovereign's will would savor of lawlessness rather than of law" (q.90 a.1 ad 3). Will moves law; reason makes it law. The author is the whole people *or* a public person acting for it (a.3), not chosen here.

*Introduced:* [2.3](lessons/02-03-aquinas-on-law.md)

### Conclusion and determination

Some human laws spell out what justice demands; others choose one of many reasonable options.

Human law has the nature of law so far as it derives from natural law; a departure is a perversion of law (q.95 a.2). **Conclusion**, as in a science: from *harm no one*, *do not kill*; keeps some force from natural law. **Determination**, as a builder gives a house a particular shape: natural law says punish, human law settles the penalty; has only the force of human law. Determination is free among reasonable options, not free of reason.

*Introduced:* [2.3](lessons/02-03-aquinas-on-law.md)

### The limits of human law

Law is written for ordinary people, not saints, and it commands acts, not virtue.

"Human laws do not forbid all vices, from which the virtuous abstain, but only the more grievous vices, from which it is possible for the majority to abstain; and chiefly those that are to the hurt of others" (q.96 a.2); law leads to virtue gradually (ad 2). Law commands acts of any virtue only as they bear on the common good (a.3). The ruler is exempt from law's coercive force, not its directive force (a.5 ad 3). Act beside the letter only when harm is manifest and too sudden to refer to authority: the besieged city's gates, "necessity knows no law" (a.6). Change law only for a compensating benefit; custom can make, abolish and interpret law (q.97 aa.1-3). A just law binds in conscience because the power that made it is ordered by eternal law (q.96 a.4, Boss 2).

*Introduced:* [2.3](lessons/02-03-aquinas-on-law.md)

### Aquinas on the best regime

Rule by one is best in principle; the best actual arrangement keeps one at the top and shares power beneath him.

*De Regno* I: a community's first good is unity, called peace; what is one produces unity best; so kingship is best and tyranny, united force for evil, worst. Rule by the many breeds tyranny more often. ST I-II q.105 a.1: the best polity is "partly kingdom, since there is one at the head of all; ... partly democracy, i.e. government by the people, in so far as the rulers can be chosen from the people, and the people have the right to choose their rulers", with aristocracy between; model: Moses, the elders and their election. The reply (ad 2): kingship is best while uncorrupted, but perfect virtue is rare.

*Introduced:* [2.4](lessons/02-04-aquinas-on-kingship-tyranny-and-property.md)

### Aquinas on tyranny and resistance

A tyrant may be removed by public authority, but not by private daggers, and never at greater cost than the tyranny.

"There is no sedition in disturbing a government of this kind, unless indeed the tyrant's rule be disturbed so inordinately, that his subjects suffer greater harm from the consequent disturbance than from the tyrant's government" (II-II q.42 a.2 ad 3); the tyrant is the seditious party. *De Regno* ladder: prevent (choose well, temper power); tolerate mild tyranny; a multitude with the right to provide its king may depose or restrict him (Tarquin, Domitian); a superior may be appealed to (Archelaus to Augustus); else turn to God. Private tyrannicide rejected (apostolic counsel, the martyrs, and bad men would use it against good kings). The early *Sentences* commentary (II d.44) seems to allow killing a usurper; whether *De Regno* withdraws this or treats a different case is disputed.

*Introduced:* [2.4](lessons/02-04-aquinas-on-kingship-tyranny-and-property.md)

### Aquinas on property and necessity

Ownership is a way of managing goods whose use stays common; in extreme need what you take becomes yours.

II-II q.66 a.2: private power to procure and dispense is lawful and necessary (more care, order, fewer quarrels), and the division of goods is by human agreement added to natural law (ad 1); as to use, hold things "not as his own, but as common". a.7: when need is manifest and urgent, danger imminent and no other remedy, taking is not theft; "that which he takes for the support of his life becomes his own property by reason of that need" (ad 2), also for a neighbor (ad 3). The owner's duty to share (a.2) and the needy person's license (a.7) are different things.

### Gelasian dualism and papal hierocracy

Two powers from God, or one supreme power that delegates the other.

Gelasius to Anastasius (494): "There are two powers, august Emperor, by which this world is chiefly ruled, namely, the sacred authority of the priests and the royal power" (Robinson); priestly *auctoritas* weightier, but royal *potestas* not said to come from the priest. Boniface VIII, *Unam Sanctam* (1302): one body, one head; both swords in the Church's power, the temporal wielded *for* the Church; spiritual power may establish and judge temporal power; subjection to the Roman pontiff necessary for salvation. John of Paris, *On Royal and Papal Power* (c. 1302): spiritual primacy granted, but kingship prior to and not derived from priesthood.

*Introduced:* [2.5](lessons/02-05-marsilius-and-conciliarism.md)

### Marsilius on the human legislator

Coercive law comes from the whole body of citizens, and the clergy hold no coercive power.

*Defensor Pacis* (1324): law strictly is a command backed by coercive penalty in this life (I.10); its primary efficient cause is the people or its weightier part (I.12); the legislator sets up, corrects and deposes the ruling part (I.15, I.18); Christ gave the apostles no coercive jurisdiction, priests are physicians of souls (II.4-5); papal plenitude of power is the peculiar cause of strife Aristotle could not foresee (I.1, I.19). Disputed faith goes to a general council, laity included, convoked by the legislator (II.20-21). Condemned by John XXII, apparently 1327. "Consent of the corporate community", not democracy.

*Introduced:* [2.5](lessons/02-05-marsilius-and-conciliarism.md)

### Conciliarism

The Church's authority lives in the whole body, so a council representing it stands above the pope in faith, schism and reform.

*Haec Sancta* (1415, paraphrased): a lawful general council holds power immediately from Christ, which even the pope must obey in faith, ending schism and reform in head and members; *Frequens* (1417): councils meet regularly. Constance ended the Great Schism (1378-1417) and elected Martin V. Tierney (1955): premises from canonists' corporation theory, not Marsilius. Standing of *Haec Sancta* disputed (permanent rule, emergency measure, or not validly approved); Basel deposed Eugenius IV (1439); *Execrabilis* (1460) forbade appeals to a future council. Marsilius moves power over the Church to the secular community; conciliarists move it within the Church from head to body.

| Government of the Church | Papal monarchy | Conciliarism (Gerson, d'Ailly, Zabarella) |
| Coercive temporal rule | Hierocracy (*Unam Sanctam*); dualism (Gelasius, John of Paris) | Marsilius |

### Effectual truth over imagined republics

Advice must follow how people live, not how they ought to live, so a prince must learn to be able not to be good.

"How one lives is so far distant from how one ought to live, that he who neglects what is done for what ought to be done, sooner effects his ruin than his preservation" (*Prince* ch. 15, Marriott). A prince must "know how to do wrong, and to make use of it or not according to necessity"; the Italian *imparare a potere essere non buono* is "learn to be able to be not good", a capacity, not a vow. A claim about method, aimed at Plato and the mirror tradition. The mirrors (Cicero, *De Officiis*: love a firmer guard than fear; force and fraud unworthy) held that the good ruler and the successful ruler are the same man.

*Introduced:* [3.1](lessons/03-01-machiavelli-the-prince.md)

### Virtu and fortuna

Fortune is powerful but not sovereign, and *virtù* is largely foresight.

Fortune governs perhaps half our actions; she is a river in flood, and in calm weather men build dikes; she "shows her power where valour has not prepared to resist her" (ch. 25). Success comes from matching conduct to the times; the impetuous usually do better. Agathocles: "it cannot be called talent to slay fellow-citizens, to deceive friends, to be without faith, without mercy, without religion; such methods may gain empire, but not glory" (ch. 8), so *virtù* is more than effectiveness. Ch. 26: Italy awaits a Medici redeemer.

*Introduced:* [3.1](lessons/03-01-machiavelli-the-prince.md)

### Better feared than loved

When a prince must choose, fear is safer than love, provided he avoids hatred.

Men are ungrateful, fickle, false; love rests on obligation they break when it serves them, fear on dread of punishment, which does not fail; men love at their own will but fear at the prince's (ch. 17). Constraint: avoid hatred by keeping from subjects' property and women. Borgia, whose cruelty pacified the Romagna, was more merciful than Florence, whose mercy let Pistoia be destroyed. Crux with Aquinas: whether love reliably binds subjects in adversity; both agree hatred destroys rulers.

### The republic of conflict

Rome was free because its two orders fought and the fighting, through lawful channels, wrote the laws.

"While in every republic there are two conflicting factions, that of the people and that of the nobles, it is in this conflict that all laws favourable to freedom have their origin" (*Discourses* I.4, Thomson). The quantifier is *all*. The humours cannot be cured, only vented: ordinary outlets (Coriolanus summoned by the tribunes, I.7) build liberty, extraordinary ones destroy it. The Agrarian Law (I.37) left the ordinary channels and ended in Marius, Sulla and Caesar. Guard liberty with the people, whose desire is only not to be dominated (I.5). "The prince who can do what he pleases is a madman, and the people which can do as it pleases is never wise" (I.58).

*Introduced:* [3.2](lessons/03-02-machiavelli-the-discourses.md)

### Rome or Sparta and Venice

A republic can have quiet or greatness, not both.

Sparta (one lawgiver, Lycurgus) and Venice kept liberty's guard with the nobles, stayed quiet and lasted longer, but stayed small. A republic that may have to expand needs a numerous armed people, which will not be quiet (I.5-6); since necessity may force growth, follow Rome. Supports: citizen arms, not mercenaries (I.21, I.43; II.20; *Prince* chs. 12-13); religion, Numa doing more than Romulus (I.11), fostered even if thought untrue (I.12); renewal to first principles (III.1). II.2 blames an interpretation of Christianity that makes men "brave to suffer rather than to do".

### One teaching or two

How *The Prince* and the *Discourses* fit is argued, not settled.

**Two teachings, a development:** Baron (1950s), a disputed dating. **One teaching, republican at the core:** Rousseau, *Social Contract* III.6. **One theory, two situations:** founding or reforming needs one man (I.9, I.18), maintaining needs many; princes are better at founding, peoples at maintaining (I.58). **One teaching, beyond morality:** Strauss (1958), I.9's excuse of Romulus, I.12 on untrue religion. Context: Pocock, *The Machiavellian Moment* (1975); Skinner.

### Hobbes on mechanistic psychology

Humans are matter in motion whose desires never come to rest, and "good" names what a person desires.

Sensation is motion pressed on the organs (ch. 1); imagination decaying motion (ch. 2); endeavour is appetite or aversion; good names what one desires, with no good in things (ch. 6); happiness is desire moving on, with no final end (ch. 11); reason is reckoning the consequences of names (ch. 5). "A perpetuall and restlesse desire of Power after power, that ceaseth onely in Death" (ch. 11), from insecurity, not greed. Geometry, which fixes meanings and reasons strictly, is the model for political science (ch. 4).

*Introduced:* [3.3](lessons/03-03-hobbes-matter-motion-and-the-state-of-nature.md)

### The state of nature as war

Rough equality plus distrust, without a common power, makes war of every man against every man.

"In the nature of man, we find three principall causes of quarrel. First, Competition; Secondly, Diffidence; Thirdly, Glory" (ch. 13). Life is "solitary, poore, nasty, brutish, and short"; nothing is unjust, since where there is no law there is no injustice. Not a historical era (never generally so over all the world) and not a doctrine of human wickedness: the passions are no sin. Evidence: civil war, the locked door, kings in the posture of gladiators.

*Introduced:* [3.3](lessons/03-03-hobbes-matter-motion-and-the-state-of-nature.md)

### Laws of nature as theorems

Hobbes's laws of nature are reason's theorems of self-preservation, laws properly only as commands.

First law: seek peace where there is hope of it, otherwise use every advantage of war; second: lay down the right to all things when others will too (ch. 14). "They are but Conclusions, or Theoremes ... whereas Law, properly is the word of him, that by right hath command over others. But yet if we consider the same Theoremes, as delivered in the word of God, ... then are they properly called Lawes" (ch. 15). They always bind *in foro interno*, *in foro externo* only where there is security. **Readings:** prudential; Taylor (1938) and Warrender (1957): genuinely obligatory as God's commands; Strauss: the politics rests on moral experience, not the physics.

*Introduced:* [3.3](lessons/03-03-hobbes-matter-motion-and-the-state-of-nature.md)

### Authorization and absolute sovereignty

Every subject authorizes the sovereign, so whatever he does, they did, and he cannot wrong them.

The covenant: "I Authorise and give up my Right of Governing my selfe, to this Man, or to this Assembly of men, on this condition, that thou give up thy Right to him, and Authorise all his Actions in like manner" (ch. 17). The covenant runs between subjects; the sovereign is not a party (ch. 18). He "may commit Iniquity; but not Injustice, or Injury in the proper signification" (ch. 18); he answers to God (ch. 30). Rights include legislation, judicature, war and peace, officers, and judging doctrines. Institution and acquisition give the same rights; fear does not void a covenant (ch. 20). Absolute is not totalitarian (belief cannot be commanded), and representative is not elected.

*Introduced:* [3.4](lessons/03-04-hobbes-the-sovereign-and-the-church.md)

### The sovereign as head of the church

There are not two governments in this life, so the civil sovereign governs religion.

The Kingdom of God properly is a civil kingdom over Israel, restored at Christ's return (chs. 35, 41); until then clergy teach, as schoolmasters, and do not command (ch. 42); a church that can command is a commonwealth of Christians (ch. 39), with the sovereign as supreme pastor. "Temporall and Spirituall Government, are but two words brought into the world, to make men see double, and mistake their Lawfull Soveraign" (ch. 39). Necessary to salvation: faith that Jesus is the Christ, and obedience to laws (ch. 43). Compelled profession is the sovereign's act (Naaman, ch. 42); martyrdom honoured, not required. "The Papacy, is no other, than the Ghost of the deceased Romane Empire, sitting crowned upon the grave thereof" (ch. 47). **Readings:** Martinich (1992): sincere Protestant theology; Curley: ironic undermining; Pocock (1971): the eschatology is structural.

### Filmer and the reply of the First Treatise

Filmer grounds kingship in Adam's fatherly dominion; Locke answers that the grant was to mankind and the heir cannot be found.

*Patriarcha* (published 1680; Filmer died 1653): God gave Adam dominion over the world and fatherly authority over his descendants; kings inherit it; no one is born free, and natural freedom is a novelty of schoolmen and Jesuits. Filmer's trap: if the world was common (Grotius, Pufendorf), private property needs everyone's consent, which no one gave. *First Treatise*: the Genesis grant was to mankind; honour father *and mother*; Adam's heir is unidentifiable. Filmer and Locke agree universal consent is an impossible test and draw opposite conclusions.

*Introduced:* [4.1](lessons/04-01-locke-against-filmer-and-property.md)

### Locke on the law of nature and workmanship

The law of nature forbids harming another because all are God's workmanship; reason is how it is known.

"Being all equal and independent, no one ought to harm another in his life, health, liberty, or possessions: for men being all the workmanship of one omnipotent, and infinitely wise maker" (*Second Treatise* §6); men are God's servants and property. "For" gives the ground; reason's role is epistemic. The state of nature is not war: it has a law, each enforces it (§7-8), and the trouble is judging one's own case (§13). No one can enslave himself (§23). Waldron (2002): equality depends on the theistic frame.

*Introduced:* [4.1](lessons/04-01-locke-against-filmer-and-property.md)

### Locke on property

Labour makes things yours without anyone's consent, and money, by consent, makes them unequally yours.

"Every man has a property in his own person: this no body has any right to but himself" (§27); mixing one's labour excludes others' common right "at least where there is enough, and as good, left in common for others". If consent were needed, "man had starved" (§28). Spoilage limit (§31); an enclosed acre yields many times a waste one (§37); "in the beginning all the world was America" (§49); money allows unequal possession by consent (§46-50). **Readings of the servant's turfs (§28):** Macpherson (1962): possessive individualism, labour sold for wages; Tully (1980): household service, provisos in force. Waldron (1979): "enough and as good" a sufficient, not necessary, condition. Colonial context: Carolina's *Fundamental Constitutions* (1669); Tully, Arneil.

*Introduced:* [4.1](lessons/04-01-locke-against-filmer-and-property.md)

### Locke on consent and trust

No one is subject without consent, and government is a trust whose breach dissolves it, with the people as judge.

"No one can be put out of this estate, and subjected to the political power of another, without his own consent" (§95). Tacit consent binds while you enjoy protection; only express consent makes a member (§119-122). No arbitrary power can be transferred (§135); the legislative rules by standing laws, for the people's good, taxes only with consent, and cannot hand over lawmaking (§140, §142). Breach forfeits the trust (§149, §222); a prince who stops the legislative meeting dissolves the government (§215); the society survives (§211). "The people shall be judge" (§240); appeal to heaven (§241-42). Brake: only a long train of abuses reveals a design (§225, §230).

*Introduced:* [4.2](lessons/04-02-locke-limited-government-and-toleration.md)

### Locke on toleration

The magistrate's sword reaches civil interests and outward conduct, never belief, except where belief threatens the civil bond.

"True and saving religion consists in the inward persuasion of the mind, without which nothing can be acceptable to God. And such is the nature of the understanding, that it cannot be compelled to the belief of anything by outward force" (*Letter*, Popple). Care of souls was committed to the magistrate neither by God nor by the people; a church is a voluntary society whose penalty is expulsion. **Exclusions:** opinions contrary to moral rules civil society needs; churches whose members serve a foreign prince (illustrated by the Mufti, read as aimed at Rome); atheists, since "promises, covenants, and oaths ... can have no hold upon an atheist". Proast (1690): moderate penalties can make people consider. A limit on jurisdiction, not equal religious liberty.

### Rousseau on the state of nature

Hobbes's savage was a social man in disguise: the war and the passions come later.

"Every one of them ... constantly dwelling on wants, avidity, oppression, desires and pride, has transferred to the state of nature ideas which were acquired in society; so that, in speaking of the savage, they described the social man" (*Discourse*, Part I preamble, Cole). A charge of method. Natural man: solitary, few needs, moved by *amour de soi* and pity, pre-moral (not "noble"), perfectible. "Let us begin then by laying facts aside, as they do not affect the question."

*Introduced:* [4.3](lessons/04-03-rousseau-discourse-on-inequality.md)

### Rousseau on the origin of inequality

Comparison, dependence and property made inequality, and a fraudulent contract made it law.

Huts and families (the first revolution); comparison round the tree breeds *amour-propre*; metallurgy and agriculture bring dependence and property; a war of rich and poor; the rich man proposes equal rules ("let us institute rules of justice and peace, to which all without exception may be obliged to conform"), which "bound new fetters on the poor, and gave new powers to the rich". Three stages: rich and poor, powerful and weak, master and slave. Conclusion: moral inequality out of proportion to physical inequality conflicts with natural right, not that all law is illegitimate. Part II opens with the first man to enclose a piece of ground (Boss 4). **Readings:** Neuhouser (2008): *amour-propre* has healthy and inflamed forms; darker readers: inherently inflammatory.

*Introduced:* [4.3](lessons/04-03-rousseau-discourse-on-inequality.md)

### The general will

Give yourself wholly to all and you are subject to no one, obeying laws you make that bind everyone alike.

"The problem is to find a form of association which will defend and protect with the whole common force the person and goods of each associate, and in which each, while uniting himself with all, may still obey himself alone, and remain as free as before" (I.6, Cole). "Man is born free; and everywhere he is in chains" (I.1). The general will comes from all and applies to all (II.4); "take away from these same wills the pluses and minuses that cancel one another, and the general will remains as the sum of the differences" (II.3), reliable only with informed citizens and no partial associations. A majority aimed at a named target, or produced by a faction, carries no general will. The outvoted citizen was mistaken about the general will (IV.2). Article 6 of the 1789 Declaration calls law its expression.

*Introduced:* [4.4](lessons/04-04-rousseau-the-social-contract.md)

### Forced to be free

Whoever refuses to obey the general will is compelled by the whole body, which protects him from dependence on any particular person.

I.7: each person can have a particular will contrary to his general will as a citizen, wanting a citizen's rights without a subject's duties; the compact silently includes that such a one is compelled, "forced to be free"; Rousseau calls it the key to the political machine. **Readings:** epistemic (Grofman and Feld, 1988, via the jury theorem); totalitarian (Talmon, *The Origins of Totalitarian Democracy*, 1952); republican (Joshua Cohen, 2010: institutional conditions prevent capture).

*Introduced:* [4.4](lessons/04-04-rousseau-the-social-contract.md)

### Sovereignty cannot be represented

Power may be delegated, will may not, so the people legislates in person in a small republic.

Sovereignty is inalienable (II.1); laws not ratified by the people in person are not laws, deputies are stewards (III.15). Liberty shrinks as the state grows (III.1). Democratic *government* suits only a people of gods (III.4); elective aristocracy may be best (III.5). The lawgiver (II.7); civil religion with intolerance as its negative dogma, and banishment for unbelievers and for those who teach no salvation outside the Church (IV.8).

### The three governments

Montesquieu sorts governments by rule of law versus will, each moved by its own principle.

| Government | Nature | Principle | Corrupted when (Book VIII) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Republic (democracy or aristocracy) | The people, or part of it, holds supreme power | Virtue (love of country and equality); moderation in aristocracy | Equality lost, or pushed to where no one obeys |
| Monarchy | One rules by fixed laws through intermediate powers | Honour | Prince strips orders and cities of privileges |
| Despotism | One rules by will and caprice | Fear | Corrupt by nature |

Principles say what each government *requires*, not what it has (III.11). Laws made for one nation seldom suit another (I.3).

### Climate, commerce and the general spirit

Laws must fit a people, but climate is one cause among seven, and legislators should resist its vices.

Heat makes people languid, cold vigorous (Books XIV-XVII); good legislators work against the climate's vices (XIV.5); by XIX.4 climate is one of seven causes of the general spirit, the others weakening as one strengthens. Commerce softens manners and cures destructive prejudices (XX.1); peace is its natural effect, since trading nations depend on each other (XX.2); among individuals it turns humane acts into things done for money. Relativity is about fit, not fatalism.

*Introduced:* [5.1](lessons/05-01-montesquieu-the-spirit-of-the-laws.md)

### Separation of powers

Liberty needs the three powers in different hands, each able to stop the others.

"The political liberty of the subject is a tranquillity of mind arising from the opinion each person has of his safety"; "When the legislative and executive powers are united in the same person, or in the same body of magistrates, there can be no liberty" (XI.6, Nugent). "Every man invested with power is apt to abuse it" (XI.4). England: two chambers each reject, the monarch rejects but does not resolve, the legislature examines ministers, juries try crimes; the judiciary is "close to nothing", judges the voice of the law. Venice's councils are "almost one and the same power" because one body staffs them. Checks run through social orders (king, nobles, commons). Not the American co-equal branches. **Minister in Parliament:** strict reading (separate personnel), checks reading (Madison, *Federalist* 47), historical reading (an ideal in English dress).

*Introduced:* [5.1](lessons/05-01-montesquieu-the-spirit-of-the-laws.md)

### Faction and the extended republic

Faction cannot be cured without destroying liberty, so enlarge the republic until no majority faction can form.

A faction is "a number of citizens, whether amounting to a majority or a minority of the whole, who are united and actuated by some common impulse of passion, or of interest, adverse to the rights of other citizens, or to the permanent and aggregate interests of the community" (*Federalist* 10). "Liberty is to faction what air is to fire." The most common and durable source is unequal property. "Extend the sphere, and you take in a greater variety of parties and interests." Diversity does the work virtue was supposed to do. Probabilistic, and it depends on the *shape* of divisions: a concentrated sectional interest (slavery) is not diluted. Reverses the small-republic tradition (Aristotle, Montesquieu, Rousseau, Brutus). Canonical only later: Beard (1913), Dahl (1956).

*Introduced:* [5.2](lessons/05-02-the-federalist-faction-and-the-extended-republic.md)

### Ambition counteracting ambition

Where *Federalist* 10 relies on diversity in society, 51 relies on division inside government.

Give each department constitutional means and personal motives to resist the others, so that ambition counteracts ambition; federalism divides power twice, between nation and states and within each; in so varied a republic a majority coalition could seldom form except on justice and the general good (*Federalist* 51). Diversity in society is not the same as checks in government; do not collapse "extended republic" into gridlock. Full reconstruction is Boss 5.

### Burke on prescription and the partnership of generations

A constitution's long, beneficial continuance is its title, and the state is a partnership across generations.

The state is not a partnership in pepper or coffee but in all science, art and virtue; "as the ends of such a partnership cannot be obtained in many generations, it becomes a partnership not only between those who are living, but between those who are living, those who are dead, and those who are to be born" (*Reflections*, 1790). Each state's contract is a clause in the "great primeval contract of eternal society" (religious or rhetorical reading). "To love the little platoon we belong to in society, is the first principle ... of public affections", the first link in a series to country and mankind. Burke accepts contract, and accepts change: a state without means of change lacks means of preservation.

*Introduced:* [5.3](lessons/05-03-burke-prescription-against-abstract-rights.md)

### Burke against abstract rights

Burke does not deny rights; he denies they can build a state.

Real rights (to justice, the fruits of industry, inheritance, instruction and consolation) are the advantages civil society provides; a share in power is conventional; "these metaphysic rights entering into common life, like rays of light which pierce into a dense medium, are, by the laws of Nature, refracted from their straight line". Draw on the "bank and capital of nations and ages". 1688 was necessity, not a precedent: the Declaration of Right (1689) declared ancient liberties, the Act of Settlement (1701) fixed the succession. The chivalry passage: the age of "sophisters, economists and calculators"; manners keep power gentle. America (1774-75): colonists defending an English inheritance. **Price** (1789): 1688 gave rights to choose, cashier and frame governments. **Paine**, *Rights of Man* (1791): "The vanity and presumption of governing beyond the grave is the most ridiculous and insolent of all tyrannies." Crux: whether political obligation arises only from the will of those bound.

*Introduced:* [5.3](lessons/05-03-burke-prescription-against-abstract-rights.md)

### Wollstonecraft on virtue and dependence

Virtue borrowed from a master is not virtue, so women must be educated as rational beings and made independent.

"It is a farce to call any being virtuous whose virtues do not result from the exercise of its own reason. This was Rousseau's opinion respecting men: I extend it to women" (*Rights of Woman* ch. 2). One standard of virtue for all rational beings; dependence breeds cunning; women's faults are effects of education and station, and Rousseau "takes the effect of habit for evidence of nature". Against Sophie: good conduct from the wish to please is not virtue and fails under strain. "I do not wish them to have power over men; but over themselves." Proposals: coeducational government day schools (ch. 12); women as physicians and in business, a "hint" of representation (ch. 9). Most women still called to be wives and mothers, as rational beings. **Readings:** liberal; republican (Alan Coffee); religious (Barbara Taylor, 2003).

*Introduced:* [5.4](lessons/05-04-wollstonecraft-rights-virtue-and-education.md)

### Wollstonecraft against Burke

Rights come from God-given reason, not forefathers, so no prescription can cancel them.

*Rights of Men* (1790): the birthright is "such a degree of liberty, civil and religious, as is compatible with the liberty of every other individual with whom he is united in a social compact". Burke's inheritance protects hereditary property and rank, which corrupt rich and poor; by his logic the slave trade could never be abolished. His sensibility is selective (the Regency debate, 1788-89). To Talleyrand: excluding women from a constitution resting on reason is prescription too.

*Introduced:* [5.4](lessons/05-04-wollstonecraft-rights-virtue-and-education.md)

### Hegel on freedom realized in the state

The state is the whole in which property, conscience, family and market become a freedom you recognize as yours.

Freedom is not doing as you like nor bare inner self-legislation but being at home in rational institutions. Morality alone is empty: duty for duty's sake yields no specific duties (§ 135, against Kant), and conscience can will evil (§§ 139-140). The state is "the actuality of the ethical idea" (§ 257), not a protection agency with optional membership (§ 258 note), and lets subjective freedom run to its extreme while bringing it back into the common life (§ 260). Constitutional monarchy, civil service, estates assembly (§§ 273-320), public trials and juries (§§ 224-228), more than Prussia had. Faults Plato for suppressing subjective freedom (Preface, § 185).

*Introduced:* [6.1](lessons/06-01-hegel-freedom-realized-in-the-state.md)

### The rational and the actual

Philosophy does not issue blueprints; it comprehends a form of life once it has matured.

"What is rational is real; And what is real is rational" (Preface, Dyde; *wirklich* is better "actual"). "When philosophy paints its grey in grey, one form of life has become old, and by means of grey it cannot be rejuvenated, but only known. The owl of Minerva takes its flight only when the shades of night are gathering." Aimed at reformers such as Fries. Not "might is right": contingent existence is not actual (*Encyclopaedia*, 1827, § 6). "The march of God in the world" is an Addition (§ 258), plausibly "it is God's way in the world that there be a state".

*Introduced:* [6.1](lessons/06-01-hegel-freedom-realized-in-the-state.md)

### Hegel on civil society and the rabble

Civil society makes selfish people depend on everyone, piles up wealth and a pauper class, and cannot supply the solidarity it undermines.

Each pursues his own ends through a system of mutual dependence (§§ 182-256). The rabble is poverty plus inner rebellion; want in society takes the form of a wrong done to a class (Addition to § 244). § 245: support from the rich violates independence and honour; providing work overproduces; England tried both, Scotland left the poor to beg. § 246 onward: trade and colonies as outlet. The corporation covers only its members. **Readings:** Haym (1857) and Popper (1945): apology for Prussia; Avineri (1972): reformist design honestly naming an unsolved problem; Marx: the crack through which the mediation collapses.

*Introduced:* [6.1](lessons/06-01-hegel-freedom-realized-in-the-state.md)

### Equality of conditions

Equality's advance is a providential fact; the only choice left is between free and despotic democracy.

Nothing in America struck Tocqueville more (Introduction). Every fifty years in France the noble went down and the commoner up. "A new science of politics is indispensable to a new world" (Reeve). "Aristocracy had made a chain of all the members of the community, from the peasant to the king: democracy breaks that chain, and severs every link of it" (Vol. II, Part 2, ch. 2). A social state, not a form of government or equal wealth. Final chapter of Vol. I (ch. 18) on the three races sits outside the main argument; Vol. II, Part 3, ch. 12: sexes equal in worth, separate spheres.

*Introduced:* [6.2](lessons/06-02-tocqueville-equality-of-conditions.md)

### Tocqueville on individualism and association

Equality breeds individualism, and local liberty, association, self-interest rightly understood and religion counter it.

Individualism is a calm withdrawal from public life from "erroneous judgment", not egotism; isolated citizens suit despotism (Vol. II, Part 2, chs. 2, 4). "Local assemblies of citizens constitute the strength of free nations. Town-meetings are to liberty what primary schools are to science" (Vol. I, ch. 5). Necessity becomes habit, habit taste. Political associations are schools of the art of association (ch. 7); interest rightly understood yields small daily self-command (ch. 8). Religion, though it takes no part in government, is the foremost American political institution, strong because separate from the state (Vol. I, ch. 17). A local undertaking may succeed less well than the state would; the gain is in the citizens.

*Introduced:* [6.2](lessons/06-02-tocqueville-equality-of-conditions.md)

### Tyranny of the majority

A democratic majority that holds every office and moral authority rules minds, not only bodies.

"I know no country in which there is so little true independence of mind and freedom of discussion as in America" (Vol. I, ch. 15, Reeve); under democracy "the body is left free, and the soul is enslaved". The sanction is exclusion, not punishment. The Pennsylvania note: free Black voters stay from the polls for fear, and magistrates cannot protect them. A claim about missing barriers, not constant persecution. **Against Madison:** a majority of opinion, not a coalition of interests (one reading); the abolitionist press shows dissent survived (the other). Softeners (Vol. I, ch. 16): decentralized administration, lawyers, the jury.

*Introduced:* [6.3](lessons/06-03-tocqueville-tyranny-of-the-majority-and-soft-despotism.md)

### Soft despotism

Equality, individualism and administrative centralization can produce a mild guardian that relieves people of governing themselves.

Vol. II, Part 4, ch. 6: a crowd of equal, private individuals under an immense tutelary power that secures their pleasures and manages their affairs, softening wills rather than breaking them; people "console themselves for being in tutelage by the reflection that they have chosen their own guardians". Election diminishes but does not remove the evil. Governmental centralization is necessary; administrative centralization enervates (Vol. I, ch. 5). Remedies (Vol. II, Part 4, ch. 7): secondary elected bodies, associations, free press, courts, respect for forms and rights. *The Old Regime and the Revolution* (1856): French centralization built by the monarchy and left standing by 1789. Full close reading is Boss 6.

*Introduced:* [6.3](lessons/06-03-tocqueville-tyranny-of-the-majority-and-soft-despotism.md)

### Mill on the active citizen

The best government is the one that improves its citizens, and participation is the school.

Two criteria (ch. II): use of existing good qualities, and, more important, their improvement. The good despot yields good administration over a nation of spectators (ch. III). Office makes a citizen "weigh interests not his own; to be guided, in case of conflicting claims, by another rule than his private partialities" (ch. III). The ideal is representative, and only where a people can sustain it (chs. IV, XVIII, including government of dependencies). The assembly should "watch and control the government", not administer (ch. V). Not *On Liberty* (1859); do not import the harm principle.

*Introduced:* [6.4](lessons/06-04-mill-representative-government.md)

### Mill on proportional representation plural voting and the open ballot

One educative criterion yields representation of all minorities, extra votes for education, and a public vote.

**True democracy** is the whole people equally represented; Hare's scheme (1859) gives the instructed minority an organ (ch. VII). **Suffrage** universal except those who cannot read, write and do sums, pay no taxes, or receive parish relief; sex irrelevant (ch. VIII). **Plural voting** for proved education, never property; occupation as proxy strains toward class. **Open ballot** (ch. X): the vote is a trust over others, so cast it publicly; the ballot may be the lesser evil where voters are coerced (late republican Rome), a conditional empirical judgment. Mill credits Tocqueville with shaping his fear of majority power.

*Introduced:* [6.4](lessons/06-04-mill-representative-government.md)

### Marx against Hegel on the state

Marx kept Hegel's split between civil society and state, denied that the state heals it, and located its cause in production.

Following Feuerbach's inversion (*Essence of Christianity*, 1841): the state is a heaven where people are equal members of a community, while in civil society they are isolated competitors. "On the Jewish Question" (against Bauer, 1843): political emancipation (a state free of religion, as in North America) is a great step but not human emancipation; the rights of man are the rights of the egoistic member of civil society. Religion is "the opium of the people" and also the sigh of the oppressed (1844). The proletariat as a class with nothing particular to defend.

*Introduced:* [6.5](lessons/06-05-marx-from-hegel-to-historical-materialism.md)

### Historical materialism

Explain politics by production, not production by politics.

"The sum total of these relations of production constitutes the economic structure of society, the real foundation, on which rise legal and political superstructures and to which correspond definite forms of social consciousness" (1859 Preface, Stone). Social existence determines consciousness. Thesis XI: "Philosophers have only interpreted the world differently, but the point is to change it" (Lewis). *Manifesto*: the history of all hitherto existing society is the history of class struggles. **Legal-expression problem** (Plamenatz, 1954): Cohen (1978): relations as effective power, functional explanation; Engels to Bloch (1890): the economy determines "ultimately". **Young versus mature Marx:** Althusser (1965) break; Avineri (1968) continuity; the Manuscripts appeared only in 1932. Not Hobbes's materialism.

*Introduced:* [6.5](lessons/06-05-marx-from-hegel-to-historical-materialism.md)

### Marx on the state

The state is class rule, sometimes wearing a mask of independence, to be replaced, not seized, and finally made unnecessary.

"The executive of the modern State is but a committee for managing the common affairs of the whole bourgeoisie" (*Manifesto* I, Moore). *Brumaire*: "Man makes his own history, but he does not make it out of the whole cloth; he does not make it out of conditions chosen by himself, but out of such as he finds close at hand" (De Leon); the peasants as potatoes in a sack, a class and not a class. *Civil War in France* (1871): the working class cannot simply lay hold of the ready-made state machinery; the Commune's elected, recallable officials at workmen's wages. *Gotha* (1875): dictatorship of the proletariat as transition; first phase by labour; higher phase by need. Engels: the state dies out (*Anti-Dühring*, 1878). **Readings of the dictatorship:** word-history (Draper, 1986), democratic socialist (Kautsky, 1918), vanguard (Lenin, 1917), anarchist objection (Bakunin, *Statism and Anarchy*, 1873); Kołakowski on betrayal or development.

*Introduced:* [6.6](lessons/06-06-marx-the-state-revolution-and-after.md)

### Thrasymachus challenge

1. In every city the ruling group makes laws with a view to its own interest (338e).
2. Justice is subjects obeying those laws (338e-339a).
3. A ruler "in the strict sense" does not err, so what he commands is really to his advantage (340d-341a).
4. ∴ Justice is the advantage of the stronger (339a, 341a).
5. So justice is "another's good", the loss of whoever practices it; injustice is one's own profit (343c).
6. This is clearest in the tyrant, who is called happy (344a-c).
7. ∴ The unjust life is better than the just.

**Weak point:** 3, which invites Socrates' craft analogy: every craft, taken strictly, serves its object (341c-342e). **Crux:** whether ruling strictly aims at the good of the ruled; the shepherd speech (343b-344c) denies it.

*From* [1.1](lessons/01-01-platos-republic-justice-in-city-and-soul.md)

### Glaucon contract

1. By nature, doing injustice is good and suffering it is evil, and the evil exceeds the good.
2. Those who have tasted both and cannot do the one without suffering the other agree neither to do nor suffer injustice.
3. The law they make is called just; justice is "a mean or compromise" between the best (injustice unpunished) and the worst (suffering it without power to retaliate) (358e-359b).
4. Anyone with the ring of Gyges, able to act unjustly with impunity, would (359b-360d).
5. ∴ Justice is chosen only from weakness, not for itself.

Offered as a challenge, not a belief (358c). Ancestor of Hobbes's route to the commonwealth ([3.3](lessons/03-03-hobbes-matter-motion-and-the-state-of-nature.md)).

*From* [1.1](lessons/01-01-platos-republic-justice-in-city-and-soul.md)

### The city-soul argument

1. Justice is said both of a person and of a city (368e).
2. The city is larger, so its justice is easier to see (368e-369a).
3. A city arises because no one is self-sufficient, and each does best doing the one work his nature fits (369b-370c).
4. A good city has three classes: rulers, auxiliaries, producers (412b-415d), held together by the noble lie.
5. It is wise through its rulers, courageous through its auxiliaries, temperate through agreement about who should rule (427e-432a).
6. Justice is each class doing its own work (433a-b); a trader pushing into ruling ruins the city (434a-c).
7. The soul has three matching parts: reason, spirit, appetite (434d-441c).
8. So a person is just when each part does its own work, reason ruling; justice concerns the inner self first and outward acts as a result (441d-444a).
9. ∴ Justice is the soul's health and injustice its civil war (444c-e), so no one would trade it for Gyges' ring.

**Pressure points:** does the analogy hold (step 7 is argued separately, but the same-name principle at 435a favours a match); is the city held together by a lie (4); has Socrates changed the subject from treating others to inner order (8).

*From* [1.1](lessons/01-01-platos-republic-justice-in-city-and-soul.md)

### The Republic case for rule by knowledge

1. Ruling well is a craft requiring knowledge of what is good for the city as a whole.
2. That knowledge is rare, and only philosophy reaches it.
3. The many cannot tell the knower from the flatterer; their judges are their appetites (ship, beast-tamer).
4. So cities ruled by the many, or by the ignorant, will not end their evils.
5. ∴ Philosophers must rule (473c-d).
6. Such rulers need a way of life that removes private interest: no private property or families (416d-417b), reluctance to rule (520d).

*From* [1.2](lessons/01-02-plato-against-democracy-and-the-second-best-city.md)

### The ship of state

*(outline; full reconstruction is Boss 1)* A ship's owner is large but half-deaf and short-sighted; the crew brawl for the helm, none has learned navigation, and they deny it can be taught; whoever helps them seize the helm is praised, the true pilot who studies stars and winds is called a useless star-gazer (*Republic* VI 488a-489a). The load falls on the claim that political knowledge is a craft like navigation that the many can neither possess nor recognize, which is what Aristotle's summation argument presses.

*From* [1.2](lessons/01-02-plato-against-democracy-and-the-second-best-city.md), [1.4](lessons/01-04-aristotle-constitutions-citizens-and-the-polity.md)

### From democracy to tyranny

1. Democracy defines its good as freedom, living as one likes (557b, 562b).
2. Pursued without limit, freedom makes citizens resent every authority: magistrates, parents, teachers, finally the laws.
3. The city is left divided, the many fearing the rich and the rich pushed into resistance.
4. The people set up a single champion to protect them (565c-d).
5. A champion with the mob at his disposal and no restraint turns on his enemies and becomes a tyrant, the wolf of the Arcadian tale.
6. ∴ Extreme freedom passes into extreme slavery (564a).

**Weak points:** 2 (why must unlimited freedom erode *all* authority?) and 4 to 5 (a champion need not become a tyrant). Same template as oligarchy's fall through wealth.

*From* [1.2](lessons/01-02-plato-against-democracy-and-the-second-best-city.md)

### The Laws concession

1. No law or order is above knowledge; a true mind should rule, not serve (875c).
2. But there is no such mind anywhere, or not much (875c-d).
3. And even one who knew the public good would not stay firm holding absolute, unaccountable power (875b-c, 691c-d).
4. ∴ Choose law and order, which are second best (875d).
5. So rulers must be servants of the law (715c-d).
6. And the constitution should mix monarchy and democracy (693d-e), with election as a mean (756e).

**Crux with the *Republic*:** premises 2-3 (whether knowledge adequate to rule can be embodied and trusted with power). Premise 1 is shared.

*From* [1.2](lessons/01-02-plato-against-democracy-and-the-second-best-city.md)

### The naturalness of the polis

1. Male and female pair for offspring; natural ruler and natural subject (master and slave) for preservation; from these the household arises, for everyday needs.
2. Several households form a village, for needs beyond the daily.
3. Several villages form a complete, self-sufficient community, the polis: it comes into being for life and continues for the good life (1252b27-30).
4. The earlier associations exist by nature.
5. The nature of a thing is what it is when its development is complete: its end (1252b32-34).
6. The end is best, and self-sufficiency is an end and best.
7. ∴ The polis exists by nature, and a human is by nature a political animal.

**The speech argument (1253a7-18):** 8. Nature makes nothing in vain. 9. Humans alone have speech, which sets out the expedient and so the just and unjust. 10. A shared perception of just and unjust makes a household and a city. 11. ∴ Humans are political more than bees or any herd animal.

**Where it lives:** premise 5 ("natural" means the completion of a natural process). Premise 1 contains slavery, which I.3's critics attack.

*From* [1.3](lessons/01-03-aristotle-the-city-exists-by-nature.md)

### The priority argument

1. A whole is prior by nature to its parts.
2. Things are defined by their work and power; a part cut off is that part in name only (the stone hand).
3. Separated from the polis, an individual is not self-sufficient.
4. So the individual stands to the polis as part to whole.
5. ∴ The polis is prior by nature to the individual (1253a18-29).

**Weak point:** 3 to 4 (dependence is not parthood; the exile, unlike a severed hand, still speaks and reasons).

*From* [1.3](lessons/01-03-aristotle-the-city-exists-by-nature.md)

### The six regimes derivation

1. A citizen, strictly, shares in judging and ruling (III.1).
2. So the citizen differs by constitution.
3. The good citizen need not be a good man (III.4).
4. A constitution is the arrangement of offices, above all the supreme one; the governing body is the constitution (III.6).
5. Rule over free equals exists for the ruled, like the arts; so constitutions for the common interest are correct and those for the rulers' interest deviant, being despotic (III.6).
6. Supreme authority rests with one, few or many; crossed with 5, six regimes (III.7).
7. But number is accidental; wealth is essential (III.8).
8. Each class holds a partial justice; shares in rule should track contribution to the good life (III.9).
9. The many may collectively judge better than the few best (III.11).
10. ∴ For most cities the best attainable constitution is a polity anchored by a large middle class (IV.8-9, IV.11).

*From* [1.4](lessons/01-04-aristotle-constitutions-citizens-and-the-polity.md)

### The summation argument outline

*(outline; full reconstruction is Boss 1)* Each of the many has some share of virtue and practical wisdom; pooled, they are like one person with many hands, feet and senses, as a feast many contribute to beats one from a single purse; so the many, taken together, may judge better than the few best, and should at least elect and audit officials (*Politics* III.11). It does not hold of every multitude. Crux with the ship of state: whether political knowledge is a craft the many cannot share or a judgment that partial insights can assemble.

### Cicero commonwealth argument

1. A commonwealth is the affair of a people (I.25 (39)).
2. A people is an association bound by agreement on right and by shared advantage (I.25 (39)).
3. Humans associate by nature, not chiefly from weakness (I.25).
4. Every commonwealth needs a directing authority, one, few or many; each is tolerable while the bond of 2 holds, and each slides toward its corruption (I.26, I.28-29, I.44).
5. So the best form blends royal, aristocratic and popular elements (I.45 (69)); Rome is the best instance.
6. Where a tyrant, faction or mob holds everything, nothing is the people's affair (III.31-33 (43-45)).
7. ∴ A tyranny is not a corrupt commonwealth but no commonwealth at all.

**Strains:** no threshold for how much agreement on right, and no procedure for who judges.

*From* [2.1](lessons/02-01-cicero-the-commonwealth-and-natural-law.md)

### Cicero true law argument

1. Law is the highest reason, implanted in nature, commanding what ought to be done and forbidding the contrary (*On the Laws* I.6 (18)).
2. Nature gave reason to all, so right reason, and therefore law and right, belong to all (I.12 (33)).
3. If votes and decrees made right, a vote could make robbery or forgery right (I.16 (43-44)).
4. So enactments contrary to right reason do not deserve the name of law, any more than robbers' agreements (II.5 (13)); "Are then the laws of tyrants just, simply because they are laws?" (I.15 (42)).
5. ∴ True law is one, eternal and universal; Rome's laws are genuine laws to the degree they agree with it, and no senate or people can release anyone from it.

Part B supplies what "right" means in the definition of a people; without it, any regime whose subjects agreed would count.

*From* [2.1](lessons/02-01-cicero-the-commonwealth-and-natural-law.md)

### Augustine on Rome and the redefined people

1. Two loves have made two cities (XIV.28).
2. The earthly city is ruled by the lust to rule (preface to Book I; XIV.28).
3. Justice gives each his due, and what is due includes God (XIX.21).
4. Cicero's people requires agreement about right, and right flows only from justice; so "Rome never was a republic, because true justice had never a place in it" (II.21, XIX.21).
5. Without justice, rule is organized robbery in form (IV.4).
6. So redefine a people by its shared loves (XIX.24).
7. Earthly peace is a real good the heavenly city uses and obeys (XIX.17, XIX.26).
8. Rule of man over man is not natural; slavery is a penalty of sin (XIX.15).

**Crux with Cicero:** not whether a republic needs justice (both agree) but what is due, and to whom (3). **Open:** once justice leaves the definition of a people, what makes an earthly law binding? ([2.3](lessons/02-03-aquinas-on-law.md))

### Aquinas on the definition of law argument

1. Law is a rule and measure of acts, binding us to act or not act.
2. The rule and measure of human acts is reason, which directs to the end (a.1).
3. The first principle of practical reason is the last end, and one person is part of a complete community, so law regards the common good (a.2).
4. Ordering to the common good belongs to the whole people or to a public person who has care of it; a private person can only advise (a.3).
5. A rule binds only those to whom it is applied, and it is applied by being made known (a.4).
6. ∴ Law is an ordinance of reason for the common good, made by one who has care of the community, and promulgated (ST I-II q.90).

*From* [2.3](lessons/02-03-aquinas-on-law.md)

### Aquinas on deriving and limiting human law

7. Human law has the nature of law to the extent it derives from natural law (q.95 a.2).
8. Derivation runs by conclusion (as in a science) or by determination (as in a craft).
9. Conclusions keep some force from natural law; determinations have only the force of human law.
10. Law must fit an imperfect majority, so it forbids the graver vices, chiefly harms to others (q.96 a.2).
11. Law commands acts of any virtue only as they bear on the common good, and the act, not the virtuous spirit (a.3).
12. The ruler is exempt from law's coercive but not its directive force (a.5 ad 3).
13. A subject may act beside the letter to save the common good only when harm is manifest and too sudden to refer to authority (a.6).
14. Law may change as reason improves or conditions change, but change costs custom's force and needs a compensating benefit (q.97 aa.1-3).

**Strain:** if a determination has "no other force" than human law, what binds conscience to it? Aquinas's answer runs through authority ordered by eternal law (q.96 a.4).

### Aquinas on why one should rule

1. Humans are social by nature, and a multitude needs something directing it to its common good.
2. Rule is just when it serves the common good of the ruled, unjust when it serves the ruler's private good; so six regimes.
3. The first good of a community is its unity, called peace.
4. What is itself one brings about unity better than a plurality (the heart; the single ruler of the universe; cities torn by faction).
5. ∴ Kingship is the best form of rule.
6. By the same reasoning tyranny is the worst; and rule by the many breeds tyranny more often.

**Then:** the mixed regime (q.105 a.1) keeps one at the head and shares power as insurance, because perfect virtue is rare.

### Necessity and property

1. People have two relations to external goods: power to procure and dispense them, and their use (II-II q.66 a.2).
2. As to managing, private property is lawful and necessary; the division of goods is by human agreement added to natural law (a.2 ad 1).
3. As to use, hold things not as one's own but as common, ready to share with those in need.
4. Human law cannot override natural law, by which goods exist to meet human need (a.7).
5. ∴ When need is manifest and urgent, danger imminent and no other remedy, taking another's goods is not theft; what is taken becomes one's own by reason of the need (a.7 ad 2), also for a neighbor (ad 3).

### Marsilius on the legislator

1. The city is a natural community for living and living well, made of parts (farmers, artisans, soldiers, priests) with a ruling part that judges and enforces (I.4-5).
2. Law in the strict sense is a command backed by coercive penalty in this life (I.10).
3. The primary efficient cause of law, the human legislator, is the whole body of citizens or its weightier part, judged by quantity and quality of persons (I.12).
4. Because the whole judges the common benefit better than any part; a law each citizen has in a sense imposed on himself is better obeyed; no one knowingly legislates to his own harm, while the few legislate for their advantage (I.12-13).
5. The legislator establishes the ruling part and can correct or depose it (I.15, I.18).
6. Christ gave no coercive jurisdiction to his apostles; priests teach and warn but compel no one (II.4-5).
7. ∴ Papal plenitude of power is a usurpation and the peculiar cause of strife in Italy (I.1, I.19).

Premise 4's first reason is Aristotle's summation argument ([1.4](lessons/01-04-aristotle-constitutions-citizens-and-the-polity.md)).

### Unam Sanctam and Constance

**Unam Sanctam (paraphrased):** 1. The Church is one body with one head, Christ and his vicar. 2. Both swords are in the Church's power, the temporal wielded for the Church at the priest's will and sufferance. 3. So councils must meet regularly (*Frequens*). **Strain:** "immediately" (standing constitution) versus necessity and *epieikeia* (Gerson).

### Prince chapter 15

1. Useful counsel must follow the effectual truth, not imagined states.
2. A prince lives among many who are not good.
3. So a prince who does in all things what goodness requires will be ruined.
4. Human conditions do not permit a prince to possess or practise all the praised qualities at once.
5. The prince's end is to maintain his state.
6. ∴ A prince must learn to be able not to be good, and use this or not according to necessity; avoid the vices that would lose the state, and not fear reproach for those without which it is hard to save.

### Prince chapter 17

1. Being both loved and feared is best, but hard to combine; so which is safer, if one must go?
2. Men in general are ungrateful, fickle, false, cowardly and covetous, offering everything while danger is far off.
3. Love rests on a bond of obligation, which men break whenever it serves their advantage.
4. Fear rests on dread of punishment, which does not fail.
5. Men love at their own will but fear at the prince's, and a wise prince relies on what is in his control.
6. Constraint: fear must not become hatred; keep from subjects' property and women, kill only with manifest cause.
7. ∴ It is safer to be feared (without being hated) than loved.

**Weak points:** 2 (a general claim about human nature) or 4 (6 concedes fear can curdle). **Crux with Aquinas:** 3.

### Prince 18 and Leviathan 21

*(outline; full reconstruction is Boss 3)* *Prince* ch. 18: a prince must know how to use the beast as well as the man, the fox as well as the lion; since men are not good and will not keep faith with him, he need not keep faith when doing so turns against him and the reasons for his promise are gone; he must appear to have the virtues. *Leviathan* ch. 21: the liberty of subjects lies in what the sovereign has passed over in silence, and in rights that cannot be transferred by covenant (such as defending one's own body); obligation lasts as long as the sovereign's power to protect. ### Discourses I.4 and the republic of conflict

1. Every city contains two humours: the great desire to dominate, the people only not to be dominated (I.5; *Prince* ch. 9).
2. Each simple form is unstable; a mixed form, each part checking the others, lasts longer (I.2, after Polybius).
3. A mixed order holds only if each humour has an ordinary, lawful outlet (I.7).
4. Rome won its popular element through conflict, so the conflict produced the laws that made it free (I.2-4).
5. Liberty is safest guarded by those who desire only not to be dominated; a law-bound people judges better than a law-bound prince (I.5, I.58).
6. A republic that may have to expand needs a numerous armed people, and cannot have that and quiet at once (I.6).
7. All orders corrupt and survive only if brought back to their beginnings (III.1), with religion binding oaths (I.11).
8. ∴ A durable, great and free republic is managed conflict among an armed and religious citizenry, periodically renewed.

**I.4's own moves:** good armies presuppose good order; the tumults exiled and killed few in three centuries; virtue from education, education from good laws, good laws from the tumults; the rowdy methods were Rome's ordinary way for the people to voice demands. **Weak points:** the causal claim that the laws *originated* in tumults; the low-harm count, which stops at the Gracchi.

### Hobbes chapter 13 and the laws of nature

1. Every person seeks to preserve their life and satisfy desires that never finally end; hence a restless desire of power after power, from insecurity (ch. 11).
2. People are roughly equal: the weakest can kill the strongest (ch. 13).
3. Equality of ability breeds equality of hope, so two who want the same thing become enemies (competition).
4. Since some take pleasure in conquest beyond what security needs, and no one can tell who, even the moderate must anticipate (diffidence).
5. People want to be valued as they value themselves and punish contempt (glory).
6. So without a common power there is war of every man against every man.
7. In it there is no industry and no injustice; life is solitary, poor, nasty, brutish and short.
8. The right of nature is each one's liberty to use his power for his preservation, extending in war to everything (ch. 14).
9. A law of nature forbids what destroys one's life: seek peace; lay down the right to all things when others will (ch. 14).
10. ∴ These are theorems of self-preservation, properly laws only as the command of one with right to command (ch. 15), and they point to a common power.

**Engine:** 4, diffidence, which makes even the peaceful strike first; it fails if dispositions are reliably known. **Crux with Aristotle:** whether speech and reason tend to shared or rival judgments of justice (ch. 17 against *Politics* I.2).

### Hobbes on authorization

1. A person is one whose words or actions are taken as his own (natural) or as representing another's (artificial) (ch. 16).
2. When an actor acts by authority, the author owns the act and is bound by it, as far as the commission goes (ch. 16).
3. A multitude becomes one person by being represented by one representative, with each member's consent (ch. 16).
4. Only a common power strong enough to overawe everyone gives the laws of nature security (ch. 17).
5. So each covenants with each to authorize one man or assembly to bear their person in what concerns common peace and safety (ch. 17).
6. The sovereign makes no covenant with the subjects, so he cannot breach one (ch. 18).
7. Every subject is author of all the sovereign's actions and judgments (ch. 18).
8. ∴ The sovereign cannot injure a subject, cannot justly be punished by one, and holds all rights needed for the end of peace, including judging doctrines (ch. 18).

**Why no forfeiture (ch. 18, second right):** a prior covenant by the sovereign would be with the multitude as one party (impossible: not yet one person) or with each severally (void: any alleged breach is done in every subject's person); and with no judge, a dispute over breach returns to the sword. **Pressure points:** authorization is elsewhere limited by the commission, so why is this one unlimited? Locke denies 6 ([4.2](lessons/04-02-locke-limited-government-and-toleration.md)).

### Locke on property from self-ownership

*(outline; full reconstruction with both provisos is Boss 4)* God gave the world to mankind in common (§25-26); it must be appropriated to be of use (§26); every man has property in his person, so his labour is his, and mixing it with what is common makes the thing his, where enough and as good is left (§27); no consent of the commoners is needed, or man had starved (§28-29); take only what you can use before it spoils (§31); land is acquired the same way and cultivation increases the common stock (§32, §37); money by consent permits unequal holdings without spoilage (§46-50). ∴ Property, including unequal property, exists before government, which exists to preserve it (§123-124).

### Locke on consent and dissolution

1. No one is subject without consent (§95).
2. Consent can be tacit: enjoying a government's protection binds you to its laws while you do, though only express consent makes you a member (§119-122).
3. No one can transfer more power than he has; no one has arbitrary power over life, so the legislative cannot (§135).
4. So the legislative is bound: standing laws, the people's good, no tax without consent, no transfer of lawmaking (§140, §142).
5. Political power is held in trust for an end; betraying the end forfeits it (§149).
6. Breach dissolves the government, not the society (§211, §215, §222).
7. ∴ The people judge whether the trust is broken (§240); where no earthly judge will hear, appeal to heaven (§241-42).

**Pressure points:** 2 (can the poor traveller really leave?); 7 (Hobbes: private judgment of rulers means civil war), answered by the long-train brake (§225). **Crux with Hobbes:** whether what is handed over is a power held in trust for an end, or the right of judgment itself.

### Locke on toleration argument

1. The commonwealth is for civil interests only.
2. The care of souls was not committed to the magistrate, by God or by the people.
3. True religion is inward persuasion, and the understanding cannot be compelled by outward force; even if it could, magistrates disagree, so salvation would depend on birthplace.
4. A church is a voluntary society whose only penalty is expulsion.
5. ∴ The magistrate may not use force for religion.
6. Except: opinions contrary to the moral rules civil society needs; churches serving a foreign prince; atheists, whom oaths cannot bind.

**Weak point:** the step from "force cannot produce belief" to "the magistrate has no right to use it" (Proast).

### Rousseau conjectural history

1. The state of nature must contain only what does not presuppose society.
2. Natural man is solitary, with few needs, easily met.
3. His motives are *amour de soi* and pity, which moderates self-preservation.
4. So the state of nature is peaceful, and Hobbes imported social passions into it.
5. Man alone is perfectible.
6. Small advances produce settled life: huts, families, neighbours, languages.
7. Proximity breeds comparison, and comparison *amour-propre*.
8. Metallurgy and agriculture create mutual dependence and property.
9. Property without law produces a war of rich and poor.
10. The rich propose a contract of equal rules, which binds new fetters on the poor.
11. Inequality passes through property and law, magistracy, arbitrary power.
12. ∴ Moral inequality has almost no basis in nature; where out of proportion to physical inequality it conflicts with natural right.

**Strain:** nascent society, already with *amour-propre*, is the happiest epoch; so the decisive corruption is dependence plus property. **Crux with Locke:** what makes first appropriation legitimate (Boss 4).

### Social Contract I.6-7

1. Isolated forces no longer suffice; people must pool force or perish.
2. But each one's force and liberty are his chief means of self-preservation.
3. The clauses reduce to total alienation of each, with all his rights, to the whole: conditions are equal, no one keeps a private reserve he judges himself, and giving himself to all he gives himself to no one (I.6).
4. The compact creates a collective moral body; members are citizens and subjects.
5. The general will must be general in source and object (II.4).
6. Sovereignty is inalienable and cannot be represented (II.1, III.15).
7. ∴ The citizen trades natural liberty for civil and moral liberty, obedience to a law he prescribes to himself (I.8).

**I.7:** the sovereign, made of the individuals, has no interest contrary to theirs; but each man can have a particular will contrary to his general will and want rights without duties; that would ruin the body; so the compact tacitly includes compulsion of whoever refuses; being bound to the whole protects each from dependence on any particular person (the *Discourse*'s disease); ∴ he is forced to be free. **Crux with Hobbes:** whether a will can be represented.

### Montesquieu XI.6

1. Political liberty is a tranquillity of mind arising from each person's opinion of his safety.
2. "Every man invested with power is apt to abuse it" (XI.4).
3. So abuse is prevented only if, by the arrangement of things, power checks power (XI.4).
4. There are three powers: making law, executing it, judging under it.
5. One person or body holding legislative and executive power can make tyrannical laws and execute them tyrannically.
6. Judging joined to the legislative makes the judge a legislator; joined to the executive, an oppressor.
7. All three in one hand, as in Turkey, is the end of everything.
8. ∴ Liberty requires the three powers in different hands *and* each able to stop the others.

**Objections:** Venice separates councils but one class staffs them; England's ministers sit in Parliament; I.3 says laws must fit a people, so why one rule for liberty everywhere? **Crux between strict and checks readings:** which half of 8 carries the weight.

### Federalist 10

1. Faction is the mortal disease of popular government.
2. There are two cures: remove its causes, or control its effects.
3. Causes can be removed only by destroying liberty (worse than the disease) or giving everyone the same opinions, passions and interests (impossible: reason is fallible and tied to self-love, and faculties for acquiring property differ).
4. So relief lies only in controlling effects.
5. A minority faction is controlled by the republican principle: the majority outvotes it.
6. The real danger is a majority faction, and moral and religious motives are no reliable check on it.
7. A pure democracy has no cure: a common passion easily grips a majority, and the assembly supplies concert.
8. A republic delegates to a chosen few, which may refine and enlarge the public views (or elevate schemers), and can extend over more citizens and territory.
9. Size helps: more fit candidates per seat, larger constituencies harder to corrupt, and above all more variety of parties and interests, so a majority with a common motive is less likely and harder to organize.
10. ∴ The large Union controls faction better than the states, as a republic does better than a democracy.

**Crux with Brutus:** whether a free republic needs a people similar in manners, sentiments and interests. **Strain:** 9 needs crosscutting interests.

### Burke against the rights of man

1. Men have real rights: to justice, to the fruits of their industry, to what their parents acquired, to instruction in life and consolation in death.
2. These are the advantages civil society exists to provide; government is a contrivance of human wisdom to provide for human wants, including restraint of passions.
3. So a share in power is not a natural right but conventional; all have equal rights, but not to equal things.
4. Abstract rights are refracted when they enter common life.
5. No one generation's private stock of reason is equal to that complexity; prejudice and prescription store tested experience.
6. So reform must conserve and correct, confined to the part that failed and following precedent, as in 1688.
7. ∴ Rebuilding a state from the rights of man mistakes abstraction for political reason and destroys the real rights of 1; Price's reading of 1688 is false history.

**Attacked at:** 5 (Paine: the dead cannot bind the living); 6 (Price). **Strain:** prescription covers only *beneficial* continuance, so benefit, not age, justifies, and someone must judge it.

### Wollstonecraft on virtue and education

1. Reason raises humans above brutes, and virtue raises one being above another (ch. 1).
2. There is one standard of virtue for all rational beings (ch. 2).
3. A virtue is genuine only if it results from the agent's own reason (ch. 2).
4. Reason grows strong only by exercise, and virtue needs liberty (chs. 2, 13).
5. Dependence replaces one's own reason with another's favour, and so breeds cunning (chs. 4, 9).
6. Women's observed faults are effects of education and station, not nature (Introduction, ch. 13).
7. ∴ Women must be educated as rational beings, with the same studies as men, and given enough civil and economic independence for their virtue to be their own (chs. 9, 12).

**Rousseau at full strength** denies 2, not 3: same end, different means, complementary virtues. **Counter:** his own politics makes dependence on a particular will the root of corruption. **Unsettled:** 6, a causal claim neither could test.

**Against Burke (*Rights of Men*):** 1. Rational creatures inherit rights from God, not forefathers, so prescription cannot undermine them. 2. The birthright is liberty compatible with everyone else's. 3. Burke's inheritance protects hereditary property and rank, which corrupt. 4. His sentiment is selective, so no test of right.

### Hegel from civil society to the state

1. The will is free when it wills its own freedom (§§ 21-29).
2. Abstract right: a person gives freedom external existence in property, exchanges it by contract, and has it restored by punishment (§§ 41-104).
3. Crime shows right needs a will that wills right for its own sake: morality (§§ 104-140).
4. But morality alone is empty (§ 135) and conscience can will evil (§§ 139-140).
5. Ethical life is freedom existing as institutions the individual knows as his own; there duty is liberation (§§ 142-149).
6. Its moments: the family, unity through love (§ 158); civil society, particularity released into a system of mutual dependence (§§ 182-256).
7. Civil society left to itself piles up wealth and a pauper class it cannot absorb and is driven to trade and colonies (§§ 243-248); the corporation covers only its members (§§ 250-256).
8. ∴ The state is the actuality of the ethical idea (§ 257), not an optional protection agency (§ 258), but the whole in which subjective freedom runs to its extreme and is brought back into the common life (§ 260).

**Pressure points:** Rousseau (is the rational will only what citizens will?); Marx 1843 (does the state solve or mask the rabble?). Each stage is contained, not replaced.

### Tocqueville on individualism

1. Equality of conditions is advancing throughout Christendom and cannot be stopped.
2. In aristocratic society each person is bound to others across classes and across time.
3. Equality dissolves both bonds; many become self-sufficient.
4. So equality breeds individualism: calm withdrawal into a private circle.
5. Isolated citizens suit despotism, which asks only that subjects not combine (Vol. II, Part 2, ch. 4).
6. Free local institutions force cooperation, and necessity becomes habit and then taste.
7. Associations give weak equal citizens the power a lord once had; political associations teach the art of association (ch. 7).
8. Interest rightly understood yields many small daily acts of self-command (ch. 8).
9. Religion restrains what people dare conceive and is strong because separate from the state (Vol. I, ch. 17).
10. ∴ Equality can be free rather than despotic, but only through mores, local liberty and association, which a new political science must study and protect.

*From* [6.2](lessons/06-02-tocqueville-equality-of-conditions.md)

### Tocqueville on the tyranny of the majority

1. In American democracy every power flows from the majority: opinion, legislature, executive, jury, often judges.
2. The majority's authority is moral as well as physical: more wisdom in many than one; the many's interests outrank the few's.
3. Among equals that belief is hard to resist; each feels insignificant before the mass (Vol. II, Part 1, ch. 2).
4. A king controls actions, not the will; the majority reaches the will.
5. In Europe a dissenter finds shelter with a rival power; in America there is one sole authority.
6. The sanction is exclusion, not death: career, esteem and company lost.
7. ∴ Within the majority's barriers one may write anything; beyond them few even wish to.

**Softeners (Vol. I, ch. 16):** decentralized administration, lawyers, the jury. **Crux with Madison:** majority of interest versus community of opinion.

### The tutelary power passage

*(outline; full reconstruction is Boss 6)* Equality makes people alike and isolated, each withdrawn into petty private pleasures; above them rises an immense tutelary power, absolute, minute, regular, provident and mild, which secures their enjoyments and manages their affairs and would spare them the trouble of thinking and living; its small, uniform rules gradually take from each the use of his own will; periodic election of the guardians does not restore it, since people who never govern small affairs lose the capacity to choose well in great ones (Vol. II, Part 4, ch. 6).

### Mill on representative government

1. Good government depends on the good qualities of those who compose, choose and watch it.
2. So a form is judged by how well it uses existing qualities and how far it improves them; the second matters most.
3. Each person's rights and interests are secure only when he is able and disposed to stand up for them.
4. Human beings improve only by active effort; the active character is better than the passive.
5. Rule by one or a few breeds passivity, even when benevolent; participation breeds activity and public spirit.
6. ∴ The ideally best form puts sovereignty in the whole community, with every citizen sometimes taking a real part.
7. In any community larger than a small town most can take part in person only in minor functions.
8. ∴ The ideal type of a perfect government must be representative, where a people can sustain it.

*From* [6.4](lessons/06-04-mill-representative-government.md)

### Mill on the open ballot

1. The vote is power over others, so it is a trust, not a personal right (ch. X).
2. Secret voting tells the elector he may cast it as he pleases.
3. Open voting subjects his selfish and class motives to the opinion of fellow citizens.
4. Where voters are coerced by landlords or employers, secrecy may be the lesser evil.
5. In Mill's England coercion was waning, and the voter's own sinister interest was the greater danger (empirical premise).
6. ∴ Votes should be cast openly.

**Where the condition lives:** 5; change the facts and by Mill's reasoning the verdict changes. **Plural voting strains** by his own criteria: the occupational proxy tracks class, and unequal votes may teach passivity.

### Marx on emancipation and the 1859 Preface

**Stage 1:** 1. Hegel makes the Idea the subject and real institutions its predicates. 2. Invert it, as Feuerbach inverted theology. 3. Political emancipation is real but limited: religion moves into civil society. 4. The rights of man are the rights of the member of civil society. 5. Human emancipation would end the split between citizen and egoist.

**Stage 2:** 6. Humans are species-beings realizing themselves in free, conscious, social production. 7. Under wage labour this is alienated from product, activity, species-being and others.

**Stage 3:** 8. Practice, not contemplation, is the point (Thesis XI). 9. People enter relations of production corresponding to a stage of the forces of production. 10. Those relations are the foundation; law, state and consciousness rise on it; social existence determines consciousness. 11. When forces outgrow relations, the relations become fetters and social revolution begins. 12. No order passes before its productive forces are developed; bourgeois society is the last antagonistic form.

∴ The state is part of a superstructure resting on relations of production, and human emancipation requires changing those relations.

### Marx on the state and Bonapartism

1. Political power is the organized power of one class for oppressing another (*Manifesto* II).
2. The modern executive is a committee for managing the common affairs of the whole bourgeoisie (*Manifesto* I).
3. Where no class can rule in its own name, the executive can make itself look independent: the bourgeoisie gives up political power to keep social power, and scattered smallholding peasants are represented by a master from above (*Brumaire* VII).
4. The centralized machinery was built for class rule, so the working class cannot simply take it over (*Civil War in France*).
5. The Commune shows the replacement: armed people, elected recallable officials at workmen's wages, one working body.
6. The transition can only be the revolutionary dictatorship of the proletariat (*Gotha* IV; letter to Weydemeyer, 1852).
7. The first phase distributes by labour, an equal standard unequal in effect (*Gotha* I).
8. A higher phase, with abundance, distributes by need.
9. ∴ With classes abolished, public power loses its political character (*Manifesto* II).

**Gap:** 5 and 6 are never tied together in Marx's words. **Layers:** 2 is instrumentalist, 3 structural (Miliband against Poulantzas).

*From* [6.6](lessons/06-06-marx-the-state-revolution-and-after.md)

### The Manifesto on the state

*(outline; full reconstruction is Boss 6)* The bourgeoisie, having established modern industry and the world market, has conquered exclusive political sway in the modern representative state; that state's executive is but a committee for managing the common affairs of the whole bourgeoisie; so political power is the organized power of one class for oppressing another, and when class distinctions disappear public power loses its political character. Crux with Tocqueville: whether the state is shaped by civil society's class relations or can itself reshape civil society.

## Pitfalls

### Module 1: The Greek polis

- *Dikaiosyne* is not modern social justice; Book IV has almost nothing on fair distribution. *([1.1](lessons/01-01-platos-republic-justice-in-city-and-soul.md))*
- The three classes are functional, not Marxian classes or hereditary castes; the myth provides for moving between them. *([1.1](lessons/01-01-platos-republic-justice-in-city-and-soul.md))*
- Glaucon does not endorse the contract view (358c); he states it to have it defeated. *([1.1](lessons/01-01-platos-republic-justice-in-city-and-soul.md))*
- Thrasymachus and Glaucon both debunk justice but tell opposite stories: the strong write the law against the weak, or the weak write it together against the strong. *([1.1](lessons/01-01-platos-republic-justice-in-city-and-soul.md))*
- Plato's *demokratia* is Athenian direct rule by lot and assembly, and his *eleutheria* is doing what one likes; transfer to representative democracy is a separate question. *([1.2](lessons/01-02-plato-against-democracy-and-the-second-best-city.md))*
- Book VIII is a psychological typology, not a history of Greece; Aristotle's attack on it as history (*Politics* V.12) misses if so. *([1.2](lessons/01-02-plato-against-democracy-and-the-second-best-city.md))*
- Two "second bests": the colony second to the communistic city (739b-e), law second to living knowledge (875d). *([1.2](lessons/01-02-plato-against-democracy-and-the-second-best-city.md))*
- The *Laws* makes law sovereign, not the demos; democratic elements are hedged by property classes and the Nocturnal Council. *([1.2](lessons/01-02-plato-against-democracy-and-the-second-best-city.md))*
- "Political animal" is not "social animal": bees are gregarious, humans argue about justice. *([1.3](lessons/01-03-aristotle-the-city-exists-by-nature.md))*
- Jowett's "state" is the *polis*; priority of the city is not a charter for bureaucracy. *([1.3](lessons/01-03-aristotle-the-city-exists-by-nature.md))*
- II.3's neglect of common things is not the tragedy of the commons; the target is guardian communism, and the remedy is private ownership with common use. *([1.3](lessons/01-03-aristotle-the-city-exists-by-nature.md))*
- The critique of Plato is not individualism: a city can be too much one, but the city is still prior. *([1.3](lessons/01-03-aristotle-the-city-exists-by-nature.md))*
- *Politeia* is not a written constitution; "unconstitutional" has no Aristotelian meaning. *([1.4](lessons/01-04-aristotle-constitutions-citizens-and-the-polity.md))*
- Aristotle's democracy is rule of the poor for themselves, deviant because it serves one class, not because many rule; election counts as oligarchic. *([1.4](lessons/01-04-aristotle-constitutions-citizens-and-the-polity.md))*
- The summation argument is not the Condorcet jury theorem: complementary partial insights, not independent guesses. *([1.4](lessons/01-04-aristotle-constitutions-citizens-and-the-polity.md))*
- Aristotle's middle class is citizens of moderate property in a small agrarian city, not an income band. *([1.4](lessons/01-04-aristotle-constitutions-citizens-and-the-polity.md))*

### Module 2: Rome and Christendom

- *Res publica* is not "republic" as opposed to monarchy: a just kingship is a commonwealth, and ranks first among the simple forms (I.45). *([2.1](lessons/02-01-cicero-the-commonwealth-and-natural-law.md))*
- *Iuris consensu* is agreement on a right with a standard in nature, not Locke's consent of the governed. *([2.1](lessons/02-01-cicero-the-commonwealth-and-natural-law.md))*
- Cicero did not invent natural law; he took it from the Stoics and made it political. *([2.1](lessons/02-01-cicero-the-commonwealth-and-natural-law.md))*
- "No senate or people can dispense us" says true law cannot be repealed, not that private disobedience is licensed. *([2.1](lessons/02-01-cicero-the-commonwealth-and-natural-law.md))*
- The two cities are not Church and State: membership is by love, which only God sees. *([2.2](lessons/02-02-augustine-the-two-cities.md))*
- Augustine did not invent the secular state; *saeculum* means this age (Markus and Milbank dispute the rest). *([2.2](lessons/02-02-augustine-the-two-cities.md))*
- IV.4 is conditional ("justice being taken away"); it does not simply say states are gangs. *([2.2](lessons/02-02-augustine-the-two-cities.md))*
- Augustine and Cicero agree a true republic needs justice; they part over what is due, and to whom. *([2.2](lessons/02-02-augustine-the-two-cities.md))*
- q.96 a.2 is not Mill's harm principle: harm to others marks the chief cases, and the restraint is prudential. *([2.3](lessons/02-03-aquinas-on-law.md))*
- "Determination" does not mean arbitrary; it is free among reasonable options. *([2.3](lessons/02-03-aquinas-on-law.md))*
- "Him who has care of the community" is the whole people or a public person; Aquinas does not choose in q.90. *([2.3](lessons/02-03-aquinas-on-law.md))*
- The ruler's exemption (q.96 a.5) is from coercion, not direction; Hobbes's sovereign is not subject to civil law at all. *([2.3](lessons/02-03-aquinas-on-law.md))*
- "Democracy" in q.105 a.1 is choosing rulers from and by the people, not popular sovereignty. *([2.4](lessons/02-04-aquinas-on-kingship-tyranny-and-property.md))*
- Aquinas has no right of revolution: deposition by public authority is not sedition, under a harm proviso. *([2.4](lessons/02-04-aquinas-on-kingship-tyranny-and-property.md))*
- Private property is not a natural right for Aquinas but a human arrangement added to natural law; do not merge with Locke. *([2.4](lessons/02-04-aquinas-on-kingship-tyranny-and-property.md))*
- a.7 does not say the poor may take from the rich; it needs urgent, manifest need and no other remedy. *([2.4](lessons/02-04-aquinas-on-kingship-tyranny-and-property.md))*
- The *legislator humanus* is not modern popular sovereignty: the weightier part counts quality, and citizens exclude women, slaves, foreigners. *([2.5](lessons/02-05-marsilius-and-conciliarism.md))*
- Conciliarists were not proto-Protestants: Constance elected a pope and condemned Hus. *([2.5](lessons/02-05-marsilius-and-conciliarism.md))*
- "Two swords" is not separation of church and state; every position assumes one Christian society. *([2.5](lessons/02-05-marsilius-and-conciliarism.md))*
- Do not grade a bull's or decree's doctrinal weight here; report what it claimed and how it was used. *([2.5](lessons/02-05-marsilius-and-conciliarism.md))*

### Module 3: Machiavelli and Hobbes

- *Virtù* is not "virtue", and not mere skill either: Agathocles is denied it despite success. *([3.1](lessons/03-01-machiavelli-the-prince.md))*
- "The end justifies the means" is not in the book; Machiavelli's terms are necessity, maintaining the state and glory. *([3.1](lessons/03-01-machiavelli-the-prince.md))*
- Feared is not hated: ch. 17's constraint does half the work. *([3.1](lessons/03-01-machiavelli-the-prince.md))*
- *Lo stato* is the prince's position and dominion, not the modern impersonal state. *([3.1](lessons/03-01-machiavelli-the-prince.md))*
- *Libertà* is a self-governing city, not a zone of individual non-interference. *([3.2](lessons/03-02-machiavelli-the-discourses.md))*
- The humours are not Marx's classes: the conflict is meant to be balanced, not won. *([3.2](lessons/03-02-machiavelli-the-discourses.md))*
- Machiavelli does not simply praise tumult: he praises ordinary channels and condemns extraordinary ones (I.7, I.37). *([3.2](lessons/03-02-machiavelli-the-discourses.md))*
- The religion chapters make him neither a pagan enemy nor a pious reformer by themselves; "civil religion" is Rousseau's term. *([3.2](lessons/03-02-machiavelli-the-discourses.md))*
- The state of nature is not a historical era; Hobbes says it was never generally so. *([3.3](lessons/03-03-hobbes-matter-motion-and-the-state-of-nature.md))*
- Hobbes did not hold humans evil: the passions are no sin; war comes from equality, scarcity and uncertainty. *([3.3](lessons/03-03-hobbes-matter-motion-and-the-state-of-nature.md))*
- Hobbes's "law of nature" is a theorem of self-preservation, not Thomist natural law or a modern moral duty. *([3.3](lessons/03-03-hobbes-matter-motion-and-the-state-of-nature.md))*
- Hobbes's "right" is a liberty, the absence of obligation, not a claim others must respect. *([3.3](lessons/03-03-hobbes-matter-motion-and-the-state-of-nature.md))*
- "Absolute" is not totalitarian, and "representative" is not elected. *([3.4](lessons/03-04-hobbes-the-sovereign-and-the-church.md))*
- Hobbes does not separate church and state; he denies there are two governments. *([3.4](lessons/03-04-hobbes-the-sovereign-and-the-church.md))*
- The sovereign is not a party to the covenant; importing Locke's trust makes ch. 18 unintelligible. *([3.4](lessons/03-04-hobbes-the-sovereign-and-the-church.md))*
- Ch. 47 targets a political claim, papal power over princes, from Protestant premises; whether it reaches Christianity is the Martinich-Curley dispute. *([3.4](lessons/03-04-hobbes-the-sovereign-and-the-church.md))*

### Module 4: Consent, property and the general will

- Locke's "property" often means life, liberty and estate (§123), not just holdings. *([4.1](lessons/04-01-locke-against-filmer-and-property.md))*
- Self-ownership is a right against other humans, not absolute dominion: no one can enslave himself (§23); modern libertarian versions drop the theistic frame. *([4.1](lessons/04-01-locke-against-filmer-and-property.md))*
- "Enough and as good" may not be a proviso at all (Waldron); only spoilage is stated outright as a limit. *([4.1](lessons/04-01-locke-against-filmer-and-property.md))*
- Filmer was mainstream royalism, and "all the world was America" means real land in a colonial context. *([4.1](lessons/04-01-locke-against-filmer-and-property.md))*
- "Government exists to preserve property" is not a claim about protecting the rich. *([4.2](lessons/04-02-locke-limited-government-and-toleration.md))*
- Locke's toleration is a limit on jurisdiction with civil exclusions, not modern religious liberty or the First Amendment. *([4.2](lessons/04-02-locke-limited-government-and-toleration.md))*
- Locke does not license individual revolt at will: the people judge collectively, after a long train of abuses. *([4.2](lessons/04-02-locke-limited-government-and-toleration.md))*
- Whether the religious premises are ornament is disputed (Dunn, Waldron against Strauss); do not assume a secular Locke. *([4.2](lessons/04-02-locke-limited-government-and-toleration.md))*
- "Noble savage" is not Rousseau's phrase; natural man is pre-moral, not noble. *([4.3](lessons/04-03-rousseau-discourse-on-inequality.md))*
- The conjectural history is a hypothetical model, not bad anthropology; criticise it as a model. *([4.3](lessons/04-03-rousseau-discourse-on-inequality.md))*
- Cole's "self-respect" is *amour de soi* and "egoism" is *amour-propre*. *([4.3](lessons/04-03-rousseau-discourse-on-inequality.md))*
- Rousseau does not call all law and property illegitimate; the actual first contract entrenched usurpation. *([4.3](lessons/04-03-rousseau-discourse-on-inequality.md))*
- The general will is not public opinion or the majority; a faction's majority is at best the will of all. *([4.4](lessons/04-04-rousseau-the-social-contract.md))*
- Rousseau does not champion democratic government: the people is sovereign, but democracy as government is for gods (III.4). *([4.4](lessons/04-04-rousseau-the-social-contract.md))*
- Rousseau's civil religion is a sovereign-fixed creed with intolerance as its negative dogma, not Bellah's shared public piety. *([4.4](lessons/04-04-rousseau-the-social-contract.md))*
- "Forced to be free" is deliberate, the key to the political machine, not a slip. *([4.4](lessons/04-04-rousseau-the-social-contract.md))*

### Module 5: The age of revolutions

- Montesquieu's separation of powers is not three co-equal branches: the judiciary is near nothing, with no judicial review, and checks run through hereditary orders. *([5.1](lessons/05-01-montesquieu-the-spirit-of-the-laws.md))*
- Republican "virtue" is political love of country and equality, and honour is the wish for distinction, not integrity. *([5.1](lessons/05-01-montesquieu-the-spirit-of-the-laws.md))*
- The principles state what each government requires, not what it has; a republic without virtue is not a counterexample. *([5.1](lessons/05-01-montesquieu-the-spirit-of-the-laws.md))*
- Montesquieu is not a climate determinist: one cause among seven, resisted by good legislators. *([5.1](lessons/05-01-montesquieu-the-spirit-of-the-laws.md))*
- Madison's "democracy" is a small assembly governing in person, and his "republic" is representative government, today's representative democracy. *([5.2](lessons/05-02-the-federalist-faction-and-the-extended-republic.md))*
- A faction is defined by adverseness to rights or the aggregate interest, not by being a party or organized group. *([5.2](lessons/05-02-the-federalist-faction-and-the-extended-republic.md))*
- Diversity in society (No. 10) is not checks in government (No. 51). *([5.2](lessons/05-02-the-federalist-faction-and-the-extended-republic.md))*
- Publius is an advocate in a ratification campaign, and No. 10's canonical status is a twentieth-century reading. *([5.2](lessons/05-02-the-federalist-faction-and-the-extended-republic.md))*
- Burke was a Whig (1688, America, Ireland's Catholics, Hastings); "conservative" as a party label came later. *([5.3](lessons/05-03-burke-prescription-against-abstract-rights.md))*
- Burke's "prejudice" is a judgment formed before deliberation, not bigotry. *([5.3](lessons/05-03-burke-prescription-against-abstract-rights.md))*
- Burke does not oppose change; he opposes rebuilding from first elements. *([5.3](lessons/05-03-burke-prescription-against-abstract-rights.md))*
- Burke accepts contract; he denies it is one the living can dissolve. *([5.3](lessons/05-03-burke-prescription-against-abstract-rights.md))*
- "Feminist" and suffrage are anachronisms for Wollstonecraft; representation is one tentative hint. *([5.4](lessons/05-04-wollstonecraft-rights-virtue-and-education.md))*
- Her severity toward women is evidence for her causal claim, not contempt. *([5.4](lessons/05-04-wollstonecraft-rights-virtue-and-education.md))*
- Her rights are grounded in God-given reason and come with duties; a secular version must supply a substitute ground. *([5.4](lessons/05-04-wollstonecraft-rights-virtue-and-education.md))*
- Her "independence" is freedom from another's arbitrary will, not market self-reliance. *([5.4](lessons/05-04-wollstonecraft-rights-virtue-and-education.md))*

### Module 6: The nineteenth century

- Hegel's *Staat* is not the government, and his civil society is not the voluntary sector: it includes the market, courts and public welfare administration. *([6.1](lessons/06-01-hegel-freedom-realized-in-the-state.md))*
- "The rational is actual" does not mean whatever exists is justified; *wirklich* is technical. *([6.1](lessons/06-01-hegel-freedom-realized-in-the-state.md))*
- "The march of God in the world" is from a posthumous Addition and plausibly misread. *([6.1](lessons/06-01-hegel-freedom-realized-in-the-state.md))*
- Hegel's state does not swallow the individual: § 260 defines the modern state by letting subjective freedom develop fully. *([6.1](lessons/06-01-hegel-freedom-realized-in-the-state.md))*
- Tocqueville's "individualism" is a democratic disorder, not rugged self-reliance. *([6.2](lessons/06-02-tocqueville-equality-of-conditions.md))*
- Equality of conditions is not equality of wealth; it concerns rank, mobility and mutual regard. *([6.2](lessons/06-02-tocqueville-equality-of-conditions.md))*
- "Democracy" names the social state first; democratic peoples can live under despots. *([6.2](lessons/06-02-tocqueville-equality-of-conditions.md))*
- Interest rightly understood is neither an endorsement of egoism nor a sneer: attainable, not sufficient for virtue. *([6.2](lessons/06-02-tocqueville-equality-of-conditions.md))*
- Tyranny of the majority works through opinion and missing barriers, not unjust statutes or constant persecution. *([6.3](lessons/06-03-tocqueville-tyranny-of-the-majority-and-soft-despotism.md))*
- Soft despotism is mild and can be elected; do not confuse it with the passionate tyranny of the majority. *([6.3](lessons/06-03-tocqueville-tyranny-of-the-majority-and-soft-despotism.md))*
- Governmental centralization is not the danger; administrative centralization is. *([6.3](lessons/06-03-tocqueville-tyranny-of-the-majority-and-soft-despotism.md))*
- *Representative Government* is not *On Liberty*; do not import the harm principle into chs. VIII or X. *([6.4](lessons/06-04-mill-representative-government.md))*
- Mill's "false democracy" is majority-only representation; calling plural voting simply anti-democratic, or Mill a modern egalitarian, is anachronism. *([6.4](lessons/06-04-mill-representative-government.md))*
- The open ballot argument is conditional on an empirical judgment about coercion. *([6.4](lessons/06-04-mill-representative-government.md))*
- Mill restricts the ideal to peoples ready for it and defends governing dependencies; report it as part of the theory. *([6.4](lessons/06-04-mill-representative-government.md))*
- Marx's materialism is the priority of material production, not Hobbes's matter in motion. *([6.5](lessons/06-05-marx-from-hegel-to-historical-materialism.md))*
- The young-versus-mature Marx debate is a twentieth-century construction, since the 1844 Manuscripts appeared in 1932. *([6.5](lessons/06-05-marx-from-hegel-to-historical-materialism.md))*
- "Opium of the people" is not simply a sneer: religion is also the sigh of the oppressed. *([6.5](lessons/06-05-marx-from-hegel-to-historical-materialism.md))*
- "On the Jewish Question" is mainly about the state; Stage 1's argument does not depend on its second part. *([6.5](lessons/06-05-marx-from-hegel-to-historical-materialism.md))*
- "Dictatorship" in 1850 is neither a one-party police state nor clearly parliamentary majority rule; the form is left open. *([6.6](lessons/06-06-marx-the-state-revolution-and-after.md))*
- The "committee" sentence is not the whole theory; the *Brumaire* adds a structural layer. *([6.6](lessons/06-06-marx-the-state-revolution-and-after.md))*
- "Withering away" is Engels's image, and the need-based slogan predates Marx (often traced to Louis Blanc). *([6.6](lessons/06-06-marx-the-state-revolution-and-after.md))*
- The Gotha critique attacks a program; it is not a blueprint for the higher phase. *([6.6](lessons/06-06-marx-the-state-revolution-and-after.md))*
