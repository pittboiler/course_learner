# History of Political Thought · Lesson 4.1: Locke I: against Filmer, and the origin of property

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 4: Consent, property and the general will · Builds on: [3.3 Hobbes I: matter, motion and the state of nature](03-03-hobbes-matter-motion-and-the-state-of-nature.md) · Unlocks: [4.2 Locke II: limited government, revolution and toleration](04-02-locke-limited-government-and-toleration.md)

## Why this matters

"I built it, so it's mine" is one of the most confident sentences in modern politics. It turns up in homesteading law, in arguments about intellectual property, in libertarian theory, and in anti-colonial criticism of all three. Most of its users are borrowing from one chapter of a book written to refute a now-forgotten royalist. Read the chapter as that reply and it looks different: less a hymn to acquisitiveness than an answer to a specific dare. Explain how anything can be yours if God gave the world to everyone.

## The idea

In 1680, at the height of the Exclusion Crisis (the fight over whether the Catholic James could be barred from the succession), Tory writers published *Patriarcha* by Sir Robert Filmer, a Kentish royalist who had died in 1653 and probably wrote it decades earlier. Filmer's thesis was simple and, to many readers of the day, obvious. God gave Adam dominion over the world and fatherly authority over his descendants. Kings inherit that fatherly power. Nobody is born free, since everyone is born under a father, so the doctrine that mankind is naturally free is a dangerous novelty of the schoolmen and Jesuits.

Locke's *Two Treatises of Government* were published anonymously in late 1689 (the title page says 1690). Peter Laslett's edition (1960) argued they were mostly written earlier, around 1679–83, as Exclusion propaganda; the dating is widely accepted but still debated. The *First Treatise* takes Filmer apart line by line. Adam's grant in Genesis was to mankind, not to one man. Scripture's command is to honour father *and mother*, so fatherly power is not monarchy. And even if Adam had been a king, nobody can now identify his heir. The *Second Treatise* then builds the alternative.

Two moves carry it. First, the natural condition of humans is **freedom and equality under a law**. Locke's state of nature is not Hobbes's war of all against all: it has a law of nature, knowable by reason, and every person holds the power to enforce it (§7–8). Its trouble is not lawlessness but that everyone is judge in his own case (§13).

Second, Filmer had set a trap. If the world was originally common, as Grotius and Pufendorf held, then private property needs everyone's consent, which no one ever gave, so the common-ownership story is absurd. Locke's answer is **labour**. You do not need mankind's permission to eat an acorn.

## Source

*Second Treatise*, ch. II §6 (Locke's English). Locke has just said that reason *is* the law of nature and teaches it to anyone who consults it. Then:

> being all equal and independent, no one ought to harm another in his life, health, liberty, or possessions: for men being all the workmanship of one omnipotent, and infinitely wise maker

The sentence continues: men are God's servants and his property, made to last during his pleasure and not one another's. Watch the word "for". It tells you what the prohibition rests on.

## The argument

**From self-ownership to private property** (*Second Treatise* ch. V, §25–51).

1. **God gave the world to mankind in common**, for their support and comfort (§25–26). *In words:* the starting point is a shared inheritance, not Adam's private estate. That is the anti-Filmer premise.
2. **What is common must be appropriated before it can do anyone good**: the fruit must become part of the man it nourishes (§26). *In words:* a right to use the world is empty unless some way of making things yours exists.
3. **"every man has a property in his own person: this no body has any right to but himself"** (§27). *In words:* no other human being owns you.
4. **So the labour of his body and the work of his hands are his; whoever removes a thing from its natural state has mixed his labour with it** (§27). *In words:* your effort is yours, and it is now in the thing.
5. **That labour excludes others' common right, "at least where there is enough, and as good, left in common for others"** (§27). *In words:* taking is innocent when it leaves others no worse placed to take.
6. **No consent of the commoners is needed; if it were, man had starved** amid God's plenty (§28–29). *In words:* the Grotian demand for universal agreement is answered.
7. **You may take only as much as you can use before it spoils; the rest belongs to others** (§31). *In words:* waste, not quantity, is the injury.
8. **Land is acquired the same way, and cultivating it increases the common stock**: an enclosed acre yields many times what an acre left waste does (§32, §37). *In words:* the encloser gives more to mankind than he takes.
9. **Money, durable and valued only by consent, lets a man hold more than he can use without anything spoiling; by agreeing to money, men agreed to unequal possessions** (§46–50). *In words:* inequality is legitimate because the spoilage limit no longer bites and everyone accepted the medium that dissolved it.

**C.** Property, including unequal property, exists before and apart from government, and preserving it (life, liberty and estate, in Locke's broad sense, §123) is what government is for (§124).

## Map of positions

The question Filmer forced: *was the world originally common, and if so, how did anything become private?*

| | World originally common? | How private property begins | Consent of all needed? |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Filmer** | No: private dominion given to Adam | Grant from God to Adam, passed down by inheritance and gift | No, and the fact that it would be impossible is his argument against common ownership |
| **Grotius, Pufendorf** (as Filmer read them) | Yes | Agreement, express or tacit, to divide | Yes, in some form |
| **Locke** | Yes | Labour mixed with what is common, within the limits of spoilage and "enough and as good" | No, not for first appropriation; consent enters later, with money (§50) and civil law (§45) |

Filmer and Locke agree that universal consent is an impossible test. They draw opposite conclusions: Filmer, that the world was never common; Locke, that property never needed consent.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: the acorns (§28).** A man gathers acorns under an oak and eats them. Locke asks when they became his: when he digested, ate, boiled, brought them home, or picked them up? Run the argument. The acorns were common (P1). They were no use to him until appropriated (P2). Picking them up is labour, which is his (P3–4). Plenty of acorns remain (P5), he eats them before they rot (P7), and nobody was asked (P6). So they became his at the gathering, and if gathering did not make them his, nothing later could. The case is clean because every limit is slack. It shows the theory working exactly where Filmer said common ownership could not work.

**Example 2: the servant's turfs (§28).** In the same section Locke lists as *his* property the grass his horse has bitten, the ore he has dug, and the turfs his servant has cut. But the servant did the labour. How does it become the master's?

- **Macpherson's reading** (*The Political Theory of Possessive Individualism*, 1962): labour is property, property can be sold, so the servant sold his labour for wages and its product belongs to the buyer. On this reading Locke writes the logic of a market society into the state of nature, and the provisos turn out to be temporary.
- **Tully's reading** (*A Discourse on Property*, 1980): a servant in Locke's day was a member of the household, bound by a limited contract for set services (§85 describes such a contract). The line reflects household relations of the time and says nothing about unlimited wage labour, while the provisos and the duty of charity stay in force.

The case is hard because one phrase carries both. Whichever you favour, notice that P4 ("the work of *his* hands") has to be stretched to cover it.

## Watch out

- **Anachronism: "property" is not only estates.** Locke often uses the word for life, liberty and estate together (§123). Read "government exists to preserve property" as "to protect your holdings" and you have shrunk the thesis.
- **Self-ownership is not absolute dominion.** In §6 men are God's workmanship and property; in §27 each has property in his own person. These fit because the second is a right *against other humans*. Locke draws the consequence himself: no one can enslave himself or hand anyone power over his own life, since he has no such power to give (§23). Modern libertarian self-ownership drops the theistic frame. Jeremy Waldron (*God, Locke, and Equality*, 2002) argues that without it Locke's equality has no foundation. Others think it can stand alone; that dispute belongs to [political philosophy](../../political-philosophy/syllabus.md).
- **"Enough and as good" may not be a proviso at all.** Waldron argued, in a 1979 article, that §27's clause reads more naturally as a condition under which appropriation is *certainly* harmless than as a necessary condition. Only spoilage is stated outright as a limit (§31). Say "the provisos" knowing this is a live dispute.
- **Filmer was no crank, and America was not a metaphor.** Patriarchalism was a mainstream royalist view. And when Locke writes "in the beginning all the world was America" (§49), he means real land he treats as uncultivated. He was secretary to the proprietors of Carolina and had a hand in its *Fundamental Constitutions* (1669), and scholars such as James Tully and Barbara Arneil read ch. V partly as a defence of colonial appropriation. How far that context determines the argument is disputed.

## One-liner

> Filmer said that if the world had been common, nothing could ever become yours without everyone's consent; Locke answered that labour makes things yours without anyone's consent, and it takes money to make them unequally yours.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢) *(Exegetical.)*** Reread the §6 passage in **Source**. (a) Reader A says Locke's equality is secular: "reason teaches" it, and the reference to God is decoration. Reader B says the prohibition on harm rests on God's workmanship. Which reading do the words support? Name the word that decides it, and say what job *reason* does in the passage if not grounding. (b) Locke calls men God's property here and says in §27 that every man has property in his own person. Show in two sentences how both can hold. 150 words or fewer in total.

**P2 (🟡) *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)*** Filmer and Locke both take God as the ultimate source of authority and Scripture as evidence about it. (a) On the question *does anyone hold political authority by nature?*, name the single premise about fatherhood that Filmer affirms and Locke denies, and say what kind of evidence or argument each side would need to move the other. (b) A later patriarchalist concedes Locke's objection that nobody can identify Adam's true heir, but replies: "It does not matter. Whoever providence has placed on a settled throne holds the fatherly office." Does this save patriarchalism, or turn it into a different theory? Any verdict. 150 words or fewer for (b).

**P3 (🔴, optional) *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)*** In a state of nature without land shortage, three gatherers each pick 300 kg of plums, which rot within a week. Ada eats what she can and lets the rest rot. Ben trades his surplus before it rots for nuts that keep a year and eats them all. Cal trades his surplus for gold coins, which he buries and never spends. (a) Using §31 and §46, say who has taken more than their share, and why the others have not. (b) A critic says that once Cal's move is allowed, the spoilage limit no longer constrains how much anyone may accumulate. Is Locke's appeal to consent in §50 an adequate answer? Any verdict. 150 words or fewer for (b).

<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical — strict.)*

**Must hit, strict (a):**
- Reader B. The word **"for"** introduces the reason no one ought to harm another: men are all the workmanship of one maker. The continuation (God's servants, his property, made to last during his pleasure) confirms that the ground is God's ownership.
- Reason's job is **epistemic**: it is how the law is known (it teaches the law to anyone who consults it), not why it binds. The law can be known without revelation and still be grounded in God.

**Must hit, strict (b):** property in one's own person is a right held *against other humans* (no body has any right to it but himself). It is not dominion against God, so it gives no power to destroy oneself or sell oneself into slavery (§23).

**Wrong turns:** taking "reason teaches" to show the ground is secular, which confuses how a law is known with what makes it binding. Treating the two property claims as a contradiction Locke failed to notice.

**Model answer:** (a) B. "For" gives the ground of the prohibition: we are God's workmanship, hence his property, and so not made for one another's use. Reason tells us the law; it does not make the law bind. (b) God owns us against all comers, including ourselves; each of us owns himself against every other human. So I may exclude you from my body and labour, but I cannot destroy myself or sell myself into slavery, since I have no such power to give.

---

**P2** *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)*

**Must hit, strict (a):**
- The crux: **begetting (fatherhood) confers a natural dominion that is political in kind and passes to heirs.** Filmer affirms this; Locke denies it. For Locke, parental power is a different kind of power from political power (§2). It is shared with the mother (§52), exists for the child's good, and ends when the child reaches the age of reason.
- What would move either side: **scriptural exegesis** (Genesis 1:28 as a grant to Adam alone or to mankind; the fifth commandment naming both parents) and **conceptual argument** about whether care of the young and the right to make laws with penalties of death are one power or two.

**Must hit, any verdict (b):**
- State the reply precisely: authority now attaches to *possession of the throne under providence*, not to descent from Adam.
- Test it: does fatherhood still do any work? If any settled ruler counts, the title comes from successful possession, which is closer to a providential or de facto theory than to patriarchalism. Ask whether the reply also legitimates a successful usurper, and whether a patriarchalist can accept that.
- Either verdict, with the step that decides it.

**Wrong turns:** naming "God is the source of authority" as the crux, which both sides accept. Treating (b) as settled by (a).

**Model answer (b), one of several:** The reply keeps the vocabulary of fatherhood but changes the ground. Filmer's title ran through blood: kings rule as Adam's heirs. The new title runs through possession: whoever holds a settled throne rules. That brings two costs. It makes fatherhood idle, since a childless usurper qualifies once settled. And it concedes to Locke that no natural relation picks out the ruler. So it rescues divine right, but not patriarchalism. A defender could answer that Filmer himself seems to have allowed that present kings are only taken to be the heirs of the first fathers, so the providential element was always there. Even so, the argument from fatherhood no longer bears any weight.

---

**P3** *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)*

**Must hit, strict (a):**
- **Ada** has taken more than her share. Under §31 she may keep only what she can use before it spoils; the rotting plums were beyond that and belonged to others.
- **Ben** is innocent. §46: trading perishable plums for durable nuts wastes nothing of the common stock.
- **Cal** is innocent too. §46: exchanging for a durable thing (metal, shells, a pebble) and keeping it for life injures no one, since the injury lies in things perishing uselessly, not in the size of the holding.

**Must hit, any verdict (b):**
- State the charge: durable money removes the only stated upper limit on accumulation.
- State Locke's answer: consent to money is consent to its consequences, including unequal possession (§50). Then test it. Is tacit agreement to use gold really agreement to *unlimited* inequality? Did those left with less consent, and does anything of "enough and as good" survive once land is scarce?
- A verdict, with the step that decides it.

**Wrong turns:** saying Cal wronged others because he holds "too much". Locke explicitly locates the wrong in spoilage, not size. Assuming Locke overlooked the problem, when §50 is his deliberate answer to it.

**Model answer (b), one of several:** Locke's answer is stronger than it looks. The spoilage limit was never a cap on wealth. It forbade wasting what God gave for use, and gold wastes nothing. What needs consent is the *means* of unlimited holding, and money exists only because people treat metal as valuable. But the answer strains at one point. Agreeing that gold may stand for goods is not obviously agreeing to whatever distribution follows, and people born after the agreement, into a world with no land left, agreed to nothing. So §50 settles the spoilage worry but reopens the "enough and as good" worry. A reader who takes that clause as a real proviso will find the answer incomplete; a reader who follows Waldron in treating it as no proviso will not.

## Flashback

**From Lesson 3.3 (Hobbes I: matter, motion and the state of nature):** *(Exegetical.)* Close reading and application. In *Leviathan* ch. 13, having said that in the war of all against all nothing is unjust, Hobbes adds that justice and injustice "are Qualities, that relate to men in Society, not in Solitude," and that in this condition there is

> no Propriety, no Dominion, no Mine and Thine distinct; but onely that to be every mans that he can get; and for so long, as he can keep it

(a) Which reading do the words support? **(i)** In the state of nature no one can hold or use anything at all. **(ii)** People do get and keep things, but holding them gives no title anyone else is bound to respect. Name the deciding words. (b) With no common power over them, a settler clears and sows a field and a stranger carries off the harvest. Using this passage and the right of nature, say whether the stranger acts unjustly, whether he violates any right of the settler, and what would have to exist for the harvest to be the settler's in Hobbes's sense. Three sentences or fewer per part.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):** reading (ii). "That to be every mans that he can get" concedes that things are got and held; "for so long, as he can keep it" makes the holding last only as long as the force behind it. What is denied is "Propriety" and "Mine and Thine *distinct*": a line between yours and mine that binds others, not possession itself.
**Must hit, strict (b):**
- No injustice: where there is no common power there is no law, and where no law, no injustice (ch. 13).
- No right violated: the right of nature is a *liberty* to use one's own power for one's preservation, which in this condition extends to all things, the harvest included. The stranger has the same right to it as the settler, and a liberty imposes no duty on anyone else.
- For the harvest to be the settler's, there must be a commonwealth: Hobbes says in ch. 15 that where there is no coercive power there is no propriety, and that injustice is breach of a valid covenant, which exists only once a civil power can compel performance.

**Wrong turns:** (a) choosing (i) because "no Propriety" is read as "no possessions"; the next clause says what people can get and keep. (b) Saying the settler's labour gives him a claim; that is Locke's move in this lesson, and Hobbes's passage leaves no room for it. Saying the stranger breaks the first law of nature and so acts unjustly: the laws of nature bind outward action only where there is security, and violating them is not injustice in ch. 13's sense.

**Model answer:** (a) Reading (ii). "That to be every mans that he can get" grants that people hold things; "for so long, as he can keep it" says the hold lasts only while force sustains it. What is missing is propriety, a "Mine and Thine distinct" that others must respect. (b) The stranger does nothing unjust, since without a common power there is no law and so no injustice. Nor does he violate a right: each has a right of nature to all things, a liberty that binds no one else, so the harvest was as much the stranger's to take as the settler's to keep. It becomes the settler's only when a commonwealth exists whose coercive power makes a distinct mine and thine, which for Hobbes is where property begins.

</details>
