# History of Political Thought · Lesson 4.4: Rousseau II: the *Social Contract*

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 4: Consent, property and the general will · Builds on: [4.3 Rousseau I: the *Discourse on Inequality*](04-03-rousseau-discourse-on-inequality.md) · Unlocks: [5.1 Montesquieu: the spirit of the laws](05-01-montesquieu-the-spirit-of-the-laws.md)

## Why this matters

The *Discourse on Inequality* ([4.3](04-03-rousseau-discourse-on-inequality.md)) ended with society as a trap: the rich talked the poor into a contract that locked in their losses. The *Social Contract* (1762) opens with the most famous line in political theory, "Man is born free; and everywhere he is in chains" (I.1, Cole), and then changes the question. Rousseau does not ask how the chains arose. He asks what could make them legitimate. His answer, the *general will*, became Article 6 of the 1789 Declaration of the Rights of Man, which calls law the expression of the general will. It has been claimed by radical democrats ever since, and blamed by critics for the Terror and worse. Both camps are reading the same short book.

## The idea

Hobbes ([3.4](03-04-hobbes-the-sovereign-and-the-church.md)) bought safety with obedience, and Locke ([4.2](04-02-locke-limited-government-and-toleration.md)) bought it with a limited trust. Rousseau thinks both deals leave you under someone else's will, and for him that is the whole disease. The *Discourse* traced corruption to *dependence*: once people need each other's labor and esteem, each lives at the mercy of particular others. So the question is not "how much freedom must I trade for protection?" He demands a trade that costs no freedom at all.

His solution sounds like the opposite. **Give everything to everyone.** If each person hands over himself and all his rights to the whole community, three things follow. The terms are the same for all, so no one gains by making them heavy. No one keeps a private reserve he can judge for himself. And since you give yourself to all and to no one in particular, you depend on no particular person. You obey only laws that you, as a member of the sovereign people, make, and that bind everyone alike.

Every citizen therefore has two wills. As a private person you want what is good for you: your *particular will*. As a citizen you can ask what is good for all of us: your share in the *general will*. Add up everyone's private wants and you get the *will of all*, which can be a coalition of interests against the rest. The general will is different in kind: it aims only at the common interest, and it speaks only through laws that are general. A law must come from all and apply to all (II.4).

That machinery comes with demanding conditions. The republic must be small, since the more citizens there are, the less each one's vote counts, and Rousseau concludes that liberty shrinks as the state grows (III.1). Sovereignty cannot be represented. A people has to found its institutions before it has the civic spirit those institutions are meant to produce, so it needs a quasi-divine lawgiver (II.7). And it needs a civil religion to hold it together (IV.8).

## Source

Rousseau, *The Social Contract* I.6 (Cole's translation, public domain). Rousseau states the problem the whole book exists to solve:

> "The problem is to find a form of association which will defend and protect with the whole common force the person and goods of each associate, and in which each, while uniting himself with all, may still obey himself alone, and remain as free as before."

Notice the two clauses. The first (protection by the common force) is what Hobbes and Locke already delivered. The second is new and far harder: *obey himself alone*, *as free as before*. A solution that gave security at any cost to freedom would fail Rousseau's own test.

## The argument

**From the problem to civil freedom (I.6–I.8, II.1, II.4, III.15).**

1. **Isolated forces no longer suffice.** Obstacles to survival in the state of nature have outgrown what any individual can resist, and humans cannot create new force, only combine existing force. *In words:* people have to pool their strength or perish.
2. **But each person's force and liberty are his chief means of self-preservation**, so pledging them seems to betray his own interest. *In words:* the only thing you can contribute is exactly what you cannot afford to lose.
3. **The clauses reduce to one: total alienation of each associate, with all his rights, to the whole community.** Equal conditions mean no one has an interest in burdening others. No reserve means no private judge, whereas if individuals kept rights, each would judge his own case and the state of nature would continue. And giving himself to all, each gives himself to nobody, gaining over every other associate the same right he yields. *In words:* hand everything to everyone and nobody ends up holding you.
4. **The compact creates a collective moral body.** Its members are *citizens* as sharers in sovereignty and *subjects* as bound by its laws. *In words:* you are at once one of the rulers and one of the ruled.
5. **The general will must be general in source and object.** It must come from all and apply to all, and it loses its rectitude when directed at a particular person or fact (II.4). *In words:* the sovereign can only legislate rules; it cannot name names.
6. **Sovereignty is inalienable and cannot be represented.** Power may be transmitted, but not will (II.1); laws the people has not ratified in person are not laws, and deputies are only stewards (III.15). *In words:* someone can carry out your decision, but no one can make it for you.
7. **So the citizen trades natural liberty for civil and moral liberty.** He loses a liberty bounded only by his strength, and gains one limited by the general will, plus the moral liberty of obeying a law he prescribes to himself (I.8). *In words:* being ruled by a law you make is not a loss of freedom but a higher kind of it.

Between steps 4 and 7 sits I.7, where a free rider who refuses to obey is compelled by the whole body, which Rousseau says means he "will be forced to be free." Reconstructing that step is P2.

## Map of positions

Three contracts, compared on the question Rousseau thinks his predecessors got wrong.

| | **Hobbes** ([3.4](03-04-hobbes-the-sovereign-and-the-church.md)) | **Locke** ([4.2](04-02-locke-limited-government-and-toleration.md)) | **Rousseau** |
|---|---|---|---|
| What each gives up | The right to govern himself, by authorizing a sovereign | The private power to enforce the law of nature | Himself and all his rights |
| To whom | A man or assembly who bears their person | The community, which entrusts a legislature | The whole community, of which each is a part |
| What is kept | The right to resist those who would kill him (ch. 21) | Natural rights to life, liberty and estate; the right to resist a government that breaks its trust | Nothing held *against* the community; civil liberty and property come back as rights under law |
| Can the sovereign be represented? | It *is* a representative | Yes: an elected legislature | No: the people legislates in person; government is only its agent (III.1) |

The two outer columns look alike: both give up everything. The crux lies in the last row. Hobbes thinks a will can be carried by a representative, so total alienation creates a master. Rousseau denies it, so for him total alienation creates a people with no master. Rousseau does not keep Locke's reserved rights, and even he concedes a strain here: each gives up only what the community needs to control, but the sovereign alone judges what that is (II.4).

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean: law or decree?).** A small republic's assembly has two proposals before it. (a) In any year the harvest falls below a fixed level, every household delivers a tenth of its grain to the public store. (b) The mill owned by one named family is taken for public use.

Run step 5. Proposal (a) comes from all and applies to all. Each voter will bear its terms without knowing whether he will be a giver or a receiver in a lean year, so the question he faces is the right one: is this rule good for us? The general will can speak here. Proposal (b) has a particular object, a named family. According to II.4, the sovereign cannot pronounce on it: the family is one party and the public the other, and no impartial rule governs the case. If the assembly votes on it anyway, it is acting as a magistrate, not as sovereign (Rousseau's example is Athens issuing decrees on individuals). The mill question belongs to the government, applying some general law of public necessity that the sovereign has already made. A *vote* is not what makes something an act of the general will. Generality of object is.

**Example 2 (hard: the outvoted citizen).** In a vote on a genuinely general law, you lose. Are you now subject to a will not your own? Rousseau meets this head-on in IV.2. The assembly was not asked what each person *prefers* but whether the proposal conforms to the general will. Voting states an opinion on that question, and when the majority goes against you, it shows only that you were mistaken about what the general will was. Had your opinion prevailed, you would have got something other than what you, as a citizen, willed.

This is where the theory strains. The argument works only if the question put to voters really was about the common interest, and Rousseau adds that it presupposes that the qualities of the general will still reside in the majority. The dissenter has no procedure for checking that presupposition, because the vote is his only evidence. Readers divide over what to make of this. On an *epistemic* reading, the general will is a fact about the common good that majorities of public-minded voters are likely to track. Grofman and Feld (1988) read it through Condorcet's jury theorem ([`social-choice`](../../social-choice/syllabus.md) 5.3). On a *totalitarian* reading, most associated with J. L. Talmon's *The Origins of Totalitarian Democracy* (1952), a doctrine that tells dissenters they are wrong about their own will licenses whoever claims to speak for the true will. Republican readers such as Joshua Cohen (*Rousseau: A Free Community of Equals*, 2010) answer that the institutional conditions (general laws, equality, no dominant factions) are exactly what stop that capture. The text supports asking each of these questions. It does not settle them.

## Watch out

- **Anachronism: "the general will" is not public opinion or the majority.** A poll, a landslide or "the people have spoken" gives you at best the will of all. For Rousseau, a majority aimed at a named target, or produced by one dominant faction, carries no general will at all (II.3–II.4).
- **You might think Rousseau champions democratic *government*.** He requires the people to be sovereign and to legislate. But *government*, the body that executes laws, is a separate question. Of a true democracy he says that only a people of gods would be governed that way (III.4), and he calls rule by the wisest the best and most natural arrangement when it serves the many (III.5).
- **Anachronism: "civil religion" here is not Robert Bellah's American civil religion (1967).** Bellah described a shared public piety. Rousseau's is a creed fixed by the sovereign, with a few positive dogmas and one negative one, intolerance. Anyone who will not believe it may be banished as anti-social, and anyone who teaches that there is no salvation outside the Church should be driven from the state (IV.8). Set this next to Locke's exclusions ([4.2](04-02-locke-limited-government-and-toleration.md)) before crowning either as the tolerant one.
- **You might think "forced to be free" is a slip of the pen.** Rousseau places it at the hinge of the compact and calls it the key to the whole political machine (I.7). Whether it is a deep point about autonomy or a dangerous one, it is deliberate.

## One-liner

> Give yourself wholly to all and you are subject to no one: the general will is each citizen's own will for the common good, speaking only in laws that bind everyone alike, and nobody can will it on your behalf.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢) *(Exegetical.)*** In II.3, after distinguishing the will of all from the general will, Rousseau writes (Cole): "take away from these same wills the pluses and minuses that cancel one another, and the general will remains as the sum of the differences." (a) Two readings: **A**, the general will belongs to the collective body as something over and above what any citizen wills; **B**, it is a component already present in the citizens' own wills, left over once their purely private pulls cancel. Which does this sentence support? Name the two words or short phrases that decide it. (b) The next sentences add that the result is reliable only if citizens are well informed and form no partial associations, and that where one faction dominates, what prevails is a merely particular opinion. What does that imply about whether an actual vote reveals the general will? 100 words or fewer in total.

**P2 (🟡) *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)*** Here is I.7 in paraphrase. The sovereign, composed entirely of the individuals, can have no interest contrary to theirs. But each person, as a man, can have a particular will contrary to the general will he has as a citizen: he may treat his contribution as a free gift and want the rights of a citizen without the duties of a subject. If that persisted, the body politic would be ruined. So the compact silently includes an undertaking that whoever refuses to obey the general will is compelled to by the whole body. Rousseau says this means he is forced to be free, because being bound to the whole is what protects each citizen from dependence on any particular person. (a) Reconstruct the argument in numbered premises (P1, P2, … ∴ C), in Rousseau's terms. Include at least one premise he needs but I.7 does not state, taken from I.6, I.8 or the *Discourse*'s account of dependence. (b) Flag the weakest premise and say whether it can be repaired without giving up the conclusion. Any verdict; 100 words or fewer for (b).

**P3 (🔴, optional) *(Exegetical.)*** An invented speech by a party leader after a national election:

> "Fifty-two percent of the nation chose our movement. That is the general will, and there is no appeal from it. Those who voted against us were not outvoted; they were simply wrong about what the nation wants, and a free people has every right to make them fall in line. Parliament exists to carry out that will. The courts and the press, which speak only for narrow interests, must be brought under it, starting with the judges who blocked our programme last year."

Whose ideas is it borrowing, with chapter references? Name at least three conditions Rousseau attaches to those ideas that the speech has dropped or contradicts. 150 words or fewer.

<details>
<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical — strict.)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** Reading **B**. The deciding words are "these same wills" (the general will is extracted from the citizens' own wills, not added from outside them) and "remains" (it was already present and is revealed when the canceling private pulls are removed). Nothing in the sentence posits a will located anywhere other than in the citizens.

**Must hit, strict (b):** a vote yields the general will only conditionally. Under the stated conditions, the many small private differences cancel and the common element shows through. Where partial associations form, there are fewer and larger differences, and one dominant faction reduces the result to a single particular will. So a majority count is evidence of the general will, not its definition, and can simply fail to contain it (compare IV.2's presupposition).

**Wrong turns:** choosing A because "general" sounds collective, or because I.6 speaks of a collective body with a will of its own. That is true of I.6, but the question is what *this* sentence supports. Reading "the sum of the differences" as the vote tally, which is the will of all, the very thing being contrasted.

**Model answer:** (a) B: "these same wills" places the general will inside the citizens' wills, and "remains" says it is what is left when opposed private interests cancel, not something added. (b) Only under conditions: informed citizens and no dominant factions. Otherwise the tally expresses a particular will, so an actual vote is fallible evidence of the general will, never its definition.

---

**P2** *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** premises close to these, in any clear order:
- P1. The sovereign is composed of the individuals, so it has no interest contrary to theirs.
- P2. Each individual has a particular will as a man and a general will as a citizen, and the two can conflict.
- P3. Someone acting on the conflicting particular will takes the benefits of association without its burdens, and if this persists the body politic is destroyed.
- P4. So the compact, if it is not to be an empty formula, must include the undertaking that refusers are compelled by the whole body.
- P5 (unstated, needed). The general will a person has as a citizen is genuinely *his own* will (I.6: each "obey himself alone"; I.8: obedience to a law one prescribes to oneself is liberty).
- P6 (unstated, needed). The chief threat to freedom is dependence on particular persons (the *Discourse*), and being bound equally to the whole is what removes it.
- ∴ C. Compelling someone to obey the general will compels him to obey his own will and secures him from personal dependence: he is forced to be free.

At least one of P5 or P6 must appear, with its source. Credit for merging P1–P3.

**Must hit, any verdict (b):** name one premise as weakest, with the reason. P5 is the usual candidate: when the dissenter himself denies that the law expresses his will, what makes it his? P6 is also defensible: why is dependence on the collective not itself a form of unfreedom? Then say whether the premise can be repaired, for example by reading P5 as "the will he would have as a public-minded citizen", and what the repair costs, such as making freedom depend on a counterfactual self.

**Wrong turns:** writing "force is justified because the majority rules". Majority rule is not in I.7, and IV.2 is a different argument. Leaving out every unstated premise, so that the move from "compelled" to "free" is a bare non sequitur. In (b), grading Rousseau right or wrong without naming a premise.

**Model answer (b), one of several:** P5 is the weakest. I.7 needs the citizen's general will to be *his* will even when he rejects it, and nothing in I.6–I.8 shows how a will can be mine while I disown it. One repair reads it as the will he has in his capacity as a party to the compact: he willed the association, and the association's laws follow from its terms. That keeps the conclusion, but the freedom it secures is freedom as fidelity to one's earlier commitment, not to one's present choice. Whether that still counts as being "as free as before" is exactly what is disputed.

---

**P3** *(Exegetical — strict.)*

**Must hit, strict:** the borrowing is Rousseau: the general will and the claim that the outvoted were mistaken (IV.2), and compelling dissenters as freedom (I.7). At least three dropped conditions:
- **The question put to voters.** IV.2's argument holds only when voters are asked whether a law conforms to the common interest, not which party they prefer. A party vote is a will of all at best.
- **Factions.** A movement voting as a bloc is a partial association. Where one prevails, there is no general will, only a particular one (II.3).
- **Generality of object.** The general will cannot pronounce on particular persons (II.4). Targeting named judges is a decree, not a law.
- **Representation.** Sovereignty cannot be represented, so neither a parliament nor a party can embody it (III.15).
- **IV.2's own presupposition** that the general will's qualities still reside in the majority, asserted here without any check.

**Wrong turns:** attributing the speech to Hobbes because it wants concentrated power. Hobbes's sovereign is a representative, which the speech's "that is the general will" framing rules out. Rebutting the speech politically rather than showing what it dropped from Rousseau.

**Model answer:** It borrows Rousseau: the outvoted as mistaken about the general will (IV.2), and dissenters made to comply as a form of freedom (I.7). It drops the conditions that made those claims coherent. Voters must be asked what serves the common interest, not which party they back. A bloc vote is a partial association, and one that dominates yields only a particular will (II.3). The general will cannot target named persons, so moving against the judges who blocked the programme is a decree, not law (II.4). Sovereignty cannot be represented, so neither parliament nor a movement embodies it (III.15). And IV.2 presupposes that the general will still resides in the majority, which the speech simply asserts.

## Flashback

**From Lesson 4.2 (Locke II: limited government, revolution and toleration):** *(Exegetical.)* *Second Treatise* §119–122 separate tacit from express consent. Of the man who has given express consent, §121 says he

> "is perpetually and indispensably obliged to be, and remain unalterably a subject to it, and can never be again in the liberty of the state of nature"

unless the government he was under is dissolved, or a public act cuts him off. (a) For each invented case, say whether the person is bound to obey the laws, and whether he is a member of the commonwealth. One line each. (i) A Dutch merchant lodges in London for a season to trade. (ii) An Englishman inherits his father's estate, never swears anything, and after ten years sells it and sails off to found a settlement on unclaimed land. (iii) A man who swore allegiance on taking office later sells everything and asks to become a subject of France. (b) Case (iii) again, but Parliament has been dissolved by the king's breach of trust. Does §121's exception free him? Say why the answer turns on §211. Three sentences or fewer.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):**
- (i) **Bound, not a member.** Lodging, even travelling the highway, is tacit consent to obey during the enjoyment (§119); living under a government's protection gives only "local protection and homage," not membership (§122).
- (ii) **Bound while he holds the land; not a member; free once he sells.** Land annexed to a commonwealth is taken on condition of submitting to its government (§120), but a purely tacit obligation "begins and ends with the enjoyment," so after the sale he may join another commonwealth or begin a new one (§121). Inheritance alone does not make him a member.
- (iii) **Bound and a member, and not free to leave.** Express consent binds perpetually; selling his property changes nothing, since his membership never rested on it (§121).

**Must hit, strict (b):** §211 distinguishes the dissolution of the **government** from the dissolution of the **society**, and says the society is almost only dissolved by foreign conquest; when that happens, each person returns to the liberty to seek safety in some other society. A breach of trust dissolves only the government: the society survives and may set up a new legislative (§222). His express consent was to be a member of the commonwealth, so on that reading he stays a member and owes allegiance to the new legislative. An answer that reads §121's "government" loosely, as freeing him, passes only if it deals with the §211 distinction.

**Wrong turns:** making the merchant a member because he obeys; treating the heir as a member because his father consented, which Locke denies (a father cannot bind his children); treating (iii) as free to leave because he no longer owns land, which applies the tacit rule to an express consenter.

**Model answer:** (a) (i) Bound to obey while he lodges, not a member. (ii) Bound while he holds the estate, since land is taken on condition of submission, but his obligation ends with the sale, and he may found a new commonwealth. (iii) Bound and a member for life: express consent is perpetual, whatever he sells. (b) Probably not. §211 separates dissolving a government from dissolving a society, and a breach of trust only does the first. The society he expressly joined survives to set up a new legislative, so he is still its member. What frees an express consenter is the society's end, which Locke ties to conquest.

</details>
