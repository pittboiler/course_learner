# History of Political Thought · Lesson 6.6: Marx II: the state, revolution and the society after

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 6: The nineteenth century: freedom, capital and democracy · Builds on: [6.5 Marx I: from Hegel to historical materialism](06-05-marx-from-hegel-to-historical-materialism.md) · Unlocks: [`social-theory`](../../social-theory/syllabus.md) Module 2, [`political-philosophy`](../../political-philosophy/syllabus.md)

## Why this matters

Every thinker in this course asked what the state is *for*: justice, peace, the common good, security, rights, freedom realized. Marx gives the most deflationary answer in the tradition. The state is not for the common good at all; it is how a ruling class manages its own affairs, and it will disappear when classes do. He never wrote the book on the state he planned, so the theory has to be pieced together from a pamphlet, a piece of contemporary history, a defense of an insurrection and some angry marginal notes on a party program. Those scattered texts were then argued over by Lenin, Kautsky, anarchists, social democrats and cold-war critics, and read against the history of states that called themselves Marxist. You cannot follow twentieth-century politics without knowing what they actually say.

## The idea

Marx's view of the state has three layers, and most misreadings come from keeping only one.

**The blunt formula (1848).** In the *Manifesto*, the modern representative state serves the class that owns the means of production. Laws, police and parliaments look like neutral servants of everyone, but they protect a particular property order. [6.5](06-05-marx-from-hegel-to-historical-materialism.md) showed why: the state belongs to the superstructure raised on relations of production.

**The complication (1852).** In *The Eighteenth Brumaire of Louis Bonaparte*, Marx explains a coup that the formula does not obviously fit. In December 1851 an adventurer seized power, dissolved the elected assembly and ruled over the heads of the bourgeoisie itself. Marx's answer is *Bonapartism*. When no class can rule in its own name, the state apparatus can rise above all of them and look independent. It is still tied to class interests, but only at a distance. Later Marxists called this the state's *relative autonomy*.

**What replaces it (1871–75).** The Paris Commune showed Marx, he thought, the political form of workers' rule. Revolution cannot simply capture the existing state; it must replace its machinery. A transitional *dictatorship of the proletariat* follows, then a first phase of communist society, then a higher phase in which classes, and with them political power in the strict sense, are gone.

Think of the state as a tool. The *Manifesto* says whose hand holds it. The *Brumaire* says the tool can seem to wield itself when the hands are paralyzed. The Commune writings say the new class needs a different tool, one designed to put itself out of work.

## Source

Marx, *The Eighteenth Brumaire of Louis Bonaparte* (1852), ch. I, opening (Daniel De Leon's translation, 1898, public domain):

> "Man makes his own history, but he does not make it out of the whole cloth; he does not make it out of conditions chosen by himself, but out of such as he finds close at hand."

Later translations render the first clause as men making their own history, but not just as they please. The next sentence adds that the tradition of the dead generations weighs on the living, which is why revolutionaries borrow old costumes. The sentence has two halves, and the whole book depends on keeping both: people really do act, and they act under constraints they did not choose. P1 asks you to read it closely.

## The argument

**Marx's theory of the state and its end (*Manifesto* II; *Brumaire* VII; *Civil War in France* III; *Critique of the Gotha Programme* I and IV).**

1. **Political power is the organized power of one class for oppressing another** (*Manifesto* II, paraphrased). *In words:* the state exists because society is divided into classes with opposed interests.
2. **In the modern representative state, "The executive of the modern State is but a committee for managing the common affairs of the whole bourgeoisie"** (*Manifesto* I, Moore). *In words:* government handles the business capitalists share as a class, including refereeing quarrels among individual capitalists.
3. **Where no class can rule in its own name, the executive can make itself look fully independent** (*Brumaire* VII). The bourgeoisie gives up its political power to keep its social power, and the smallholding peasants, too scattered to organize, are represented by a master from above. *In words:* the state serves class interests at arm's length, not on instruction.
4. **The centralized machinery of standing army, police, bureaucracy and judiciary was built for class rule.** So the working class cannot simply take over that ready-made machine and use it for its own purposes (*Civil War in France*, 1871, paraphrased). *In words:* the tool has class rule designed into it.
5. **The Commune shows the replacement**: the standing army replaced by the armed people; officials and judges elected, answerable and recallable at short notice; public service at workmen's wages; one working body that legislates and executes. *In words:* power is kept in reach of those it governs.
6. **Between capitalist and communist society lies a political transition in which the state can only be the revolutionary dictatorship of the proletariat** (*Gotha* IV; already in the letter to Weydemeyer, 5 March 1852). *In words:* the majority class rules openly, as a class, to dismantle the old order.
7. **The first phase of communist society still bears the marks of the old one.** Each receives according to labor contributed, an equal standard that is unequal in effect, since workers differ in ability and in need (*Gotha* I). *In words:* the principle is fairer, but it is still a rule of exchange.
8. **In a higher phase, once labor is life's prime want and productive forces have grown, society can inscribe on its banners: "From each according to his ability, to each according to his needs!"** (*Gotha* I). *In words:* distribution by need becomes possible only after scarcity and the division of labor lose their grip.
9. **∴ With classes abolished, public power loses its political character** (*Manifesto* II). Engels later said the state is not abolished but dies out (*Anti-Dühring*, 1878). *In words:* nothing remains for a class-oppression machine to do, so what is left is administration.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean: why Bonaparte?).** On 2 December 1851 Louis Bonaparte, the elected president, dissolved the National Assembly. A year later he made himself emperor. The blunt formula would predict a state run on bourgeois instructions. Yet he dispersed the bourgeoisie's own parliament. Run steps 3 and 2 in order.

First, the bourgeoisie. Its parliamentary factions (Legitimists and Orleanists, landed and financial wealth) could not agree and feared the workers after June 1848. Its parliament produced disorder instead of order. It accepted losing *political* rule so that its *social* rule, property and the order that protects it, would survive. Bonaparte, Marx says, sees himself as the guardian of bourgeois order even though he broke the bourgeoisie's political power.

Second, the base. Bonaparte won millions of peasant votes. The peasants lived in the same conditions without real ties to one another, so they formed a class in one sense and not in another. That is the passage P2 asks you to reconstruct. The executive therefore rested on a class that could not govern, served a class that would not, and drew on the army, the bureaucracy and his street organization, the Society of December 10. The state looks free-floating. On Marx's analysis it is balanced on class forces that cancel out.

**Example 2 (hard: what is the dictatorship of the proletariat?).** Marx gives the phrase no institutional description, and his heirs split exactly there.

- **A word-history reading.** In 1850 "dictatorship" still carried its Roman meaning: temporary emergency rule, not necessarily by one person or a party. Hal Draper's multi-volume *Karl Marx's Theory of Revolution* (vol. 3, 1986) argues that Marx meant the rule of the working-class majority, which is compatible with broad suffrage. Engels in 1891 pointed to the Commune, with its elections and recall, as what the dictatorship of the proletariat looks like.
- **A democratic socialist reading.** Karl Kautsky (*The Dictatorship of the Proletariat*, 1918) held that it describes the class character of a regime, which a working-class majority could reach through parliament and universal suffrage.
- **A vanguard reading.** Lenin, in *The State and Revolution* (1917) and against Kautsky (1918), read step 4 as a demand to smash the old state. He held that the dictatorship is rule unrestricted by law and exercised against the bourgeoisie.
- **The anarchist objection.** Bakunin, in *Statism and Anarchy*, pressed the premise all three share: a class cannot rule as a class. Someone governs in its name, and former workers who become rulers soon represent only themselves. Marx's marginal notes on the book (c. 1874–75) reply, roughly, that a workers' government would resemble the executive of a trade union, and that it starts from communal self-government. Critics such as Leszek Kołakowski (*Main Currents of Marxism*, 1976–78) later argued over whether Soviet one-party rule betrayed Marx or developed a gap he left open.

The text supports asking which reading is right. It does not settle the question, because steps 5 and 6 are never tied together in Marx's own words. Whether later history refutes the theory, or shows only that its conditions were never met, belongs to [`political-philosophy`](../../political-philosophy/syllabus.md) and [`social-theory`](../../social-theory/syllabus.md) 2.3.

## Watch out

- **Anachronism: "dictatorship" in 1850 does not mean what it meant after 1930.** Do not read a one-party police state back into the word. Do not assume the opposite either, that Marx meant parliamentary majority rule. The text leaves the institutional form open, which is exactly what Kautsky and Lenin fought over.
- **You might think the "committee" sentence is the whole theory.** Read alone, it gives an *instrumentalist* view in which capitalists direct the state. The *Brumaire* gives a *structural* view: the state serves class interests even against capitalists' wishes. The 1969–70 exchange between Ralph Miliband and Nicos Poulantzas is essentially an argument over which layer to weight.
- **Attribution traps.** "Withering away" is Engels's image, not a phrase from Marx's own texts. The need-based slogan was not coined by Marx: versions circulated among French socialists before him (it is often traced to Louis Blanc). What is Marx's own is its place in a phased sequence, and the claim that it presupposes abundance.
- **You might think the Gotha critique is a blueprint.** Marx attacks a program. He gives no institutions for the higher phase and elsewhere declined to write recipes for the kitchens of the future. The famous slogan describes a condition, not a policy.

## One-liner

> For Marx the state is class rule, sometimes wearing a mask of independence when no class can rule openly. The workers must replace it rather than seize it, rule through a transitional dictatorship whose form he left open, and so make political power itself unnecessary.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢) *(Exegetical.)*** Reread the *Brumaire* sentence in the Source section. (a) Two readings: **A**, history is made by impersonal conditions, and human choice is only their vehicle; **B**, humans are real agents, but they act within inherited conditions they did not choose. Which does the sentence support? Name the words that decide it. (b) The next sentences say that revolutionaries summon the spirits of the past and borrow their names and costumes. Does that detail tell for or against your reading, and why? 100 words or fewer in total.

**P2 (🟡) *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)*** In *Brumaire* VII, Marx describes the smallholding peasants. They live in identical conditions but in isolation, each family nearly self-sufficient, so that the nation is made up by simple addition, like potatoes in a sack. They form a class insofar as their conditions set their interests against other classes, and not a class insofar as they have no unity, national connections or political organization. Then (De Leon):

> "They can not represent one another, they must themselves be represented. Their representative must at the same time appear as their master, as an authority over them, as an unlimited governmental power"

(a) Reconstruct, in numbered premises (P1, P2, … ∴ C), Marx's argument that the Bonapartist state can look independent of all classes while still resting on class forces. Include the bourgeoisie's position as well as the peasants'. (b) Flag the weakest premise, and say whether a critic can use it to show that Bonapartism makes the theory unfalsifiable. Any verdict; 150 words or fewer for (b).

**P3 (🔴, optional) *(Exegetical.)*** Marx and Bakunin both wanted a society without a state. Marx held that a transitional dictatorship of the proletariat is needed to get there; Bakunin held that any such state would make former workers into a new ruling minority. Name the single premise one of them must deny, and say what kind of evidence or argument would move a defender of each side. Do not use Tocqueville. 150 words or fewer.

<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical — strict.)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** Reading **B**. The deciding words: "Man makes his own history", where the grammatical subject and the verb of agency belong to humans, not to conditions; and "not ... chosen by himself" together with "such as he finds close at hand", which limit the agency without cancelling it. The sentence is a *but* sentence: agency first, constraint second, and neither half eliminates the other.

**Must hit, strict (b):** It supports B. Borrowed costumes are something people *do*, as a choice, and the choice is shaped by inherited tradition. Agents act, and act with the materials the past leaves them. A pure A reading would have no use for the actors' self-understanding.

**Wrong turns:** choosing A because Marx is "a materialist", which reads 6.5's theory into a sentence that says something more specific; treating "close at hand" as meaning conditions are chosen for convenience.

**Model answer:** (a) B. "Man makes his own history" gives agency to human beings; "not ... chosen by himself" and "such as he finds close at hand" limit it without abolishing it. (b) For B: dressing up as Romans is a choice, but one made with inherited materials. That is agency under constraint.

---

**P2** *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** premises close to these:
- P1. Classes that can organize politically rule, or contest rule, in their own name through parliament or assembly.
- P2. The bourgeoisie, divided into factions and frightened by the workers, could no longer rule in its own name without endangering its social power.
- P3. It therefore gave up political rule to preserve property and order.
- P4. The smallholding peasants share conditions and interests but, isolated by their mode of production, have no ties or organization, so they cannot represent themselves.
- P5. A class that cannot represent itself must be represented from above, by a master who protects it.
- P6. An executive that rests on a class which cannot rule, and serves one that has stepped aside, faces no class able to direct it.
- ∴ C. The executive looks fully independent, but it rests on the peasants' support and on protecting bourgeois social order: independent in appearance, class-based in fact.

Credit for merging premises; both classes must appear.

**Must hit, any verdict (b):** name one premise. P6 is the usual candidate: why should "no class directs it" still leave a *class* state rather than one with interests of its own, such as the army or officialdom? P2 or P3 is also defensible. Then state the unfalsifiability charge precisely: if a state serving capitalists confirms the theory, and a state defying them confirms it too via Bonapartism, can any state count against it? Say what observation would count against Marx's version, or concede that none is specified.

**Wrong turns:** making Bonaparte simply the tool of the bourgeoisie, which drops the independence the passage exists to explain; treating the peasants as the ruling class; in (b), giving a verdict on Marxism without naming a premise.

**Model answer (b), one of several:** P6 is the weakest. From "no class can direct the state" Marx infers it still serves class ends, but the same facts fit a rival claim: the bureaucracy and army became an interest of their own. The critic can press this into an unfalsifiability charge: loyal states confirm the theory, and defiant ones confirm it as Bonapartism. A defender can reply that the claim is testable at another level. Over time, Bonapartist policy should protect property and peasant smallholding rather than attack them, and a regime that expropriated both would count against it. Whether that test is precise enough is the open question.

---

**P3** *(Exegetical — strict on isolating one premise; any defensible statement passes.)*

**Must hit, strict:**
- The crux: *whether a class as such can hold and exercise state power*, as opposed to power always being exercised by some subset who acquire a separate interest. Marx affirms it (the proletariat raised to the ruling class; the Commune's elections, recall and workmen's wages keep delegates bound to the class). Bakunin denies it.
- It is *not* a dispute about the goal: both want statelessness. Nor is it about whether capitalism is exploitative.
- What would move each: for Marx's side, evidence that recall, short terms and low pay durably prevent officials from forming a separate stratum, or examples of mass self-government at scale. For Bakunin's side, an argument that such mechanisms decay under the pressures of a transition (war, scarcity, counter-revolution), or historical evidence that revolutionary states entrenched their personnel. A defender of either side must say which evidence they would accept.

**Wrong turns:** naming "whether violence is justified" (both accepted revolution); naming the end goal; answering with twentieth-century history as a verdict rather than as the kind of evidence that bears on the crux.

**Model answer:** The premise is that a class can rule *as a class*, so that a proletarian state is the majority governing itself rather than a minority governing in its name. Marx needs it: the dictatorship is transitional only if its holders have no interest apart from the class, and the Commune's recallable, workman-paid delegates are his mechanism. Bakunin denies it: whoever governs acquires a governor's interest. A Marxist might be moved by evidence that such mechanisms reliably erode in revolutionary conditions. An anarchist might be moved by sustained cases where delegates under recall stayed answerable at national scale.

## Flashback

**From Lesson 6.4 (Mill: representative government):** *(Sort the cases: exegetical (a) · evaluative (b).)* Three things a national parliament does in one session. (i) After a debate on the candidates, it elects the new director of the statistics office by a vote of the whole house. (ii) A committee summons the minister over a failed and costly purchase of military equipment, publishes the evidence, and the house passes a censure motion that forces him out. (iii) It instructs a small standing commission of legal experts to draft a railway-safety law; when the bill returns, members move forty floor amendments rewriting its technical clauses. (a) By *Considerations on Representative Government* ch. V, which of these are proper work for a representative assembly and which are not? Give Mill's reason for each. (b) Mill would let parliament only pass, reject or send back the commission's bill. Does that leave the assembly in real control of legislation, or turn it into a ratifying body? Any verdict. Three sentences or fewer per part.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):**
- (i) **Improper.** A numerous body judges candidates by party connection, private jobbing or popularity, not special qualifications, and it cannot be held responsible for a bad choice. Mill wants appointments made by a responsible minister, with the assembly deciding at most who heads the government.
- (ii) **Proper,** and central to ch. V: to watch and control the government, throw the light of publicity on its acts, compel justification, censure, and expel those who abuse their trust.
- (iii) **Split.** Directing the commission to prepare a law is proper, since the assembly supplies the will and may refer any subject for drafting. Rewriting technical clauses on the floor is not: a large assembly is unfit to draft, and on Mill's plan it could only pass, reject or send the bill back for reconsideration.

**Must hit, any verdict (b):**
- State Mill's division: the commission supplies intelligence in the construction of laws, parliament supplies will, and no measure becomes law without parliament's sanction.
- Name the powers parliament keeps: initiative (binding instructions the commissioners must obey or resign), rejection, remission for improvement, and removal of commissioners on an address from both houses.
- Weigh them against what it loses, the power to shape a bill's detail, and decide whether that amounts to control.

**Wrong turns:** calling (ii) improper because the assembly is interfering with administration; censure and dismissal are exactly its office. Treating (iii) as wholly proper or wholly improper. In (b), saying the commission can block legislation; it cannot refuse to draft what both houses demand.

**Model answer:** (a) (i) is improper: large assemblies appoint by party and popularity and answer to no one for the choice, so a responsible minister should appoint. (ii) is the assembly's proper office: publicity, demanding justification, censure and dismissal. (iii) is half right: commissioning the law is proper, but floor-drafting technical clauses is the work Mill says an assembly is unfit for. (b) It keeps real control. Parliament decides what laws are made, can force a draft on any subject, can refuse or return any bill, and can remove commissioners who refuse. It loses only the drafting pen, which Mill thinks it used badly. A critic can reply that whoever frames a bill sets the options, so a house confined to yes, no or try again ratifies more than it controls.

## Closing: the question the course began with

Thrasymachus told Socrates in [1.1](01-01-platos-republic-justice-in-city-and-soul.md) that justice is the advantage of the stronger, and that rulers make laws in their own interest. Plato spent the *Republic* answering him. Marx is, in one sense, Thrasymachus with a theory of history. He agrees that law has served the stronger, and adds that "the stronger" is a class, that this has a history, and that the history can end. Everyone in between offered some account of why political power could be more than that: nature, natural law, the peace of Babylon, consent, the general will, divided power, prescription, reason, ethical life, association. The course has not crowned a winner, and it was not meant to. You now know what each of them was answering, and that is where [`political-philosophy`](../../political-philosophy/syllabus.md) picks up the argument.
