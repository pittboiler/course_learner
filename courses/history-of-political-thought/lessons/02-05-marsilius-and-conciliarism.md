# History of Political Thought · Lesson 2.5: Pope, emperor and council: Marsilius and conciliarism

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 2: Rome and Christendom · Builds on: [2.3 Aquinas on law](02-03-aquinas-on-law.md), [2.4 Aquinas on kingship, tyranny and property](02-04-aquinas-on-kingship-tyranny-and-property.md) · Unlocks: [3.1 Machiavelli's *Prince*](03-01-machiavelli-the-prince.md)

## Why this matters

Aquinas told you what law is for and what a good king does. He did not settle who is *supreme* when two rulers claim the same people, and for three centuries Latin Christendom fought over exactly that: pope against emperor, pope against king, and finally council against pope. Out of that fight came the ideas you probably think of as modern: that a ruler's authority comes from the community he rules, that an assembly can represent a whole people, and that a head may be judged by the body. This lesson traces how, in their own terms.

## The idea

Every medieval theory of authority answers two questions. *Which way does authority flow?* Either it **descends**, from God through a head who then commissions lesser officers, or it **ascends**, from the community to the officers it sets up. The pair is Walter Ullmann's, from *Principles of Government and Politics in the Middle Ages* (1961): too tidy for many later historians, but a good first map. And *how many supreme powers are there?* One, which delegates the rest, or two, each supreme in its own sphere.

Run the four answers you will meet today through those questions:

- **Gelasius (494):** two powers, both from God, neither simply derived from the other.
- **Boniface VIII (1302):** one supreme power, the pope's; authority descends, and temporal rule is exercised under spiritual direction.
- **Marsilius of Padua (1324):** authority ascends, from the whole body of citizens; the clergy hold no coercive power at all.
- **The Council of Constance (1415):** inside the Church itself, a general council representing the whole Church holds a power that even a pope must obey, at least in certain matters.

Notice that the last two are not the same move. Marsilius moves *power over the Church* to the secular community. The conciliarists keep the Church's power in the Church and move it from the head to the body.

## Source

Two texts sat behind the argument. First, Luke 22:38 (Douay–Rheims), at the Last Supper:

> But they said: Lord, behold, here are two swords. And he said to them: It is enough.

Read allegorically (Bernard of Clairvaux, in the twelfth century, is the usual reference point), the two swords became the spiritual and temporal powers, and the question became whose hand each sword is in.

Second, Pope Gelasius I to the Emperor Anastasius, 494 (trans. J. H. Robinson, *Readings in European History*, 1905):

> There are two powers, august Emperor, by which this world is chiefly ruled, namely, the sacred authority of the priests and the royal power.

Look at the nouns. The Latin opposes the priests' *auctoritas* to the king's *potestas*. The letter goes on to call the priestly charge the weightier, since priests answer for kings at the divine judgment, but it does not say the king receives his power *from* the priest. That gap is what the next eight centuries argued over: the dualist reads two sources; the hierocrat reads a ranking that implies delegation.

## The argument

**A. Boniface VIII, *Unam Sanctam* (1302), paraphrased.** Issued amid Philip IV of France's quarrel with the pope over the clergy.

1. **The Church is one body with one head, Christ and his vicar, Peter's successor.** *In words:* a body with two heads is a monster, so Christendom cannot have two supreme authorities.
2. **Both swords are in the Church's power:** the spiritual wielded *by* the Church, the temporal wielded *for* the Church by kings and soldiers, at the priest's will and sufferance. *In words:* kings really do the ruling, but as agents of an order whose direction is spiritual.
3. **Lower things are brought to order through higher ones, so spiritual power may establish temporal power and judge it if it errs; the highest spiritual power is judged by God alone.** *In words:* the ranking of ends becomes a ranking of jurisdictions.
4. **The bull closes by declaring subjection to the Roman pontiff necessary for salvation for every human creature.** *In words:* the claim ends in the order of salvation, not only of politics.

How much of this is binding teaching, which clause carries the weight, and how the final sentence has been read since, are questions for theology: see [`fundamental-theology` 4.5](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-05-reading-a-documents-weight.md) and [`ecclesiology-and-mariology`](../../ecclesiology-and-mariology/syllabus.md) 5.1. This course takes the bull as a political theory. The dualist reply came at once: John of Paris, *On Royal and Papal Power* (c. 1302), granted the pope's spiritual primacy but held that kingship existed before the priesthood and does not derive from it.

**B. Marsilius of Padua, *Defensor Pacis* (1324), paraphrased** (no public-domain English translation). Written amid Louis of Bavaria's conflict with Pope John XXII. Discourse I argues from reason, explicitly from Aristotle's *Politics*; Discourse II from scripture.

1. **The city is a natural community for living and living well, made of parts (farmers, artisans, soldiers, priests), with a ruling part (*pars principans*) that judges and enforces** (I.4–5). *In words:* this is Aristotle's city ([1.3](01-03-aristotle-the-city-exists-by-nature.md)), with the priesthood as one part among others.
2. **Law, in the strict sense, is a command backed by coercive penalty in this life** (I.10). *In words:* what makes something law is enforceability here, not its moral content alone.
3. **The primary efficient cause of law, the human legislator, is the people, the whole body of citizens, or its "weightier part" (*valentior pars*), judged by the quantity and quality of persons** (I.12). *In words:* the right to make coercive law belongs to the community, acting as a whole or through its weightier part.
4. **Why the whole?** The whole judges the common benefit better than any part (Aristotle's summation argument from [1.4](01-04-aristotle-constitutions-citizens-and-the-polity.md)); a law each citizen has in a sense imposed on himself is better obeyed; and no one knowingly legislates to his own harm, whereas the few legislate for their own advantage (I.12–13). *In words:* consent makes law both wiser and more stable.
5. **The legislator establishes the ruling part and can correct or depose it** (I.15, I.18). *In words:* the government is the community's instrument, not its source.
6. **Christ did not exercise coercive jurisdiction in this world or give it to his apostles; priests teach and warn, like physicians of souls, but compel no one** (Discourse II, esp. II.4–5). *In words:* the clergy's power is real but non-coercive.
7. **So the papal claim to a plenitude of power (*plenitudo potestatis*) is a usurpation, and the peculiar cause of strife in Italy that Aristotle could not foresee** (I.1, I.19). *In words:* the defender of peace must strip the Church of coercive power.

Marsilius adds that disputed points of faith belong to a general council of the faithful, laity included, convoked by the human legislator (II.20–21). The work was condemned by John XXII, apparently in 1327.

**C. Conciliarism.** The Great Schism (1378–1417) produced two popes, then three after the Council of Pisa (1409). The Council of Constance acted on an argument, paraphrased here:

1. **The Church's authority resides in the whole body of the faithful; the pope is its head and minister, not its owner.** *In words:* the head acts for the body.
2. **A general council, lawfully assembled, represents the whole Church.** *In words:* the body can act through an assembly that stands for it.
3. **So a council holds power immediately from Christ, which everyone, the pope included, must obey in matters of faith, the ending of schism, and reform of the Church in head and members** (*Haec Sancta*, 1415, paraphrased). *In words:* in these matters the body is above the head.
4. **Councils must therefore meet regularly** (*Frequens*, 1417). *In words:* supremacy needs an institution, not just an emergency.

Brian Tierney, *Foundations of the Conciliar Theory* (1955), argued that the premises came less from Marsilius than from canon lawyers' theory of corporations, in which a body's authority lives in its members and its head acts for them, and from the canonists' old admission that a pope who strays from the faith can be judged. The standing of *Haec Sancta* is disputed: a permanent constitutional rule, an emergency measure, or not validly approved. The Council of Basel pushed it to deposing Eugenius IV (1439); Pius II's *Execrabilis* (1460, as usually dated) forbade appeals from a pope to a future council.

## Map of positions

|  | **Authority descends** (from God, through the head) | **Authority ascends** (from the community) |
|---|---|---|
| **Government of the Church** | Papal monarchy: the pope holds plenitude of power; no one judges the first see | Conciliarism: the whole Church, represented in council, over the pope in faith, schism and reform (Constance; Gerson, d'Ailly, Zabarella) |
| **Coercive temporal rule** | Hierocracy: temporal power exercised for the Church and judged by it (*Unam Sanctam*); its rival, dualism (Gelasius, John of Paris), also descends, but through two channels | Marsilius: the human legislator makes law and sets up the ruling part; clergy hold no coercive power |

Marsilius fills *both* right-hand cells, since his general council answers to the secular legislator; the mainstream conciliarists fill only one and would have rejected his lay control of the Church.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean: Marsilius on an excommunication).** A bishop excommunicates a merchant for usury and orders the city's officials to seize his goods. Run the premises. The bishop may teach that usury is sin and warn the merchant of its penalty in the next life (B6). Seizure is a coercive penalty in this life, so it requires a law (B2), and law comes only from the human legislator (B3), enforced by the ruling part it set up (B5). If the city's legislator has made usury a crime, the officials act on *that* law, not on the bishop's order; if not, the order binds no one. Marsilius's own view is that even excommunication, because of its civil effects, should involve the judgment of the community of the faithful, not a priest alone. Spiritual judgment and coercive jurisdiction come completely apart.

**Example 2 (hard: 1414, three popes).** Each has cardinals and kingdoms behind him. Who can end the schism?

- **The papalist premise, *prima sedes a nemine iudicatur* (the first see is judged by no one),** blocks every remedy: any council one claimant calls, the other two reject, and no body may judge a true pope.
- **The conciliar route** runs through C1–C3: if authority lives in the whole Church, the whole Church, represented, can decide which head it has, or remove all three. Constance did roughly that, and then elected Martin V (1417).
- **The strain** is in the word "immediately." If the council's power comes straight from Christ, *Haec Sancta* is a standing constitution and the papacy is a limited monarchy. If it holds only when the Church lacks a certain head, it is necessity: Gerson, among others, invoked *epieikeia*, the equity that lets a ruler act beside the letter of a law when obeying it would defeat its purpose. That is the Aristotelian principle behind Aquinas's besieged-city gates in [2.3](02-03-aquinas-on-law.md) (ST I-II q.96 a.6). The text alone does not fix which reading is right, which is why the dispute outlived the council.

## Watch out

- **Anachronism: the *legislator humanus* is not popular sovereignty in the modern democratic sense.** The *valentior pars* is weighed by "quality" as well as number, and Marsilius's citizens exclude women, children, slaves and foreigners, following Aristotle. Scholars split on how majoritarian he is (Alan Gewirth's 1951 study reads him more so than many later readers do). Say "consent of the corporate community," not "democracy."
- **Conciliarists were not anti-papal reformers or proto-Protestants.** Constance wanted a pope, elected one, and condemned Jan Hus. The quarrel was over the pope's relation to the Church, not whether there should be one.
- **"Two swords" does not mean separation of church and state.** Every position above assumes one Christian society.
- **Don't grade a bull's or a decree's authority here.** Saying *Unam Sanctam*'s final sentence, or *Haec Sancta*, is (or is not) binding dogma is an exegetical claim about theological weight, owned by [`fundamental-theology`](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-05-reading-a-documents-weight.md). This course reports what each text claimed and how it was used.

## One-liner

> The medieval fight over who is supreme, pope, emperor or council, forced the question of where authority comes from, and Marsilius and the conciliarists answered: from the body it governs.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢) *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)*** (a) Reconstruct, in 4–6 numbered premises and a conclusion, Marsilius's argument in *Defensor Pacis* Discourse I that the power to make coercive law belongs to the human legislator. (b) Flag the premise you think is weakest *on Marsilius's own Aristotelian terms*, and say why. 150 words or fewer for (b).

**P2 (🟡) *(Exegetical.)*** Boniface VIII and Marsilius disagree about whether the clergy may exercise coercive judgment over temporal rulers. Name the single premise one must deny, show that John of Paris's position proves it is not "whether the pope has spiritual primacy," and say what kind of argument would move each side. 150 words or fewer.

**P3 (🔴, optional) *(Exegetical.)*** An invented memo:

> *To the shareholders' committee.* Our chief executive is not the company; he is its servant. Authority in a corporation lives in its members, and when the head fails, the body may act without him. We propose that the annual general meeting be declared superior to the executive in all matters, that it may remove him at any time, and that it meet whenever a tenth of shares so request.

Which conciliar ideas is the memo borrowing, and name two things it has dropped or changed from *Haec Sancta*'s actual claim. 150 words or fewer.

<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)*

**Must hit, strict (a):**
- Law in the strict sense is a coercive command enforced by penalty in this life.
- The legislator, the primary efficient cause of law, is the whole body of citizens or its weightier part.
- At least two of the supporting reasons: the whole judges the common benefit better than any part (Aristotle's summation); self-imposed law is better obeyed; no one knowingly legislates to his own harm, while a few legislate for their own advantage.
- Conclusion: the authority to make coercive law belongs to the community, which sets up the ruling part (optional extra: so no priest or pope holds it by office).

**Must hit, any verdict (b):** name one premise precisely; give a reason internal to Aristotle or to Marsilius's own text (not a modern democratic complaint); say what would repair or rescue it.

**Wrong turns:** starting from scripture (that is Discourse II's route, not this argument's); writing "the majority" for the legislator, which drops the quality clause; making the priestly argument (B6) a premise of the legislator argument.

**Model answer (b), one of several:** The weakest step is the summation premise. In *Politics* III.11 Aristotle offers it cautiously: the many *may* judge better, and not every multitude qualifies. Marsilius needs it to show that the whole is the proper *cause* of law, not just a useful adviser. His qualifier "weightier part," weighed by quality, quietly concedes the worry, but it then reopens the question of who judges quality, which is where oligarchy re-enters. A defender can reply that Marsilius needs only the weaker claim, that consent makes law more stable, which does not depend on the many's wisdom.

---

**P2** *(Exegetical, strict on identifying one premise.)*

**Must hit, strict:**
- The crux: *whether the power serving the higher end thereby holds jurisdiction, including coercive judgment, over the power serving the lower end.* Boniface affirms it (lower things are ordered through higher; spiritual power judges temporal). Marsilius denies it: coercion belongs to human law and its legislator, whatever the dignity of the priesthood.
- John of Paris grants the pope's spiritual primacy and still denies temporal jurisdiction, so primacy and jurisdiction can come apart; the disagreement must lie in the step from one to the other.
- What would move each side: scriptural exegesis (the two swords, the power to bind and loose in Matthew 16:19, whether Christ exercised or gave coercive power), and whether "law" is essentially coercive, as Marsilius defines it.

**Wrong turns:** naming God's existence or the truth of Christianity (all three parties accept it); naming "church vs state" as the crux (all assume one Christian society); naming two premises.

**Model answer:** The disputed premise is that the higher spiritual end confers jurisdiction over those who serve lower ends, including the right to judge and correct kings. Boniface affirms it; Marsilius denies it, holding that coercive power is by definition the human legislator's. It cannot be primacy as such: John of Paris accepts papal primacy in spiritual things and still denies temporal jurisdiction. Boniface's side would be moved by a reading of the Petrine and swords texts showing Christ gave no coercive power; Marsilius's, by an argument that spiritual ends cannot be protected without coercion.

---

**P3** *(Exegetical.)*

**Must hit, strict:**
- Borrowed: authority resides in the body, the head is its minister (corporation theory, Tierney's thesis); the body may act when the head fails (the schism argument); a standing assembly meeting on a rule (*Frequens*).
- Two of the changes: *Haec Sancta* grounds the council's power *immediately in Christ*, not in members' interest or ownership; it claims superiority in *specified* matters (faith, ending schism, reform), not "all matters"; it speaks of a council *representing* the whole Church, not a meeting of owners weighted by shares; Constance kept and elected a head, with no rule for removal at any time.

**Wrong turns:** calling the memo Marsilian (Marsilius places authority in the civil legislator, not in the corporate body of the Church); treating "declared superior in all matters" as what *Haec Sancta* claimed.

**Model answer:** The memo borrows the conciliar core: authority lives in the whole corporation, the head is its minister, and the body may act when the head fails, plus *Frequens*'s regular assemblies. It drops two things. First, the source: Constance claimed its power came immediately from Christ, not from the members' stake, so the council was a trustee of an authority it did not own. Second, the scope: *Haec Sancta* limited the council's supremacy to faith, ending schism, and reform in head and members, and its authority even there is disputed, whereas the memo claims superiority in everything and removal at will.

## Flashback

**From Lesson 2.3 (Aquinas on law):** *(Exegetical.)* Close reading. Aquinas asks whether custom can obtain the force of law (ST I-II q.97 a.3). His reply to the third objection distinguishes two kinds of people. Of a people that is "free, and able to make their own laws", he writes (English Dominican):

> the consent of the whole people expressed by a custom counts far more ... [than] does the authority of the sovereign, who has not the power to frame laws, except as representing the people

For a people not free to make its own laws, he says a custom has force insofar as those who make law for that people tolerate it.

(a) Which reading does the reply support? **(i)** In every political community lawmaking power belongs to the people, and any prince legislates only as their representative. **(ii)** That holds for a free people; other peoples receive law from a higher authority. Name the deciding words. (b) In q.90 a.3, law is made by "the whole people" or by a public person who has care of it. In two sentences or fewer, say what this reply adds to that "or". 100 words or fewer in total.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):** reading (ii). The representation clause is governed by "if they are free, and able to make their own laws" (the full sentence opens with "if"). The second case then describes a people that lacks that power and cannot abolish a law made by a higher authority; its custom binds only as far as whoever makes its laws tolerates it. So the reply does not make popular authority universal.
**Must hit, strict (b):** in a free people the two options in q.90 a.3 are not rival sources. The public person makes law *as representing* the people, so the people's consent, even unwritten in custom, outweighs his authority. Where the people is not free, the "public person" can hold lawmaking power that does not derive from them.
**Wrong turns:** choosing (i) by reading the quoted clause without the "if" that governs it. Treating this as Marsilius's thesis: Aquinas makes popular lawmaking depend on a people's condition, and he does not say the people may correct or depose the ruler.
**Model answer:** (a) Reading (ii). The sovereign legislates only "as representing the people" in the case of a people "free, and able to make their own laws". For a people lacking that power, law comes from a higher authority and custom counts only as tolerated. (b) For a free people, the "or" is one source, not two: the public person holds the people's lawmaking power as their representative. Only for an unfree people can lawmaking authority sit elsewhere.

</details>
