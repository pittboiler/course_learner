# History of Political Thought · Lesson 1.4: Aristotle: constitutions, citizens and the polity

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 1: The Greek polis · Builds on: [1.3 Aristotle: the city exists by nature](01-03-aristotle-the-city-exists-by-nature.md) · Unlocks: [2.1 Cicero: the commonwealth and the law of nature](02-01-cicero-the-commonwealth-and-natural-law.md)

## Why this matters

Every time someone calls a regime "an oligarchy dressed up as a democracy," they are using a tool Aristotle built: a grid of who rules crossed with whom they rule for. *Politics* III–IV also holds the first surviving argument that the many, pooled, might judge better than the few best, and the first case for a large middle class as the ballast of a stable state. [1.2](01-02-plato-against-democracy-and-the-second-best-city.md) left Plato's question open: who should rule? Aristotle's answer is that it depends, and he tells you on what.

## The idea

Start with a question that sounds bureaucratic: **who is a citizen?** Not whoever lives in the city (resident aliens and slaves do too), and not whoever can sue in its courts (treaty partners can). A citizen, strictly, is someone who takes a turn at *ruling*: sits on juries, votes in the assembly, holds office. Citizenship is a share in rule.

That makes the next question obvious. If citizens are the people who share in rule, cities differ by **how that share is arranged**: who holds the supreme authority, and what they use it for. That arrangement is the *politeia*. Jowett translates it "constitution," but it is not a document. It is the city's organization of offices, and beyond that its way of life: which people count, what the city honours, what it is for.

Now the grid. Supreme authority sits with **one, a few, or many**. And it is used either for **the common interest** or for **the rulers' own interest**. Three times two is six. Rule by one for all is kingship; for himself, tyranny. By a few for all, aristocracy; for themselves, oligarchy. By the many for all, what Aristotle calls simply *politeia*, "polity"; for themselves, democracy.

Then Aristotle kicks his own grid. The number was never the point. Oligarchy is really rule by the rich and democracy rule by the poor; they only *look* like few and many because the rich are usually few. That relabelling changes everything downstream. The fight in a Greek city is a class fight, each class with its own idea of justice. Aristotle's practical answer, for most cities, is a mixture of the two, anchored by the people in the middle.

## Source

*Politics* III.8, 1279b39–1280a3 (Jowett, public domain):

> For the real difference between democracy and oligarchy is poverty and wealth. Wherever men rule by reason of their wealth, whether they be few or many, that is an oligarchy, and where the poor rule, that is a democracy.

Two phrases carry the argument. "The real difference" demotes the number criterion from definition to symptom. "Whether they be few or many" makes the test explicit: hold wealth fixed, vary the headcount, and the classification should not move. The very next sentences add that as a matter of fact the rich are few and the poor many. That is why the number criterion worked well enough to fool people.

## The argument

1. **A citizen, strictly, is one who shares in judging and ruling.** His "special characteristic is that he shares in the administration of justice, and in offices" (III.1, 1275a22–23), including open-ended "offices" like juror and assemblyman. *In words:* citizenship is a job share in government, not a residence permit or a passport.
2. **So the citizen differs by constitution.** Aristotle admits the definition fits a democracy's citizen best (1275b5–6); where the people have no regular assembly, fewer residents qualify. *In words:* who counts as a citizen is itself one of the things a constitution decides.
3. **The good citizen need not be a good man** (III.4). Citizen virtue is relative to the constitution, as a sailor's virtue is relative to the ship's safety. Only the good ruler must have practical wisdom. The citizen of a free city must know both how to rule and how to obey, as a freeman. *In words:* being excellent *at* a regime is not the same as being excellent *simply*; the two coincide only in the best city.
4. **A constitution is the arrangement of offices, above all the supreme one; the governing body is the constitution** (III.6, 1278b8–11). *In words:* to know a city's *politeia*, find out who has the final say.
5. **Rule over free equals exists for the ruled, like the arts.** A trainer or pilot aims at the good of those in his care and benefits only incidentally, as one of the crew. So constitutions aiming at the common interest are correct; those aiming at the rulers' interest are deviant, because they are despotic and a city is a community of free men (III.6, 1279a17–21). *In words:* ruling citizens for your own benefit treats free men as slaves.
6. **Supreme authority rests with one, few or many; crossing that with premise 5 yields six regimes** (III.7). *In words:* two questions, who and for whom, fix the map.
7. **But number is accidental; wealth is essential** (III.8). *In words:* oligarchy and democracy name class rule, and each serves its class, not the city.
8. **Each class holds a partial justice** (III.9). Both agree justice is equality for equals and inequality for unequals. The oligarch thinks unequal wealth makes men unequal in everything; the democrat thinks equal free birth makes them equal in everything. But the city exists for the good life, not for trade or mutual defence, so shares in it should track contribution to *that*. *In words:* each side generalizes one real difference, and the end of the city decides which differences matter.
9. **The many may collectively judge better than the few best** (III.11). Each ordinary person has some share of virtue and practical wisdom. Pooled, they become like one person with many hands and senses, as a meal many contribute to beats one paid for from a single purse. So the many should at least elect and audit officials. *In words:* partial knowledge adds up. (Module 1's boss problem takes this argument apart; here, just know its shape.)
10. **For most cities, the best attainable constitution is a polity anchored by a large middle class** (IV.8–9, IV.11). A polity is a fusion of oligarchy and democracy. Those of moderate property can both rule and obey, neither covet nor are coveted, and tip the balance against either extreme. *In words:* when you cannot have the best men, mix the classes and weight the middle.

## Map of positions

| Who holds supreme authority | For the common interest (correct) | For the rulers' interest (deviant) |
|---|---|---|
| **One** | Kingship | Tyranny |
| **Few** (really: the rich, or the best) | Aristocracy | Oligarchy: rule of the wealthy, for the wealthy |
| **Many** (really: the poor, or those bearing arms) | Polity (*politeia*) | Democracy: rule of the poor, for the needy |

Read the parentheticals. The left column is not a pure headcount. III.7 already ties polity to the citizens who bear arms, since military virtue is the kind the many can share. III.8 ties the few and the many to wealth and poverty. And IV.8 redefines polity as a *mixture* of the two deviant class regimes. It is less a third cell than the place where two cells meet.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean: classify by the two criteria).** An invented city, Kallion. Its assembly is open only to men owning a farm of a set size, about a fifth of the free adult men. The assembly just voted to seize the common pasture the smallholders grazed and divide it among its members.

Run the grid. *Who rules?* Few, and specifically the propertied: premise 7's criterion, not only the headcount. *For whom?* The pasture vote benefits the rulers at the expense of the excluded, so premise 5 says deviant. Few plus wealth plus self-interest: **oligarchy**. Now change one fact: 70 percent of free men meet the property bar, and the vote is the same. Headcount says "many." III.8 says it is still an oligarchy, because they rule *by reason of their wealth* and for it. The Source passage exists for exactly this case.

**Example 2 (hard: when the criteria pull apart).** A second invented city, Mesa. The poor majority controls the assembly. Magistrates are *elected*, not chosen by lot. There is no property bar for the assembly. The rich are fined if they skip jury duty, and the poor are paid to attend. The assembly has just rejected a proposal to cancel debts, on the ground that it would ruin the city's credit, which everyone relies on.

The poor rule, so III.8 says democracy. They rule for the common interest, so III.7 says polity. Which wins?

Aristotle's answer comes in IV.9, where he gives three ways to mix oligarchy and democracy. You can *combine* both sets of rules, fining the rich *and* paying the poor. You can take a *mean* between them, such as a moderate property bar. Or you can *borrow* a piece from each, such as election (oligarchic, in Greek eyes) plus no property bar (democratic). Mesa does the first and third. His test for a good mixture: observers can fairly call the same city a democracy *or* an oligarchy, as people did with Sparta. So Mesa is a **polity**, and its debt vote shows the mixture working. The poor hold the numbers but not the whole of the city's direction.

Where the scheme strains: nothing tells you *how much* self-interest turns a polity into a democracy. Every assembly passes some measures that favour its majority. Aristotle's classification is a set of ideal types for judging cases, not a sorting algorithm, and he says as much when he calls most actual regimes blends.

## Watch out

- **You might think *politeia* means a written constitution. That is an anachronism.** No Greek city had a single founding document in the American sense. The *politeia* is the arrangement of offices and, in Aristotle's phrase, "the constitution is in a figure the life of the city" (IV.11, 1295a40–b1). Asking whether a law is "unconstitutional" has no Aristotelian meaning.
- **You might think "democracy" here is the modern liberal democracy.** It is the direct rule of the poor through assembly, juries and lot, and Aristotle lists it as *deviant*. He does not classify it as bad because many rule. He classifies it as deviant because it rules for one class. Election, which moderns treat as the democratic device, he counts as oligarchic.
- **You might think the summation argument is the Condorcet jury theorem.** The jury theorem has voters making independent guesses at a yes-or-no question, each right more than half the time, so errors cancel. Aristotle's many *complement* each other: one grasps one part and another a different part, so the parts add up to the whole. Different mechanism, different conditions of failure. He himself says it cannot hold for every multitude (III.11, 1281b15–20).
- **You might think Aristotle's "middle class" is a modern income band.** It is the citizens of moderate property in a small agrarian city, close to the arms-bearing farmers of III.7. The argument is about who can rule and obey as equals, not about consumer spending.

## One-liner

> Ask who holds the final say and whom it serves, then look past the headcount to the class behind it: rich or poor rule for themselves, and a middle that can both rule and obey holds the mixture together.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢) *(Exegetical.)*** (a) Classify each invented city under Aristotle's six regimes and name the criterion from III.6–8 or IV.9 that decides it. One line each.

> (i) In Argyra, most free men own shares in the silver mines. Only shareholders may attend the assembly, and it votes a tax on grain, which the landless buy, to fund dividends.
> (ii) In Thessa, one man holds office for life under the city's laws. His subjects accept his rule, and he spends the revenues on walls and grain stores for everyone.
> (iii) In Kome, the assembly of all free men, most of them poor, chooses officials by lot. It has just voted to cancel all debts owed to the rich and share out their land among the voters.
> (iv) In Heptapolis, officials are elected, there is no property bar for the assembly, and rich absentees from juries are fined while poor jurors are paid. Visitors argue about whether it is a democracy or an oligarchy.

(b) A commentator writes: "Whatever else it is, it can't be an oligarchy — the ruling party won 60 percent of the vote in a free election." Name the two things in III.8 and IV.9 this sentence ignores. 50 words or fewer.

**P2 (🟡) *(Exegetical.)*** *Politics* IV.11, 1295b35–39 (Jowett):

> those states are likely to be well-administered, in which the middle class is large, and larger if possible than both the other classes, or at any rate than either singly; for the addition of the middle class turns the scale, and prevents either of the extremes from being dominant.

(a) Does the passage claim that the middle class should *govern* as a ruling majority, or that it stabilizes the city as a *counterweight*? Point to the words that decide it. (b) The passage gives one kind of reason for favouring the middle. Name it, and name the other kind of reason IV.11 gives earlier in the chapter. 150 words or fewer in total.

**P3 (🔴, optional) *(Exegetical.)*** In III.9 an oligarch and a democrat argue over who should share in rule. Name the principle of justice they share, the single premise on which they actually disagree, and the consideration Aristotle thinks should decide it. 150 words or fewer.

<details>
<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical, strict.)*

**Must hit, strict (a):**
- (i) **Oligarchy.** The rulers are *many*, but they rule by reason of wealth (share ownership is the bar), and the tax serves the propertied against the landless. III.8: "whether they be few or many."
- (ii) **Kingship.** One ruler, ruling by law over willing subjects, for the common interest (III.7; tyranny would be the same rule used for himself).
- (iii) **Democracy.** The poor rule (lot and the open assembly are democratic devices), and the debt cancellation serves the needy against the rich, not the common good (III.7–8).
- (iv) **Polity.** A fusion of oligarchy and democracy by IV.9's *combining* mode (fines plus pay) and *borrowing* mode (election plus no property bar). It passes IV.9's test that observers can call it either.

**Must hit, strict (b):** (1) III.8: number is accidental; whether rule is oligarchic depends on whether it is rule *by and for wealth*, not on the size of the majority. (2) IV.9: election itself is an oligarchic device in Aristotle's scheme, so "free election" is no evidence against oligarchy.

**Wrong turns:** calling (i) democracy or polity because the rulers are many; calling (iv) democracy because the poor are paid; in (b), saying only that the commentator ignores "who benefits," which misses the class criterion.

**Model answer (b):** It treats a majority as proof of rule by the many. But for Aristotle, number is an accident: a majority ruling through and for wealth is still oligarchy. And it treats election as democratic, where Aristotle calls election oligarchic and lot democratic.

---

**P2** *(Exegetical, strict.)*

**Must hit, strict (a):**
- **Counterweight.** The decisive words are "turns the scale" and "prevents either of the extremes from being dominant": the middle's job is to tip a balance, not to monopolize rule.
- "Or at any rate than either singly" confirms it: the middle need *not* outnumber rich and poor combined. It needs only to be large enough that neither extreme can dominate once the middle adds its weight to the other side.
- The passage is about which states are "well-administered," not about restricting office to the middle.

**Must hit, strict (b):** The passage's reason is **stability**: balance prevents domination and, as the chapter goes on, faction. The other reason is **ethical**: the happy life is virtue, virtue is a mean (citing the *Ethics*), and those with moderate fortune most readily follow reason and can both rule and obey, where the very rich can only command and the very poor only obey (1295a35–b20).

**Wrong turns:** reading "larger if possible than both the other classes" as the whole claim, which ignores the fallback clause; treating the stability argument as the only one; reading "middle class" as a modern income band.

**Model answer:** The passage makes the middle a counterweight, not a ruling bloc. It wants the middle larger than both extremes "if possible," but settles for larger than "either singly," and its reason is that the middle "turns the scale." That is the language of a balance tipped against whichever extreme would dominate. The reason here is stability. Earlier in IV.11 Aristotle gives an ethical one: virtue is a mean, and people of moderate fortune most readily listen to reason and can rule and obey as equals.

---

**P3** *(Exegetical, strict on the crux; any accurate phrasing passes.)*

**Must hit, strict:**
- **Shared principle:** justice is equality for equals and inequality for unequals, a proportional distribution that considers persons as well as things.
- **The crux:** *which respect of equality is relevant to a share in rule.* The oligarch takes wealth: unequal in property, so unequal in everything. The democrat takes free birth: equal in freedom, so equal in everything. Each must deny the other's choice of respect, not the principle.
- **What decides it for Aristotle:** the end of the city. If the city were a joint-stock venture or a defence pact, property shares would settle it. But it exists for the good life, so shares should track contribution to that, which Aristotle calls political virtue.
- He adds a diagnostic point: both sides are judges in their own case.

**Wrong turns:** stating the crux as "equality versus inequality" (both accept both, for different persons); saying the democrat rejects proportional justice; stating Aristotle's resolution as "rule by the middle class," which is IV.11, not III.9.

**Model answer:** Both hold that justice gives equal shares to equals and unequal shares to unequals. They disagree about which difference makes people unequal *for the purpose of ruling*. The oligarch says wealth; the democrat says free birth. Each generalizes one respect in which people really are unequal or equal, and each is judging his own case. What should decide it, for Aristotle, is what the city is for. Were it a business partnership, a man who put in one mina of a hundred should not share equally with the man who put in ninety-nine. But the city exists for the good life, not for trade or security, so political rights should follow contribution to living well: political virtue. An oligarch would be moved only by accepting that end.

## Flashback

**From Lesson 1.2 (Plato against democracy, and the second-best city):** *(Exegetical.)* Close reading. In *Laws* III (690a–d, Jowett) the Athenian lists seven claims to rule: parents over children, noble over ignoble, elder over younger, master over slave, stronger over weaker, then the sixth, "the greatest of all", that "the wise should lead and command, and the ignorant follow and obey", which he calls "the rule of law over willing subjects, and not a rule of compulsion". The seventh is rule by lot, which he calls "quite just". He concludes that these titles are "naturally opposed to each other", "a fountain-head of seditions".

(a) Which reading do the words support? **(i)** Wisdom is the only valid title, and the other six are pretenders. **(ii)** Wisdom is the highest of several genuine titles that clash, so the lawgiver must manage the clash. Name the deciding words. (b) In one or two sentences, say what is new in describing rule by the wise as "the rule of law", compared with the *Republic*'s philosopher-kings. 100 words or fewer in total.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):** reading (ii). "The greatest of all" ranks wisdom *among* the titles; it does not strike the rest from the list. Lot is called "quite just", so at least one title besides wisdom is endorsed outright. "Naturally opposed to each other" and "fountain-head of seditions" make the clash between titles, not the invalidity of six of them, the lawgiver's problem. That is the route to the *Laws*' mixed constitution (693d–e) and its election procedure as a mean (756e).
**Must hit, strict (b):** wisdom now rules *through* law. In the *Laws*, law stands in for living knowledge that no one can safely hold (875c–d), and rulers serve it (715c–d). The *Republic*'s rulers rule by their own knowledge, with law as their instrument. Consent itself is not new: the *Republic*'s temperance was already agreement about who should rule (431d–432a).
**Wrong turns:** choosing (i) because "greatest" sounds exclusive. Answering (b) with "willing subjects" as the novelty, when the *Republic* already required agreement between rulers and ruled.
**Model answer:** (a) Reading (ii). Wisdom is "the greatest of all", a rank among real titles, and lot is called "quite just". The titles are "naturally opposed", a "fountain-head of seditions", so the lawgiver's task is to reconcile them, which the *Laws* does by mixing monarchy and democracy. (b) The wise rule as law, not over it. That is the *Laws*' retreat: law stands in for knowledge, where the *Republic*'s kings used law as their tool. Willing obedience was already the *Republic*'s temperance.

</details>
