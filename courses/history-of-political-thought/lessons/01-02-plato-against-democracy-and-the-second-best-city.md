# History of Political Thought · Lesson 1.2: Plato against democracy, and the second-best city

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 1: The Greek polis · Builds on: [1.1 Plato's *Republic*: justice in the city and the soul](01-01-platos-republic-justice-in-city-and-soul.md) · Unlocks: [1.3 Aristotle: the city exists by nature](01-03-aristotle-the-city-exists-by-nature.md)

## Why this matters

Every modern argument that some decisions should go to people who know better — independent central banks, expert commissions, courts that override majorities — has a first draft, and Plato wrote it. He also wrote the first serious worry about that argument, twenty-odd years later, in a book almost nobody reads: the *Laws*. Read together, they give you both the strongest classical case against democracy and its author's own account of what that case cannot deliver in practice. Most later arguments about rule by knowledge take up a position somewhere between the two books.

## The idea

Start where [1.1](01-01-platos-republic-justice-in-city-and-soul.md) left off. Justice is each part doing its own work, and the work of ruling belongs to the part that *knows*. So who should rule a city is a question of competence, like who should steer a ship or treat a fever. You don't put the helm to a vote among passengers.

Book VI dramatizes this with two images. In the **ship of state** (488a–489a) the owner is large but half-deaf and short-sighted, the crew brawl over who gets to steer, none of them has learned navigation, and they insist it can't be taught. Whoever helps them seize the helm gets called a real sailor; the man who studies stars and winds gets called a useless star-gazer. The **beast-tamer** (493a–c) makes the same point from another side: the popular teachers of Athens study the moods of a huge strong animal, learn what soothes and what enrages it, and then call "good" whatever it enjoys. Politics in a democracy, on this picture, is expertise in the crowd's appetites passed off as knowledge of the good.

Book VIII adds a *dynamic* argument. Regimes decay in a fixed order, each one ruined by the very thing it prizes. Aristocracy (the city of Books II–VII) slides into **timocracy**, the rule of honor-lovers; timocracy into **oligarchy**, the rule of wealth; oligarchy into **democracy**; democracy into **tyranny**. Democracy prizes freedom, the power to live as you like (557b). Chased without limit, freedom dissolves every authority — parents, teachers, finally the laws — until a frightened people hands itself to a champion who becomes a tyrant.

Then, late in life, Plato wrote the *Laws*, set in Crete, where three old men design a colony for real settlers. No philosopher-kings. The rulers serve written law, offices mix election with lot, and the constitution blends monarchy with democracy. Plato calls this city *second*. The question this lesson turns on is second to what, and why he settled for it.

## The argument

**The Republic's case for rule by knowledge (Books V–VI).**

1. **Ruling well is a craft; it requires knowledge of what is good for the city as a whole.** *In words:* governing is like navigation or medicine, and knowing how is what qualifies you.
2. **That knowledge is rare, and only philosophy reaches it** (Books VI–VII; the epistemology of the Line and Cave is [`ancient-medieval-philosophy` 2.2](../../ancient-medieval-philosophy/lessons/02-02-recollection-the-line-and-the-cave.md)). *In words:* most people have opinions about justice, not knowledge of it.
3. **The many cannot tell the knower from the flatterer.** Their judges are their appetites, so they reward whoever pleases them (ship, beast-tamer). *In words:* a crowd picks captains the way the mutineers do.
4. **So cities ruled by the many, or by the ignorant, will not end their evils.** *In words:* the problem is structural, not a matter of bad luck with leaders.
5. **Therefore philosophers must rule.** At 473c–d Socrates, braced for a wave of ridicule, says:

> "Until philosophers are kings, or the kings and princes of this world have the spirit and power of philosophy, and political greatness and wisdom meet in one, ... cities will never have rest from their evils"
> — *Republic* V, 473c–d (Jowett)

*In words:* either rulers become philosophers or philosophers become rulers; nothing short of that union fixes politics.

6. **Such rulers need a way of life that removes private interest:** no private property or families for the guardians (416d–417b), and a preference for rulers who do not want the job (520d). *In words:* knowledge is necessary but not sufficient; the Republic also removes the temptation to rule for oneself.

**The *Laws*' concession (Books IV and IX).**

7. **The principle stands: knowledge outranks law.** And the premise that changes is whether it can be had, and held safely:

> "there is no law or order which is above knowledge ... But then there is no such mind anywhere, or at least not much; and therefore we must choose law and order, which are second best."
> — *Laws* IX, 875c–d (Jowett)

*In words:* if a perfect knower existed he would need no laws, but none does, so law stands in for him. Just before this (875b–c) the Athenian adds that even a man who knew the public good would not stay firm holding absolute, unaccountable power (compare 691c–d).

8. **So rulers must be subordinate to law.**

> "when I call the rulers servants or ministers of the law ... the state in which the law is above the rulers, and the rulers are the inferiors of the law, has salvation"
> — *Laws* IV, 715c–d (Jowett)

*In words:* officials hold power as the law's servants, not its masters.

9. **And the constitution should mix the two "mother" forms**, monarchy and democracy (693d–e), since Persia was ruined by too much subjection and Athens by too much freedom (Book III); the council's election procedure is a mean between them (756e). *In words:* a dose of democracy buys friendship and consent; a dose of monarchy buys order and wisdom.

The *Statesman*, written between the two, states the pivot in one line: law is like an obstinate, ignorant tyrant who can't adapt to cases, so rule by a true statesman is best, but where he is absent strict rule of law is the second-best thing (294a–c, 297d–e).

## Map of positions

| | *Republic* | *Statesman* | *Laws* |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Title to rule** | Knowledge of the good | Knowledge (the kingly art) | Knowledge in principle; obedience to law in practice |
| **Status of law** | The rulers' instrument; the guardians' education matters more than a detailed code (425a–e) | Inflexible stand-in for knowledge; second best | Sovereign over officials, who are its servants |
| **Place of the many** | Obey; share in the city's good, not its rule | Democracy is the worst of the lawful regimes and the best of the lawless (303a–b) | Real share: assembly, election, lot, mixed with monarchic elements |
| **Where knowledge survives** | In the philosopher-rulers | In the ideal statesman, if found | In the Nocturnal Council (951d–952a, 961a–968e), which guards the laws' purpose |

Read across each row. The title to rule barely moves; what moves is the confidence that knowledge can be found and trusted with power.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean: the Book VIII mechanism).** Run the pattern once, on the step before democracy. Oligarchy defines the good as wealth, sets a property qualification for office, and lets citizens sell everything and fall into poverty (552a–b). The city splits into rich and poor, two cities in one (551d); the rulers, profiting from debt, don't check the spending of the young. Eventually the poor, who now see their rulers are soft, win, and democracy follows, with offices commonly assigned by lot (557a).

The same template then runs on democracy (562a–564a). One good is pursued insatiably, everything else neglected, the ruling class loses legitimacy, and the regime flips into its opposite. Oligarchy's wealth produces a mass of poor; democracy's freedom produces a people that "will have no one over them" and then finds itself a protector (565c–d). The protector, once he has tasted his fellow citizens' blood, becomes the wolf of the Arcadian tale: a tyrant. Notice the *parallel structure*: the charge is not that one value is evil, but that each regime makes one good the whole of the good.

**Example 2 (hard: did Plato change his mind?).** A developmentalist reads the *Laws* as a retreat: the old Plato, perhaps chastened by his failed visits to Syracuse (the story comes mostly from the *Seventh Letter*, whose authenticity is disputed), gave up on philosopher-kings and embraced the rule of law. Test that against 875c–d.

The passage keeps premise 1 intact: no law is above knowledge, and a true mind should rule, not serve. What it drops is the claim that such a mind is available, and it adds a claim about human nature: unaccountable power corrupts even the knower. So the *Laws* is not a conversion to democracy. It is the same argument run with a different factual premise. Plato says this himself at 739b–e: the best city is the one where "friends have all things in common", the city of the *Republic*, and the colony is second to it.

But the strain shows at the end. The Nocturnal Council studies virtue and the unity of the good, and the Athenian hands the city to its keeping (end of Book XII). Is that law's supremacy, or philosopher-rule returning through the back door? Scholars divide (Strauss read the *Laws* as a dialogue about the limits of what philosophy can do in a city; Popper, in *The Open Society and Its Enemies*, 1945, read both books as blueprints for a closed society). The text supports reading the council as guardian of the law's purpose, and it leaves open how far that guardianship reaches.

## Watch out

- **Anachronism: *dēmokratia* is not modern democracy.** Plato's target is Athens: adult male citizens voting in person in the assembly, most offices filled by lot, no representatives, no entrenched rights; women, slaves and resident foreigners excluded. His *eleutheria* is "doing what one likes", not a liberal bill of rights. Whether his argument transfers to representative, rights-limited democracy is a real question, and it belongs to [`political-philosophy`](../../political-philosophy/syllabus.md) (5.1, epistocracy), not to this lesson.
- **You might think Book VIII is a history of Greece.** It is a typology ordered by psychology, each regime matched to a type of soul (the democratic man lives day to day, taking up one pursuit after another, 561c–d). Aristotle attacks the sequence as history in *Politics* V.12; that attack misses if Plato meant a logic of decay rather than a chronicle.
- **Two different "second bests".** In *Laws* V (739b–e) the colony is second to the *Republic*'s communistic city. In *Laws* IX (875d) and the *Statesman*, law is second to living knowledge. Related, not identical; keep them apart.
- **You might think the *Laws* makes the people sovereign.** The law is sovereign, not the demos. Democratic elements are in the mix for the sake of friendship and stability, and they are hedged by property classes and the Nocturnal Council.

## One-liner

> The *Republic* says rule belongs to knowledge; the *Laws* agrees, and then admits no one can safely hold it — so it gives the crown to law.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢) *(Exegetical (a) · Exegetical, with a small evaluative flag (b).)*** Read *Republic* VIII, 562b–c (Jowett):

> "democracy has her own good, of which the insatiable desire brings her to dissolution? What good? Freedom, I replied ... the insatiable desire of this and the neglect of other things introduces the change in democracy, which occasions a demand for tyranny."

(a) Two readings: **(i)** Plato holds that freedom is bad, so a regime built on it must fail; **(ii)** Plato holds that democracy fails by pursuing its own genuine good without limit, at the expense of other goods. Which does the passage support? Point to the words that decide it, and name the parallel with oligarchy the passage relies on. 150 words or fewer. (b) Reconstruct Book VIII's argument from democracy to tyranny (562b–566a) in 4–6 numbered premises in Plato's terms, and flag the weakest premise.

**P2 (🟡) *(Exegetical.)*** The *Republic* makes philosophers kings; the *Laws* makes rulers servants of the law. Name the crux: the single premise the *Laws* denies that the *Republic* relies on (or adds that the *Republic* lacks). Say which passage shows the premise the two books still *share*, and say what kind of evidence would bear on the disputed premise. 150 words or fewer.

**P3 (🔴, optional) *(Exegetical.)*** Diagnose this invented memo.

> "Voters reward whoever promises them most, and a politics where anything goes ends, sooner or later, with a strongman. We therefore propose a permanent Stewardship Board of the nation's most credentialed economists, scientists and jurists, appointed for life, with final authority over long-range policy. Its decisions would not be subject to legislative or judicial review, since review by the unqualified would defeat the purpose."

(a) Which Platonic arguments is it borrowing? (b) Name two things it has dropped from the *Republic* and one it contradicts in the *Laws*. 150 words or fewer.

<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical (a) · Exegetical with evaluative flag (b).)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** reading (ii). Deciding words: "her own good" (freedom is called a *good*, not an evil); "insatiable desire" (the fault is the lack of limit); "the neglect of other things" (the failure is crowding out other goods). The parallel: oligarchy was ruined by the insatiable desire of *its* good, wealth, and neglect of everything else (562b, immediately before). The argument is about measure, not about freedom as such.

**Must hit, strict (b):** premises roughly: (1) democracy defines its good as freedom, living as one likes; (2) pursued without limit, freedom makes citizens resent every authority, including magistrates, parents, teachers and finally the laws; (3) this leaves the city divided and disordered, with the many fearing the rich and the rich pushed into resistance; (4) the people then set up a single champion to protect them; (5) a champion with the mob at his disposal and no restraint turns on his enemies and becomes a tyrant; ∴ extreme freedom passes into extreme slavery (564a). Flag a weakest premise with a reason: the standard choice is (2) (why must unlimited freedom erode *all* authority rather than settle at some equilibrium?) or (4)→(5) (a champion need not become a tyrant).

**Wrong turns:** choosing (i) because Plato is "anti-freedom", which ignores "her own good"; reconstructing the ship of state instead of Book VIII; writing modern premises about populism or rights that are not in the text.

**Model answer (a):** Reading (ii). Plato calls freedom democracy's "own good", so he is not classing it as an evil. What destroys the regime is the "insatiable desire" of that good and "the neglect of other things": the fault is unlimited pursuit of one good at the expense of the rest. The passage leans on the preceding step, where oligarchy fell through the same insatiable desire for its good, wealth. Book VIII gives each regime the same death: making one good the whole of the good.

---

**P2** *(Exegetical.)*

**Must hit, strict:** the crux is the premise that *knowledge adequate to rule can be found in a human being and can be trusted with unaccountable power*. The *Republic* relies on it (philosopher-rulers, secured by education and communism); the *Laws* denies it (875c–d: "no such mind anywhere, or at least not much"; 875b–c and 691c–d: absolute power corrupts even the knower). The shared premise: knowledge outranks law, shown at 875c ("no law or order which is above knowledge"), which the *Statesman* also holds. The disputed premise is partly empirical and psychological, so the relevant evidence is about human nature and history: whether any ruler has held unchecked power without corruption, and whether rulers can be selected and educated as the *Republic* requires.

**Wrong turns:** saying the *Laws* rejects rule by knowledge in favor of consent or democracy (875c keeps knowledge on top); naming "the mixed constitution" as the crux, which is a *consequence* of the denied premise, not the premise itself.

**Model answer:** Both books hold that knowledge outranks law: *Laws* 875c says no law is above knowledge. The *Republic* also relies on a further premise, that such knowledge can be embodied in rulers and made safe by education and the guardians' way of life. The *Laws* denies exactly that: no such mind exists, or hardly, and even a knower would not stay firm with irresponsible power (875b–d). Law is second best because the best is unavailable, not because it is wrong. Evidence bearing on the crux is about human beings, not about the good: whether unchecked rulers ever resist corruption, and whether a selection and education system can reliably produce knowers.

---

**P3** *(Exegetical.)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** the beast-tamer and ship of state (voters reward flatterers; the knower should steer); Book VIII (freedom without limit ends in tyranny).

**Must hit, strict (b), any two of:** the *Republic*'s qualification is knowledge of the good reached through a long education in philosophy, not professional credentials; guardians have no private property or families, so no private interest to serve (416d–417b); the best rulers are reluctant to rule (520d). **And the contradiction:** the *Laws* holds that no one, however knowledgeable, stays firm with unaccountable power (875b–d, 691c–d), so rulers must be subject to the law (715c–d) and audited (Book XII examiners); the memo's "not subject to review" is exactly what the *Laws* forbids.

**Wrong turns:** calling the memo Aristotelian; judging whether the board is a good idea, which is not asked.

**Model answer:** The memo borrows the *Republic*: voters as the beast whose tastes politicians study, the steering that belongs to the knower, and Book VIII's slide from unlimited freedom to the strongman. It drops two conditions the *Republic* treats as essential: rulers qualify by philosophical knowledge of the good, not credentials, and they live without property or families so that ruling cannot serve private interest. It contradicts the *Laws*, which Plato wrote to answer exactly this design: no human mind stays firm holding irresponsible power, so officials must be servants of the law and answer to scrutiny.
