# History of Political Thought · Lesson 3.1: Machiavelli's *Prince*: *virtù* and *fortuna*

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 3: Machiavelli and Hobbes: the state without a common good · Builds on: [2.4 Aquinas on kingship, tyranny and property](02-04-aquinas-on-kingship-tyranny-and-property.md) · Unlocks: [3.2 Machiavelli's *Discourses*: the republic of conflict](03-02-machiavelli-the-discourses.md)

## Why this matters

For about fifteen hundred years, advice to rulers said roughly one thing: be good, and your rule will be secure and glorious. Cicero said it, Aquinas said it, and the humanists of Machiavelli's own day were still saying it. *The Prince* (written 1513, printed 1532) takes the old genre's form and turns its central claim upside down. Nearly every later argument about dirty hands, raison d'état or "realism" borrows from this short book, usually without knowing what it has left out. The first job is to read what it actually says, and in its own vocabulary.

## The idea

Start with the author's situation. Machiavelli served the Florentine republic as a secretary and diplomat from 1498 to 1512, dealing with Cesare Borgia, the French king and the pope. In 1512 Spanish troops restored the Medici, the republic fell, and he was dismissed. Early in 1513 he was accused of a part in a plot against the Medici, imprisoned and tortured, then freed in the amnesty that followed the election of a Medici pope, Leo X. In a letter to his friend Francesco Vettori that December he describes exiled days in the countryside and evenings in his study in conversation with the ancients, and says he has written a little book *On Principalities* for a new prince. He dedicated it in the end to Lorenzo de' Medici. It is, among other things, a job application from an out-of-work expert.

Now the genre. A *mirror for princes* listed the virtues a ruler should have: justice, clemency, liberality, good faith. Cicero's *De Officiis* (44 BC) had set the tone: love is a far firmer guard of power than fear (Book II), and injustice by force, like a lion, or fraud, like a fox, is unworthy of a man (Book I). Aquinas's *On Kingship* (2.4) and Erasmus's *Education of a Christian Prince* (1516) kept the core: the good ruler and the successful ruler are the same man.

Machiavelli keeps the question ("what qualities should a prince have?") and changes the test. A quality is good for a prince if it helps him *hold his state* (*mantenere lo stato*) and win glory. Sometimes the classical virtues pass that test. Often they fail it: generosity bankrupts you, then forces you to tax, and you end up hated. So the prince needs *virtù*, an ability to do whatever the moment requires, including what the mirrors called vice. Against *virtù* stands *fortuna*, the flux of events no one controls. The whole book is a study of how much *virtù* can wrest from *fortuna*.

## Source

*The Prince*, chapter 15 (tr. W. K. Marriott, 1908, public domain). Machiavelli, admitting that many have written on how princes should behave, announces that he will depart from them:

> …how one lives is so far distant from how one ought to live, that he who neglects what is done for what ought to be done, sooner effects his ruin than his preservation…

Machiavelli's Italian here speaks of following the *verità effettuale della cosa*, the effectual truth of the matter, rather than imagined republics and principalities. The next sentence concludes that a prince who wants to keep his position must "know how to do wrong, and to make use of it or not according to necessity." Two cautions. First, Marriott flattens the Italian, *imparare a potere essere non buono*: literally "learn to be able to be not good." That is a *capacity* to be used or not, not a vow of wickedness. Second, the "imagined republics" jab probably targets Plato ([1.1](01-01-platos-republic-justice-in-city-and-soul.md)) and the whole mirror tradition. It is a claim about method, not a denial that "ought" means anything.

## The argument

**Chapter 15's argument, in Machiavelli's terms.**

1. **Useful counsel must follow the effectual truth, not imagined states.** *In words:* advice built on how people ought to behave fails whenever they don't.
2. **A prince lives among many who are not good.** *In words:* he acts in a competitive, treacherous world, not an ideal one.
3. **So a prince who does in all things what goodness requires will be ruined.** *In words:* unilateral goodness among bad actors is a losing strategy.
4. **Human conditions do not permit a prince to possess or practise all the praised qualities at once.** *In words:* even if he wanted every virtue, circumstances force trade-offs.
5. **The prince's end is to maintain his state.** *In words:* the test of a quality is whether it keeps him in power, secure and able to act.
6. **Therefore a prince must learn to be able not to be good, and use or not use this according to necessity.** He should avoid the vices that would lose him his state, avoid the others if he can, and not worry about the reproach of vices without which the state is hard to save. *In words:* some things that look like virtue ruin you, and some that look like vice bring security and prosperity.

**Chapter 8's rule for cruelty.** Cruelties are well used (*bene usate*), Machiavelli says, adding that this is so if one may speak well of evil, when they are (a) done at one stroke, (b) necessary for security, (c) not kept up afterwards, and (d) turned as far as possible to the subjects' advantage. Badly used cruelties begin small and multiply. So injuries are best done all at once and benefits handed out little by little. *In words:* judge cruelty by its economy, the least harm that settles things for good.

**Chapter 25's account of *fortuna*.** Fortune governs perhaps half of our actions and leaves the other half to us. She is like a river in flood that sweeps everything away. But in calm weather men can build dikes and channels, and the flood does harm where no *virtù* has prepared against it. Italy, undefended, is the flooded plain. Success comes from matching one's conduct to the times, and since character rarely changes, the impetuous usually do better than the cautious. He closes the chapter by picturing fortune as a woman who yields to the bold, an image of mastery by force that needs reading in its sixteenth-century setting, not softening. *In words:* fortune is powerful but not sovereign, and *virtù* is largely foresight.

Chapter 26 then turns the book into an exhortation: Italy, enslaved, leaderless and overrun by foreign armies, awaits a redeemer from the house of Medici. It ends with Petrarch's lines promising that *virtù contro a furore*, virtue against fury, will take up arms.

## Map of positions

How should a book like this be read? Five major readings, each with serious defenders:

| Reading | Is the advice meant sincerely? | What it makes of morality | Representative |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Teacher of evil** | Yes | Rejected: a deliberate break with Christian and classical ethics | Leo Strauss, *Thoughts on Machiavelli* (1958), who professed the "old-fashioned" view |
| **Republican warning** | No, or not only: it exposes princes to the people | Morality intact; tyranny laid bare | Rousseau, *Social Contract* III.6 (the book of republicans); Garrett Mattingly's 1958 essay on the book as satire |
| **Science of politics** | Yes, as technique | Set aside: how power works, value-neutral | A common modern reading, often traced to the idea of politics as autonomous from morals |
| **Two moralities** | Yes | Not amoral: pagan civic virtue chosen over Christian virtue, and the two cannot be combined | Isaiah Berlin, "The Originality of Machiavelli" (c. 1972) |
| **Humanist revolution** | Yes | *Virtù* redefined inside the mirror genre's own vocabulary | Quentin Skinner, *The Foundations of Modern Political Thought* (1978) and *Machiavelli* (1981) |

Which reading is right is a question of interpretation you can argue, not something this lesson settles. The *Discourses* ([3.2](03-02-machiavelli-the-discourses.md)) are the main evidence for the republican readings.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean): Borgia, Pistoia and the arithmetic of cruelty.** Chapter 17 compares two cases. Cesare Borgia had a reputation for cruelty, yet his harshness pacified and united the Romagna. Chapter 7 tells how he set a severe governor, Remirro de Orco, to restore order, then had him executed and displayed in the piazza at Cesena. Florence, anxious to avoid seeming cruel, let factional fighting destroy Pistoia. Run chapter 8's four tests. Borgia's act was a single stroke, necessary for security, not repeated, and it left the subjects with order. Florence's mercy let the killing multiply. So Borgia was, Machiavelli says, the more merciful of the two. Mercy here is measured by outcomes for the whole population, not by the ruler's intentions or reputation. That shift of measure, not a taste for blood, is what's new.

**Example 2 (hard): Agathocles.** Agathocles, a potter's son, made himself king of Syracuse by having soldiers kill the senate and the richest citizens at a signal. He then defended the city brilliantly against Carthage. By chapter 8's tests his cruelties were well used: one stroke, secure rule, no conspiracies. Yet Machiavelli writes:

> Yet it cannot be called talent to slay fellow-citizens, to deceive friends, to be without faith, without mercy, without religion; such methods may gain empire, but not glory.

"Talent" is Marriott's word for *virtù*. So the same chapter both denies Agathocles *virtù* and praises his courage and greatness of mind. If *virtù* were pure effectiveness, this denial would be incoherent. The readings split here. For Skinner, *virtù* includes what earns *glory*, and glory has a limit that bare power does not. For Strauss, the denial is a rhetorical veil the rest of the chapter lifts. Berlin's reader hears civic, not Christian, standards at work. The text alone shows that "effectiveness" is too thin a gloss on *virtù*. It does not show which richer gloss is right.

## Watch out

- **Anachronism trap: *virtù* is not "virtue".** In *The Prince* it names the qualities that let a man hold his state and win glory against *fortuna*: energy, boldness, foresight, flexibility. Marriott renders the one word as "talent" (ch. 8), "valour" (ch. 25) and "virtue" (ch. 26). Reading Christian or Aristotelian virtue into it misses the point. So does reading plain "skill", as Example 2 shows.
- **You might think "the end justifies the means" is Machiavelli's.** The slogan is not in the book, and neither is the vocabulary of justification. His terms are necessity, maintaining the state and glory. Whether necessity *justifies* is a question for ethics and [`political-philosophy`](../../political-philosophy/syllabus.md), not something to put in his mouth.
- **Feared is not hated.** Chapter 17 says to be feared *while avoiding hatred*, chiefly by keeping away from subjects' property and women. A reading that says "rule by terror" has dropped the constraint that does half the work.
- ***Lo stato* is not the modern state.** In *The Prince* it often means the prince's position and dominion, what he holds, more than an impersonal legal order. The abstract state comes later ([3.4](03-04-hobbes-the-sovereign-and-the-church.md)).

## One-liner

> Machiavelli keeps the mirror-for-princes question and changes the test: a quality is good for a prince if it holds his state and wins glory against *fortuna*, and *virtù* is the capacity to be good or not as necessity requires.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢) *(Exegetical.)*** From chapter 25 (Marriott), just after the comparison of fortune to a river in flood, and before the remark that Italy is a country without dikes:

> So it happens with fortune, who shows her power where valour has not prepared to resist her, and thither she turns her forces where she knows that barriers and defences have not been raised to constrain her.

(a) Three readers disagree. **R1:** fortune rules human affairs and preparation is futile. **R2:** a prince with enough *virtù* can abolish fortune's power entirely. **R3:** fortune's power is real, but how much damage it does depends on what human beings did beforehand. Which reading does the passage support? Point to the words that decide it. (b) "Valour" translates *virtù*. What does that rendering capture here, and what does it leave out? 150 words or fewer in total.

**P2 (🟡) *(Exegetical (a) · Exegetical (b).)*** (a) Reconstruct chapter 17's argument that it is safer for a prince to be feared than loved, in numbered premises (4–6) and a conclusion, in Machiavelli's terms. Include the constraint he places on fear, and flag the weakest premise. (b) In *On Kingship* (Book I, in the chapters comparing the fortunes of kings and tyrants), Aquinas argues that rule held by fear is unstable: fear breeds hatred, and subjects rise against a tyrant when they can. Rule grounded in the subjects' love is the firmest, and a king should seek his reward from God, not from human glory. Name the single premise on which Machiavelli and Aquinas really disagree about love and fear. Say which apparent disagreement is *not* the crux, and why. (a) as premises; (b) 150 words or fewer.

**P3 (🔴, optional) *(Exegetical.)*** Diagnose this invented passage from a business column:

> "A turnaround CEO's first hundred days decide everything. Make every cut in one announcement, never in slow drips, then hand back perks one by one so people keep feeling the upswing. Forget being liked. Goodwill is bought with bonuses and gone the week the bonuses stop. What lasts is people knowing that missed targets have consequences. Nice executives get eaten. Results are the only morality a company has."

Which chapters of *The Prince* is it borrowing, and which elements of each? Name two things it has dropped or distorted from the original. 150 words or fewer.

<details>
<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical, strict.)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** R3. The deciding words are conditional: fortune shows her power "where valour has not prepared to resist her", and turns her force where "barriers and defences have not been raised". Her power is real (against R2, the flood is not abolished, only channelled; the surrounding text gives her half of our actions). Its reach depends on prior human preparation (against R1). The dikes are built in fair weather, beforehand, so *virtù* here is largely foresight.
**Must hit, strict (b):** "valour" captures the active, spirited, martial side of *virtù*, which suits the image of resistance and Italy's lack of defence. It leaves out foresight, prudence and the capacity to adapt to the times, which this very passage needs, since dikes are built in calm weather. It also hides the link between this chapter and *virtù* elsewhere in the book (ch. 8's "talent", ch. 26's "virtue").
**Wrong turns:** choosing R2 from the book's bold ending, which is a different claim (boldness succeeds more often), not the abolition of fortune. Treating "valour" as simply wrong: it is a partial rendering, not a mistranslation.
**Model answer:** (a) R3. Fortune shows her power "where valour has not prepared" and strikes where barriers "have not been raised". Both clauses make her damage depend on what humans failed to do beforehand. She is not abolished (the flood still comes), and preparation is not futile. (b) "Valour" catches the courage and energy of resistance. It misses what the dike image actually requires: foresight in calm times and adaptability. It also severs the word from *virtù*'s other appearances in the book.

---

**P2** *(Exegetical, strict, both parts.)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** a reconstruction close to:
- P1. Being both loved and feared is best, but the two are hard to combine, so when one must be given up the question is which is safer.
- P2. Men in general are ungrateful, fickle, false, cowardly and covetous. They offer everything while danger is far off and turn when it comes.
- P3. Love rests on a bond of obligation, which men break whenever it serves their advantage.
- P4. Fear rests on dread of punishment, which does not fail.
- P5. Men love at their own will but fear at the will of the prince, and a wise prince relies on what is in his own control.
- P6 (constraint). Fear must not become hatred, which is avoided by keeping away from subjects' property and women and by killing only with manifest cause.
- ∴ It is safer to be feared (without being hated) than loved.
Weakest premise: P2 (the general claim about human nature) or P4 (that fear never fails, given that P6 concedes fear can curdle into hatred). Either is accepted with a reason.
**Must hit, strict (b):** the crux is P3, whether a bond of love or obligation is reliable enough to hold subjects when holding costs them. Aquinas must affirm it and Machiavelli denies it. *Not* the crux: whether hatred is dangerous. Machiavelli's own constraint (P6) concedes Aquinas's point that hated rulers fall, so the two agree on that.
**Wrong turns:** reconstructing without P6, which turns the argument into a case for terror. Naming the difference in ends (glory vs heavenly reward) as the crux of *this* dispute: it is a real difference, but the love/fear argument does not turn on it. It turns on what holds subjects.
**Model answer (b):** Both accept that fear which breeds hatred destroys a ruler. Machiavelli builds that concession into his advice. The disagreement is over love. Aquinas treats it as the firmest bond. Machiavelli holds that obligation is broken whenever advantage suggests, because men are base, while fear of punishment stays in the ruler's control. The premise one must deny is: *love reliably binds subjects in adversity.*

---

**P3** *(Exegetical, strict.)*

**Must hit, strict:** borrowings: ch. 8 (injuries all at once, benefits little by little); ch. 17 (fear over love, love as a bond that breaks when payments stop, since friendships bought with payments are not secured); ch. 15 in flattened form ("nice executives get eaten": the good man ruined among the not-good). Two losses or distortions from: (i) ch. 17's *avoid hatred* constraint is gone; (ii) the end: Machiavelli's test is maintaining the state and *glory*, and ch. 8 denies glory to success by any means (Agathocles), whereas "results are the only morality" erases that limit; (iii) "the only morality" turns a claim about necessity into a claim that success *is* morality, a vocabulary of justification the book doesn't use; (iv) the new prince's setting of mortal danger, arms and a disordered state is swapped for a firm with law, courts and exit.
**Wrong turns:** tracing "results are the only morality" to *The Prince* as a quotation. Crediting ch. 18: nothing here is about keeping faith.
**Model answer:** It borrows ch. 8's timing rule (all cuts at once, benefits by drips) and ch. 17's preference for fear over love, including the point that bought loyalty fails. "Nice executives get eaten" echoes ch. 15. It drops ch. 17's warning to avoid hatred, and it replaces maintaining the state and winning glory with "results". Machiavelli refused Agathocles glory despite his success, and never claimed that success *is* morality.

</details>

## Flashback

**From Lesson 2.4 (Aquinas on kingship, tyranny and property):** *(Exegetical.)* Find the crux. *De Regno* I argues that kingship is best: a community's first good is its unity, and what is itself one produces unity better than many. In *Politics* II.2 ([1.3](01-03-aristotle-the-city-exists-by-nature.md)), Aristotle faults Plato for making the city too much one. In III.11 ([1.4](01-04-aristotle-constitutions-citizens-and-the-polity.md)), he argues that the many, pooled, may judge better than the few. A reader concludes: "The crux is simple. Aquinas prizes unity, and Aristotle distrusts it." (a) Say why that is not the crux, using what Aquinas means by unity. (b) Name the premise of Aquinas's argument that Aristotle's III.11 actually presses on, and one premise both men accept. 150 words or fewer.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):** the two thinkers use "unity" in different senses. Aquinas's unity is *peace*, a community holding together instead of splitting into factions. Aristotle's target in II.2 is unity as *sameness*: Plato's city, where citizens share one set of feelings and the plurality of different kinds of people is erased. Aristotle wants a city at peace too, and Aquinas nowhere asks for a city of identical people. So the apparent disagreement dissolves once the term is split.
**Must hit, strict (b):** the premise pressed is *what is itself one produces unity better than a plurality* (A4, with its conclusion that one should rule). III.11 says a plurality, pooled, can judge better than one or a few, so many hands need not pull in different directions. The shared premise: rule is just when it serves the common good of the ruled and deviant when it serves the rulers (the six-regime grid, A2), or that humans are political or social by nature (A1).
**Wrong turns:** saying Aristotle rejects kingship outright; he allows it for a man of outstanding virtue (III.13–17). Naming "one or many rulers" as the crux, which is the conclusion in dispute, not a premise.
**Model answer:** (a) The two "unities" differ. Aquinas means peace, a community not torn by faction. Aristotle attacks unity as sameness, a city pressed toward being one person. Both want peace, and neither wants sameness. (b) The real pressure falls on Aquinas's premise that what is one produces unity better than many. III.11 claims that many judging together can do better than one, as a feast many contribute to beats one host's. Both accept the grid: rule is just when it serves the common good, and corrupt when it serves the rulers.

</details>
