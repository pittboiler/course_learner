# History of Political Thought · Lesson 6.5: Marx I: from Hegel to historical materialism

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 6: The nineteenth century: freedom, capital and democracy · Builds on: [6.1 Hegel: freedom realized in the state](06-01-hegel-freedom-realized-in-the-state.md), [6.4 Mill: representative government](06-04-mill-representative-government.md) · Unlocks: [6.6 Marx II: the state, revolution and the society after](06-06-marx-the-state-revolution-and-after.md)

## Why this matters

Hegel told the nineteenth century that freedom is realized in the modern state. Mill and Tocqueville worried the state might smother it. Marx made a stranger claim: the state is not where freedom lives at all, because the real life of society goes on underneath it, in how people produce. In about sixteen years (1843 to 1859) he moved from a line-by-line quarrel with Hegel's *Philosophy of Right* to a theory of all of history. Any modern argument that politics is "really about" economics, or that formal rights leave material unfreedom untouched, is borrowing a step from this sequence.

## The idea

Start where Marx started: as a young Hegelian, which meant a reader who believed Hegel had found the right questions and hidden the answers in the wrong place.

Hegel ([6.1](06-01-hegel-freedom-realized-in-the-state.md)) had split modern life in two. *Civil society* is the market world of needs, work and private interest; the *state* is where particular interests are taken up into a universal one. Marx accepted the split as an accurate description of modern society. What he denied was Hegel's claim that the state resolves it. His tool came from Ludwig Feuerbach, whose *Essence of Christianity* (1841) argued that God is humanity's own best qualities projected outward and then worshipped as something alien. Marx turned the same diagnosis on politics. The state, he said, is a heaven too: in it people are equal members of a community, while down on earth, in civil society, they remain isolated competitors. The community is real, but it has been moved to somewhere people do not actually live.

Then he asked what keeps that split in place. His answer, reached in Paris and Brussels between 1844 and 1846, was *labor*, and the relations people enter into to carry it out. Change those, and law, the state and ideas change with them. That is historical materialism in one breath: explain politics by production, not production by politics.

## Source

Marx, Preface to *A Contribution to the Critique of Political Economy* (1859), N. I. Stone translation (1904), public domain:

> The sum total of these relations of production constitutes the economic structure of society―the real foundation, on which rise legal and political superstructures and to which correspond definite forms of social consciousness.

Watch the two verbs. Law and politics "rise" on the foundation; consciousness "correspond[s]" to it. The sentence is an architectural metaphor, and it says less than its reputation: it places the state *on* the economy without yet saying how tightly one fixes the other. A few lines earlier in the same Preface Marx tells us where the idea came from: his critical revision of Hegel led him to conclude that legal relations and forms of state are rooted in the material conditions Hegel had summed up as civil society.

## The argument

**Stage 1: against Hegel (the 1843 *Critique* and "On the Jewish Question").**

1. **Hegel makes the Idea the subject and real institutions its predicates.** Family and civil society appear as stages the state generates for itself. *In words:* Hegel writes as if the state produces society, when society is what the state presupposes.
2. **Invert the relation, as Feuerbach inverted theology.** *In words:* start from people as they actually live, and treat the state as their product.
3. **Political emancipation is real but limited.** Bruno Bauer had argued (1843) that Jews and Christians alike must give up their religion to be politically free. Marx replied that a state can free *itself* from religion, as he said the North American states had, while its citizens remain religious: religion is simply moved out of the state into civil society as a private matter. He called that a great step forward. *In words:* a secular state with equal rights is progress, but it does not by itself change how people live.
4. **The rights of man are the rights of the member of civil society.** Liberty, property and security, as the French declarations define them, protect the individual *from* others. *In words:* each person is a citizen in the state and a private egoist in the market, and the rights guard the second.
5. **Human emancipation would end the split.** *In words:* freedom would mean living as members of a community in everyday working life, not only on election day.

In the Introduction to the *Critique* (published 1844) Marx calls religion "the opium of the people", in a passage that also calls it the expression of, and a protest against, real distress. The criticism of religion, he says, must become the criticism of the conditions that need it. The agent of that change will be the proletariat, a class with nothing particular to defend.

**Stage 2: alienated labor (the 1844 Manuscripts).**

6. **Humans are species-beings (*Gattungswesen*) who realize themselves by transforming the world through free, conscious, social production.** *In words:* work is not just a means of survival; it is how humans express what they are.
7. **Under wage labor this activity is alienated in four ways:** from the *product* (it belongs to another and confronts the worker as a power over him), from the *activity* (work is forced, a means to live rather than living), from *species-being* (the distinctively human capacity becomes a mere means), and from *other people* (who meet as buyer and seller of labor). *In words:* the market takes the one thing that should be most one's own and makes it a thing done for someone else.

**Stage 3: the materialist conception of history (*Theses on Feuerbach* 1845, *German Ideology* 1845–46, the 1859 Preface).**

8. **The old materialism only contemplated; practice is the point.** Thesis XI, in Austin Lewis's 1903 translation: "Philosophers have only interpreted the world differently, but the point is to change it." *In words:* ideas are not fixed by passively mirroring matter; people make their world by acting on it.
9. **People enter relations of production that correspond to a stage of the forces of production.** *In words:* how work is organized (lord and serf, owner and wage-worker) fits what tools, skills and techniques can do.
10. **Those relations are the foundation; law, the state and forms of consciousness rise on it.** Social existence determines consciousness, not the reverse. *In words:* to understand a legal system, look first at who owns what and who works for whom.
11. **When the forces outgrow the relations, the relations become fetters, and an epoch of social revolution begins.** *In words:* property rules that once fostered growth start to block it, and the superstructure is transformed along with them.
12. **No order passes before its productive forces are fully developed; bourgeois society is the last antagonistic form.** *In words:* history has a direction, and capitalism builds the conditions for its own successor.

∴ **C.** The state is not the realization of freedom but part of a superstructure resting on relations of production; human emancipation requires changing those relations. The *Manifesto* (1848) gives this a political voice: its opening line makes the history of every society so far a history of class struggles.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean: the four alienations).** A warehouse picker is paid per shift. An app routes her to shelves, sets a pick rate and flags her when she falls behind. Run premise 7. *Product:* the goods she moves belong to the firm; the faster she works, the more wealth accumulates that is not hers and that funds the system timing her. *Activity:* she did not choose the route, the pace or the method; Marx's test is whether one avoids the work as soon as no compulsion requires it. *Species-being:* planning, the most human part of the task, has been moved into the software, so the capacity that separates her from a machine is exactly what the job does not use. *Others:* her co-workers appear on the leaderboard as competitors, and her employer as the buyer of her time. Notice what the analysis does *not* need: low wages. For the 1844 Marx, a well-paid picker is still alienated, because the problem is the structure of the activity, not the size of the paycheck.

**Example 2 (hard: the legal-expression problem).** The Preface calls property relations merely "a legal expression" of relations of production. But property is a legal category, and law is supposed to be superstructure. If the foundation can only be described in the vocabulary of the superstructure, the metaphor looks circular. John Plamenatz pressed a version of this objection (*German Marxism and Russian Communism*, 1954, and later writing).

Readers answer in different ways. G. A. Cohen (*Karl Marx's Theory of History: A Defence*, 1978) redescribes relations of production as relations of *effective power* over labor and means of production, statable without legal terms, with law explaining itself by stabilizing those powers: a functional explanation. Engels, in a letter to Joseph Bloch (1890), had already insisted that the economic factor determines only "ultimately" and that superstructural elements act back on it. Other readers take the metaphor loosely, as a research priority rather than a causal law. The passage itself does not decide among them, and whether any of the readings survives the historical record is [`social-theory`](../../social-theory/syllabus.md)'s question.

## Watch out

- **You might read "materialism" as Hobbes's materialism ([3.3](03-03-hobbes-matter-motion-and-the-state-of-nature.md)).** Hobbes's is a metaphysics of matter in motion. Marx's is a claim about the priority of *material life*, meaning production, and the *Theses* attack older materialism for leaving out human activity.
- **Anachronism trap: the "young Marx versus mature Marx" debate is a twentieth-century construction.** It exists because the 1844 Manuscripts appeared in 1932. Louis Althusser (*For Marx*, 1965) posited an "epistemological break" around 1845; humanist readers such as Shlomo Avineri (1968) read one continuous project. Marx himself never framed his work this way.
- **"Opium" is not simply a sneer.** In the same passage religion is the sigh of the oppressed creature and a protest against real suffering, and opium was then a common painkiller. Marx still wants religion's illusory happiness abolished; both halves of the passage belong in a fair reading.
- **"On the Jewish Question" is mainly about the state.** Its first part is the argument on political emancipation above. Its second part identifies Judaism with commerce in terms taken from the period's stereotypes; scholars disagree over how to read that part, and the argument of Stage 1 does not depend on it.

## One-liner

> Marx kept Hegel's split between civil society and state, denied that the state heals it, and found the split's cause in how people produce.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢) *(Exegetical.)*** The *Manifesto* (Moore, 1888), directly after its opening sentence on class struggles:

> "Freeman and slave … in a word, oppressor and oppressed … carried on an uninterrupted, now hidden, now open fight, a fight that each time ended, either in a revolutionary re-constitution of society at large, or in the common ruin of the contending classes."

A reader says: "Here Marx and Engels claim that class struggle always ends in a higher form of society." Does the passage support that reading? Point to the words that decide it, and say what "in a word, oppressor and oppressed" tells you about how the passage defines a class. 120 words or fewer.

**P2 (🟡) *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)*** (a) Reconstruct the 1859 Preface's account of historical change in four to six numbered premises and a conclusion, in Marx's terms, and mark the premise at which the state first enters. (b) Does the argument, as the Preface states it, leave the state any independent causal power, or does it make politics a mere effect? Argue either way in 150 words or fewer, using the Preface's own wording.

**P3 (🔴, optional) *(Exegetical.)*** An invented campaign speech:

> "In the market we are rivals: employers and employees, buyers and sellers. But at the ballot box we are equals. Give every adult the same vote and the same rights, keep religion and wealth out of the constitution, and the state becomes the one place where we stand together as a single people, whatever divides us the rest of the week."

(a) Which of Marx's 1843 concepts names what the speech celebrates, and what would Marx say it achieves and leaves undone? (b) Hegel would agree the state unites what civil society divides. Name the single premise about the state and civil society that Hegel affirms and Marx denies. 150 words or fewer in total.

<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical, strict.)*

**Must hit, strict:**
- The reading is **not** supported. The fight "each time ended, either in a revolutionary re-constitution of society at large, **or** in the common ruin of the contending classes." The disjunction leaves open that a struggle destroys both sides without producing a higher order.
- "Re-constitution" itself says only that society is remade, not that it is improved.
- "In a word, oppressor and oppressed" defines classes relationally, by a relation of domination between pairs across epochs (freeman/slave, and so on), not by income level or occupation taken alone.

**Wrong turns:** citing the 1859 Preface's claim of progressive epochs as if it were in this passage (the task is what *these* words support); reading "class" as a modern income bracket.

**Model answer:** No. The passage gives two outcomes: struggle ended "either in a revolutionary re-constitution of society at large, or in the common ruin of the contending classes." The "or" means class struggle can end in shared destruction, so the text does not assert that it always produces a higher society; even "re-constitution" says remade, not improved. "In a word, oppressor and oppressed" collapses every listed pair into one relation, so a class is defined by its position in a relation of domination with another class, not by wealth or trade alone.

---

**P2** *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)*

**Must hit, strict (a):**
- Premises covering: (i) people enter relations of production independent of their will; (ii) these correspond to a stage of the material forces of production; (iii) their sum is the economic structure, the real foundation; (iv) legal and political superstructures rise on it and forms of consciousness correspond to it; (v) at a certain stage forces conflict with relations (property relations), which become fetters; ∴ social revolution, with the superstructure transformed as the foundation changes.
- The state enters at (iv), as part of the "legal and political superstructures."

**Must hit, any verdict (b):**
- Quote or cite the wording that bears on the question: the foundation "on which rise" superstructures; the mode of production "determines the general character" of political life; the superstructure "more or less rapidly transformed."
- Say what "general character" or "more or less rapidly" leaves open, or why it does not.
- A verdict, with the wording that decides it. Bringing in Engels's 1890 letter is fine if marked as Engels, not the Preface.

**Wrong turns:** placing the state among the forces of production; answering (b) from 6.6's *Eighteenth Brumaire* rather than the Preface's words.

**Model answer (b), one of several acceptable:** The Preface leaves the state some room but no independent motor. The mode of production "determines the general character" of political life: *general* character, which allows particular political forms and events to vary without being fixed by the economy. The superstructure is transformed "more or less rapidly," so politics can lag and have effects while it lags. But every causal arrow the Preface draws for *change* runs from the foundation: revolution begins when forces conflict with relations, and the superstructure follows. Nothing in the text lets the state start an epoch. Verdict: on the Preface alone, politics has influence over timing and form, not over direction. Whether Marx allowed more elsewhere is 6.6's question.

---

**P3** *(Exegetical, strict.)*

**Must hit, strict (a):**
- The concept: **political emancipation** ("On the Jewish Question"): a state freed from religion and property qualifications, with equal citizenship.
- What it achieves: Marx calls it a great step forward; it is real progress.
- What it leaves undone: it divides each person into the equal citizen of the state and the egoistic private individual of civil society; the community exists only in the political sphere, and the relations of the market ("the rest of the week") are untouched. Human emancipation would require community in everyday productive life.

**Must hit, strict (b):**
- The crux: *whether the state can genuinely realize a universal interest while civil society remains a sphere of private property and particular interest.* Hegel affirms: the state takes civil society's particular interests up into the universal and reconciles them. Marx denies: the state's universality is abstract, and its real content is fixed by civil society.

**Wrong turns:** saying Marx rejects equal rights or secular government as worthless; naming religion as the crux (both accept religion's removal from the state here); naming two premises.

**Model answer:** (a) The speech celebrates political emancipation. Marx would grant it real progress: a state that excludes religion and wealth from citizenship is freer than one that does not. But it frees the state, not people. Each person becomes a communal being at the ballot box and an isolated competitor in the market, and the speech openly leaves "the rest of the week" alone. Human emancipation would bring that community into working life. (b) Hegel holds that the state can actually reconcile particular and universal interest while civil society keeps its private property and competition. Marx denies it: so long as civil society is left intact, the state's universality is an illusion whose content civil society supplies.

## Flashback

**From Lesson 6.3 (Tocqueville II: tyranny of the majority and soft despotism):** *(Apply to a hard case: exegetical (a) · evaluative (b).)* A self-governing American county in the 1830s, thick with exactly the associations Tocqueville praises: church committees, a temperance society, a farmers' league. A local editor attacks the temperance cause. One association after another resolves to stop buying and advertising in his paper; old friends cross the street; within a year the paper folds. No law is passed and no official acts. (a) Which of Tocqueville's two dangers does the case fit, and which steps of his Vol. I, ch. 15 argument does it instantiate? (b) Tocqueville offers associations as a remedy for that danger (Vol. I, ch. 12, and Vol. II, Part 4, ch. 7). Does the case show the remedy reproducing the disease, or does his account have the resources to answer it? Any verdict. Three sentences or fewer per part.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):**
- The tyranny of the majority over opinion, not the tutelary power: there is no central administration, no mild guardian and no managing of anyone's affairs.
- The sanction is exclusion rather than punishment (step 6): the editor keeps life, property and legal rights and loses customers and company.
- The power runs through opinion carrying moral authority rather than through law (steps 2 and 4), and it leaves no shelter (step 5), since every body he might turn to shares the one opinion.

**Must hit, any verdict (b):**
- State why Tocqueville thinks associations help: in Vol. I, ch. 12 they let an outvoted minority organize, show its numbers, and set its moral authority against the majority's power; in Vol. II they stand in for the powerful persons of an aristocracy, who cannot be crushed quietly.
- Test that mechanism against the case: here the associations are the majority organized, not a minority's shelter.
- Reach a verdict either way, for instance: associations transmit whatever opinion already dominates, so the remedy fails where opinion is uniform; or the remedy was never any single association but a plurality of them, and the editor's recourse is to found a counter-association or reach a press beyond the county, so the case shows a missing counter-association, not a refuted remedy.

**Wrong turns:** filing the case under soft despotism because it concerns civic life; calling it legal persecution. In (b), assuming associations are good as such in Tocqueville; he calls unrestrained political association a dangerous expedient against a greater danger.

**Model answer:** (a) This is the tyranny of the majority over opinion. The editor keeps every legal right and is punished only by exclusion, applied by opinion with moral weight, and he has nowhere to appeal because every body around him shares that opinion. (b) The case strains the remedy but does not refute it. Tocqueville's associations protect by letting a minority organize and oppose its moral authority to the majority's power, and that works only when opinion is plural enough to form counter-associations. Where associations are simply the majority organized, they carry its tyranny rather than checking it, so his remedy presupposes some diversity it cannot itself create. A reader who stresses the free press and the wider Union as the editor's outlet can say the account survives.

</details>
