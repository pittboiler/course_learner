# History of Political Thought · Lesson 3.2: Machiavelli's *Discourses*: the republic of conflict

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 3: Machiavelli and Hobbes: the state without a common good · Builds on: [3.1 Machiavelli's *Prince*](03-01-machiavelli-the-prince.md), [2.1 Cicero: the commonwealth and natural law](02-01-cicero-the-commonwealth-and-natural-law.md) · Unlocks: [3.3 Hobbes I: matter, motion and the state of nature](03-03-hobbes-matter-motion-and-the-state-of-nature.md)

## Why this matters

The Roman republic was the model every humanist admired, and the thing they regretted about it was its quarrelling: nobles against plebs, shouting in the forum, secessions, strikes. Civic harmony was the ideal and Rome's tumults its disease. The *Discourses on Livy* turns that upside down. Rome was free, Machiavelli argues, *because* its two orders fought, and the fighting wrote the laws. Conflict channelled into institutions, not concord, is what keeps a people free. It also forces the question this lesson ends on: how can the author of *The Prince* be the same man?

## The idea

The book is a commentary on the first ten books of Livy's history of Rome, written in the years after Machiavelli's dismissal in 1512 and printed only in 1531, after his death. It is dedicated to two young friends, not to a prince.

Its starting point is medical. Every city, Machiavelli says, carries two *umori*, "humours": the great, who want to command and oppress, and the people, who want not to be commanded and oppressed (*Prince* ch. 9; *Discourses* I.5). You cannot cure a city of its humours any more than a body. A healthy body keeps them in balance and gives them a way out.

Everything turns on the way out. A republic that gives each humour an **ordinary** outlet, a tribune to protest to or a court in which to accuse a powerful man (I.7), turns their collision into law. A republic that blocks them gets **extraordinary** outlets: lynchings, private armies, a faction's strongman, foreign troops called in.

Rome is the proof. When the kings were expelled, the consuls kept the kingly element and the senate the aristocratic one (I.2). The popular element came later, when insolent nobles drove the plebs to fill the streets, shut their shops, leave the city and refuse to enlist. The concession wrung out of that was the tribunate (I.3–4). Sparta got its mixed constitution from one wise lawgiver, Lycurgus; Rome got hers by accident, out of conflict.

Then comes a choice (I.5–6). Sparta and Venice kept the guardianship of liberty with the nobles, stayed quiet, and lasted longer than Rome. But they stayed small. A republic that must grow needs a large, armed people, and a large armed people will not be quiet. Since human affairs never stand still and necessity may force expansion on a state that did not plan for it, Machiavelli follows Rome and accepts the tumults as the price of greatness.

Three supports keep the arrangement from rotting. **Citizen arms**, not mercenaries: men fight well only for their own cause (I.21, I.43; II.20; *Prince* chs. 12–13). **Religion**: Numa did more for Rome than Romulus, because Romans came to fear breaking an oath more than breaking a law (I.11). And **renewal**: every state decays unless it is periodically brought back to its beginnings (III.1).

## Source

*Discourses* I.4, Thomson's translation (1883). Machiavelli is answering those who say Rome succeeded despite its disorder:

> "while in every republic there are two conflicting factions, that of the people and that of the nobles, it is in this conflict that all laws favourable to freedom have their origin"

The chapter's other moves, paraphrased: Critics credit Rome's greatness to luck and good armies, but good armies presuppose good order. In the three centuries from the Tarquins to the Gracchi the tumults exiled few citizens and killed fewer still. Virtuous action comes from good upbringing, good upbringing from good laws, and good laws from the very tumults people condemn. And the rowdy methods themselves (shouting, closing shops, leaving the city) were simply Rome's customary way for a people to make its wishes known. Where the people's fears were unfounded, an honest speaker in an assembly could talk them out of it.

Read the quantifier. The claim is not that conflict sometimes helped. It is that *all* laws favourable to freedom *originate* in it.

## The argument

**The republican theory of the *Discourses*, in Machiavelli's terms.**

1. **Every city contains two humours:** the great desire to dominate, the people desire only not to be dominated (I.5; *Prince* ch. 9). *In words:* the division is permanent and comes from what each group wants, not from a failure of policy.
2. **Each simple form of government is unstable.** Monarchy slides into tyranny, aristocracy into oligarchy, popular government into licence, and the cycle turns. A form that mixes all three, each checking the others, lasts longer (I.2, taking over Polybius; see [2.1](02-01-cicero-the-commonwealth-and-natural-law.md)). *In words:* pure regimes rot, and a mixed one rots slowest.
3. **A mixed order holds only if each humour has an ordinary, lawful outlet.** Humours with no lawful vent find unlawful ones, and those ruin free states (I.7). *In words:* the question is never whether there is conflict, only which channel it runs through.
4. **Rome won its popular element through conflict,** so the conflict produced the laws that made it free (I.2–4). *In words:* Rome's liberty was not designed; it was fought into existence.
5. **Liberty is safest guarded by those whose desire is only not to be dominated,** and a people restrained by law judges better and holds its course more steadily than a prince restrained by law (I.5, I.58). As I.58 puts it, "the prince who can do what he pleases is a madman, and the people which can do as it pleases is never wise." *In words:* neither is trustworthy unbound, so bind both, and give the people the watchman's post.
6. **A republic that may have to expand needs a numerous armed people,** and cannot have that and quiet at once (I.6). *In words:* you can have Venice's calm or Rome's greatness, not both.
7. **All orders corrupt over time** and survive only if brought back to their beginnings, by institutions such as the tribunes and censors, by exemplary punishments or men, or by disaster (III.1), with religion binding citizens to their oaths (I.11). *In words:* a republic needs a built-in way to remember what it was.

**C.** A durable, great and free republic is not a harmony. It is managed conflict among an armed and religious citizenry, periodically renewed. *In words:* the good republic fights itself well.

## Map of positions

How do *The Prince* and the *Discourses* fit? Not one reading is crowned here.

| Reading | The relation | What it leans on |
|---|---|---|
| **Two teachings, a development** | *The Prince* was an occasional work of 1513; the republican book came after and shows a deepened commitment. Hans Baron argued for this dating in the 1950s, treating *Prince* ch. 2's reference to republics treated elsewhere as a later insertion (hedged: the dating is disputed). | Chronology; the change of tone |
| **One teaching, republican at the core** | *The Prince* exposes what princes do, for the benefit of peoples. Rousseau said as much (*Social Contract* III.6, paraphrased: in pretending to teach kings, Machiavelli taught peoples). | *Prince* ch. 9's praise of the people; the *Discourses*' contempt for tyranny (II.2) |
| **One theory, two situations** | Founding a state, or reforming a corrupt one, needs one man with absolute power (I.9, I.18); keeping a free state going needs many. I.58 says so itself: princes are better at founding, peoples at maintaining. | The *Discourses*' own cross-references |
| **One teaching, beyond morality** | Both books teach the same new doctrine, that politics is not governed by the moral virtues. Leo Strauss, *Thoughts on Machiavelli* (1958), reads them this way. | I.9's excuse of Romulus's fratricide; I.12 on supporting religion one thinks untrue |

Two contextual historians sit across these rows. J. G. A. Pocock, *The Machiavellian Moment* (1975), reads the *Discourses* as the republic confronting its own mortality in secular time. Quentin Skinner (*The Foundations of Modern Political Thought*, 1978; *Machiavelli*, 1981) places Machiavelli inside a Roman tradition of liberty while stressing his break with its praise of concord.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean: Coriolanus and the right to accuse, I.7).** A famine hits Rome, and the senate sends to Sicily for grain. Coriolanus, a noble who hates the new tribunate, urges the senate to withhold the grain until the plebs give up their tribunes. The people hear of it and would have killed him as he left the senate house. Instead the tribunes summon him to answer a formal charge.

Run premise 3. The popular humour is the same in both versions: fury at a man who would starve them. What differs is the channel. Through the tribunes' court, the anger lands on one man by public authority, within set limits, and the city is not disturbed. Through a mob it becomes private violence. The nobles would answer in kind, each side would look for a protector, and the state would begin to break. Machiavelli's lesson is that the outlet, not the anger, decides whether a tumult builds liberty or destroys it.

**Example 2 (hard: the Agrarian Law, I.37).** The same conflict also killed the republic. The agrarian laws capped landholdings and divided conquered land among the people. The Gracchi revived them, and Rome split. The magistrates could do nothing, so each side found a chief: the people Marius, the nobles Sulla, and later Caesar and Pompey. Caesar became, in Machiavelli's words, the first tyrant, and Rome was never free again. That looks like a straight counterexample to premise 4.

Machiavelli refuses to revise. Look at what he does instead. First, he reverses the arithmetic: the great are so ambitious that without constant popular pressure Rome would have fallen into servitude much *sooner*, and the land question took three hundred years to ruin it. Second, he explains why this conflict went wrong. Once the tribunes had secured the plebs, the plebs went on to demand honours and wealth. The nobles gave way on honours but fought to the end over property, and the Gracchi, whose motives he praises more than their methods, pressed a law with harsh retroactive force. The conflict left ordinary channels, and premise 3 was violated.

Here the theory strains. Whether conflict runs through ordinary channels depends on what is at stake (land more than office), on how the law is drafted, and on how corrupt the citizens have become (I.17–18). None of that follows from the theory of the humours. A critic says the theory has become unfalsifiable: good outcomes are credited to conflict and bad ones to its mischannelling. A defender replies that premise 3 was there from the start, and that I.37 applies it rather than rescuing the theory. The case settles which premise carries the weight, not who is right.

## Watch out

- **Anachronism trap: *libertà* is not a set of liberal rights.** Machiavelli's *vivere libero* means a city that governs itself, independent of foreign masters and of any one citizen's will, where people's lives and property are secure under law. It is not a zone of individual non-interference. The modern analytic descendant, freedom as non-domination, is [`political-philosophy`](../../political-philosophy/syllabus.md) 3.1's.
- **Anachronism trap: the humours are not Marx's classes.** Nobles and people are defined by a desire to dominate or to escape domination, described in Galenic medical language, and the conflict is meant to be *balanced*, not won. Reading I.4 as class struggle driving history imports [6.5](06-05-marx-from-hegel-to-historical-materialism.md).
- **You might think Machiavelli simply praises tumult.** He praises the tumults of an uncorrupted Rome *for their results*, and he condemns extraordinary methods (I.7, I.37). A city whose conflict has left the ordinary channels is on its way to a tyrant.
- **You might think the religion chapters make him a pagan enemy of Christianity, or a pious reformer.** Both have readers. I.12 tells rulers to foster beliefs even if they think them untrue, and II.2 says modern religious education teaches men to be "brave to suffer rather than to do". But II.2 blames the *interpretation* of Christianity that made it serve idleness, not the religion itself. And "civil religion" is Rousseau's term ([4.4](04-04-rousseau-the-social-contract.md)), not Machiavelli's.

## One-liner

> Every city has two humours that cannot be cured; a free republic gives them lawful vents and lets their collision write its laws, so Rome was free not in spite of its tumults but because of them.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢) *(Exegetical.)*** In *Discourses* I.5 Machiavelli asks whether the nobles or the commons should be entrusted with guarding liberty. One sentence reads ("the former" = the nobles, "the latter" = the commons):

> "we shall find in the former a great desire to dominate, in the latter merely a desire not to be dominated over, and hence a greater attachment to freedom, since they have less to gain than the others by destroying it."

(a) Is the ground offered here a claim that commoners are morally better people than nobles, or something else? Name the words that decide it. (b) Is this sentence Machiavelli's settled verdict on the question? Say what I.5–I.6 conclude and what the conclusion turns on. 100 words or fewer in total.

**P2 (🟡) *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)*** (a) Using the Source section, reconstruct *Discourses* I.4's argument that the dissensions between senate and people made Rome free, in four to six numbered premises and a conclusion, in Machiavelli's terms. Include his reply to the objection that Rome's popular behaviour was disorderly. (b) Flag the weakest premise and say what historical evidence would strengthen or undermine it. 150 words or fewer for (b).

**P3 (🔴, optional) *(Exegetical.)*** *The Prince* ch. 9 and *Discourses* I.58 both describe the people as wanting only not to be oppressed. The *Prince* treats a friendly people as the securest foundation for a prince's power; the *Discourses* argues that a people bound by law is wiser and more constant than a prince. Find the crux: the single premise about the people that one book affirms and the other does not. Then say how a "one theory, two situations" reader would deny that the books contradict each other, naming the text they rely on. 150 words or fewer.

<details>
<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical, strict.)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** the ground is structural, not moral. It is about what each group *wants* and what it *stands to gain*. The deciding words are "desire to dominate" against "merely a desire not to be dominated", and "since they have less to gain … by destroying it". The people are the safer guardians because their interest does not run against liberty, not because they are better people. "Merely" marks their desire as purely negative: they do not want to rule, only not to be ruled.
**Must hit, strict (b):** no. The sentence sets out the case *for* the Roman arrangement. I.5 gives the case for Sparta and Venice too, and notes that by results those noble-guarded republics lasted longer. The conclusion is conditional: a republic set on expanding must do as Rome did, while one content to preserve itself may follow Sparta and Venice. I.6 then sides with Rome, because necessity may force growth on any state.
**Wrong turns:** reading "attachment to freedom" as praise of popular virtue; treating the sentence as Machiavelli's flat verdict for popular guardianship.
**Model answer:** (a) Structural: the commons' desire is "merely" not to be dominated, so they have "less to gain" by destroying liberty. The argument rests on interest, not moral worth. (b) No. This is the case for Rome's side. I.5 also weighs Sparta and Venice, which lasted longer, and concludes conditionally: expansionist republics must copy Rome, self-preserving ones may copy Sparta and Venice. I.6 prefers Rome because necessity can force expansion.

---

**P2** *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** a reconstruction that includes, in some order: every republic has two factions, people and nobles; laws favourable to freedom originate in their conflict; the tumults did little harm (three centuries, few exiles, little bloodshed); virtue comes from education, education from good laws, good laws from the tumults; the rowdy methods were Rome's ordinary way for the people to voice demands, and unfounded fears could be corrected by a trusted speaker; **C:** the dissensions made Rome free (and are not a defect it overcame by luck and arms). The reply to the disorder objection must appear as a premise.
**Must hit, any verdict (b):** name one premise precisely. Say why it is weakest: likely candidates are the causal chain (tumults → laws, rather than laws despite tumults) or the low-harm claim, which is dated to end at the Gracchi, where the harm began. Name evidence that bears on it, for example whether comparable reforms came without conflict, or whether the low-harm count holds.
**Wrong turns:** premises in modern terms ("class struggle", "checks and balances"); making (b) a verdict on whether conflict is good, rather than a test of one premise; ignoring I.37 when choosing the low-harm premise.
**Model answer (b), one of several:** The weakest premise is that the good laws *originated* in the tumults. It is a causal claim, and I.4 supports it mainly with a correlation: Rome had tumults and good laws. To strengthen it, show that specific liberty-protecting institutions, like the tribunate, were conceded only under popular pressure and would not have come otherwise. To undermine it, find republics that got comparable laws through negotiation or a founder, or Roman laws passed in calm periods. The low-harm premise is also exposed: the count stops at the Gracchi, exactly where I.37 admits the conflict turned deadly.

---

**P3** *(Exegetical, strict on the crux; either statement below passes.)*

**Must hit, strict:**
- The crux: *whether the people's judgment, not only their desire, is reliable enough for them to share in rule.* The *Discourses* affirms it (I.58: better at choosing magistrates, more constant, errors curable by words). The *Prince* uses only the people's desire and numbers, as a base of power the prince manages; it neither affirms nor needs their judgment.
- The shared ground must be noted: both books hold that the people desire only not to be oppressed, so that is not the crux.
- The one-theory reply: the books address different situations. Founding or reforming, especially a corrupt city, needs one man with absolute authority (I.9, I.18), while maintaining a free state is where peoples excel. I.58 itself says princes surpass peoples in founding, and peoples surpass princes in maintaining.

**Wrong turns:** naming "whether the people are good" (both books use desire, not goodness); naming the lawfulness of cruelty, which is not about the people; citing Baron's dating as the reconciliation (that is the two-teachings view).
**Model answer:** Both books agree the people want only not to be oppressed. They part on whether the people's *judgment* can be trusted with a share in rule. The *Discourses* says yes: a law-bound people chooses magistrates better and holds its course more steadily than a prince (I.58). *The Prince* uses the people only as the prince's securest base and never relies on their judgment. A one-theory reader denies a contradiction. Founding or reforming a state needs one man's absolute authority (I.9, I.18), while preserving it needs the many, and I.58 says exactly this.

## Flashback

**From Lesson 2.5 (Pope, emperor and council: Marsilius and conciliarism):** *(Exegetical.)* Four invented theses posted for a fifteenth-century university disputation:

> (i) The emperor's sword comes to him from God directly; the pope anoints him but does not hand it to him.
> (ii) The bishops gathered from every nation stand for all the faithful, and even Peter's successor must accept their sentence ending this division.
> (iii) The assembly of citizens may make heresy a crime punishable by exile; the bishop may only preach against it.
> (iv) The whole body of citizens elects its ruler, and the ruler must carry out whatever penalties the bishop pronounces on heretics.

(a) Place (i)–(iii) on the lesson's map, naming the position each expresses (Gelasian dualism, hierocracy, conciliarism, or Marsilius). One line each. (b) Thesis (iv) fits no single cell. Say which two answers it combines, and which of Marsilius's premises its second clause denies. Three sentences or fewer.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):**
- (i) **Dualism** (Gelasius, John of Paris): two powers, each from God; authority descends, but through two channels, and the temporal sword is not delegated by the priest.
- (ii) **Conciliarism:** Church government, authority ascending. A council represents the whole Church and binds the pope in ending schism (*Haec Sancta*). A council of bishops alone, with no role for a lay legislator, marks it as mainstream conciliar rather than Marsilian.
- (iii) **Marsilius:** coercive temporal rule ascends from the human legislator; the clergy teach and warn but compel no one. Heresy is punishable in this life only if human law makes it a crime.

**Must hit, strict (b):** (iv) puts the *origin* of temporal rule in the community (the ascending answer) but gives the clergy the *direction* of coercion (the hierocratic answer: temporal power used at the priest's direction). The two questions, where rule comes from and who holds coercive jurisdiction, are separable, which is why the grid cannot place it. Its second clause denies Marsilius's premise that the clergy hold no coercive jurisdiction, and with it his account of law, since a penalty in this life needs a coercive law made by the human legislator, not a bishop's sentence.

**Wrong turns:** calling (ii) Marsilian because it puts a council above the pope, when Marsilius's council includes laity and is convoked by the secular legislator; calling (i) hierocratic because the pope anoints, when the thesis denies that the sword passes through him; reading (iv) as Marsilian because the citizens elect the ruler.

**Model answer:** (a) (i) Dualism: two powers from God, the emperor's not received from the pope. (ii) Conciliarism: a council representing the whole Church binds the pope in ending schism. (iii) Marsilius: the civil legislator makes coercive law, and the priest only teaches. (b) Thesis (iv) takes the ascending answer to where rule comes from and the hierocratic answer to who directs coercion. Its second clause denies Marsilius's claim that priests have no coercive jurisdiction, and his definition of law as a coercive command that only the human legislator can make.

</details>
