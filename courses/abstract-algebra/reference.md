# Abstract Algebra · Reference Card

> Open book. This card is meant to be open while you work — including during
> quizzes. Nothing here is something you're expected to recall cold.

Abstract algebra is one move repeated: write down the axioms a familiar
operation obeys, then study every object that obeys them. Groups (one
operation) come first, rings and fields (two) second — and the machinery that
links them, **homomorphisms → kernels → quotients → isomorphism theorems**, is
built once in Module 2 and reused verbatim in Modules 3 and 4. Mid-problem, the
three things you'll reach for most are the [structure ladder](#the-structure-ladder)
(what each axiom set adds), the [standard cast](#the-standard-cast-of-groups)
(which example is the counterexample you need), and the
[isomorphism theorems](#the-isomorphism-theorems) stated exactly.

## Definitions

### Group

A set with one operation that can always be done and always be undone.

$$\text{(G1) } ab \in G,\quad \text{(G2) } (ab)c = a(bc),\quad \text{(G3) } \exists e:\ ea=ae=a,\quad \text{(G4) } \exists a^{-1}:\ aa^{-1}=a^{-1}a=e$$

Closure, associativity, identity, inverses. Commutativity is **not** required.

*Introduced:* [1.1](lessons/01-01-group-axioms-first-examples.md)

### Abelian

The bonus axiom: $ab = ba$ for all $a,b$. The default is *non*abelian; abelian is
a property you earn by checking, and a Cayley table is abelian exactly when it's
symmetric across the main diagonal.

*Introduced:* [1.1](lessons/01-01-group-axioms-first-examples.md)

### Order of an element

How many steps until $g$ marches back to the identity — and also the size of the
cyclic subgroup it generates.

$$\operatorname{ord}(g) = \min\{\,n > 0 : g^n = e\,\} = \lvert\langle g\rangle\rvert \qquad (\infty \text{ if no such } n)$$

*Introduced:* [1.2](lessons/01-02-cyclic-groups-order.md)

### Cyclic group

A group that is one element's powers, marching around a clock.

$$G = \langle g\rangle = \{\,g^k : k \in \mathbb{Z}\,\}$$

**Classification:** every cyclic group is $\cong (\mathbb{Z},+)$ (infinite) or
$\cong (\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z},+)$ (order $n$) — one per size, no others.

*Introduced:* [1.2](lessons/01-02-cyclic-groups-order.md)

### Generator

An element whose powers sweep out the whole group. In $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$
the generators are exactly the $k$ with $\gcd(k,n)=1$ — a step size generates the
whole clock iff it shares no factor with $n$ — so there are $\varphi(n)$ of them.

*Introduced:* [1.2](lessons/01-02-cyclic-groups-order.md)

### Dihedral group

The rigid symmetries of a regular $n$-gon: $n$ rotations and $n$ flips, glued by
one relation saying a flip reverses a rotation.

$$D_n = \{e, r, \dots, r^{n-1},\ s, rs, \dots, r^{n-1}s\},\qquad r^n = s^2 = e,\qquad srs = r^{-1}$$

Equivalently $sr^k = r^{-k}s$: push every $s$ to the right to reach normal form.

*Introduced:* [1.3](lessons/01-03-dihedral-symmetric-groups.md)

### Symmetric group

All bijections of $\{1,\dots,n\}$ under composition — every shuffle of a list.
$\lvert S_n\rvert = n!$. **Convention (this course): compose right-to-left, like
functions** — in $\sigma\tau$, apply $\tau$ first.

*Introduced:* [1.3](lessons/01-03-dihedral-symmetric-groups.md)

### Cycle notation

Follow where each element goes until you loop back, then start a new cycle with
an untouched element. Disjoint cycles commute, and the decomposition is unique up
to reordering.

*Introduced:* [1.3](lessons/01-03-dihedral-symmetric-groups.md)

### Sign of a permutation

Whether it takes an even or odd number of swaps — a well-defined invariant even
though the number of swaps isn't.

$$\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma) = (-1)^{\#\text{transpositions}}, \qquad \operatorname{sgn}(\sigma\tau) = \operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)\operatorname{sgn}(\tau)$$

A $k$-cycle has sign $(-1)^{k-1}$.

*Introduced:* [1.3](lessons/01-03-dihedral-symmetric-groups.md)

### Alternating group

The even permutations — the kernel of $\operatorname{sgn}$, hence normal, of index
$2$ and order $n!/2$.

*Introduced:* [1.3](lessons/01-03-dihedral-symmetric-groups.md)

### Subgroup

A subset that is already a group under the operation it inherited — you can't
multiply or invert your way out of it. Associativity and the identity come free
from the parent.

$$\textbf{one-step test:}\quad H \neq \varnothing \ \text{ and }\ a,b\in H \Rightarrow ab^{-1}\in H$$

**Finite shortcut:** a finite nonempty subset closed under the product is
automatically a subgroup — closure alone forces inverses.

*Introduced:* [1.4](lessons/01-04-subgroups.md)

### Center

The elements that commute with absolutely everything — a measure of how far $G$ is
from abelian.

$$Z(G) = \{\,g \in G : gx = xg \ \text{for all}\ x \in G\,\}$$

Always a subgroup, always normal. $Z(G) = G$ iff $G$ is abelian.

*Introduced:* [1.4](lessons/01-04-subgroups.md)

### Coset

A subgroup shoved over by a fixed element — a perfect translate of $H$, same size,
new location.

$$gH = \{gh : h\in H\}, \qquad g_1H = g_2H \iff g_1^{-1}g_2 \in H$$

Distinct cosets are **disjoint** and together tile $G$. Only $eH = H$ contains the
identity, so no other coset is a subgroup.

*Introduced:* [1.5](lessons/01-05-cosets-lagrange.md)

### Index

The number of distinct cosets of $H$ in $G$, written $[G:H]$ — the number of tiles
needed to cover the group.

*Introduced:* [1.5](lessons/01-05-cosets-lagrange.md)

### Homomorphism

A map that respects multiplication: multiply-then-map equals map-then-multiply.
Nothing else is promised — most useful homomorphisms are deliberately lossy.

$$\varphi(ab) = \varphi(a)\varphi(b)$$

**Free consequences:** $\varphi(e_G) = e_H$ and $\varphi(g^{-1}) = \varphi(g)^{-1}$
— theorems, not hypotheses.

*Introduced:* [2.1](lessons/02-01-homomorphisms-kernels-images.md)

### Isomorphism

A homomorphism that is also a bijection. If one exists, $G \cong H$: relabel the
elements and the multiplication tables match exactly.

*Introduced:* [2.1](lessons/02-01-homomorphisms-kernels-images.md)

### Kernel

Exactly the debris of what got crushed — everything the map sends to the identity.

$$\ker\varphi = \{\,g \in G : \varphi(g) = e_H\,\} \ \trianglelefteq\ G, \qquad \varphi \text{ injective} \iff \ker\varphi = \{e_G\}$$

The fibers of $\varphi$ *are* the cosets of $\ker\varphi$.

*Introduced:* [2.1](lessons/02-01-homomorphisms-kernels-images.md)

### Normal subgroup

A subgroup closed under the group's internal relabelings — and precisely the kind
you can quotient by.

$$N \trianglelefteq G \iff gNg^{-1} = N \ \forall g \iff gN = Ng \ \forall g \iff N = \ker(\text{some homomorphism})$$

*Introduced:* [2.2](lessons/02-02-normal-subgroups-quotients.md)

### Quotient group

Stop distinguishing elements that differ by $N$; the cosets themselves become the
elements of a new group.

$$G/N = \{aN\},\qquad (aN)(bN) = (ab)N,\qquad \lvert G/N\rvert = [G:N] = \frac{\lvert G\rvert}{\lvert N\rvert}$$

Well-defined **if and only if** $N$ is normal. No normality, no $G/N$.

*Introduced:* [2.2](lessons/02-02-normal-subgroups-quotients.md)

### Group action

Each group element becomes a way of shuffling a set $X$, consistently with the
group law.

$$e\cdot x = x, \qquad (gh)\cdot x = g\cdot(h\cdot x)$$

Equivalently — and this is the useful phrasing — an action **is** a homomorphism
$\varphi: G \to \mathrm{Sym}(X)$, its **permutation representation**.

*Introduced:* [2.4](lessons/02-04-group-actions.md)

### Faithful action

Only the identity acts as the do-nothing shuffle: $\ker\varphi = \{e\}$, so
$G \hookrightarrow \mathrm{Sym}(X)$ — the group literally *is* a permutation group.

*Introduced:* [2.4](lessons/02-04-group-actions.md)

### Transitive action

You can get from any point to any other: for all $x,y$ there's a $g$ with
$g\cdot x = y$. Equivalently, there is exactly one orbit. Independent of
faithfulness — they measure different things.

*Introduced:* [2.4](lessons/02-04-group-actions.md)

### Orbit

Everywhere a point can be sent, $\mathrm{Orb}(x) = \{g\cdot x : g\in G\}$. Orbits
partition $X$: "reachable from" is an equivalence relation.

*Introduced:* [2.5](lessons/02-05-orbits-stabilizers-conjugacy.md)

### Stabilizer

Everything that leaves $x$ untouched, $\mathrm{Stab}(x) = \{g : g\cdot x = x\}$.
Always a subgroup of $G$.

*Introduced:* [2.5](lessons/02-05-orbits-stabilizers-conjugacy.md)

### Conjugacy class

The orbit of $x$ under $G$ acting on itself by conjugation — elements that are "the
same up to a change of coordinates inside the group."

$$\mathrm{Cl}(x) = \{gxg^{-1} : g\in G\}, \qquad \mathrm{Cl}(x) = \{x\} \iff x \in Z(G)$$

*Introduced:* [2.5](lessons/02-05-orbits-stabilizers-conjugacy.md)

### Centralizer

What commutes with the *one* element $x$ — the stabilizer for the conjugation
action. The center is the intersection of all of them.

$$C_G(x) = \{\,g : gx = xg\,\}, \qquad Z(G) = \bigcap_{x} C_G(x)$$

*Introduced:* [2.5](lessons/02-05-orbits-stabilizers-conjugacy.md)

### Ring

An abelian group that learned to multiply. Distributivity is the *only* bridge
between the two operations.

$$(R,+) \text{ abelian group};\quad \times \text{ associative};\quad a(b+c) = ab+ac,\ (a+b)c = ac+bc$$

This course assumes **unital** ($1$ exists) and flags commutative vs. not.

*Introduced:* [3.1](lessons/03-01-rings-ring-homomorphisms.md)

### Unit

An element with a multiplicative inverse. The units form a group $R^\times$ — the
part of the ring where division works — even when $R$ itself is wild.

*Introduced:* [3.1](lessons/03-01-rings-ring-homomorphisms.md)

### Ring homomorphism

A structure-preserving map that honours *all three* pieces of data:

$$\varphi(a+b) = \varphi(a)+\varphi(b),\qquad \varphi(ab) = \varphi(a)\varphi(b),\qquad \varphi(1_R) = 1_S$$

*Introduced:* [3.1](lessons/03-01-rings-ring-homomorphisms.md)

### Zero divisor

A **nonzero** element that can multiply another nonzero element down to zero
($ab = 0$ with $a,b \neq 0$). By convention $0$ itself is never called a zero
divisor.

*Introduced:* [3.2](lessons/03-02-integral-domains-fields.md)

### Integral domain

A commutative unital ring ($1 \neq 0$) with no zero divisors — so a product of
nonzero things is nonzero, and you may cancel.

$$ab = 0 \Rightarrow a=0 \text{ or } b=0 \iff \big(a\neq 0 \text{ and } ab=ac \Rightarrow b=c\big)$$

"No zero divisors" and "cancellation" are the same axiom, not two facts.

*Introduced:* [3.2](lessons/03-02-integral-domains-fields.md)

### Field

A domain where you can also divide: every nonzero element is a unit.

$$\text{commutative},\ 1\neq 0,\ \text{and } \forall a\neq 0\ \exists a^{-1} \text{ with } aa^{-1}=1$$

Every field is a domain. The converse fails ($\mathbb{Z}$) — **except in the finite
case**, where a domain is forced to be a field.

*Introduced:* [3.2](lessons/03-02-integral-domains-fields.md)

### Field of fractions

The smallest field a domain embeds in, built as formal fractions:
$\tfrac{a}{b} = \tfrac{c}{d} \iff ad = bc$ with $b,d \neq 0$. This is
$\mathbb{Z} \hookrightarrow \mathbb{Q}$ done abstractly, and it **requires** the
no-zero-divisor law (that's the step that makes the relation transitive).

*Introduced:* [3.2](lessons/03-02-integral-domains-fields.md)

### Ideal

A chunk that swallows every product with the ring — the ring-world normal
subgroup. A subring is closed under its *own* products; an ideal absorbs
*outsiders* too.

$$I \text{ an additive subgroup, and } r\in R,\ x\in I \Rightarrow rx,\ xr \in I$$

*Introduced:* [3.3](lessons/03-03-ideals-quotient-rings.md)

### Principal ideal

All the multiples of a single element, $(a) = aR = \{ar : r\in R\}$. Both
$\mathbb{Z}$ and $F[x]$ have *only* principal ideals; $\mathbb{Z}[x]$ does not
($(2,x)$ needs two generators).

*Introduced:* [3.3](lessons/03-03-ideals-quotient-rings.md)

### Quotient ring

Cosets $a+I$ with $(a+I)+(b+I) = (a+b)+I$ and $(a+I)(b+I) = ab+I$. The product is
well-defined **iff** $I$ absorbs multiplication — exactly as normality was forced
for $G/N$. Its zero element is the coset $I$ itself.

*Introduced:* [3.3](lessons/03-03-ideals-quotient-rings.md)

### Prime ideal

An ideal whose quotient is a domain: $R/P$ has no zero divisors. Equivalently
$ab \in P \Rightarrow a\in P$ or $b\in P$.

*Introduced:* [3.3](lessons/03-03-ideals-quotient-rings.md)

### Maximal ideal

An ideal whose quotient is a **field** — equivalently, no ideal sits strictly
between $M$ and $R$. Every maximal ideal is prime; the converse fails
($(x) \trianglelefteq \mathbb{Z}[x]$).

*Introduced:* [3.3](lessons/03-03-ideals-quotient-rings.md)

### Irreducible polynomial

A non-constant $p \in F[x]$ that can't be written $p = gh$ with both factors of
degree $\geq 1$ — the "prime" of $F[x]$. **Irreducibility is always relative to a
named field**: $x^2 - 2$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$, reducible over
$\mathbb{R}$.

*Introduced:* [3.4](lessons/03-04-polynomial-rings.md)

### Characteristic

How many copies of $1$ you must add to reach $0$; $0$ if it never happens.

$$\operatorname{char}(R) = \min\{\,n>0 : \underbrace{1+\cdots+1}_{n} = 0\,\}$$

For a field or domain it is $0$ or a **prime** — never $6$, because composite
would manufacture zero divisors.

*Introduced:* [3.5](lessons/03-05-characteristic-prime-fields.md)

### Prime subfield

What $1$ alone generates: the unique smallest subfield. It is $\cong \mathbb{Q}$
in characteristic $0$ and $\cong \mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ in characteristic $p$.
Every field has one of exactly two ancestors.

*Introduced:* [3.5](lessons/03-05-characteristic-prime-fields.md)

### Frobenius map

In characteristic $p$, raising to the $p$-th power is a *ring homomorphism* —
and an automorphism on a finite field.

$$\mathrm{Frob}(x) = x^p, \qquad (a+b)^p = a^p + b^p \ \text{(the "freshman's dream", true here)}$$

*Introduced:* [3.5](lessons/03-05-characteristic-prime-fields.md)

### Field extension

$K/F$ just names the pair "$K$ is a bigger field containing $F$." The slash is
**not** a quotient. Forget that you can multiply two elements of $K$ and it is a
vector space over $F$.

*Introduced:* [4.1](lessons/04-01-field-extensions-degree.md)

### Degree of an extension

The size of the gap between $F$ and $K$, as a single integer:

$$[K:F] = \dim_F K$$

Measures dimension, not element count — $[\mathbb{C}:\mathbb{R}] = 2$.

*Introduced:* [4.1](lessons/04-01-field-extensions-degree.md)

### Algebraic and transcendental

$\alpha$ is **algebraic over $F$** if some nonzero $f \in F[x]$ has
$f(\alpha) = 0$; otherwise **transcendental** ($\pi$, $e$ over $\mathbb{Q}$).

*Introduced:* [4.2](lessons/04-02-adjoining-roots-algebraic-elements.md)

### Minimal polynomial

Among all polynomials in $F[x]$ killing $\alpha$, the monic one of smallest
degree. It is automatically **irreducible**, and it **divides** every polynomial
that vanishes at $\alpha$. It's the generator of the kernel of "plug in $\alpha$."

*Introduced:* [4.2](lessons/04-02-adjoining-roots-algebraic-elements.md)

### Primitive element (finite fields)

A generator of the cyclic group $\mathbb{F}_{p^n}^{*}$ — one lucky element whose
powers hit every nonzero element exactly once before returning to $1$.

*Introduced:* [4.3](lessons/04-03-finite-fields.md)

### Field automorphism

A bijection $\sigma: K \to K$ preserving both operations —
$\sigma(x+y) = \sigma(x)+\sigma(y)$ and $\sigma(xy) = \sigma(x)\sigma(y)$. A
symmetry of a field.

*Introduced:* [4.4](lessons/04-04-galois-automorphisms-taste.md)

### Galois group

The automorphisms of $K$ that fix every element of $F$ (pointwise, not merely
setwise), a group under composition.

$$\mathrm{Gal}(K/F) = \{\,\sigma \in \operatorname{Aut}(K) : \sigma(a) = a \ \forall a \in F\,\}$$

*Introduced:* [4.4](lessons/04-04-galois-automorphisms-taste.md)

### Fixed field

Freeze a subgroup $H \leq \mathrm{Gal}(K/F)$; the elements no symmetry in $H$
disturbs form a field between $F$ and $K$.

$$K^{H} = \{\, x\in K : \sigma(x) = x \ \forall \sigma \in H \,\}$$

*Introduced:* [4.4](lessons/04-04-galois-automorphisms-taste.md)

## Formulas and rules

### The structure ladder

Each rung keeps every axiom below it and adds one demand. Read left to right and
you're watching arithmetic get progressively better behaved.

| Rung | Operations | What this rung **adds** | What it still **lacks** | Model |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Group** | one | closure, associativity, identity, inverses | commutativity; a second operation | $S_n$, $D_n$, $\mathbb{Z}$ |
| **Abelian group** | one | $ab = ba$ | a second operation | $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ |
| **Ring** | two | $(R,+)$ abelian; $\times$ associative; distributivity | commutative $\times$; a $1$; inverses; may have zero divisors | $M_n(\mathbb{R})$ |
| **Commutative unital ring** | two | $ab = ba$ and a multiplicative $1$ | inverses; still may have zero divisors | $\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$ |
| **Integral domain** | two | no zero divisors $\iff$ cancellation | multiplicative inverses | $\mathbb{Z}$, $F[x]$, $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ |
| **Field** | two | every nonzero element is a unit | nothing — this is the top | $\mathbb{Q}$, $\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$, $\mathbb{F}_{p^n}$ |

Three facts that make the ladder usable:

$$\text{field} \Rightarrow \text{domain} \Rightarrow \text{comm.\ unital ring} \Rightarrow \text{ring} \Rightarrow (R,+) \text{ abelian group}$$
$$\textbf{finite domain} \Rightarrow \textbf{field} \qquad\text{(multiplication by } a \text{ is injective, hence onto, hence hits } 1)$$
$$\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z} \text{ is a field} \iff \text{it is a domain} \iff n \text{ is prime}$$

### The isomorphism theorems

Stated exactly — this is what you look up mid-proof. The strategy they encode:
to name a quotient, **don't enumerate cosets — find a surjection with that kernel.**

**First.** Let $\varphi: G \to H$ be a homomorphism. Then $\ker\varphi \trianglelefteq G$, and

$$\overline{\varphi}: G/\ker\varphi \longrightarrow \operatorname{im}\varphi, \qquad g\,\ker\varphi \longmapsto \varphi(g)$$

is a well-defined isomorphism; hence $G/\ker\varphi \cong \operatorname{im}\varphi$.
For finite $G$ this gives $\lvert G\rvert = \lvert\ker\varphi\rvert \cdot \lvert\operatorname{im}\varphi\rvert$.
*(Four checks: well-defined, homomorphism, injective, surjective — in that order.)*

**Second (diamond).** Let $H \leq G$ and $N \trianglelefteq G$. Then
$HN = \{hn\}$ is a subgroup, $N \trianglelefteq HN$, $H\cap N \trianglelefteq H$, and

$$HN/N \;\cong\; H/(H\cap N).$$

*Enlarging $H$ by $N$ and dividing $N$ back out costs exactly the overlap.*

**Third.** Let $N \trianglelefteq K \trianglelefteq G$ with both normal in $G$
(so $N \subseteq K$). Then $K/N \trianglelefteq G/N$ and

$$(G/N)\big/(K/N) \;\cong\; G/K.$$

*The $N$'s cancel like a fraction.*

**Ring version.** Word for word, with ideals in place of normal subgroups: for a
ring homomorphism $\varphi: R \to S$,

$$R/\ker\varphi \;\cong\; \operatorname{im}\varphi.$$

**Worked instances to recognize on sight:**

| Map | Kernel | Image | Conclusion |
|---|---|---|---|
| $\det: GL_n(\mathbb{R}) \to \mathbb{R}^*$ | $SL_n(\mathbb{R})$ | $\mathbb{R}^*$ | $GL_n/SL_n \cong \mathbb{R}^*$ |
| $\operatorname{sgn}: S_n \to \{\pm1\}$ | $A_n$ | $\{\pm1\}$ | $S_n/A_n \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ |
| $\mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$, reduce | $n\mathbb{Z}$ | all of it | $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ *is* a quotient |
| $x \mapsto e^{2\pi i x}$, $\mathbb{R}\to S^1$ | $\mathbb{Z}$ | $S^1$ | $\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z} \cong S^1$ |
| $p \mapsto p(i)$, $\mathbb{R}[x]\to\mathbb{C}$ | $(x^2+1)$ | $\mathbb{C}$ | $\mathbb{R}[x]/(x^2+1) \cong \mathbb{C}$ |
| $f \mapsto f(0)$, $\mathbb{Z}[x]\to\mathbb{Z}$ | $(x)$ | $\mathbb{Z}$ | $(x)$ prime, not maximal |

### Counting identities

Every structural theorem in Modules 1–2 is one of these six counts.

$$\textbf{Lagrange:}\quad \lvert G\rvert = [G:H]\cdot\lvert H\rvert \ \Rightarrow\ \lvert H\rvert \mid \lvert G\rvert,\qquad \operatorname{ord}(g) \mid \lvert G\rvert,\qquad g^{\lvert G\rvert} = e$$
$$\textbf{Fermat (Lagrange in } (\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})^\times):\quad a^{p-1} \equiv 1 \pmod p \ \text{ for } p \nmid a$$
$$\textbf{Homomorphism count:}\quad \lvert G\rvert = \lvert\ker\varphi\rvert\cdot\lvert\operatorname{im}\varphi\rvert,\qquad \lvert\operatorname{im}\varphi\rvert \ \text{divides}\ \gcd(\lvert G\rvert, \lvert H\rvert)$$
$$\textbf{Orbit-stabilizer:}\quad \lvert\mathrm{Orb}(x)\rvert = [G:\mathrm{Stab}(x)] = \frac{\lvert G\rvert}{\lvert\mathrm{Stab}(x)\rvert}$$
$$\textbf{Class equation:}\quad \lvert G\rvert = \lvert Z(G)\rvert + \sum_i [G : C_G(x_i)], \qquad \lvert\mathrm{Cl}(x)\rvert = \frac{\lvert G\rvert}{\lvert C_G(x)\rvert}$$
$$\textbf{Burnside:}\quad \lvert X/G\rvert = \frac{1}{\lvert G\rvert}\sum_{g\in G}\lvert\mathrm{Fix}(g)\rvert, \qquad \lvert\mathrm{Fix}(g)\rvert = k^{\,c(g)} \ \text{for } k\text{-colorings}$$

Consequences worth having ready: every group of prime order is cyclic; a group of
order $p^k$ has nontrivial center (every non-central class size is divisible by
$p$); a group of order $p^2$ is abelian; $G/Z(G)$ is never a nontrivial cyclic
group.

*From* [1.5](lessons/01-05-cosets-lagrange.md), [2.1](lessons/02-01-homomorphisms-kernels-images.md), [2.5](lessons/02-05-orbits-stabilizers-conjugacy.md), [2.6](lessons/02-06-burnside-counting.md)

### Permutation arithmetic

| Question about $\sigma \in S_n$ | Answer |
|---|---|
| its order | $\operatorname{lcm}$ of its disjoint-cycle lengths (**not** the sum, not the max) |
| its sign | $(-1)^{\sum (\ell_i - 1)}$; a $k$-cycle is $k-1$ transpositions |
| its inverse | reverse each cycle |
| when is it conjugate to $\tau$ | **in $S_n$:** exactly when they have the same cycle type |
| conjugating | $\tau\,(a\,b\,c)\,\tau^{-1} = (\tau a\ \ \tau b\ \ \tau c)$ — conjugation just relabels |
| size of its class in $S_n$ | $\dfrac{n!}{\prod_k k^{m_k}\,m_k!}$, with $m_k$ = number of $k$-cycles |

### Polynomial rings

Over a **field** $F$, the ring $F[x]$ is $\mathbb{Z}$ with degree for size and
irreducibles for primes.

$$\deg(fg) = \deg f + \deg g, \qquad F[x]^\times = F \setminus \{0\} \ \text{(the nonzero constants)}$$
$$\textbf{division algorithm:}\quad f = qg + r \ \text{ with } r = 0 \text{ or } \deg r < \deg g \quad (\text{unique})$$
$$\textbf{factor theorem:}\quad f(a) = 0 \iff (x-a) \mid f \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad \deg f = n \Rightarrow \text{at most } n \text{ roots in } F$$
$$\textbf{PID:}\quad \text{every ideal of } F[x] \text{ is } (p) \text{ for a single } p \qquad (f)+(g) = (\gcd(f,g))$$
$$\boxed{\,F[x]/(p) \text{ is a field} \iff (p) \text{ is maximal} \iff p \text{ is irreducible over } F\,}$$

**Irreducibility toolkit:**

| Test | Statement | Caveat |
|---|---|---|
| degree $2$ or $3$ | irreducible over $F$ $\iff$ **no root in $F$** | **fails for degree $\geq 4$**: $(x^2+1)^2$ has no real root yet factors |
| rational root | a root $p/q$ in lowest terms has $p \mid a_0$, $q \mid a_n$ | only finds *linear* factors |
| Eisenstein (over $\mathbb{Q}$) | a prime $\pi$ divides every coefficient but the leading one, and $\pi^2 \nmid a_0$ $\Rightarrow$ irreducible | failure proves **nothing**; try the shift $x \mapsto x+1$ first |
| reduction mod $p$ | monic integer $f$ keeping its degree and irreducible over $\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ $\Rightarrow$ irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$ | a finite check |

**Small irreducibles over $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$** — the ones the finite-field
constructions use:

| Degree | Irreducible polynomials over $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ | Builds |
|---|---|---|
| $2$ | $x^2+x+1$ (the only one) | $\mathrm{GF}(4)$ |
| $3$ | $x^3+x+1$, $x^3+x^2+1$ | $\mathrm{GF}(8)$ (isomorphic either way) |
| $8$ | $x^8+x^4+x^3+x+1$ | $\mathrm{GF}(2^8)$ — the AES field |

Note $x^2+1 = (x+1)^2$ over $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ — reducible there, irreducible
over $\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$ (which builds $\mathbb{F}_9$).

*From* [3.4](lessons/03-04-polynomial-rings.md), [3.5](lessons/03-05-characteristic-prime-fields.md), [4.3](lessons/04-03-finite-fields.md)

### Field extensions and degree

$$\textbf{tower law:}\quad F \subseteq K \subseteq L \ \Rightarrow\ [L:F] = [L:K]\,[K:F] \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad [K:F] \ \text{divides}\ [L:F]$$

The divisibility is the field-theory echo of Lagrange: an intermediate field's
degree has no freedom. A basis of $L$ over $F$ is the product set $\{a_i b_j\}$.

$$\textbf{adjoining a root:}\quad F(\alpha) = F[\alpha] \cong F[x]/(m_\alpha), \qquad [F(\alpha):F] = \deg m_\alpha$$

with $F$-basis $\{1, \alpha, \alpha^2, \dots, \alpha^{n-1}\}$, $n = \deg m_\alpha$.

**Computing inside $F(\alpha)$:** multiply as polynomials, then reduce using
$m_\alpha(\alpha) = 0$; to invert $g(\alpha)$, run the extended Euclidean
algorithm on $g$ and $m_\alpha$ to get $ag + bm_\alpha = 1$, then
$g(\alpha)^{-1} = a(\alpha)$. ("Rationalizing the denominator," systematized.)

| Extension | Degree | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| $\mathbb{C}/\mathbb{R}$ | $2$ | $\{1, i\}$ |
| $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt2)/\mathbb{Q}$ | $2$ | $\{1,\sqrt2\}$ |
| $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2})/\mathbb{Q}$ | $3$ | $\{1,\sqrt[3]{2},\sqrt[3]{4}\}$ |
| $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt2,\sqrt3)/\mathbb{Q}$ | $4$ | $\{1,\sqrt2,\sqrt3,\sqrt6\}$ — and it equals $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt2+\sqrt3)$ |
| $\mathbb{F}_{p^n}/\mathbb{F}_p$ | $n$ | any $\mathbb{F}_p$-basis; hence $\lvert\mathbb{F}_{p^n}\rvert = p^n$ |

*From* [4.1](lessons/04-01-field-extensions-degree.md), [4.2](lessons/04-02-adjoining-roots-algebraic-elements.md)

### Finite fields

$$\textbf{existence and uniqueness:}\quad \text{a field of size } q \text{ exists} \iff q = p^n; \text{ and it is unique up to isomorphism}$$
$$\textbf{construction:}\quad \mathbb{F}_{p^n} \cong \mathbb{F}_p[x]/(f) \ \text{ for any irreducible } f \in \mathbb{F}_p[x] \text{ of degree } n$$
$$\mathbb{F}_{p^n}^{*} \cong \mathbb{Z}/(p^n-1) \ \text{(cyclic)}, \qquad x^{p^n} = x \ \text{ for every } x \in \mathbb{F}_{p^n}$$

So $\mathbb{F}_{p^n}$ is exactly the root set of $x^{p^n} - x$, and its
multiplicative structure is one clock: to multiply, add exponents mod $p^n-1$; to
add, fall back to polynomials. **$\mathbb{F}_{p^n} \neq \mathbb{Z}/(p^n)$** for
$n>1$ — the latter has zero divisors and isn't a field.

**$\mathrm{GF}(8) = \mathbb{Z}/2[x]/(x^3+x+1)$, log table** (reduce with
$\alpha^3 = \alpha+1$):

| $\alpha^0$ | $\alpha^1$ | $\alpha^2$ | $\alpha^3$ | $\alpha^4$ | $\alpha^5$ | $\alpha^6$ | $\alpha^7$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $1$ | $\alpha$ | $\alpha^2$ | $\alpha+1$ | $\alpha^2+\alpha$ | $\alpha^2+\alpha+1$ | $\alpha^2+1$ | $1$ |

### Galois: the two rules that compute everything

**Root-permuting principle.** If $\alpha$ has minimal polynomial $m$ over $F$ and
$\sigma \in \mathrm{Gal}(K/F)$, then

$$m(\sigma(\alpha)) = \sigma(m(\alpha)) = \sigma(0) = 0,$$

so $\sigma(\alpha)$ is another **root of the same $m$**. Since $\sigma$ is pinned
down by where it sends the generators, the Galois group is a finite permutation
problem on root sets.

**The correspondence (stated for a Galois extension).** The maps
$L \mapsto \mathrm{Gal}(K/L)$ and $H \mapsto K^H$ are mutually inverse bijections
between intermediate fields and subgroups, and they **reverse inclusions**:

$$F \subseteq L \subseteq K \ \longleftrightarrow\ \{e\} \leq \mathrm{Gal}(K/L) \leq \mathrm{Gal}(K/F), \qquad [K:L] = \lvert\mathrm{Gal}(K/L)\rvert$$

Bigger field $\leftrightarrow$ smaller group. The whole group sits under the
*base* field; the trivial subgroup under the *top* field.

| Extension | Galois group | Note |
|---|---|---|
| $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt5)/\mathbb{Q}$ | $\mathbb{Z}/2$ | one sign flip |
| $\mathbb{C}/\mathbb{R}$ | $\mathbb{Z}/2$ | complex conjugation |
| $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt2,\sqrt3)/\mathbb{Q}$ | Klein four | one $\mathbb{Z}/2$ per independent square root; three subgroups ↔ $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt2),\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt3),\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt6)$ |
| $\mathbb{F}_{p^n}/\mathbb{F}_p$ | $\mathbb{Z}/n$ | cyclic, generated by Frobenius $x\mapsto x^p$ |

## Pitfalls

### Axioms and notation

- **The operation is half the data.** "$\mathbb{Z}$" is not a group; "$(\mathbb{Z},+)$" is — the same set fails under $\times$. *([1.1](lessons/01-01-group-axioms-first-examples.md))*
- Closure is a real condition, not bookkeeping: the odd integers die immediately under $+$. *([1.1](lessons/01-01-group-axioms-first-examples.md))*
- Abelian is earned, not assumed. $(ab)^{-1} = b^{-1}a^{-1}$ — socks and shoes — never $a^{-1}b^{-1}$. *([1.1](lessons/01-01-group-axioms-first-examples.md))*
- **"Order" is two different words.** Order of an *element* = steps back to $e$; order of a *group* = element count. Never conflate them mid-sentence. *([1.2](lessons/01-02-cyclic-groups-order.md), [1.5](lessons/01-05-cosets-lagrange.md))*
- Additive and multiplicative dialects: $g^k = e$ and $kg = 0$ are the same equation. Track which room you're in. *([1.2](lessons/01-02-cyclic-groups-order.md))*
- $\varphi$ is overloaded across the course — Euler's totient (1.2), a homomorphism (2.1), the Frobenius (3.5). Read it from context, not from the letter. *([1.2](lessons/01-02-cyclic-groups-order.md), [3.5](lessons/03-05-characteristic-prime-fields.md))*

### Cyclic groups and permutations

- Cyclic is the easy case, not the general one; a "generator" needn't be small — $11$ generates $\mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}$ just as surely as $1$ does. Coprimality is the test. *([1.2](lessons/01-02-cyclic-groups-order.md))*
- $\operatorname{ord}(g^k) = n/\gcd(n,k)$ needs a *finite cyclic group of known order $n$* — find the ambient cyclic order first. *([1.2](lessons/01-02-cyclic-groups-order.md))*
- Order of a permutation is the **lcm** of its cycle lengths, not the sum or the max. *([1.3](lessons/01-03-dihedral-symmetric-groups.md))*
- Disjoint cycles commute; overlapping ones do not. Reorder freely only once they're disjoint. *([1.3](lessons/01-03-dihedral-symmetric-groups.md))*
- Fix the composition convention (this course: **right-to-left**) and never waver — flipping it swaps $rs \leftrightarrow sr$ everywhere. *([1.3](lessons/01-03-dihedral-symmetric-groups.md))*
- $s^2 = e$ does not make $s$ central. "Self-inverse" is about $s$ alone; "commutes" is about $s$ and $r$ together. *([1.3](lessons/01-03-dihedral-symmetric-groups.md))*

### Subgroups, cosets, Lagrange

- Closure alone suffices only for **finite** subsets; for infinite ones you still owe the inverse check (the non-negative integers are the standing counterexample). *([1.4](lessons/01-04-subgroups.md))*
- The **union** of two subgroups is almost never a subgroup — take the subgroup they *generate*, $\langle H\cup K\rangle$. Intersections, however, always work. *([1.4](lessons/01-04-subgroups.md))*
- $Z(G)$ is not "the abelian part": two non-central elements can still commute with each other. *([1.4](lessons/01-04-subgroups.md))*
- **The converse of Lagrange is false.** $6 \mid 12$ but $A_4$ has no subgroup of order $6$. Lagrange forbids sizes; it never manufactures subgroups. *([1.5](lessons/01-05-cosets-lagrange.md))*
- Nor is every divisor an element order: $S_4$ has order $24$ and no element of order $24$. *([1.5](lessons/01-05-cosets-lagrange.md))*
- Cosets aren't subgroups — only $eH = H$ contains the identity. *([1.5](lessons/01-05-cosets-lagrange.md))*

### Normality and quotients

- Normality is a **relationship between $N$ and $G$**, not a property of $N$: $\langle s\rangle$ is a fine group, just not normal inside $D_3$. It is also not transitive. *([2.2](lessons/02-02-normal-subgroups-quotients.md))*
- $gNg^{-1} = N$ is a **set** equality. You do *not* need $gng^{-1} = n$ elementwise — that's centrality, far stronger. Likewise $gN = Ng$ does not mean $gn = ng$. *([2.2](lessons/02-02-normal-subgroups-quotients.md))*
- Elements of $G/N$ are cosets — bags of elements acting as one point. Multiplying by representatives is safe **only** when $N$ is normal. *([2.2](lessons/02-02-normal-subgroups-quotients.md))*
- In $R/I$ the zero is the coset $I$ itself: "$\bar a = 0$" means "$a \in I$," not "$a = 0$ in $R$." *([3.3](lessons/03-03-ideals-quotient-rings.md))*

### Homomorphisms and the isomorphism theorems

- **Kernel is upstream, image is downstream.** $\ker\varphi \leq G$ lives in the domain; $\operatorname{im}\varphi \leq H$ in the codomain. *([2.1](lessons/02-01-homomorphisms-kernels-images.md))*
- "Homomorphism" does not mean injective — $\det$, $\operatorname{sgn}$, and reduction mod $n$ are deliberately lossy. Injectivity *is* the trivial-kernel condition. *([2.1](lessons/02-01-homomorphisms-kernels-images.md))*
- Check the multiplication axiom and **nothing else**: identity-to-identity and inverse-to-inverse are theorems. But a *ring* homomorphism owes you a third check, $\varphi(1_R) = 1_S$ — which is why the zero map isn't one. *([2.1](lessons/02-01-homomorphisms-kernels-images.md), [3.1](lessons/03-01-rings-ring-homomorphisms.md))*
- **Always check well-defined first.** A map "defined on cosets by a representative" is a promise, not yet a function — this is the single most common gap in a first-isomorphism proof. *([2.3](lessons/02-03-isomorphism-theorems.md))*
- The isomorphism lands in $\operatorname{im}\varphi$, not in all of $H$. To conclude $G/\ker\varphi \cong H$ you must separately verify surjectivity. *([2.3](lessons/02-03-isomorphism-theorems.md))*

### Actions and counting

- Axiom (2) is composition: $(gh)\cdot x = g\cdot(h\cdot x)$, "apply $h$ first." It is *not* $g\cdot(h\cdot x) = h\cdot(g\cdot x)$. *([2.4](lessons/02-04-group-actions.md))*
- **Faithful** (about $\ker\varphi$) and **transitive** (about reaching every point) are independent adjectives. *([2.4](lessons/02-04-group-actions.md))*
- Cayley embeds $G$ into $S_{\lvert G\rvert}$, which is enormous — the theorem is existence, not efficiency. *([2.4](lessons/02-04-group-actions.md))*
- Orbit size is an **index**, stabilizer size an **order**; they multiply to $\lvert G\rvert$, they are not equal. *([2.5](lessons/02-05-orbits-stabilizers-conjugacy.md))*
- A conjugacy class is (almost) never a subgroup — it usually misses $e$. Only its *cardinality* divides $\lvert G\rvert$. *([2.5](lessons/02-05-orbits-stabilizers-conjugacy.md))*
- **"Same cycle type $=$ conjugate" is an $S_n$ fact.** Inside $A_4$ or $D_4$ it can fail — conjugacy is always relative to the ambient group. *([2.5](lessons/02-05-orbits-stabilizers-conjugacy.md))*
- Centralizer $\neq$ center: $C_G(x)$ commutes with one element, $Z(G)$ with all. *([2.5](lessons/02-05-orbits-stabilizers-conjugacy.md))*
- Burnside is an **average of fixed-point counts**, not $\lvert X\rvert/\lvert G\rvert$ — that shortcut is usually not even an integer. And $c(g)$ counts cycles on the *slots being colored*, never on the colors. *([2.6](lessons/02-06-burnside-counting.md))*
- Get the group right: rotations of a cube (order $24$) versus rotations *and* reflections (order $48$); a flippable necklace uses $D_n$, not $C_n$. *([2.6](lessons/02-06-burnside-counting.md))*

### Rings, domains, fields

- Don't import "$ab=0 \Rightarrow a=0$ or $b=0$" from $\mathbb{R}$ — that's a *privilege* (the domain axiom), not a default. In $\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$, $2\cdot3 = 0$. *([3.1](lessons/03-01-rings-ring-homomorphisms.md), [3.2](lessons/03-02-integral-domains-fields.md))*
- **Units and "nonzero" are different sets** everywhere except in a field. $\mathbb{Z}$ has two units and infinitely many nonzero elements. *([3.1](lessons/03-01-rings-ring-homomorphisms.md))*
- Domain does **not** mean field — $\mathbb{Z}$ and $F[x]$ are the counterexamples. The "domain $\Rightarrow$ field" theorem needs **finiteness**. *([3.2](lessons/03-02-integral-domains-fields.md))*
- You cannot build $\operatorname{Frac}(\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z})$: the fraction construction needs the no-zero-divisor law or the relation stops being transitive. *([3.2](lessons/03-02-integral-domains-fields.md))*
- **A subring is not an ideal.** $\mathbb{Z} \subseteq \mathbb{Q}$ is a subring; multiply by $1/2$ and you're out. Closure under *own* products is weaker than absorption. *([3.3](lessons/03-03-ideals-quotient-rings.md))*
- If $1 \in I$ then $I = R$ — so a proper ideal contains no units, and a field has only the two ideals $\{0\}$ and $R$. *([3.3](lessons/03-03-ideals-quotient-rings.md))*
- Prime $\neq$ maximal: $(x) \trianglelefteq \mathbb{Z}[x]$ is prime (quotient $\mathbb{Z}$) but not maximal. *([3.3](lessons/03-03-ideals-quotient-rings.md))*

### Polynomials, characteristic, extensions

- "No root $\Rightarrow$ irreducible" holds **only** for degree $2$ and $3$. A quartic can hide behind quadratic factors. *([3.4](lessons/03-04-polynomial-rings.md))*
- Irreducibility is meaningless without naming the field, and the division algorithm needs $F$ to be a field — $\mathbb{Z}[x]$ is not a PID. *([3.4](lessons/03-04-polynomial-rings.md))*
- Eisenstein failing tells you nothing; it only ever certifies irreducibility. *([3.4](lessons/03-04-polynomial-rings.md))*
- $\operatorname{char}(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}) = n$ even for composite $n$ — "characteristic is $0$ or prime" is a theorem about **fields and domains only**. *([3.5](lessons/03-05-characteristic-prime-fields.md))*
- $n\cdot 1$ is repeated addition, not a product; keep the integer $p$ and the ring element $p\cdot 1$ mentally separate. *([3.5](lessons/03-05-characteristic-prime-fields.md))*
- The freshman's dream needs the exponent to be the characteristic (or a power of it) — $(a+b)^p$, not $(a+b)^3$ in characteristic $2$. *([3.5](lessons/03-05-characteristic-prime-fields.md))*
- **$\mathbb{F}_{p^n} \neq \mathbb{Z}/(p^n)$** for $n>1$: same size, totally different multiplication, and only one of them is a field. *([3.5](lessons/03-05-characteristic-prime-fields.md), [4.3](lessons/04-03-finite-fields.md))*
- "Cyclic" in a finite field is about $\times$, not $+$: the additive group of $\mathbb{F}_{2^n}$ is $(\mathbb{Z}/2)^n$, not cyclic. And not every element is primitive — check the order. *([4.3](lessons/04-03-finite-fields.md))*
- $K/F$ is **not** a quotient, and degree measures dimension, not element count. *([4.1](lessons/04-01-field-extensions-degree.md))*
- Before multiplying degrees up a tower, verify the top step is genuinely new — if the element was already there, that step has degree $1$ and the total collapses. *([4.1](lessons/04-01-field-extensions-degree.md))*
- A polynomial vanishing at $\alpha$ isn't automatically minimal: $\sqrt2$ satisfies $x^4-4$, but $[\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt2):\mathbb{Q}] = 2$. Confirm monic, smallest degree, **irreducible**. *([4.2](lessons/04-02-adjoining-roots-algebraic-elements.md))*
- $F[\alpha] = F(\alpha)$ **only because $\alpha$ is algebraic**; for transcendental $\alpha$ they genuinely differ. *([4.2](lessons/04-02-adjoining-roots-algebraic-elements.md))*
- An automorphism can't send $\sqrt2$ to $\sqrt3$ — only to a root of the *same* minimal polynomial. And "fixes $F$" means pointwise, not setwise. *([4.4](lessons/04-04-galois-automorphisms-taste.md))*
- The Galois correspondence **reverses** inclusions: the whole group sits under the base field. Drawing both lattices the same way up gets every pairing backwards. *([4.4](lessons/04-04-galois-automorphisms-taste.md))*
