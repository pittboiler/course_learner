# Abstract Algebra · Lesson 3.5: Characteristic and prime fields

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 3: Rings and Fields · Builds on: [3.4 Polynomial rings](03-04-polynomial-rings.md) · Unlocks: Module 4 — [4.1 Field extensions and degree](04-01-field-extensions-degree.md)

## Why this matters

Every field you have met so far falls into one of exactly two worlds, and one number tells you which. In $\mathbb{Q}$, $\mathbb{R}$, $\mathbb{C}$ you can add $1$ to itself forever and never hit $0$ — these are the "$\mathbb{Q}$-like" fields. In $\mathbb{Z}/5\mathbb{Z}$, adding $1$ to itself five times lands you back at $0$ — a whole different arithmetic universe where $5 = 0$ and squaring is a *homomorphism*. That single number, the **characteristic**, is the arithmetic backbone of the field: it decides what its smallest subfield looks like, forces finite fields to have exactly $p^n$ elements, and hands you a tool (the Frobenius map) that Galois theory later runs on. This lesson names the split and then does the thing Module 3 has been building toward — takes a polynomial ring mod an irreducible and hatches an honest-to-goodness *new field* out of it.

## The idea

In any ring there is a distinguished element $1$. Start adding it to itself: $1,\ 1+1,\ 1+1+1,\dots$ Two things can happen. Either you never return to $0$ (as in $\mathbb{Z}$, $\mathbb{Q}$) — the characteristic is $0$ — or you eventually do, and the *first* time you hit $0$ is the characteristic. In $\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$ it takes six $1$s; the characteristic is $6$.

Here is the punchline that makes characteristic worth a whole lesson: **in a field, that wrap-around number can only be $0$ or a prime.** It can never be $6$. The reason is exactly the reason $\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$ is *not* a field — $6 = 2 \cdot 3$ factors, and $2$ and $3$ become zero divisors ($2 \cdot 3 = 0$ with neither factor zero). Fields forbid zero divisors, so they forbid composite characteristic. The absence of zero divisors — the very thing that makes a field a field — is what quantizes its characteristic to primes.

Once you know the characteristic $p$, you know the field's DNA. It contains a tiny copy of $\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ (the **prime subfield**), it is a vector space over that copy, and so if it is finite it must have $p^n$ elements — never $12$, never $6$, only prime powers. And in characteristic $p$ a small miracle occurs: the "mistake" every algebra student wants to make, $(a+b)^p = a^p + b^p$, is *actually true*. The cross-terms all carry a factor of $p$, and $p = 0$.

## The formal version

**Characteristic.** For a ring $R$ with identity $1$, the **characteristic** $\operatorname{char}(R)$ is the smallest integer $n > 0$ such that $\underbrace{1 + 1 + \cdots + 1}_{n} = 0$ (write this as $n \cdot 1 = 0$). If no such $n$ exists, $\operatorname{char}(R) = 0$.

> In words: how many copies of $1$ you must add to get $0$; zero if it never happens.

Here $n \cdot 1$ means "$1$ added to itself $n$ times," **not** multiplication by an integer inside $R$ — it is shorthand for repeated addition. Note $n \cdot x = (n\cdot 1)\,x$ for every $x$, so if $n \cdot 1 = 0$ then $n \cdot x = 0$ for *all* $x$ at once.

**Theorem (characteristic of a field).** *If $F$ is a field (or any integral domain), then $\operatorname{char}(F)$ is either $0$ or a prime $p$.*

> In words: no field can have characteristic $6$ — the wrap-around number is always prime or never happens.

*Proof.* Suppose $\operatorname{char}(F) = n > 0$ and $n = ab$ with $1 < a, b < n$. Then $0 = n \cdot 1 = (ab)\cdot 1 = (a \cdot 1)(b \cdot 1)$ (distributivity: adding $1$ to itself $ab$ times equals the product of the $a$-fold and $b$-fold sums). A field has no zero divisors, so $a \cdot 1 = 0$ or $b \cdot 1 = 0$. But $a, b < n$ contradicts $n$ being the *smallest* positive integer with $n \cdot 1 = 0$. Hence $n$ has no such factorization: it is prime. $\blacksquare$

**Prime subfield.** Every field $F$ contains a unique smallest subfield, its **prime subfield**, generated by $1$:

- $\operatorname{char}(F) = 0$: the elements $n \cdot 1$ give a copy of $\mathbb{Z}$, and taking quotients $\frac{m\cdot 1}{n \cdot 1}$ gives a copy of $\mathbb{Q}$. So the prime subfield is $\cong \mathbb{Q}$.
- $\operatorname{char}(F) = p$: the elements $\{0, 1, 2\cdot 1, \dots, (p-1)\cdot 1\}$ form a copy of $\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$. So the prime subfield is $\cong \mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$.

> In words: strip a field down to what $1$ alone generates and you always land on either $\mathbb{Q}$ or some $\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$. Two ancestors for every field alive.

**Consequence (order of a finite field).** A finite field $F$ has characteristic some prime $p$ (it can't be $0$ — that would need infinitely many distinct $n\cdot 1$). Then $F$ is a vector space over its prime subfield $\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ (addition is the vector addition, and $\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ scales). A finite vector space of dimension $n$ over a field of $p$ elements has exactly $p^n$ elements. **So $|F| = p^n$** for some prime $p$ and integer $n \geq 1$ — never anything else.

**Freshman's dream.** In a commutative ring of characteristic $p$ (prime),
$$(a + b)^p = a^p + b^p.$$

> In words: the algebra-student error becomes a theorem once $p = 0$.

*Why:* the binomial theorem gives $(a+b)^p = \sum_{k=0}^{p} \binom{p}{k} a^k b^{p-k}$. For $0 < k < p$, the coefficient $\binom{p}{k} = \frac{p!}{k!\,(p-k)!}$ is divisible by $p$ (the $p$ in the numerator has nothing in the smaller-factorial denominators to cancel it). In characteristic $p$, any multiple of $p$ equals $0$, so every middle term vanishes, leaving $a^p + b^p$.

**Frobenius map.** In characteristic $p$, the map
$$\varphi : F \to F, \qquad \varphi(x) = x^p$$
is a ring homomorphism: $\varphi(a+b) = (a+b)^p = a^p + b^p = \varphi(a) + \varphi(b)$ (Freshman's dream), $\varphi(ab) = (ab)^p = a^p b^p = \varphi(a)\varphi(b)$ (commutativity), and $\varphi(1) = 1$. On a finite field it is even an *automorphism* — the seed of everything in [4.4](04-04-galois-automorphisms-taste.md).

## Concrete instance — building a field with four elements

The boss problem of Module 3. Three fields to inspect, one to build from scratch.

**$\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$ is not a field.** Here $6 = 0$, and $6 = 2 \cdot 3$ factors, so $2 \cdot 3 = 0$ with neither $2$ nor $3$ equal to $0$. They are **zero divisors**; a field forbids these, so $2$ has no inverse (if $2u = 1$ then $3 = 3 \cdot 1 = 3 \cdot 2u = 6u = 0$, false). Composite modulus, composite failure.

**$\mathbb{Z}/5\mathbb{Z}$ is a field.** Five is prime, so there are no zero divisors, and every nonzero element inverts:

| $x$ | $1$ | $2$ | $3$ | $4$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $x^{-1}$ | $1$ | $3$ | $2$ | $4$ |

(Check: $2\cdot 3 = 6 = 1$, $4 \cdot 4 = 16 = 1$.) Characteristic $5$, five elements — a $p^1$ field.

**Now build $\mathbb{F}_4$.** We want a field of $4 = 2^2$ elements. The recipe from [3.3](03-03-ideals-quotient-rings.md)/[3.4](03-04-polynomial-rings.md): take polynomials over $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$, quotient by an irreducible quadratic. Candidate $f(x) = x^2 + x + 1$. It is **irreducible over $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$**: a quadratic is reducible only if it has a root, and $f(0) = 1$, $f(1) = 1 + 1 + 1 = 1$ — no roots in $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$.

So $\mathbb{F}_4 = \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}[x]/(x^2 + x + 1)$. Let $\alpha$ be the class of $x$; then $\alpha^2 + \alpha + 1 = 0$, i.e. (in characteristic $2$, where $-1 = 1$)
$$\boxed{\alpha^2 = \alpha + 1.}$$
Every element is a remainder of degree $< 2$: the four elements are $\{0,\ 1,\ \alpha,\ \alpha + 1\}$. Four of them, exactly as the $p^n$ count promised.

**Addition** (coefficient-wise mod $2$, so $x + x = 0$):

| $+$ | $0$ | $1$ | $\alpha$ | $\alpha+1$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $0$ | $0$ | $1$ | $\alpha$ | $\alpha+1$ |
| $1$ | $1$ | $0$ | $\alpha+1$ | $\alpha$ |
| $\alpha$ | $\alpha$ | $\alpha+1$ | $0$ | $1$ |
| $\alpha+1$ | $\alpha+1$ | $\alpha$ | $1$ | $0$ |

**Multiplication** (reduce with $\alpha^2 = \alpha + 1$; showing nonzero elements):

| $\times$ | $1$ | $\alpha$ | $\alpha+1$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| $1$ | $1$ | $\alpha$ | $\alpha+1$ |
| $\alpha$ | $\alpha$ | $\alpha+1$ | $1$ |
| $\alpha+1$ | $\alpha+1$ | $1$ | $\alpha$ |

Sample reductions: $\alpha \cdot \alpha = \alpha^2 = \alpha + 1$; $\ \alpha(\alpha+1) = \alpha^2 + \alpha = (\alpha+1) + \alpha = 2\alpha + 1 = 1$; $\ (\alpha+1)^2 = \alpha^2 + 1 = (\alpha+1)+1 = \alpha$.

Read off the inverses from the $1$s in the table: $1^{-1} = 1$, $\alpha^{-1} = \alpha + 1$, $(\alpha+1)^{-1} = \alpha$. Every nonzero element inverts, so **$\mathbb{F}_4$ is a field** — of characteristic $2$ (since $1 + 1 = 0$) and order $4$. Notice the nonzero elements are just powers of $\alpha$: $\alpha^1 = \alpha$, $\alpha^2 = \alpha+1$, $\alpha^3 = 1$ — a cyclic group of order $3$, foreshadowing that *every* finite field's nonzero elements are cyclic ([4.3](04-03-finite-fields.md)).

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (the theorem, in slow motion).** *Show $\operatorname{char}$ of a field is $0$ or prime, and locate where "field" is used.*

If $\operatorname{char}(F) = 0$ we are done. Otherwise $\operatorname{char}(F) = n > 0$. Suppose for contradiction $n$ is composite, $n = ab$ with $1 < a,b < n$. Distributing the $n$-fold sum of $1$:
$$0 = n\cdot 1 = (ab)\cdot 1 = (a\cdot 1)(b\cdot 1).$$
The single load-bearing step is the *next* one: since $F$ has **no zero divisors**, a product being $0$ forces a factor to be $0$, so $a \cdot 1 = 0$ or $b\cdot 1 = 0$ — a smaller positive multiple of $1$ vanishing, contradicting minimality of $n$. Remove "no zero divisors" and the argument collapses: indeed $\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$ has characteristic $6$ precisely because $2\cdot 1$ and $3\cdot 1$ are nonzero zero divisors. $\blacksquare$

**Example 2 (the build, verified end to end).** *Confirm $x^2 + x + 1$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$, construct $\mathbb{F}_4$, and prove every nonzero element is invertible.*

*Irreducible:* degree $2$, and reducibility of a degree-$2$ polynomial over a field means it factors into two linear pieces, i.e. it has a root. Test all of $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$: $f(0) = 1 \neq 0$, $f(1) = 1+1+1 = 1 \neq 0$. No roots ⇒ irreducible. By [3.4](03-04-polynomial-rings.md)'s theorem, $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}[x]/(f)$ is a **field**, because $(f)$ is maximal when $f$ is irreducible over a field.

*The elements:* remainders mod a degree-$2$ polynomial are $a + b\alpha$ with $a,b \in \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ — four of them: $0, 1, \alpha, \alpha+1$, with relation $\alpha^2 = \alpha + 1$.

*Invertibility, directly:* $1 \cdot 1 = 1$; $\alpha \cdot (\alpha + 1) = 1$ (computed above); and since $(\alpha+1)$'s partner in that product is $\alpha$, $(\alpha+1)^{-1} = \alpha$. All three nonzero elements have inverses, confirming independently of the general theorem that we have a field. It has $4 = 2^2$ elements and characteristic $2$ — a textbook $p^n$ field. (Contrast the failed $\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$ and the successful prime $\mathbb{Z}/5\mathbb{Z}$ from the Concrete instance: irreducible modulus in each world, field out.)

## Watch out

- **$\operatorname{char}(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}) = n$ even when $n$ is composite.** The theorem "characteristic is $0$ or prime" applies to *fields and integral domains only*. $\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$ has characteristic $6$ — it is a perfectly good ring, just not a field, precisely because $6$ is composite.
- **$n \cdot 1$ is repeated addition, not a product in $R$.** Writing $p = 0$ in characteristic $p$ means "$p$ copies of the identity sum to zero," not that some element literally named $p$ is zero. Keep the integer $p$ and the ring element $p\cdot 1$ mentally separate even as they collapse.
- **Freshman's dream needs characteristic $p$ *and* commutativity, and it's $(a+b)^p$, not $(a+b)^2$.** In $\mathbb{F}_4$ (char $2$), $(a+b)^2 = a^2 + b^2$ holds — but $(a+b)^3 \neq a^3 + b^3$ in general. The exponent must be the characteristic (or a power of it).
- **A field of $4$ elements is *not* $\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$.** $\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$ has characteristic $4$ and a zero divisor ($2\cdot 2 = 0$); it is not a field. The four-element field is $\mathbb{F}_4$, characteristic $2$, built as a quotient of a polynomial ring. Same size, different creature.

## One-liner

> Characteristic is the number of $1$s it takes to reach $0$ — always prime or never for a field — and it cleaves every field into the $\mathbb{Q}$-like and the $\mathbb{Z}/p$-like, the latter blessed with the Freshman's dream and finite fields of size $p^n$.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢)** Give the characteristic of each: (a) $\mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}$; (b) $\mathbb{Q}$; (c) $\mathbb{F}_4$; (d) $\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ for prime $p$; (e) the polynomial ring $\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}[x]$. For each, say whether it is a field.

**P2 (🟡)** Work in $\mathbb{F}_4 = \{0,1,\alpha,\alpha+1\}$ with $\alpha^2 = \alpha + 1$ (characteristic $2$). (a) Verify the Freshman's dream $(a+b)^2 = a^2 + b^2$ for $a = \alpha$, $b = 1$ by computing both sides. (b) Tabulate the Frobenius map $\varphi(x) = x^2$ on all four elements, and confirm it is a homomorphism that *permutes* $\mathbb{F}_4$ (in fact fixing $0,1$ and swapping $\alpha \leftrightarrow \alpha+1$).

**P3 (🔴)** The full boss problem. (a) Exhibit a pair of zero divisors in $\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$ and explain why this blocks it from being a field. (b) List the multiplicative inverse of every nonzero element of $\mathbb{Z}/5\mathbb{Z}$. (c) Prove $x^2 + x + 1$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$, use it to build a field with $4$ elements, write out its full multiplication table, and confirm every nonzero element is invertible. State the field's characteristic and order.

<details>
<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1.** (a) $\operatorname{char}(\mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}) = 12$ — **not** a field ($12$ composite; e.g. $3 \cdot 4 = 0$). (b) $\operatorname{char}(\mathbb{Q}) = 0$ — a field. (c) $\operatorname{char}(\mathbb{F}_4) = 2$ (since $1 + 1 = 0$) — a field. (d) $\operatorname{char}(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}) = p$ — a field ($p$ prime ⇒ no zero divisors). (e) $\operatorname{char}(\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}[x]) = 3$ (the constant $1$ is the same $1$, and $3 \cdot 1 = 0$) — **not** a field ($x$ has no inverse; it's an integral domain but not a field).

**P2.** (a) Left side: $a + b = \alpha + 1$, and $(\alpha+1)^2 = \alpha^2 + 2\alpha + 1 = \alpha^2 + 1$ (char $2$ kills $2\alpha$) $= (\alpha+1) + 1 = \alpha$. Right side: $a^2 + b^2 = \alpha^2 + 1 = (\alpha+1)+1 = \alpha$. Both give $\alpha$. ✓

(b) Frobenius $\varphi(x) = x^2$:

| $x$ | $0$ | $1$ | $\alpha$ | $\alpha+1$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $\varphi(x)=x^2$ | $0$ | $1$ | $\alpha+1$ | $\alpha$ |

using $\alpha^2 = \alpha+1$ and $(\alpha+1)^2 = \alpha$ from the table. Every output is a distinct element of $\mathbb{F}_4$, so $\varphi$ is a bijection — a permutation fixing $0$ and $1$ and swapping $\alpha \leftrightarrow \alpha+1$. It is a homomorphism: additivity is the Freshman's dream $(x+y)^2 = x^2 + y^2$ (part (a) is one instance), multiplicativity is $(xy)^2 = x^2 y^2$ by commutativity, and $\varphi(1) = 1$. This nontrivial automorphism is the Galois group of $\mathbb{F}_4/\mathbb{F}_2$ in miniature — see [4.4](04-04-galois-automorphisms-taste.md).

**P3.** (a) In $\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$, $2 \cdot 3 = 6 = 0$ with $2 \neq 0$ and $3 \neq 0$: they are zero divisors. A field has no zero divisors — equivalently, if $2$ had an inverse $u$, then $3 = 3\cdot 1 = 3 \cdot (2u) = (3\cdot 2)u = 6u = 0$, a contradiction. So $2$ is not invertible and $\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$ is not a field.

(b) Inverses in $\mathbb{Z}/5\mathbb{Z}$: $1^{-1} = 1$; $2^{-1} = 3$ (since $2\cdot 3 = 6 = 1$); $3^{-1} = 2$; $4^{-1} = 4$ (since $4\cdot 4 = 16 = 1$). All four nonzero elements invert ⇒ $\mathbb{Z}/5\mathbb{Z}$ is a field.

(c) *Irreducibility.* $f(x) = x^2 + x + 1$ has degree $2$, so it is reducible over the field $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ iff it has a root there. $f(0) = 1$, $f(1) = 1 + 1 + 1 = 1$; no roots, hence irreducible.

*Construction.* $\mathbb{F}_4 := \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}[x]/(x^2+x+1)$. Let $\alpha = \bar x$, so $\alpha^2 = \alpha + 1$. Elements: $\{0, 1, \alpha, \alpha+1\}$.

*Multiplication table* (nonzero elements; reduce via $\alpha^2 = \alpha+1$):

| $\times$ | $1$ | $\alpha$ | $\alpha+1$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| $1$ | $1$ | $\alpha$ | $\alpha+1$ |
| $\alpha$ | $\alpha$ | $\alpha+1$ | $1$ |
| $\alpha+1$ | $\alpha+1$ | $1$ | $\alpha$ |

Entries: $\alpha\cdot\alpha = \alpha^2 = \alpha+1$; $\ \alpha(\alpha+1) = \alpha^2 + \alpha = (\alpha+1)+\alpha = 1$; $\ (\alpha+1)^2 = \alpha^2 + 1 = \alpha$. Each row of the table contains a $1$, so $1^{-1}=1$, $\alpha^{-1} = \alpha+1$, $(\alpha+1)^{-1} = \alpha$: every nonzero element is invertible. Therefore $\mathbb{F}_4$ is a field of **characteristic $2$** and **order $4$**. ∎

## Flashback

**From Lesson 3.4 (Polynomial rings):** Is $x^2 + 1$ irreducible over $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$? Over $\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$? For whichever base makes it irreducible, how many elements does the quotient field have?

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

*Over $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$:* test roots — $f(0) = 1$, $f(1) = 1 + 1 = 0$. It **has** a root at $x = 1$, so it factors: $x^2 + 1 = (x+1)^2$ over $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ (expand: $x^2 + 2x + 1 = x^2 + 1$, since $2 = 0$). **Reducible.**

*Over $\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$:* test roots — $f(0) = 1$, $f(1) = 2$, $f(2) = 4 + 1 = 5 = 2$. No roots; a degree-$2$ polynomial with no roots over a field is **irreducible**.

So $\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}[x]/(x^2+1)$ is a field. Its elements are $a + b\beta$ with $a, b \in \mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$ and $\beta^2 = -1 = 2$ — that's $3 \times 3 = \mathbf{9}$ elements, a field $\mathbb{F}_9$ of characteristic $3$. (Same construction as $\mathbb{F}_4$, one prime over; and $\beta$ is a genuine "$i$" living in characteristic $3$.)

</details>

## Connections

- **Backward:** the whole build rests on [3.2](03-02-integral-domains-fields.md) (fields, zero divisors) and the quotient machine of [3.3](03-03-ideals-quotient-rings.md)/[3.4](03-04-polynomial-rings.md) — "$(f)$ maximal ⇔ $f$ irreducible over a field ⇒ quotient is a field." The characteristic theorem is the same no-zero-divisors reflex from 3.2, now aimed at the integer $n$ instead of at a product of ring elements.
- **Forward:** [4.1](04-01-field-extensions-degree.md) makes "$F$ is a vector space over its prime subfield" the organizing idea (degrees multiply); [4.3](04-03-finite-fields.md) proves every finite field is exactly $\mathrm{GF}(p^n)$, built just as $\mathbb{F}_4$ was; [4.4](04-04-galois-automorphisms-taste.md) reveals the Frobenius map $x \mapsto x^p$ as the generating symmetry of $\mathrm{GF}(p^n)/\mathbb{F}_p$.
- **Sideways (coding theory):** finite fields like $\mathbb{F}_4$ and $\mathrm{GF}(2^8)$ are the arithmetic of error-correcting codes and AES — the [information theory syllabus](../../information-theory/syllabus.md) runs on exactly these $p^n$ fields.
- **Sideways (number theory):** "characteristic $p$" is the doorway to arithmetic where $p = 0$ — the setting of Fermat's little theorem ($x^p = x$ in $\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$, i.e. Frobenius is the identity there) and, in plain language, of the deep analogy between integers and polynomials over finite fields that modern number theory exploits.
