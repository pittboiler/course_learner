# Fundamental Theology · Lesson 3.2: Inerrancy

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 3: Scripture and Tradition · Builds on: [3.1 Inspiration](03-01-inspiration.md) · Unlocks: [3.3 The canon as defined](03-03-the-canon-as-defined.md)

## Why this matters

Two people arguing about whether the Bible "contains errors" are almost always agreed about something false: that the Church claims every sentence of Scripture is accurate as a modern report would be. The fundamentalist defends that claim; the debunker attacks it; neither has stated the doctrine. What the Church actually teaches is narrower, older and more interesting — and at one precise point it is genuinely unsettled, with serious Catholic theologians on both sides. This lesson gets the doctrine stated, and then shows you exactly where the seam is, because knowing *which* part is disputed is the whole skill this course is teaching.

## The idea

Inerrancy is not a claim about sentences. It is a claim about **assertions**.

Scripture has God as its author ([3.1](03-01-inspiration.md)); what God asserts is true; therefore Scripture, in what it asserts, does not err. The entire weight of the doctrine then falls on one prior question: **what did the sacred writer actually assert?** A text can contain a sentence without asserting its literal content — when it quotes someone, when it speaks figuratively, when it describes how things look rather than how they are, when it is telling a parable rather than filing a report.

So before you can ask "is this true?" you have to ask "what is being claimed here?" That is not an evasion invented to rescue the doctrine. It is how you read any author, and the tradition has said so since Augustine.

The live Catholic dispute sits one step further along. Granted that inerrancy covers what is asserted — does it cover *everything* asserted, or only what is asserted for the sake of our salvation? That question is open.

## The argument

The doctrine assembled from its documents, each formal statement followed by a plain-English line.

**P1. Scripture has God for its author.** Trent, Session IV (1546), and Vatican I, *Dei Filius* ch. 2 (1870), hold the books as sacred and canonical because, written under the inspiration of the Holy Spirit, they have God as their author — not because the Church afterwards approved them.
*In words:* God is a real author of these books, not merely their approver. **Level: defined dogma.**

**P2. What God asserts is true.** God can neither deceive nor be deceived — the same premise *Dei Filius* uses for the act of faith ([2.1](02-01-the-act-of-faith.md)).
*In words:* the veracity of the divine author is what carries the doctrine; inerrancy is a corollary of inspiration, not a separate revelation.

**P3. Inspiration extends to the whole of Scripture, and is incompatible with error.** *Providentissimus Deus* (Leo XIII, 1893) rejected two restrictions then being proposed: limiting inspiration to certain parts of the books, and limiting it to matters of faith and morals. Benedict XV's *Spiritus Paraclitus* (1920) rejected the same restrictions again, against the theory that the sacred writers merely reported others' claims without endorsing them.
*In words:* twice within thirty years, the ordinary magisterium refused to confine the guarantee to the religious content of the text. **Level: authentic ordinary magisterium, taught insistently and repeatedly — which matters, since insistence and repetition are among the signs of the mind of the Magisterium ([4.3](04-03-religious-submission.md)).**

**P4. The sacred writers described sensible appearances in ordinary speech.** *Providentissimus Deus*, following Augustine and Aquinas, answers apparent conflicts with natural science this way: the writers did not set out to teach the inner constitution of the visible world, but described things as they appear, in the language their contemporaries used — the language, Leo notes, that scientists themselves still use in daily life. Leo's governing rule: nothing that the sciences have really demonstrated can contradict Scripture rightly interpreted, and apparent conflicts come from a misreading of one or the other.
*In words:* "the sun rose" is not a claim about celestial mechanics, in Genesis or in a weather report. **Level: encyclical teaching; the underlying principle is patristic and Thomistic, so it is also common theological doctrine.**

**P5. Determining what was asserted requires attention to literary genre.** *Divino Afflante Spiritu* (Pius XII, 1943) made this a method rather than an ad hoc move: the interpreter's first task is to find the literal sense, which means finding what the writer intended to say; and since he wrote as a man of his time, that requires knowing the modes of writing current in the ancient Near East — history written in various manners, poetry, prophecy, and other forms — recovered through the original languages, archaeology and the study of ancient literature.
*In words:* you cannot decide whether a text errs until you know what kind of text it is, and that is an empirical question about the ancient world, not a matter of preference. **Level: encyclical teaching; methodologically it is now simply how Catholic exegesis is done.**

**∴ C. Dei Verbum 11 (Vatican II, 1965), paraphrased:** since everything the inspired authors assert is to be held as asserted by the Holy Spirit, the books of Scripture must be acknowledged as teaching firmly, faithfully and without error *that truth which God wanted put into the sacred writings for the sake of our salvation.*

*Dei Verbum* is a dogmatic constitution — the heaviest conciliar genre — but Vatican II defined nothing with an attached canon, so this is solemn conciliar teaching rather than a new definition. It restates P1–P5 in one sentence. And it is that final clause, *for the sake of our salvation*, that the dispute is about.

**The two readings.** An earlier draft had spoken of *saving truth*; that wording was dropped after objections that it would put a material limit on inerrancy, and the present formula replaced it. Both sides cite this drafting history, and read it in opposite directions.

- **Restrictive.** The clause picks out which truth is guaranteed: the truth that bears on salvation. Scripture teaches without error in matters of faith and morals and in whatever is bound up with our salvation; assertions outside that range — incidental points of natural science, or of history with no salvific bearing — are not covered by the guarantee. The Council chose to keep a salvific reference, and words the Council kept must do some work.
- **Non-restrictive.** The clause is a **purpose** clause: it says *why* God wanted this truth committed to writing, not *which* of Scripture's assertions are true. Grammatically it attaches to God's willing, not to a subset of the content. The paragraph's own footnotes point back to *Providentissimus Deus* and *Divino Afflante Spiritu* — the very documents that refused to confine inerrancy to faith and morals — and a council does not silently reverse the authorities it cites.

**Level of the dispute itself: freely disputed.** That Scripture is inerrant is taught at the highest weight — the manuals (Ott) mark the freedom of Scripture from error as *de fide*, and no Catholic theologian denies the doctrine. Its *scope* has not been settled by any magisterial act, and exegetes and theologians in good standing hold both readings. Do not mark either reading as "the Church's teaching."

## Map of positions

| | **Restrictive** | **Non-restrictive** |
|---|---|---|
| The clause *for the sake of our salvation* | restricts *which truth* is taught without error | states *why* God willed the truth to be written |
| Inerrancy covers | what Scripture asserts touching salvation | everything Scripture asserts, full stop |
| Hard historical or scientific detail | falls outside the guarantee; nothing is at stake | falls inside; look harder at what was asserted |
| Strongest support | the Council kept salvific language after "saving truth" was dropped, so the phrase is not decorative | the grammar of the purpose clause, plus the cited encyclicals that explicitly refused this restriction |
| Chief cost | needs a workable criterion dividing salvific from non-salvific assertions — hard in a religion of events, where the Exodus and the Resurrection are both facts and saving facts | leaves standing exegetical debts, carried as unsolved problems rather than conceded errors |
| Agrees with the other on | genre, appearance-language, and authorial intention doing their work *first* — both readings dissolve most classic difficulties before scope is ever reached | |

The practical consequence: the two readings give the same answer on nearly every real case. They diverge only on a residue — assertions that survive the genre test and still look false.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (the clean case — where the doctrine actually bites).** Matthew 13:32, of the mustard seed, in the Douay–Rheims: "Which is the least indeed of all seeds; but when it is grown up, it is greater than all herbs."

Is this an error? Orchid seeds are smaller. Run the criteria before answering. *What did the writer assert?* The sentence sits inside a parable whose subject is the growth of the kingdom of heaven; the smallness is the parable's hinge. *Genre?* Parable — a form whose comparisons are drawn from the hearers' ordinary experience, here a proverbially tiny seed familiar in Galilee. *Ordinary speech about appearances?* Exactly Leo XIII's category: the writer speaks as his hearers speak, not as a botanist classifying seeds.

Verdict: nothing about seed taxonomy is asserted, so nothing about seed taxonomy is guaranteed, and nothing about seed taxonomy is denied. Notice that the scope dispute never came up — both readings reach this verdict by the same route. That is the normal case.

**Example 2 (the hard case — where it strains).** Mark 2:26 has Jesus recall David eating the loaves of proposition "under Abiathar the high priest." But in 1 Kings 21 (the Douay–Rheims numbering; 1 Samuel in most modern Bibles) the priest at Nob is Achimelech; Abiathar is his son, who becomes high priest later.

Try the criteria. Genre does not help much: this is a narrated saying inside a controversy story, not poetry or parable. Appearance-language does not apply. So something is being asserted, and it looks wrong. Three exegetical routes stay open — that the phrase is a way of pointing at the passage where Abiathar appears rather than dating the episode; that Abiathar is named as the better-known figure of the pair; or that the wording is a scribal problem, some ancient witnesses lacking the phrase.

Now the readings diverge, and this is what the dispute costs. The **restrictive** reading can say: even if the reference is simply mistaken, nothing about our salvation turns on which priest was at Nob, so the guarantee was never engaged — and it will add that this is precisely why the Council's clause is not decorative. The **non-restrictive** reading answers that the guarantee is engaged, and that the case is therefore an unsolved exegetical problem, not a conceded error — that the tradition's consistent habit has been to keep working rather than to carve out a zone of permitted mistakes, and that the restrictive reading still owes a criterion telling us when a detail is salvific, without which the exemption can be enlarged indefinitely.

Neither answer is the Church's; both are available to a Catholic. What is *not* available is saying the question has been settled.

## Watch out

- **You might think Vatican II taught that Scripture errs in matters of science or history.** Neither reading says that. The restrictive reading says such matters are outside the guarantee; it does not assert that the Bible is wrong about them.
- **You might think genre is an escape hatch.** *Divino Afflante Spiritu* authorized genre study as historical inquiry, not as a licence to declare an awkward text non-historical because it is awkward. *Humani Generis* (1950) warned against exactly that misuse, holding that the opening chapters of Genesis do pertain to history in a true though not modern sense. Genre is settled by evidence about the ancient world, or it is settled by convenience.
- **"The author did not assert it" and "the author asserted it and was wrong" are different claims.** Collapsing them is the single most common error in this area, made by defenders and critics alike.
- **You might think the Catechism settles the scope question.** It reproduces the conciliar formula — so it inherits the ambiguity along with the authority. A compendium cannot resolve what its source left open ([4.5](04-05-reading-a-documents-weight.md)).
- **The Pontifical Biblical Commission is not the Magisterium.** Since 1971 it is a body of scholars advising the doctrinal congregation; its 2014 study of inspiration and truth carries the weight of its arguments, not of a teaching act ([1.1](01-01-what-fundamental-theology-asks.md)).
- **Do not say "inerrancy was redefined in 1965."** Whether *Dei Verbum* developed, restated or narrowed the earlier teaching is itself the question — and it is a live specimen of the development problem in [5.2](05-02-newmans-theory-of-development.md).

## Dei Verbum 12: the tools the dispute is fought with

*Dei Verbum* 12, paraphrased, gives the interpreter two sets of criteria, and the whole argument above is conducted with them.

Because God spoke through men in human fashion:

1. **What the author asserted** — investigate what the sacred writer really intended to signify, and what God willed to manifest through his words.
2. **Genre and circumstance** — truth is set down differently in texts historical in various ways, in prophetic texts, in poetry, in other forms of speech; attend to the conventions of the writer's time and place.

Because Scripture must be read in the same Spirit in which it was written:

3. **The content and unity of the whole of Scripture** — no passage is read as if it stood alone.
4. **The living Tradition of the whole Church**.
5. **The analogy of faith** — the harmony among the truths of faith themselves.

And all of it stands under the judgement of the Church, which is charged with guarding and interpreting the word of God. Criteria 1–2 belong to the historian's toolkit and are shared with any careful reader; 3–5 are available only from inside the faith — which is why exegesis done well by these criteria is theology and not only history.

## One-liner

> Inerrancy guarantees what Scripture *asserts*, genre and appearance-language decide what is asserted — and whether the guarantee covers every assertion or only those touching salvation is a question Catholics are still free to argue.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢) *(Exegetical.)*** For each statement, give the level at which it is taught — or say that it is disputed — and name the act or document that fixes it. One sentence each.

(a) The books of Scripture have God as their author.
(b) Inspiration extends to the whole of Scripture, not only to matters of faith and morals.
(c) The clause "for the sake of our salvation" limits inerrancy to matters bearing on salvation.
(d) A biblical writer describing the sun's course is speaking of appearances in the ordinary language of his day.

**P2 (🟡) *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)*** (a) In one sentence each, say what the restrictive and the non-restrictive readings take the clause *for the sake of our salvation* to be doing, and name the single premise each side must deny in the other. (b) A friend says the drafting history settles it, since "saving truth" was removed from the draft. In 150 words or fewer, say what that fact does and does not establish, and argue for one reading — any verdict — stating the strongest consideration against you.

**P3 (🔴, optional) *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)*** Josue 10:13, Douay–Rheims: "the sun stood still in the midst of heaven, and hasted not to go down the space of one day" — and the same chapter cites the book of the Just for it.

(a) Which of *Dei Verbum* 12's criteria bear on this text, and what does each yield? Say whether the restrictive and non-restrictive readings give different verdicts here, and why. (b) A student concludes that since genre and appearance-language handle every hard case, the scope dispute is idle. In 150 words or fewer, assess that — any verdict, provided you say what residue, if any, the first two criteria leave behind.

<details>
<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical — strict.)*

**Must hit, strict:**
- (a) **Defined dogma.** Trent, Session IV (1546), and Vatican I's *Dei Filius* ch. 2 (1870) — the books are canonical because, written by the Spirit's inspiration, they have God as author.
- (b) **Authentic ordinary magisterium, taught insistently:** *Providentissimus Deus* (1893), repeated by *Spiritus Paraclitus* (1920). Credit for noting that the repetition raises the weight owed without making it a definition.
- (c) **Disputed** — one of the two readings of *Dei Verbum* 11, held by Catholic theologians in good standing, settled by no magisterial act. Asserting it as the Church's teaching is an error; so is asserting its denial as the Church's teaching.
- (d) **Encyclical teaching** (*Providentissimus Deus*), resting on a patristic and Thomistic principle, so also common theological doctrine — not a definition.

**Wrong turns:** promoting (b) to dogma because encyclicals taught it twice — repetition raises the assent owed, it does not change the genre of the act; marking (c) as settled in either direction; treating *Dei Verbum* as a defining act because it is a dogmatic constitution — Vatican II defined nothing with an attached canon.

**Model answer:** (a) dogma, Trent Session IV and *Dei Filius* ch. 2. (b) ordinary magisterium, *Providentissimus Deus* and *Spiritus Paraclitus*, taught with unusual insistence. (c) freely disputed; no act settles it. (d) encyclical teaching with patristic roots; not defined.

---

**P2** *(a) exegetical — strict; (b) evaluative — any verdict.*

**Must hit, strict (a):**
- Restrictive: the clause **restricts the object** — it names which truth is taught without error, namely the truth bearing on salvation.
- Non-restrictive: the clause states **purpose** — why God willed this truth to be committed to writing; it attaches to God's willing, not to a subset of the content.
- The premise each must deny: the restrictive reader must deny that the clause is purely final (i.e. must hold it does restrictive work, or the Council's wording is idle); the non-restrictive reader must deny that keeping a salvific reference implies a material limit (holding it states finality, and that the cited encyclicals rule the limit out).

**Must hit, any verdict (b):** (i) say what the drafting fact establishes — that the Council was unwilling to let "saving truth" stand, because it was heard as limiting; (ii) say what it does not establish — a replacement chosen to avoid an objection does not by itself tell you which sense the replacement bears, and both sides claim it; (iii) give a reading and name the strongest consideration against it. Either verdict passes.

**Wrong turns:** treating the removal of "saving truth" as proof of the non-restrictive reading without noticing that a salvific reference was nonetheless retained; treating its retention as proof of the restrictive reading without noticing why the earlier phrase was dropped; citing the Catechism as settling it.

**Model answer (b), one of several:** The drafting history shows the Council refused a formula that plainly limited inerrancy's object, and then chose one that keeps salvation in view without saying it limits. That is evidence of intent to avoid a limitation, not of intent to impose one — so I read the clause as final, describing why Scripture was given. Against me: if the phrase does no restricting work at all, the fathers spent a revision cycle producing a synonym for silence, which strains belief. My answer is that they were not seeking a synonym for silence but a positive statement of Scripture's purpose, in a constitution whose whole argument is that revelation is God's self-communication for our sake — the clause is doing theological work of a different kind than the one the restrictive reading assigns it.

---

**P3** *(a) exegetical — strict; (b) evaluative — any verdict.*

**Must hit, strict (a):**
- **What the writer asserted / genre:** the passage cites a poetic source (the book of the Just), and the wording is the language of poetry and of appearance — the sun's motion as seen from the ground. What is asserted is that God gave Israel the day it needed to finish the battle, not a proposition about the earth's rotation.
- **Unity of Scripture and the analogy of faith:** read alongside the rest of Scripture, the claim at stake is God's action for his people; nothing in the faith requires a cosmological reading.
- **The two readings give the same verdict here.** The difficulty dissolves at the level of what is asserted, before scope is reached. A student who says the restrictive reading is needed to handle this case has misdiagnosed it.

**Must hit, any verdict (b):** state whether any residue survives criteria 1–2 — cases where a text does seem to assert something in an ordinary, non-figurative way and the assertion still looks false (Example 2's kind of case) — and say what follows for the dispute if the residue is empty, or if it is not. Either conclusion passes if the residue question is faced rather than assumed away.

**Wrong turns:** answering (a) by saying "it is just a metaphor" without naming which criterion does the work; assuming the residue is empty because the two examples in this lesson went the same way; arguing about the astronomy.

**Model answer (b), one of several:** Not idle, but smaller than it looks. Genre and appearance-language clear nearly everything, which is why the two readings agree on almost every case a reader will meet — and that agreement is worth saying out loud, because most popular argument about biblical error never gets past cases these criteria dissolve. But a residue remains: incidental historical details, asserted flatly, that resist every reading. Even if the residue is only a handful of verses, the dispute is about what the doctrine *claims*, not about how often the claim is tested — and a doctrine's extension is not decided by counting the hard cases. So I would keep the question open on principle rather than dismiss it as academic.

## Flashback

**F1 (🟡) *(Exegetical.)*** From an invented diocesan study guide:

> "Because the Church has made Thomism her official philosophy, a Catholic philosopher's task is to defend Aquinas's conclusions; philosophy has no business setting its own questions, since theology already knows the answers."

Name two things this gets wrong about the account of faith and reason in *Fides et Ratio* (John Paul II, 1998), and state what that document does claim about Aquinas. Three sentences or fewer.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict:**
- The Church proposes **no official philosophy** — *Fides et Ratio* says so explicitly; it does not canonize a system.
- Philosophy has a **legitimate autonomy**: it proceeds by its own method and its own questions, and the document insists on this rather than merely tolerating it. Faith and reason are the two wings by which the mind rises to truth — a partnership, not a chain of command.
- What it does claim about Aquinas: he holds a **special and enduring standing** as a model of how faith and reason are rightly joined, proposed as an exemplar rather than as a required set of conclusions.

**Wrong turns:** reading "no official philosophy" as indifference among philosophies — the document is sharply critical of positions it judges incompatible with faith; or reading Aquinas's standing as merely honorary.

**Model answer:** It invents an official philosophy the Church has never adopted, and it denies philosophy the autonomy *Fides et Ratio* insists on — reason has its own method and its own questions, which is what makes it a wing rather than a passenger. What the document actually claims is that Aquinas is an enduring model of faith and reason rightly joined, which recommends his way of working rather than requiring his conclusions.

</details>
