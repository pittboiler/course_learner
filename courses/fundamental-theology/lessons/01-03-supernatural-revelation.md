# Fundamental Theology · Lesson 1.3: Supernatural revelation

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 1: Revelation · Builds on: [1.2 Natural knowledge of God](01-02-natural-knowledge-of-god.md) · Unlocks: [1.4 Public and private revelation](01-04-public-and-private-revelation.md)

## Why this matters

[1.2](01-02-natural-knowledge-of-god.md) left you with a capacity: reason can reach God as the cause of the things that are made. A capacity is not a conversation. Everything the rest of this course studies — an inspired book, a living Tradition, a teaching office, a ladder of authority — exists only if God has actually spoken, and only to transmit *that*. So this lesson asks what revelation is, and the answer decides a surprising amount downstream. Get it wrong in one direction and the faith becomes a list of divinely guaranteed sentences with nobody at the far end; wrong in the other and it becomes an experience with no content anyone can be asked to hold, at which point "the Church teaches X" means nothing at all.

## The idea

**Revelation is God's self-communication**: he makes known not merely facts about himself but himself, and invites us into fellowship with him. That is the headline of *Dei Verbum*, the Second Vatican Council's dogmatic constitution on divine revelation (1965), whose first chapter is the charter text for this lesson.

Three features of that self-communication do all the work:

- **It happens in deeds and words together.** The Exodus is a deed; the covenant at Sinai interprets it. Neither half stands alone: the deeds would be mute events and the words unanchored claims.
- **It is historical and it culminates.** It runs through a history — patriarchs, prophets, the people Israel — and it ends somewhere, in a person rather than a document.
- **It exceeds us.** Some of what is communicated could never have been reached from the created world, and stays beyond our grasp even after we have received it. Those are the *mysteries*, and Vatican I defined that there are some.

Notice what is *not* being claimed: not that God dictated a book (that is a theory of inspiration, and a rejected one — see below), and not that revelation is a private feeling. It is a public act of self-disclosure with content.

## The argument

**A. What revelation is** — *Dei Verbum*, ch. 1 (1965), closely paraphrased; Vatican II is under copyright, so this is a summary, not its words.

> **1.** God, out of goodness and wisdom, chose to reveal himself and to make known the hidden purpose of his will — that human beings should have access to the Father through Christ and share the divine life.
> **2.** This plan is carried out by deeds and words that have an inner unity: the deeds confirm and display what the words teach, and the words proclaim the deeds and bring out the mystery contained in them.
> **3.** The plan unfolds through a history reaching its term in Christ, who is himself both the **mediator** of revelation — the one through whom it comes — and its **fullness** — the one in whom there is no more to come.
> **4.** Therefore no new public revelation is to be expected before Christ's glorious manifestation at the end.

In words: God tells us about himself by *doing things and saying what they mean*, over a long history, and the last and complete thing he says is a person. After that there is nothing further to be published; there is only understanding it better.

Scripture states the same shape in one sentence (Hebrews 1:1–2, Douay–Rheims):

> "God, who at sundry times and in divers manners, spoke in times past to the fathers by the prophets, last of all, in these days hath spoken to us by his Son."

Note the grammar: *spoke by the prophets*, then *spoke by his Son*. The Son is not the latest messenger in the series; he is the message, which is why the series stops.

**B. That some revealed truths exceed reason** — *Dei Filius* (Vatican I, 1870), ch. 4 with its first canon. The chapter teaches a **double order of knowledge**, distinct in principle and in object: reason working from nature, and faith working from what God has revealed. And it insists that hidden in God are **mysteries properly so called** (*vera et proprie dicta mysteria*), which no created mind could discover, and which — once revealed and believed — remain veiled and dark to us in this life. The first canon attached to the chapter condemns the contrary: that revelation contains no mysteries properly so called, but that reason, once properly developed, can understand and demonstrate every dogma from natural principles.

In words: some of what God has told us is not merely *news* — it is above what any created intellect could work out, before revelation or after it. **Level:** this is a solemn conciliar definition, so divinely revealed and proposed as such; denying it obstinately would be heresy.

Two consequences the canon *does not* draw, and which the same chapter explicitly leaves standing:

- **A mystery is not a contradiction.** Reason cannot demonstrate a mystery, but it can show that the arguments against it are not conclusive — the standing division of labour in this tradition (compare *Summa Theologiae* I, q.1, a.8, where sacred doctrine argues from authority and answers objections rather than proving the articles).
- **A mystery is not unintelligible.** *Dei Filius* ch. 4 says reason enlightened by faith reaches some understanding of the mysteries — by analogy with what we know naturally, from the way the mysteries hang together, and from their relation to our final end. Some understanding, never comprehension.

**Mysteries strictly so called, and the class next door.** The distinction that matters here:

| | Reachable by reason in principle? | Comprehended once revealed? | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Mystery strictly so called** | No | No | the Trinity; the Incarnation; Christ's presence under the eucharistic species |
| **Naturally knowable truth, also revealed** | Yes | Yes, in principle | that God exists and is one; that he created all things |

The second row is why [1.2](01-02-natural-knowledge-of-god.md) mattered. Revelation contains truths reason could have reached; they are revealed anyway, for the reason Aquinas gives in *ST* I, q.1, a.1 — otherwise they would be known by few, late, and mixed with error. Calling those "mysteries" blurs exactly the line Vatican I drew.

**C. Revelation is not inspiration.** Two different charisms, constantly confused:

- **Revelation** makes a truth known that was hidden. Its subject is a *truth*, and the recipient learns something.
- **Inspiration** moves a human writer so that what he writes is God's own word, with God and the writer both true authors. Its subject is a *text*, and the writer may be writing down what he already knew perfectly well from ordinary sources.

Neither entails the other. A biblical author can be inspired while receiving no new revelation at all; a prophet can receive revelation and write nothing. [3.1](03-01-inspiration.md) is where inspiration is actually taught — including why dictation theories were rejected — and this lesson only plants the flag.

## Map of positions

Avery Dulles's *Models of Revelation* (1983) sorts modern accounts into five types. Dulles was a theologian — a cardinal from 2001, but these are his books, not acts of teaching — so the grid has the authority of its argument and nothing more. It is worth knowing because the two poles the Catholic account has to hold together are the first two rows, and the live misreadings live in rows 3 and 5.

| Model | Revelation is essentially… | Associated with | Catholic verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Doctrine** | a body of truths God has stated, guaranteed by his authority | neo-scholastic manuals; conservative Protestant theology | true as far as it goes; incomplete on its own |
| **History** | God's mighty deeds, read as acts | mid-century biblical theology (Cullmann, Pannenberg) | true as far as it goes; needs the interpreting word |
| **Inner experience** | an immediate encounter of the soul with God | Schleiermacher; Modernist theology | condemned in its Modernist form as a *substitute* for the other two |
| **Dialectical presence** | God's word as an event that confronts, never a possession | Barth, Brunner, Bultmann | the divine freedom it protects is real; the deposit it denies is not |
| **New awareness** | a shift in human consciousness under grace | various twentieth-century transcendental accounts | too thin to carry a deposit anyone could be bound to |

Dulles's own move is to look for what the models are each half-seeing, and to treat revelation as mediated through **symbol** — an event or person that discloses and involves you at once, rather than merely informing you. Note the shape of the move: it does not pick a winner, it asks what each model gets right.

**Why the tradition holds the first two rows together.** *Dei Verbum*'s "deeds and words" is exactly that refusal to choose. And the propositional side is not optional decoration: the mysteries canon presupposes that revelation contains truths that can be *stated*, or there would be nothing for a dogma to define. But the personal side has priority in this precise sense — Aquinas says the believer's act does not come to rest in the proposition but in the reality (*ST* II-II, q.1, a.2, in the replies). The formulas are how the self-communication is received, held, and handed on; they are the road, not the destination. Denying the road still leaves you nowhere.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (the clean case — running the mystery test).** Four claims a Catholic holds. For each, ask two questions in order: *is it part of public revelation?* and *could created reason have reached it, and can it comprehend it now?*

- **"God is one."** Revealed (it is all over scripture), and also naturally knowable in principle, per [1.2](01-02-natural-knowledge-of-god.md). So: a revealed truth, **not** a mystery strictly so called.
- **"The one God is Father, Son and Holy Spirit."** Revealed, unreachable by reason, and still beyond comprehension after we believe it. **Mystery strictly so called**, the standard example.
- **"The Word was made flesh."** Same verdict, and a useful contrast: the *fact* that a man lived in first-century Palestine is an ordinary historical claim anyone can investigate; what is a mystery is that this man is the eternal Son. The mystery is in the identification, not in the biography.
- **"Christ is truly present under the eucharistic species."** Revealed; unreachable and incomprehensible; **mystery strictly so called**. Note that its *definition* (Trent, Session XIII) is a later act — defining a mystery does not dissolve it, and does not add to revelation either.

The pattern: "mystery" is not a grade of difficulty. It is a claim about the relation between a truth and any created intellect whatever.

**Example 2 (the hard case — where a good instinct goes wrong).** A homilist says: *"Revelation is a person, not a set of propositions. The Council taught us that. So the old formulas are a stage we have moved past — what matters is the encounter they once pointed to."*

Grant what is right, because a good deal is. Revelation really does culminate in a person; *Dei Verbum*'s language really is personal where the manuals' was propositional; Aquinas really does say faith terminates in the reality and not in the sentence. There was also a real shift of emphasis at the Council — the preparatory draft on "the sources of revelation" was set aside early in the first session and eventually replaced, which is why the final text reads as it does. (The politics of that belongs to [`church-history`](../../church-history/syllabus.md).)

Now the three failures.

1. **"Not a set of propositions" is a false alternative.** *Dei Verbum* ch. 1 says God reveals himself *and* the hidden purpose of his will, in deeds *and* words. It does not oppose the person to the content; it says the content is how the person is given to us.
2. **"The Council taught us" overstates and misreports.** A dogmatic constitution is a heavyweight genre, but *Dei Verbum* explicitly builds on Vatican I rather than retiring it — the mysteries canon and the double order of knowledge stand exactly as they stood in 1870. A council does not repeal a previous council's definition, and no text of Vatican II claims to.
3. **"The formulas are a stage we have moved past" misstates what a dogma is.** That formulas are historically conditioned, and that their conditioning does not make them dispensable or reversible, is the subject of *Mysterium Ecclesiae* (1973) and of [5.2](05-02-newmans-theory-of-development.md). Growth in understanding is precisely what closure leaves room for; retirement of what was defined is not.

The symmetric error is worth naming because it is the same mistake: someone who says "revelation is the deposit of propositions, full stop" also collapses the two into one and loses the reason revelation ends in a person rather than a book.

## Watch out

- **You might think closure of public revelation means nothing more can be learned.** It means nothing more is *published*. *Dei Verbum* holds both: the deposit is complete, and understanding of it grows — Module 5's entire subject. The manuals put the closure at the death of the last apostle; the doctrine is that the apostolic age is the terminus, not a calendar date.
- **You might think a solemn definition adds to revelation.** It cannot, by this lesson's own doctrine. A definition proposes as revealed something already contained in the deposit. This is why 1950 defines a dogma without being a new revelation — and the confusion is the commonest popular objection to Marian dogma.
- **You might think "mystery" means "we haven't figured it out yet."** In the strict sense it means above created reason as such — no amount of figuring reaches it. Conversely, do not call a merely difficult doctrine a mystery to end a conversation; Vatican I's own chapter insists reason has real work to do on the mysteries.
- **You might think Dulles's models are a ladder of authority.** They are a theologian's taxonomy. Models 1 and 2 name emphases within Catholic theology, not permitted and forbidden positions; only the Modernist form of model 3 was actually condemned, and it was condemned as a replacement for revelation's objective content.
- **Don't merge revelation, inspiration and infallibility.** Revelation makes truth known; inspiration makes a text God's word ([3.1](03-01-inspiration.md)); the Magisterium's charism is assistance in guarding and proposing what was revealed, not a new communication ([4.2](04-02-infallibility-and-its-conditions.md)). Three charisms, three different questions.

## One-liner

> Revelation is God giving himself in deeds and words, completed in Christ and closed with the apostles — so a dogma can only unfold the deposit, never add to it.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢) *(Exegetical.)*** For each claim, say (i) whether it belongs to public revelation, and (ii) if so, whether it is a mystery strictly so called. One sentence each.

(a) God is one and is the creator of all things.
(b) The one God is Father, Son and Holy Spirit.
(c) Christ is truly present under the eucharistic species.
(d) Mary appeared at Fatima in 1917.

**P2 (🟡) *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)*** An invented paragraph from a diocesan formation handout:

> "Vatican II taught that revelation is a personal encounter with Christ, not a set of propositions. That is why the old doctrinal formulas no longer bind: what matters is the experience they once pointed to. *Dei Verbum* replaced the propositional model of Vatican I, and since a council is the highest authority there is, the earlier definitions have lapsed."

(a) Name three things this gets wrong — at least one about what *Dei Verbum* ch. 1 actually says, and at least one about levels of authority and what a later council can do to an earlier one. (b) A reader replies that the paragraph's instinct is sound even if its execution is sloppy: the personal model really is the deeper one, and the propositions really are secondary. In 150 words or fewer, assess the reply — any verdict, provided you say in what sense the tradition does make the formulas secondary and in what sense it does not.

**P3 (🔴, optional) *(Exegetical.)*** For each, name which charism is at work — revelation, inspiration, both, or neither — and say whether any *new* public revelation is involved. One sentence each, then one sentence on what the three cases together show.

(a) The author of 2 Maccabees explains in his preface that he has condensed a longer five-volume history by another writer into one book.
(b) Paul tells the Galatians that the gospel he preaches came to him not from men but by revelation (Galatians 1:11–12).
(c) In 1950 a pope defines the bodily Assumption of Mary.

<details>
<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical — strict.)*

**Must hit, strict:**
- (a) Public revelation, yes — but **not** a mystery strictly so called: *Dei Filius* ch. 2 holds this is reachable by natural reason in principle ([1.2](01-02-natural-knowledge-of-god.md)). Being revealed and being above reason are different properties.
- (b) Public revelation; **mystery strictly so called** — unreachable before revelation, uncomprehended after it.
- (c) Public revelation; **mystery strictly so called**. Its definition at Trent fixes the doctrine's level, not its intelligibility.
- (d) **Not public revelation at all.** Private revelation, whose weight is [1.4](01-04-public-and-private-revelation.md)'s subject; approval of an apparition does not place it in the deposit, and no Catholic is bound in faith to it.

**Wrong turns:** calling (a) a mystery because it is about God — the *subject* is divine, the truth is not above reason; calling (d) a mystery because it is supernatural, which confuses a supernatural event with a revealed truth; treating a later definition as making something more or less mysterious.

**Model answer:** (a) revealed, not a strict mystery. (b) revealed, strict mystery. (c) revealed, strict mystery. (d) not part of public revelation; private revelation, binding no one in faith.

---

**P2** *(a) exegetical — strict; (b) evaluative — any verdict.*

**Must hit, strict (a):** any three of —
1. "*Not* a set of propositions" is a false alternative; *Dei Verbum* ch. 1 has God revealing himself *and* the purpose of his will, through deeds *and* words that interpret each other.
2. The Council did not "replace" Vatican I. *Dei Verbum* builds on it; the mysteries canon and the double order of knowledge stand.
3. "A council is the highest authority there is, so earlier definitions have lapsed" is a level error twice over — a solemn definition is irreformable in what it defines, and a later council has no power to repeal one. Councils do not outrank each other.
4. "The formulas no longer bind" misstates what a dogma is: historically conditioned language is still determinately true (the question *Mysterium Ecclesiae*, 1973, and [5.2](05-02-newmans-theory-of-development.md) take up).

**Must hit, any verdict (b):** state the sense in which the formulas *are* secondary — the believer's act terminates in the reality, not the sentence (*ST* II-II, q.1, a.2), and revelation culminates in a person; **and** the sense in which they are not — the mysteries canon presupposes statable truths, and without them "the Church teaches X" has no content and nothing can be handed on. Either verdict on the paragraph's "instinct" passes if both senses are given.

**Wrong turns:** treating (b) as settled by (a) — the paragraph can be wrong in every particular while its instinct is defensible; arguing that Vatican II was invalid or that Vatican I was superseded, neither of which is what was asked.

**Model answer (b), one of several:** The instinct is right and the execution destroys it. *Dei Verbum*'s personalism is a genuine recovery: revelation is God giving himself, and faith comes to rest in him, not in a sentence about him. But "secondary" has to mean *ordered to* rather than *dispensable*. The propositions are how a self-communication given once in history reaches someone who was not there — they are the only thing that can be handed on, defined, or disputed. Drop them and the encounter has no identifiable object, and no one can say what the Church teaches, which is precisely the state the handout's author is in. The instinct survives; it needs the word half of "deeds and words" to have anything to be an instinct about.

---

**P3** *(Exegetical — strict.)*

**Must hit, strict:**
- (a) **Inspiration, with no new revelation.** The epitomist is working from an existing history by ordinary means; what makes his book God's word is the charism moving him as a true human author, not any new communication.
- (b) **Revelation** — Paul claims a genuine new communication — and his letter is also **inspired**, so both, in one person, for different reasons. Being a recipient of revelation does not make one's writing inspired, and vice versa; here both happen to hold.
- (c) **Neither.** A definition is an act of the teaching office under the charism of assistance ([4.2](04-02-infallibility-and-its-conditions.md)): it proposes as revealed something already in the deposit. Public revelation closed with the apostolic age, so 1950 adds nothing to it.
- **Together:** the three charisms are independent, and only the apostolic-age ones involve new public revelation.

**Wrong turns:** saying (a) must involve revelation because the book is scripture — that is the dictation picture [3.1](03-01-inspiration.md) rejects; saying (c) is revelation because a dogma was defined, which is the commonest popular error about Marian dogma and contradicts closure.

**Model answer:** (a) inspiration only, no new revelation. (b) both, distinctly. (c) neither — an act of the Magisterium's assistance, drawing on a closed deposit. Revelation, inspiration and magisterial assistance are three different charisms answering three different questions.

</details>

## Flashback

*(Exegetical.)* A footnote in a book on the Eucharist reads:

> See Trent, Session XIII, ch. 4 and canon 2; *Mysterium Fidei* (1965); *Summa Theologiae* III, q.75; and the treatment in Ott, *Fundamentals of Catholic Dogma*.

Rank these four by teaching weight, highest first, and for each say in one clause what it can and cannot settle. Which one is not an act of teaching at all?

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict:**
1. **Trent, Session XIII, ch. 4 and canon 2 (1551)** — a conciliar act, the heaviest genre. The chapter expounds; the **canon** is where the definition bites, so that is what you read to know what is defined.
2. ***Mysterium Fidei* (1965)** — a papal encyclical: authentic ordinary magisterium. It teaches and can insist; it normally defines nothing, and here it restates and applies Trent rather than adding to it.
3. ***ST* III, q.75** — Aquinas, a theologian, however eminent. Settles nothing by authority; its force is the argument in it.
4. **Ott** — a twentieth-century manual. **Not an act of teaching at all.** It is a handbook reporting the tradition's own qualifications, so it is useful evidence of how theologians have labelled a claim, and no evidence of the claim's level by itself.

**Wrong turns:** promoting Ott because it prints theological notes — the note is a report, not a source of authority, exactly like a DH number locating a text rather than weighting it; ranking Aquinas above an encyclical because he is more famous; reading Trent's chapter for the definition instead of its canon.

</details>
