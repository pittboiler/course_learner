# Economics of Debt - Syllabus

> Economics & Finance · Tier 2 · ~30 lessons · Prereqs: [`grad-micro`](../grad-micro/syllabus.md), [`grad-macro`](../grad-macro/syllabus.md) · Roadmap id: `economics-of-debt`

## Goal

Learn why debt exists, what it does to the people and governments who owe it, and what happens when it cannot be paid. The course keeps asking one question: *a fixed promise meets an uncertain world, so who bears the loss, and does anticipating that loss change what gets borrowed, built and risked beforehand?* It starts with the contract. Debt is the optimal contract when outcomes are costly to verify (Townsend, Gale-Hellwig). The same information problems ration credit (Stiglitz-Weiss) and make net worth and collateral matter (Holmström-Tirole). Next comes the firm: Modigliani-Miller and the three ways it breaks (taxes, bankruptcy costs, agency), then Myers's debt overhang. The course moves on to banks and runs (Diamond-Dybvig), collateral and amplification (Bernanke-Gertler-Gilchrist, Kiyotaki-Moore, Geanakoplos's leverage cycle), and then to Fisher's debt-deflation, Minsky made formal, and the household-debt evidence on 2008 (Mian and Sufi). The second half is public and sovereign debt: debt dynamics and $r-g$, tax smoothing, fiscal limits and the fiscal theory of the price level, and sovereign default through the transfer problem, reputation (Eaton-Gersovitz), the Bulow-Rogoff critique, the Arellano model and self-fulfilling rollover crises. It ends with the machinery for bad debts: haircuts, the debt Laffer curve, holdouts and collective action clauses, bankruptcy design, and a short module that treats debt relief (the fresh start, a Jubilee-style scheduled release, contingent debt) as mechanism design.

You will finish able to derive the standard debt contract, find the interest rate at which a bank stops lending, value a levered firm with taxes and distress costs, decide whether shareholders will fund a positive-NPV project under overhang and what write-down fixes it, locate the run threshold of a bank, price an asset with and without leverage, compute the natural rate during deleveraging, find the debt-stabilizing primary surplus, check whether reputation alone can sustain sovereign borrowing, and compute nominal and present-value haircuts. This course is the formal member of the **debt thread**. [`history-of-debt`](../history-of-debt/syllabus.md) supplies the episodes (clean slates, 1688, reparations, Brady, HIPC, 2008, Greece, the holdout suits) and hands several of its cruxes to this course's models. [`philosophy-of-debt`](../philosophy-of-debt/syllabus.md) asks what is owed and whether forgiveness is just. [`theology-of-debt`](../theology-of-debt/syllabus.md) reads the Jubilee and usury from inside the Catholic tradition. This course stays positive: it computes who gains and loses from forgiveness and never rules on whether forgiveness is right. Deliberately skipped: bond and credit-derivative pricing beyond what the models need ([`mathematical-finance`](../mathematical-finance/syllabus.md)), monetary policy transmission for its own sake, bank regulation in detail (Basel), payout policy and security design beyond debt vs equity, and the econometrics behind the evidence lessons (cited to [`econometrics`](../econometrics/syllabus.md)).

## Dangerous Checklist

When you finish, you can:

- [ ] Derive the standard debt contract from costly state verification, solve for the face value that finances a project, and find the lender's revenue ceiling beyond which credit is rationed
- [ ] Show why raising the loan rate can lower a bank's expected return (Stiglitz-Weiss), and compute the minimum net worth that gets a moral-hazard-prone borrower funded (Holmström-Tirole)
- [ ] Prove Modigliani-Miller by arbitrage, then value a levered firm with a corporate tax shield and expected distress costs and find the trade-off optimum
- [ ] Explain risk-shifting as the convexity of equity's payoff, and decide whether shareholders will fund a positive-NPV project under debt overhang and which write-downs make both classes better off
- [ ] Solve Diamond-Dybvig for the optimal deposit contract, find the withdrawal fraction that triggers a run, and say what suspension, deposit insurance and a lender of last resort each fix and cost
- [ ] Explain how net worth drives the external finance premium and how collateral prices amplify shocks (Bernanke-Gertler-Gilchrist, Kiyotaki-Moore), and price an asset with and without collateralized leverage (Geanakoplos)
- [ ] Compute the market-clearing real rate in a borrower-saver deleveraging model, show how deflation on nominal debt deepens it, and classify borrowers as Minsky hedge, speculative or Ponzi units
- [ ] Read the Mian-Sufi household-debt evidence as an IV design, state its exclusion restriction and main threats, and say exactly what Adelino-Schoar-Severino dispute
- [ ] Compute a debt-ratio path, the debt-stabilizing primary surplus, and what changes when $r<g$, and derive the tax-smoothing prescription for a temporary spending shock
- [ ] Explain the fiscal limit, unpleasant monetarist arithmetic and the fiscal theory of the price level, and compute the price level that revalues nominal debt to match surpluses
- [ ] Find when reputation alone sustains sovereign borrowing, reproduce the Bulow-Rogoff argument that it cannot, derive a bond price schedule under output sanctions, and locate a Cole-Kehoe crisis zone
- [ ] Compute nominal and present-value haircuts, explain the debt Laffer curve and why buybacks favor creditors, model holdouts as a coordination failure that collective action clauses solve, and price the effect of a scheduled debt release on credit

## Modules

### Module 1: Why debt? Contracts under hidden information

Debt is odd: a fixed payment that ignores how the project turned out. This module shows that the oddness is the point. Debt minimizes how often anyone has to look, and the same information problems explain why some borrowers get no loan at any price.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1.1 | Costly state verification: debt as the optimal contract | Derive the standard debt contract from the cost of verifying outcomes | Townsend (1979), Gale-Hellwig (1985), truth-telling constraints, verification only in default, deadweight audit cost, the lender's revenue ceiling (Williamson 1987) |
| 1.2 | Credit rationing: Stiglitz-Weiss | Show why a bank may refuse to raise the rate to clear the loan market | limited liability and convex borrower payoffs, adverse selection and risk incentives through the rate, the backward-bending expected return, equilibrium rationing, de Meza-Webb over-lending as the contrast |
| 1.3 | Pledgeable income, collateral and monitors | Explain why net worth and collateral decide who gets credit | Holmström-Tirole (1997) moral hazard, pledgeable income and the minimum net worth, collateral as a screening device (Bester 1985), inalienable human capital (Hart-Moore 1994), delegated monitoring by banks (Diamond 1984) |

**Boss problem 1:** (a) An entrepreneur with no wealth needs 1 unit to fund a project whose return $y$ is uniform on $[0,4]$ and privately observed. The lender is risk-neutral, the safe rate is zero, and verifying $y$ costs $c=1$. Write the lender's expected revenue $L(D)$ from a standard debt contract with face value $D$, find the $D$ that finances the project, and compute the expected verification cost and the entrepreneur's expected payoff. (b) Find the $D$ that maximizes $L(D)$ and the maximum revenue. What happens if the project costs 1.2 instead, and why doesn't a higher face value help? (c) A bank faces two borrower types, each needing 1 unit, with no collateral and a zero outside option: 75 percent are safe (return 2 for sure) and 25 percent are risky (return 4 with probability 0.4, else 0). Write the bank's expected repayment per loan as a function of the gross rate $R$, and find the return-maximizing $R$. If 100 borrowers apply and depositors supply $50\rho$ units when paid gross return $\rho$, how many borrowers are rationed, and why can't they bid their way in? (d) A Holmström-Tirole project costs $I=1.5$ and returns 2 on success, 0 on failure. Success probability is 0.8 if the entrepreneur is diligent and 0.6 if she shirks, and shirking gives her a private benefit of 0.1. Find her minimum incentive stake, the pledgeable income, and the minimum net worth needed for funding, and confirm that the project is worth funding only with diligence.

### Module 2: Capital structure and debt overhang

Modigliani and Miller proved that financing does not matter. Every reason it does matter in practice is a violation of one of their assumptions, and the most dangerous is that old debt can stop a firm from making good new investments.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2.1 | Modigliani-Miller | Prove capital-structure irrelevance by arbitrage and read its assumptions as a checklist | Proposition I by homemade leverage (law of one price, cited), Proposition II and the cost of equity, WACC, what "no frictions" really requires |
| 2.2 | Taxes and bankruptcy costs: the trade-off theory | Value a levered firm when interest is deductible and distress is costly | the interest tax shield $T_c D$, Miller (1977) and personal taxes, direct and indirect distress costs, the interior optimum, pecking order (Myers-Majluf 1984) named as the rival |
| 2.3 | Agency costs of debt and equity | See why shareholders of a levered firm gamble, and why debt also disciplines managers | Jensen-Meckling (1976), equity as a call option on firm assets (Merton 1974; formula cited), asset substitution and risk-shifting, free cash flow (Jensen 1986), covenants |
| 2.4 | Debt overhang | Show how existing debt makes shareholders reject positive-NPV projects, and what fixes it | Myers (1977), the investment subsidy to creditors, overhang as a $q$ wedge, write-downs and debt-equity swaps, why dispersed creditors fail to renegotiate |

**Boss problem 2:** A firm earns expected operating income of 100 per year forever, and its unlevered cost of capital is 10 percent. (a) With no taxes, it issues perpetual riskless debt of 400 at 5 percent and buys back equity. Compute the new cost of equity and the WACC, and explain why the WACC did not fall even though debt is cheaper. (b) Now add a 25 percent corporate tax. Compute the unlevered value and the levered value with debt 400. (c) Add expected distress costs with present value $D^2/2000$. Find the value-maximizing debt level and the firm's value there. (d) A different firm has existing debt with face value 80, due next period. Its assets in place will be worth 60 or 100 with equal probability, and all rates are zero. Shareholders can fund a new project costing 10 today that pays 18 for sure next period. Show the project has positive NPV but shareholders reject it, and compute how the project's value would split between the classes. (e) Find the range of face values $F$ to which creditors could write their claim down such that shareholders invest *and* both classes end up better off than without the project. Explain why this deal is hard to reach when the debt is held by thousands of bondholders (a preview of 7.2).

### Module 3: Banks, runs, collateral and amplification

Banks turn illiquid loans into liquid deposits, and that makes them fragile. The whole economy's borrowing rests on collateral whose price depends on that borrowing, and that makes the economy fragile as well.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3.1 | Diamond-Dybvig | Derive the deposit contract as liquidity insurance and find its second, bad equilibrium | preference shocks, optimal risk-sharing $u'(c_1)=R\,u'(c_2)$, demand deposits, the run equilibrium as a coordination failure, the run threshold |
| 3.2 | Stopping runs | Compare the tools that remove the run equilibrium and the incentives each creates | suspension of convertibility, deposit insurance, Bagehot's lender of last resort, moral hazard and the case for capital requirements, global-games selection (Goldstein-Pauzner) as a sketch |
| 3.3 | The financial accelerator | Show how borrower net worth moves the cost of external finance and propagates shocks | Bernanke-Gertler (1989), Bernanke-Gertler-Gilchrist (1999), the external finance premium as the CSV cost from 1.1, net worth as a state variable, the flight to quality |
| 3.4 | Kiyotaki-Moore: collateral cycles | Trace how a small, temporary shock becomes a large, persistent one through land prices | collateral constraint $b_t \le q_{t+1}k_t/R$, the static and dynamic multipliers, the interaction of asset prices and credit limits, persistence through net worth |
| 3.5 | The leverage cycle | Make margins endogenous and see leverage raise asset prices in booms and crash them in busts | Geanakoplos (2010), heterogeneous beliefs and the marginal buyer, collateral equilibrium with the maximum riskless promise, margin spikes, repo haircuts and the run on repo (Gorton-Metrick), fire sales (Shleifer-Vishny) |

**Boss problem 3:** (a) In a Diamond-Dybvig economy, each depositor puts 1 unit into a bank at date 0. A share $\pi=0.25$ of depositors turn out impatient. The technology returns $R=2$ at date 2 or 1 if liquidated at date 1, and utility is CRRA with relative risk aversion 2. Find the optimal $(c_1, c_2)$ and verify the resource constraint. (b) Find the fraction $f$ of depositors withdrawing at date 1 above which a patient depositor prefers to withdraw too, and the fraction at which the bank is emptied. Explain how suspending convertibility once withdrawals reach $\pi$ removes the run equilibrium, and what it costs if $\pi$ is uncertain. (c) An asset pays 1 next period in the good state and 0.2 in the bad state, and there is 1 unit of it. A continuum of agents with belief $h \sim U[0,1]$ that the good state occurs each hold 1 unit of cash, and the riskless rate is zero. Find the price and the marginal buyer with no borrowing, and again when agents can borrow against the asset up to the maximum riskless promise. Report the implied loan-to-value ratio. (d) The bad-state payoff falls to 0.1 while nothing else changes. Recompute the leveraged price and loan-to-value ratio. Compare the price drop with the old marginal buyer's loss in expected payoff, and explain the gap. (e) Without solving: explain why a one-period productivity shock in Kiyotaki-Moore lowers land prices for many periods, and connect the mechanism to the margin spike in (d).

### Module 4: Debt-deflation, Minsky and 2008

Fisher said that in a crash the debt itself was the disease. Minsky said that stability breeds the debts that end it. This module gives both a model and then asks what the household data from 2008 actually show.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4.1 | Fisher's debt-deflation, formalized | Show how forced deleveraging drives the natural rate negative and how falling prices make it worse | Fisher (1933), borrowers vs savers and MPC heterogeneity, the Eggertsson-Krugman (2012) deleveraging shock, the natural rate and the zero lower bound (cited), the paradox of flexibility, aggregate demand externalities of leverage (Korinek-Simsek; Farhi-Werning) |
| 4.2 | Minsky, informally and formally | State the financial instability hypothesis precisely and see which modern models capture it | hedge, speculative and Ponzi finance as cash-flow inequalities, "stability is destabilizing", extrapolative and diagnostic expectations (Bordalo-Gennaioli-Shleifer 2018), credit-market sentiment predicting reversals (López-Salido-Stein-Zakrajšek 2017), credit booms and crisis risk (Schularick-Taylor 2012) |
| 4.3 | Household debt and the Great Recession: the evidence | Read the Mian-Sufi program as a set of research designs and weigh its critics | household leverage and the 2007-09 spending drop (Mian-Rao-Sufi 2013), housing supply elasticity as an instrument (Saiz 2010) and its critics, non-tradable employment (Mian-Sufi 2014), foreclosure externalities, the Adelino-Schoar-Severino (2016) middle-class-borrower challenge, cross-country household debt and cycles (Mian-Sufi-Verner 2017) |

**Boss problem 4:** (a) Three firms each face interest rate $i$. Firm A has debt 100, 10 of principal due this year, and cash flow 20. Firm B has debt 200, principal due 20, cash flow 12. Firm C has debt 300, principal due 30, cash flow 14. Classify each as a Minsky hedge, speculative or Ponzi unit at $i=4$ percent and at $i=7$ percent, and explain why this reclassification is the mechanism Minsky had in mind. (b) Borrowers and savers, of equal mass, each receive an endowment of 1 per period. Savers have discount factor $\beta=0.96$. Borrowers are at their debt limit and have been rolling over debt with face value 0.5 at the steady-state rate $\bar r = 1/\beta - 1$. At date 1 the limit falls permanently to 0.3. From date 2 on, savers consume $1 + 0.3\,\bar r/(1+\bar r)$. Write savers' date-1 consumption and Euler equation, and solve for the real rate that clears the goods market at date 1. (c) Now suppose the legacy debt of 0.5 is nominal and the price level falls 5 percent at date 1. Recompute the market-clearing real rate, and explain in one sentence why this is Fisher's mechanism. (d) If the nominal rate cannot go below zero and expected inflation is zero, explain what must adjust instead of the real rate, and why lower price flexibility could make things better here. (e) A study compares US counties. Counties with inelastic housing supply saw housing net worth fall 20 percentage points more between 2006 and 2009, and their consumption fell 6 percentage points more. Compute the Wald/IV estimate of the elasticity of consumption with respect to housing net worth. State the exclusion restriction and the strongest threat to it. Then say what a larger coefficient in high-leverage counties implies for (b), and why the Adelino-Schoar-Severino critique is about *who borrowed*, not about this elasticity.

### Module 5: Public debt

A government that is expected to outlive its creditors can roll its debt over indefinitely. What it cannot do is run deficits forever, unless growth, inflation or default pays for them. This module sets out the arithmetic and the theory behind each of those options.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5.1 | The government budget constraint and debt dynamics | Compute debt-ratio paths and the surplus that stabilizes them | the flow identity $d_{t+1} = \frac{1+r}{1+g}d_t - s_t$, the debt-stabilizing surplus $s^* = \frac{r-g}{1+g}d$, the intertemporal budget constraint and no-Ponzi as transversality (cited), fiscal reaction functions (Bohn 1998), Ricardian equivalence (cited) |
| 5.2 | When $r<g$ | Decide what low interest rates do and do not license | Blanchard (2019), deterministic rollover and the dynamic-efficiency link (cited), uncertain $r-g$ and fan-chart sustainability analysis, the marginal vs average rate, what makes $r-g$ flip sign |
| 5.3 | Tax smoothing and optimal debt | Derive why deficits should absorb temporary spending shocks | Barro (1979) random-walk taxes, convex tax distortions, war finance as the canonical case, state-contingent debt (Lucas-Stokey 1983), incomplete markets (Aiyagari-Marcet-Sargent-Seppälä 2002) |
| 5.4 | Fiscal limits and inflation | Explain how debt becomes an inflation problem and what the fiscal theory claims | the Laffer-bounded fiscal limit, seigniorage and unpleasant monetarist arithmetic (Sargent-Wallace 1981), active and passive policy (Leeper 1991), the fiscal theory of the price level $B/P = \text{PV of surpluses}$ (briefly), inflation and financial repression as debt reduction |

**Boss problem 5:** (a) Debt is 90 percent of GDP, the real interest rate on it is 3 percent and real growth is 4 percent. Compute the debt-stabilizing primary balance, then recompute it with the interest rate at 5 percent. (b) At $r=5$ percent, $g=4$ percent and a zero primary balance, how many years until debt reaches 120 percent of GDP? (c) GDP is constant at 1, the interest rate is 4 percent, and government spending is 0.2 per year forever except for a one-year war at date 0 that adds 0.2. Tax distortion costs are proportional to the square of the tax rate. Compare two policies: balancing the budget every year, and a constant tax rate from date 0 onward. Find the smoothing tax rate, the debt left after the war and the interest that services it, and the percentage reduction in the present value of distortion costs. (d) Explain why Barro's prescription implies taxes follow a random walk, and what Lucas-Stokey add if the government can issue debt contingent on the war. (e) Nominal debt is 110 and the present value of expected real primary surpluses is 100. Under the fiscal theory, what is the price level? If the surplus outlook falls to 80 with no change in nominal debt, what happens to the price level? Say which policy regime (Leeper) this requires and why a monetarist would reject the conclusion.

### Module 6: Sovereign default

No court can seize a country. So why do sovereigns repay, and why do lenders lend to them at all? This module goes from the capacity to pay, to the willingness to pay, to the quantitative model, and then to crises that happen because lenders fear them.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 6.1 | Ability to pay: the transfer problem and original sin | Show why paying foreign creditors requires a trade surplus and a real depreciation, and why that can raise the debt | the Keynes-Ohlin (1929) transfer debate (the German case is in [`history-of-debt`](../history-of-debt/syllabus.md) 5.2), trade elasticities and the required real depreciation, foreign-currency debt and original sin (Eichengreen-Hausmann), ability vs willingness to pay |
| 6.2 | Willingness to pay: reputation and Eaton-Gersovitz | Find when the threat of exclusion from credit markets sustains repayment | Eaton-Gersovitz (1981), sovereign borrowing as insurance, exclusion as a repeated-game punishment (cited), the participation constraint, maximum sustainable debt, reputation in the historical record (Tomz; see [`history-of-debt`](../history-of-debt/syllabus.md) 4.6) |
| 6.3 | Bulow-Rogoff: why reputation is not enough, and what sanctions add | Reproduce the cash-in-advance argument and see what enforcement must come from instead | Bulow-Rogoff (1989), the deviation to saving plus foreign insurance, direct sanctions (trade, asset seizure, gunboats), bargaining over repayment, domestic creditors and institutional commitment (the historians' 1688 crux in [`history-of-debt`](../history-of-debt/syllabus.md) 3.2) |
| 6.4 | The Arellano model | Set up the quantitative default model and read its predictions | Arellano (2008), the Bellman equations for repay and default, the bond price schedule $q(b', y)$, asymmetric output costs, default in bad times, countercyclical spreads, value function iteration (sketch), Aguiar-Gopinath (2006) |
| 6.5 | Self-fulfilling debt crises | Locate the crisis zone where a run on the sovereign is an equilibrium, and what shrinks it | Calvo (1988), Cole-Kehoe (2000), rollover risk and maturity, the sovereign lender of last resort (the Eurozone case is in [`history-of-debt`](../history-of-debt/syllabus.md) 6.2), multiple equilibria vs fundamentals |

**Boss problem 6:** (a) A country must turn a balanced trade account into a surplus of 3 percent of GDP to service foreign debt. Exports and imports are each 20 percent of GDP, with elasticities to the real exchange rate of 1.5 and 0.5. Approximate the required real depreciation. Then compute the new ratio if foreign-currency debt was 60 percent of GDP and GDP in domestic real terms is unchanged. (b) A small open economy has i.i.d. income of 1.2 or 0.8 with equal probability, log utility and discount factor $\beta$. Risk-neutral foreign lenders offer full insurance at fair terms: consumption 1 in every state. If the country deviates, it is excluded from all future insurance forever. Find the smallest $\beta$ for which the insurance contract is self-enforcing, and check $\beta=0.85$ and $\beta=0.9$. (c) Bulow and Rogoff let the deviating country keep buying insurance, paid for in advance with a foreign deposit. Explain in steps why this breaks the equilibrium in (b), and state which assumption a sanction-based model drops to restore lending. (d) Now there is no reputation. A default costs the country 10 percent of output, output next period is 0.8 or 1.2 with equal probability, and the country repays face value $b$ exactly when repaying is cheaper than the sanction. Lenders are risk-neutral with a 5 percent safe rate. Derive the bond price schedule $q(b)$ and find the face value that maximizes the amount borrowed today. Explain how this schedule is a one-period version of Arellano's $q(b', y)$. (e) Honoring maturing debt $b$ costs $b$ if lenders roll it over and $b + 2b^2$ if they refuse and it must be repaid at once. Default costs 1. Find the crisis zone, where the sovereign repays only if lenders roll over. Then recompute its lower edge when only a third of the debt matures each period, so a run costs $b + 2(b/3)^2$. Explain what a credible lender of last resort does to the zone.

### Module 7: Restructuring and bankruptcy

When debt cannot be paid, someone has to decide how much is written off, who takes the loss, and whether the firm or country survives. Firms have a court for this. Sovereigns have contract clauses and bargaining.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 7.1 | Haircuts, the debt Laffer curve and buybacks | Measure a restructuring's loss and see when forgiving debt raises what creditors collect | nominal vs present-value haircuts and exit yields (Sturzenegger-Zettelmeyer), haircuts and later market access (Cruces-Trebesch 2013), sovereign overhang and the debt Laffer curve (Krugman 1988; Sachs 1989), the buyback boondoggle (Bulow-Rogoff 1988), Brady and HIPC as applications ([`history-of-debt`](../history-of-debt/syllabus.md) 5.4-5.5) |
| 7.2 | Holdouts and collective action clauses | Model restructuring as a coordination game and show how majority clauses solve it | the atomistic creditor's free-ride, participation thresholds, the holdout premium, majority-restructuring and aggregation clauses (series-by-series vs single-limb), blocking stakes, the pari passu litigation as the payoff to holding out ([`history-of-debt`](../history-of-debt/syllabus.md) 6.3) |
| 7.3 | Designing bankruptcy | Explain why a collective procedure beats a creditor race, and the design choices inside it | the common-pool problem (Jackson's creditors' bargain), the automatic stay, absolute priority and its deviations, liquidation vs reorganization, class voting in Chapter 11, Bebchuk's options and Aghion-Hart-Moore auctions, why no sovereign bankruptcy court exists |

**Boss problem 7:** (a) A sovereign swaps an old bond (face 100, 8 percent annual coupon, 5 years left) for a new bond (face 70, 4 percent coupon, 10 years). At an exit yield of 10 percent, compute the nominal haircut, the present-value haircut (Sturzenegger-Zettelmeyer, both bonds discounted at the exit yield), and the market haircut against the old face value. Explain why the debtor quotes one number and creditors another. (b) A country owes face 100 but will pay 60 or 20 with equal probability whatever it owes, as long as it owes at least 60. Find the market price of its debt. The country uses 10 of its own reserves, which creditors could not otherwise seize, to buy back debt at the post-announcement market price. Find the face value retired and the new price, and say who gains. (c) A sovereign can pay at most 60 per 100 of face. It offers participants a new claim worth 50 and must pay any holdouts in full. What participation share makes the deal feasible? Show that without a collective action clause, atomistic creditors all hold out and everyone gets the default value of 20. Then show that an aggregated clause with a 75 percent threshold makes an offer of 55 pass. (d) A firm can be liquidated now for 60, or continued, in which case it is worth 150 or 30 next year with equal probability (no discounting). It owes a senior bank 50 and junior bondholders 50. Compute each class's payoff under liquidation and under continuation with absolute priority. Say which class votes for which outcome and why the choice that maximizes total value is not the senior creditor's choice. (e) Under Bebchuk's options with an agreed valuation of 90, say which options are exercised and the resulting allocation, and explain why the procedure respects absolute priority without an auction.

### Module 8: Debt relief as mechanism

Forgiveness done after the fact can be efficient and still destroy the credit it was meant to rescue. This short module treats relief as a rule written in advance, and asks what the rule does to lending before anyone needs relief.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 8.1 | Forgiveness, commitment and the fresh start | Separate the ex post gains of relief from its ex ante costs, and see personal bankruptcy as insurance | rules vs discretion (Kydland-Prescott) applied to forgiveness, strategic default and moral hazard as incentive effects, the discharge as consumption insurance vs higher borrowing costs (Livshits-MacGee-Tertilt 2007; Chatterjee-Corbae-Nakajima-Ríos-Rull 2007), exemptions, evidence on the fresh start (Dobbie-Song 2015) |
| 8.2 | The scheduled release: the Jubilee as a mechanism | Price credit under a known, periodic cancellation date and see what the rule protects against and what it costs | release laws as a contract feature (the texts in [`history-of-debt`](../history-of-debt/syllabus.md) 1.2-1.3), break-even repayment as a function of years to release, land priced as a lease running to the Jubilee, credit drying up before the release year (the worry Deuteronomy 15 itself raises), the *prosbul* as an opt-out that removes the commitment, periodic release as insurance against absorbing debt traps |
| 8.3 | Relief written into the contract | Compare state-contingent debt with ad hoc forgiveness as ways to share bad outcomes | GDP-linked bonds (Shiller; Borensztein-Mauro 2004), shared-responsibility mortgages (Mian-Sufi), conditional relief as a mechanism (HIPC's completion point), why contingent debt is rare: verification and manipulation of the index (back to 1.1) |

**Boss problem 8:** (a) A lender with a 5 percent cost of funds makes a 1-unit loan repaid in three equal annual installments at the ends of years 1-3. A general release cancels all payments due at or after the end of year $j$, counting from when the loan is made. Find the break-even installment for $j=2$, $j=3$ and $j\ge 4$, and the interest rate implied when $j=3$. What happens at $j=1$? (b) A borrower can afford an installment of at most 0.6. In which years before a release can she get this loan? Explain why a release rule meant to protect debtors can shut some of them out of credit. (c) Hillel's *prosbul* let a creditor transfer a loan to a court so that the release did not cancel it. In the model of (a), what does a universal opt-out do to pricing and to the protection the rule gave? Say what a rule-maker would need in order to keep part of both. (d) Suppose forgiveness is discretionary: each year the government cancels all outstanding one-year loans with probability $\phi=0.1$. Find the break-even gross rate and compare it with the scheduled rule. Explain why lenders may charge more than this under discretion than under a known schedule with the same average frequency. (e) In one paragraph, explain how a GDP-indexed sovereign bond delivers relief in exactly the states where a restructuring would otherwise happen, and why the verification problem of 1.1 comes back as the reason such bonds are rare.
