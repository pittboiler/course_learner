> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 3: Consensus and the CAP Theorem · Builds on: [3.3 (Paxos)](03-03-paxos.md), [3.2 (failure detectors)](03-02-failure-detectors-and-escaping-flp.md), [1.2 (failure models)](01-02-failure-models-and-the-network.md) · Unlocks: [3.6 (state-machine replication)](03-06-state-machine-replication.md), [4.2 (consensus-backed commit)](04-02-three-phase-commit-and-consensus-backed-commit.md)

## Why this matters

Paxos decides one value. Real systems need a *sequence* of them — a log — and the gap between single-decree Paxos and a working replicated log is where implementations historically went wrong, because Lamport's papers leave it as an exercise and every team filled it in differently.

Raft closes that gap by construction. It is not more powerful than Multi-Paxos; it is the same guarantees arranged so that a person can hold the safety argument in their head. The two moves that do it: **there is always exactly one leader per term, and the log is the primary object rather than a by-product.** Both are design decisions in favour of comprehensibility, and both buy real engineering simplicity — which is why Raft is what you will find under etcd, Consul, TiKV and most of the coordination layer built since 2014.

## The idea

Time is divided into **terms**, numbered 1, 2, 3, … Each term has at most one leader, and a term is a logical clock ([1.5](01-05-logical-time-lamport-and-vector-clocks.md)) in the plainest sense: every message carries a term, and any server seeing a larger one immediately steps down to follower and adopts it. **A server with a stale term cannot do damage, because everybody it talks to corrects it.**

A server is a **follower**, a **candidate** or a **leader**. Followers are passive. A follower that hears nothing from a leader for its randomised election timeout becomes a candidate, increments the term, votes for itself and asks everyone for votes. A candidate with votes from a majority becomes leader. Each server grants **at most one vote per term**, so at most one candidate can reach a majority — the same intersection argument as [3.3](03-03-paxos.md), made visible.

Once elected, the leader handles all client requests. It appends each command to its log and sends `AppendEntries` to followers. When an entry is on a majority, it is **committed**, and the leader tells followers so; they apply committed entries to their state machines in order.

The clever part is how logs are repaired. Every `AppendEntries` carries the index and term of the entry **immediately preceding** the new ones, and a follower rejects the message unless its log matches there. On rejection the leader steps its pointer back and tries again, walking backwards until it finds the last point of agreement, then overwrites everything after it. **Followers never modify their own logs on their own initiative; they are overwritten by the leader**, and a follower's log can get shorter.

Two safety rules make that overwriting harmless.

**The election restriction:** a server grants a vote only to a candidate whose log is **at least as up to date** as its own. Since committed entries sit on a majority, and a winning candidate needs a majority, the two majorities intersect at a server holding every committed entry — which then refuses to vote for anyone missing one. **A candidate that lacks a committed entry cannot win.**

**The commit restriction:** a leader may only mark an entry committed once it is replicated on a majority **and the entry was created in the leader's own current term.** This second clause looks like a technicality and is not; skipping it is the classic Raft bug, and the problems below build the execution that breaks.

## The formal version

> **Persistent state** (written to stable storage *before* responding to any RPC): `currentTerm`, `votedFor`, `log[]`. Volatile: `commitIndex`, `lastApplied`; on leaders, `nextIndex[]` and `matchIndex[]`.

The durability requirement is [1.2](01-02-failure-models-and-the-network.md)'s crash-recovery discipline, and it is load-bearing: a server that forgets its vote on restart can vote twice in one term, which is exactly the two-leaders bug Example 2 of that lesson constructs.

> **RequestVote.** A candidate in term $T$ sends its `lastLogIndex` and `lastLogTerm`. A server grants a vote if $T \ge$ its `currentTerm`, it has not already voted in $T$, and the candidate's log is at least as up to date as its own.

> **Up-to-date comparison.** Log $A$ is at least as up to date as log $B$ if
> $$\text{lastTerm}(A) > \text{lastTerm}(B), \quad\text{or}\quad \text{lastTerm}(A) = \text{lastTerm}(B) \ \text{and} \ \text{lastIndex}(A) \ge \text{lastIndex}(B).$$

**Term first, then length.** A short log ending in a high term beats a long log ending in a low one, because the high term proves the server heard from a more recent leader.

> **AppendEntries.** The leader sends `prevLogIndex`, `prevLogTerm`, the new entries and its `commitIndex`. A follower rejects unless its log contains an entry at `prevLogIndex` whose term is `prevLogTerm`. On acceptance it deletes any conflicting suffix and appends.

> **Log Matching Property.** If two logs contain an entry with the same index and term, then the logs are **identical in every entry up through that index**.

This follows by induction from the consistency check, and it is what makes the backward walk safe: agreeing at one index certifies agreement on the entire prefix, so the leader only has to find one matching point.

> **Leader Completeness.** If an entry is committed in term $T$, it is present in the log of every leader of every term $> T$.

This is the election restriction plus quorum intersection, and it is why the overwriting in `AppendEntries` never destroys committed data.

> **The commit rule (Raft §5.4.2).** A leader marks an entry committed only when it is stored on a majority **and** `log[N].term == currentTerm`. Entries from earlier terms become committed indirectly, when a later entry from the current term commits above them.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 — who can win an election, and the rule people get backwards.**

The five logs from the figure, written as their last (term, index):

| server | log | last term | last index |
|---|---|---|---|
| S1 | 1 1 2 2 3 | 3 | 5 |
| S2 | 1 1 2 2 3 | 3 | 5 |
| S3 | 1 1 2 | 2 | 3 |
| S4 | 1 1 2 4 4 4 | 4 | 6 |
| S5 | 1 | 1 | 1 |

**Can S3 win an election?** Its last is $(2, 3)$. Applying the comparison:

| voter | its last | grants to S3? |
|---|---|---|
| S1 | $(3,5)$ | no — 3 > 2 |
| S2 | $(3,5)$ | no |
| S3 | $(2,3)$ | yes (itself) |
| S4 | $(4,6)$ | no |
| S5 | $(1,1)$ | yes — 2 > 1 |

Two votes of five. **S3 cannot win.**

Now change the scenario so that S1 and S2 have not yet received index 4 and 5, and hold only `1 1 2`. Then S3's last is $(2,3)$ and so are S1's and S2's, so all three grant — **S3 wins with three votes despite two servers having longer logs at some point in the past.**

**The rule people state incorrectly is "the most up-to-date log always wins".** It does not. What Raft guarantees is narrower and is exactly what is needed: **a candidate missing a committed entry cannot win.** Entries that are merely *replicated* but not committed have no protection, and losing them is normal — S1 and S2's entries at indices 4 and 5 sit on two of five servers, are uncommitted, and may legitimately be overwritten.

**Why the narrow guarantee suffices.** A committed entry is on a majority. A winner needs votes from a majority. Two majorities intersect, so the winner was voted for by at least one server holding the entry, and that server would have refused a candidate whose log was behind. **Quorum intersection again, as in [2.6](02-06-quorum-systems.md) and [3.3](03-03-paxos.md).**

**Example 2 — repairing a divergent follower, step by step.**

Leader log (by index): `1 1 2 2 3`, five entries. Follower S4: `1 1 2 4 4 4`, six entries. The leader initialises `nextIndex` for S4 to 6, one past its own last index.

| attempt | `nextIndex` | `prevLogIndex`, `prevLogTerm` | follower's term there | result |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 6 | 5, term 3 | 4 | **reject**, `nextIndex` → 5 |
| 2 | 5 | 4, term 2 | 4 | **reject**, `nextIndex` → 4 |
| 3 | 4 | 3, term 2 | 2 | **accept** |

On acceptance the follower truncates everything from index 4 onward and appends the leader's entries 4 and 5, giving `1 1 2 2 3`. **Three round trips**, and its log shrank from six entries to five.

**What was and was not destroyed.** The three term-4 entries are gone. They were never committed — S4 was the only server holding them — so no client was ever told they succeeded, and the Log Matching Property guarantees the leader's agreement at index 3 certifies agreement on the whole prefix, so indices 1 to 3 need no checking.

**The cost model.** The backward walk is one round trip per entry of divergence, which is fine for the short divergences that actually happen and pathological for a follower that was partitioned for an hour. Real implementations have the follower return the first index of the conflicting term, letting the leader skip a whole term per round trip — an optimisation that changes nothing about safety and a great deal about recovery time.

## Watch out

- **You might think a longer log beats a shorter one in an election.** The comparison is **last term first**, and only then index. A one-entry log from term 9 beats a thousand-entry log from term 8, because the high term proves that server heard from a more recent leader.
- **You might think replicating an entry on a majority commits it.** Not if the entry is from an earlier term. The extra condition — the entry must be from the leader's current term — is Raft §5.4.2, and skipping it admits an execution where a committed entry is later overwritten. P3 builds that execution.
- **You might think a leader can trust its own log.** It can, for entries it created; it cannot assume entries inherited from previous terms are committed. This is the same point from the other side, and it is why a new leader's first act is usually to append a no-op entry in its own term: committing that entry commits everything beneath it.

## One-liner

> Raft is Multi-Paxos with the leader made explicit and the log made primary: one leader per term, one vote per server per term, a consistency check that turns agreement at one index into agreement on the whole prefix — and one easily-missed rule that a leader may only commit entries from its own term.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢)** Five servers hold these logs, written as the term of each entry in index order:

$$S_1: 1\,1\,1 \qquad S_2: 1\,1\,2\,2 \qquad S_3: 1\,1\,2\,2 \qquad S_4: 1\,1 \qquad S_5: 1\,1\,1\,1\,1$$

(a) Give each server's (last term, last index).
(b) State whether $S_5$ can win an election, listing which servers grant it a vote.
(c) State whether $S_2$ can win, listing the grants.
(d) State the general rule this illustrates, in one sentence.

**P2 (🟡)** A leader's log is `1 1 2 3 3` and a follower's is `1 1 2 2 2 2`. The leader initialises `nextIndex` for this follower to 6.

(a) Give the `prevLogIndex` and `prevLogTerm` of the first `AppendEntries`, and the follower's response.
(b) Give the full sequence of attempts until success, with `nextIndex` after each.
(c) Give the follower's log after the repair, and the number of entries it lost.
(d) The implementation is changed so the follower returns the first index of its conflicting term. Give the number of round trips this version needs here.

**P3 (🔴, optional)** Five servers. Consider this sequence, which is Raft's Figure 8.

1. In term 2, $S_1$ is leader and appends entry $E$ at index 2. It replicates $E$ to $S_2$ only, then crashes.
2. In term 3, $S_5$ is elected (with votes from $S_3$, $S_4$, $S_5$) and appends a different entry $F$ at index 2, then crashes before replicating it.
3. In term 4, $S_1$ restarts, is elected, and replicates $E$ at index 2 to $S_3$ — so $E$ is now on $S_1, S_2, S_3$, a majority.

(a) Under the **wrong** commit rule — commit anything on a majority — state what $S_1$ does at the end of step 3.
(b) Continue: $S_1$ crashes, and $S_5$ is elected in term 5 with votes from $S_2, S_3, S_4$. State whether this election is legal under the up-to-date rule, and what $S_5$ does to index 2 on the other servers.
(c) State which safety property has been violated, and name the entry involved.
(d) Give Raft's actual rule and state precisely how it prevents the above, and what $S_1$ must do instead to get $E$ committed.

**P1**

(a)

| server | log | (last term, last index) |
|---|---|---|
| $S_1$ | 1 1 1 | $(1, 3)$ |
| $S_2$ | 1 1 2 2 | $(2, 4)$ |
| $S_3$ | 1 1 2 2 | $(2, 4)$ |
| $S_4$ | 1 1 | $(1, 2)$ |
| $S_5$ | 1 1 1 1 1 | $(1, 5)$ |

(b) $S_5$ has $(1, 5)$. A voter grants if $S_5$'s last term exceeds the voter's, or ties and $S_5$'s index is at least as large.

| voter | its last | grants? |
|---|---|---|
| $S_1$ | $(1,3)$ | **yes** — terms tie, $5 \ge 3$ |
| $S_2$ | $(2,4)$ | no — $1 < 2$ |
| $S_3$ | $(2,4)$ | no |
| $S_4$ | $(1,2)$ | **yes** — terms tie, $5 \ge 2$ |
| $S_5$ | $(1,5)$ | **yes** (itself) |

Three votes of five is a majority: **$S_5$ can win**, with the longest log and the *lowest* last term.

(c) $S_2$ has $(2,4)$.

| voter | its last | grants? |
|---|---|---|
| $S_1$ | $(1,3)$ | **yes** — $2 > 1$ |
| $S_2$ | $(2,4)$ | **yes** |
| $S_3$ | $(2,4)$ | **yes** — terms tie, $4 \ge 4$ |
| $S_4$ | $(1,2)$ | **yes** — $2 > 1$ |
| $S_5$ | $(1,5)$ | **yes** — $2 > 1$ |

**All five.** Note $S_5$ grants despite having a strictly longer log, because its last term is lower.

(d) **The comparison is last term first and index only as a tiebreak, so a shorter log ending in a higher term wins — and both $S_2$ and $S_5$ can legitimately win here, because no entry beyond index 2 is on a majority and therefore nothing committed is at risk.**

**P2**

(a) `nextIndex` = 6, so `prevLogIndex` = 5 and `prevLogTerm` = the leader's term at index 5, which is **3**.

The follower's entry at index 5 has term **2**, which does not match, so it **rejects**.

(b)

| attempt | `nextIndex` | `prevLogIndex`, `prevLogTerm` | follower's term there | result | `nextIndex` after |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 6 | 5, term 3 | 2 | reject | 5 |
| 2 | 5 | 4, term 3 | 2 | reject | 4 |
| 3 | 4 | 3, term 2 | 2 | **accept** | 6 |

(c) The follower truncates from index 4 and appends the leader's entries 4 and 5:

$$\text{follower} = 1\,1\,2\,3\,3$$

**Three entries were deleted** — its old indices 4, 5 and 6, all from term 2 — and two were appended, so the log goes from 6 entries to 5. None of the deleted entries was committed: they sat on this follower alone, so no client was ever told they succeeded.

(d) With the conflicting-term optimisation, the follower's first rejection reports that its conflicting term is 2 and that term 2 first appears at **index 3**. The leader sets `nextIndex` to 4 directly and the second attempt succeeds.

**Two round trips instead of three.** The saving is small here and large in general: a follower divergent by 10,000 entries all from one term is repaired in two round trips rather than ten thousand.

**P3**

(a) Under the wrong rule, $E$ is on $S_1, S_2, S_3$ — three of five, a majority — so $S_1$ **marks $E$ committed at index 2**, applies it to its state machine and replies to the client that the command succeeded.

(b) $S_5$'s log ends with $F$ at index 2 from **term 3**. The voters $S_2$ and $S_3$ hold $E$ at index 2 from **term 2**, and $S_4$ holds nothing at index 2.

Applying the up-to-date rule: $S_5$'s last term is 3, the voters' is 2, and $3 > 2$, so **every one of them grants the vote. The election is legal.**

$S_5$ then replicates its own log, and its `AppendEntries` consistency check fails at index 2 on $S_2$ and $S_3$, so they **truncate $E$ and accept $F$**.

(c) **Leader Completeness is violated**, and with it the state-machine safety property: **the entry $E$**, which was reported committed and applied in step (a), has been overwritten on every server. A client was told its command succeeded, and the command no longer exists.

This is the worst class of bug a consensus protocol can have — not a stall, but a committed result that is silently revoked.

(d) **Raft's rule: a leader marks an entry committed only if it is replicated on a majority *and* the entry's term equals the leader's current term.**

How it prevents the above: at the end of step 3, $S_1$ is leader in **term 4** and $E$ was created in **term 2**. The terms differ, so $S_1$ may **not** mark $E$ committed, however many servers hold it. No client is told anything, and when $S_5$ later overwrites $E$, nothing committed is lost.

**What $S_1$ must do instead:** append a new entry in term 4 — typically a **no-op** appended immediately on election — and replicate *that* to a majority. Committing the term-4 entry commits every entry beneath it indirectly, including $E$, and at that point $E$ is genuinely safe: any future leader must have voted-for support from a majority holding a term-4 entry, and the up-to-date comparison then forces that leader's log to contain everything up to it.

**Why the no-op is not a hack.** It converts an entry the leader *inherited* and cannot vouch for into a prefix of an entry the leader *created* and can. A leader knows nothing about whether inherited entries are committed, and appending one of its own is the cheapest way to find out — which is why essentially every Raft implementation does it on election.

</details>

## Flashback

**From Lesson 3.3 (Paxos):** Three acceptors. $A_1$ has promised to 8 and accepted $(6, \text{“m”})$; $A_2$ has promised to 3 and accepted $(3, \text{“n”})$; $A_3$ has promised to 8 and accepted nothing.

(a) State whether any value is chosen, with the reason.
(b) A proposer runs $\mathrm{prepare}(9)$ to $A_1$ and $A_2$. Give the replies and the value it must propose.
(c) A different proposer runs $\mathrm{prepare}(9)$ to $A_2$ and $A_3$ instead. Give the value it must propose, and state whether the two answers conflict.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

(a) **No value is chosen.** A majority of three is two, and no value has been accepted by two acceptors — "m" sits on $A_1$ alone and "n" on $A_2$ alone.

(b) $A_1$: $9 > 8$, so it promises and reports $(6, \text{“m”})$. $A_2$: $9 > 3$, so it promises and reports $(3, \text{“n”})$.

Two promises is a majority. The **highest-numbered** accepted proposal is $(6, \text{“m”})$, so the proposer must propose **"m"**.

(c) $A_2$ reports $(3, \text{“n”})$; $A_3$ reports nothing accepted. The highest is $(3, \text{“n”})$, so this proposer must propose **"n"**.

**The two answers do not conflict**, because nothing was chosen when either ran. Whichever proposer completes its accept phase first puts its value on a majority; from that instant the other proposer's phase 1 — if it retries with a higher number — will intersect that majority and be forced onto the same value.

The situation is precisely P1(d) of [3.3](03-03-paxos.md): before a value is chosen, different majorities can force different values, and that is safe. The rule constrains proposers only once a majority actually holds something, which is the same boundary Raft draws between *replicated* and *committed*.

</details>

## Connections

- **Backward:** Raft is [3.3](03-03-paxos.md)'s Multi-Paxos with the distinguished proposer promoted to a first-class leader; the election restriction and the commit rule are the phase-1 "highest accepted value wins" rule expressed in terms of logs. The durability of `currentTerm` and `votedFor` is [1.2](01-02-failure-models-and-the-network.md)'s crash-recovery discipline, and forgetting it reproduces that lesson's two-leaders bug exactly.
- **Forward:** [3.6](03-06-state-machine-replication.md) puts a deterministic state machine on top of this log and adds snapshots and leader leases; [4.2](04-02-three-phase-commit-and-consensus-backed-commit.md) replaces a blocking commit coordinator with a Raft group.
- **Sideways:** terms are Lamport clocks ([1.5](01-05-logical-time-lamport-and-vector-clocks.md)) with the standard "adopt any larger value you see" update, used for the one thing Lamport clocks are genuinely good at — **forbidding** an action by a server whose number is too low, rather than asserting an order between concurrent events.
