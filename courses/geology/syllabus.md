# Geology — Syllabus

> Earth & Space · Tier 0 · ~29 lessons · Prereqs: none · Roadmap id: `geology`

## Goal

Read the Earth the way a field geologist does: name what a rock is made of and how it formed, explain why continents drift and mountains rise, work out the order of events from the structures in an outcrop, and reconstruct a landscape's history from the story written in its layers. You will finish able to identify rocks and minerals, predict what happens at any plate boundary, read folds and faults off a geologic map, date rocks both relatively and absolutely, and place any event on the geologic timescale — with the quantitative physics of the Earth deliberately left to [`geophysics`](../geophysics/syllabus.md) and comparative planetology to [`planetary-science`](../planetary-science/syllabus.md). This is the observational, field-facing half of Earth science: what the rocks say, and how you get them to say it.

## Scope discipline

Geology sits alongside two quantitative Earth courses and several built physical-science
courses. Each topic has **one owner**; a ceded topic is used freely here and cited to its
owner rather than re-derived. See the reference card's "Assumed, not taught here" table.

## Dangerous Checklist

When you finish, you can:

- [ ] Identify a mineral from its physical properties and explain why those properties follow from its silicate structure
- [ ] Classify any rock as igneous, sedimentary, or metamorphic and trace its path around the rock cycle
- [ ] Name the three ways the Earth makes magma and predict a magma's composition from where it formed
- [ ] Read an igneous rock's texture and composition to say where and how fast it cooled
- [ ] Distinguish clastic, chemical, and organic sediments and name the environment each records
- [ ] Predict a metamorphic assemblage from the pressure–temperature path its protolith followed
- [ ] Marshal the evidence for continental drift and explain why the theory needed a driving mechanism
- [ ] Predict the landforms, earthquakes, and volcanoes expected at any plate boundary
- [ ] Say whether a rock will fold or fracture given depth, temperature, and strain rate
- [ ] Classify a fault or fold from its geometry and infer the stress direction that produced it
- [ ] Explain how a mountain belt is assembled, held up, and taken apart
- [ ] Estimate an earthquake's shaking and hazard, and distinguish magnitude from intensity
- [ ] Explain why some volcanoes erupt gently and others explode, from magma composition alone
- [ ] Predict how a rock weathers, what soil it makes, and when a slope will fail
- [ ] Read a river, glacier, desert, or coastal landscape and name the process that shaped it
- [ ] Compute a groundwater flow rate from Darcy's law and explain what over-pumping does to an aquifer
- [ ] Order rock layers and cross-cutting features in time, and name the kind of unconformity between them
- [ ] Compute the age of a rock from a parent/daughter ratio, and choose the right isotope system for the job
- [ ] Place an event on the geologic timescale and explain how that timescale was built and calibrated
- [ ] Reconstruct a depositional environment from a stratigraphic sequence and correlate it between sections
- [ ] Read a geologic map: strike and dip, contacts, folds, faults, and draw the cross-section
- [ ] Sketch Earth's internal layers and state the evidence for each boundary
- [ ] Tell the story of the Earth from the Hadean to now, and say which rocks record each chapter
- [ ] Explain how a given ore, fossil fuel, or aquifer was concentrated, and what geologic hazard threatens a given site

## Modules

### Module 1: Earth Materials & the Rock Cycle

From atoms to outcrops: what minerals are, why they behave as they do, and the three ways rock is made and remade.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1.1 | What a Mineral Is | Define a mineral and predict its properties from its silicate structure | crystalline solid, silicate tetrahedron, polymerization series, ionic substitution |
| 1.2 | Identifying Minerals | Identify the rock-forming minerals by hardness, cleavage, luster, and streak | Mohs hardness, cleavage vs. fracture, streak, the dozen minerals that matter |
| 1.3 | How the Earth Melts | Name the three routes to magma and predict what melts where | decompression / flux / heat-transfer melting, partial melting, Bowen's reaction series, magmatic differentiation |
| 1.4 | Igneous Rocks & Igneous Bodies | Read texture and composition to say where an igneous rock cooled | intrusive vs. extrusive, grain size and cooling rate, felsic-to-mafic, plutons, dikes and sills |
| 1.5 | Sedimentary Rocks | Distinguish clastic, chemical, and organic sediments and their environments | weathering to lithification, sorting and rounding, grain size, depositional environment, porosity |
| 1.6 | Metamorphic Rocks & the Rock Cycle | Predict a metamorphic rock from its protolith and its P–T path | foliation, grade and index minerals, metamorphic facies, contact vs. regional, the rock cycle |

**Boss problem 1:** A single outcrop shows, from bottom to top: a coarse-grained speckled rock with interlocking pink, white, and black crystals; above it a band of the same rock recrystallized into wavy light-and-dark layers; above that a well-sorted, cross-bedded sandstone; and cutting through all three, a fine-grained dark sheet with chilled margins. (a) Name each rock and state how each formed. (b) Give the order of events. (c) The wavy-layered band is thickest next to the dark sheet — revise your answer and say which kind of metamorphism this is.

### Module 2: Plate Tectonics & the Deforming Crust

The unifying theory and its consequences: the evidence for moving plates, what happens at their edges, and how rock responds when a continent is pushed.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2.1 | The Evidence for Drift | Marshal the case for moving plates and explain why it was resisted | continental fit, fossil and rock matches, seafloor spreading, magnetic stripes, apparent polar wander |
| 2.2 | Plate Boundaries | Predict landforms and hazards at divergent, convergent, and transform boundaries | ridges, subduction zones, collision, transform faults, triple junctions |
| 2.3 | The Driving Mechanism | Explain what actually moves plates | lithosphere/asthenosphere, mantle convection, ridge push, slab pull, why slab pull dominates |
| 2.4 | How Rock Deforms | Say whether a rock will fracture or flow, given the conditions | elastic/brittle/ductile, confining pressure, temperature, strain rate, the brittle–ductile transition |
| 2.5 | Folds, Faults & Structures | Classify a structure and infer the stress that made it | strike and dip, anticline/syncline, normal/reverse/strike-slip faults, joints, stress regimes |
| 2.6 | Mountain Building | Explain how a mountain belt is assembled and how it comes down | orogeny, terrane accretion, fold-and-thrust belts, isostatic root and uplift, exhumation |
| 2.7 | Earthquakes & Seismic Hazard | Interpret an earthquake as a fault process and assess its hazard | elastic rebound, focus and epicenter, magnitude vs. intensity, ground motion, liquefaction, tsunami |
| 2.8 | Volcanoes & Volcanic Hazards | Predict eruptive style from magma composition alone | viscosity and dissolved gas, shield / stratovolcano / caldera, hotspots, lahars, pyroclastic flows, ash |

**Boss problem 2:** A chain of volcanic islands runs in a straight line; ages increase steadily with distance from the one active volcano at the end. Six hundred kilometres along the chain the ages jump and the trend bends by 60 degrees. (a) Explain what builds this chain. (b) Use two ages to estimate the plate's speed in cm/yr. (c) Interpret the bend. (d) The active island has gentle, fluid eruptions while a volcano at the nearby subduction zone erupts explosively — explain the difference from magma composition, and name the hazard each poses.

### Module 3: Surface Processes

How rock breaks down, and how water, ice, and wind move it around and carve the landscape.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3.1 | Weathering & Soils | Predict how a rock breaks down and what soil it leaves | mechanical vs. chemical weathering, hydrolysis and dissolution, the Goldich series, soil horizons, climate control |
| 3.2 | Mass Wasting & Slope Stability | Decide whether a slope will fail, and how | angle of repose, driving vs. resisting forces, the role of water, creep, slides, flows, falls |
| 3.3 | Rivers & Landscape Evolution | Read a river and the landscape it is making | discharge, competence and capacity, meanders and braiding, base level, terraces, drainage patterns |
| 3.4 | Glaciers & the Ice Ages | Identify a glaciated landscape and explain what drives ice ages | mass balance, ice flow, erosional and depositional landforms, moraines and till, Milankovitch forcing |
| 3.5 | Deserts, Wind & Coasts | Name the process behind an arid or coastal landform | aeolian transport, dunes, desert pavement, longshore drift, beaches and barrier islands, sea-level change |
| 3.6 | Groundwater, Aquifers & Karst | Compute groundwater flow and explain what over-pumping does | porosity and permeability, water table, Darcy's law, confined vs. unconfined aquifers, cones of depression, karst and caves |

**Boss problem 3:** A confined aquifer of sandstone 20 m thick has hydraulic conductivity 15 m/day; the head drops 8 m over a distance of 4 km along the flow direction. (a) Compute the Darcy flux and the volumetric flow through a 1 km-wide section. (b) The aquifer is pumped faster than it recharges — describe two distinct consequences, one hydrologic and one geologic. (c) The overlying limestone contains caves and sinkholes; explain the chemistry that made them and why the pumping raises the sinkhole risk.

### Module 4: Geologic Time & the Rock Record

How geologists tell time — from stacking principles to radioactive clocks — and how a stack of layers becomes a history.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4.1 | Relative Dating & Unconformities | Order events in rock from geometry alone | superposition, original horizontality, cross-cutting, inclusions, the three unconformities |
| 4.2 | Radiometric Dating | Compute an age and choose the right isotope system | decay law applied, parent/daughter ratio, half-life, closure temperature, isochrons, what is actually being dated |
| 4.3 | The Geologic Timescale | Place an event on the timescale and explain how the scale was built | eons/eras/periods/epochs, index fossils, biostratigraphy, calibrating relative order with absolute dates, GSSPs |
| 4.4 | Stratigraphy, Facies & Correlation | Reconstruct an environment and correlate it between sections | facies, Walther's law, transgression and regression, lithostratigraphy vs. chronostratigraphy, marker beds |
| 4.5 | Reading a Geologic Map | Extract three-dimensional structure from a two-dimensional map | contacts, strike and dip symbols, the V-rule, outcrop patterns of folds and faults, drawing a cross-section |

**Boss problem 4:** A volcanic ash bed contains sanidine in which 12.5 percent of the original potassium-40 remains (half-life 1.25 billion years). The ash rests on tilted, truncated sandstone and is overlain by flat-lying shale. (a) Give the ash's age and state precisely what event that age dates. (b) Name the surface beneath the ash and say what sequence of events produced it. (c) State what you can and cannot conclude about the ages of the sandstone and the shale. (d) A geologist proposes dating the sandstone directly using detrital zircons — explain what that would and would not tell them.

### Module 5: Earth's Interior, History & Resources

Inward and backward: the layered planet beneath our feet, the four-and-a-half-billion-year story recorded in its rocks, and what we take out of it.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5.1 | Earth's Internal Structure | Sketch Earth's layers and give the evidence for each boundary | crust/mantle/core, Moho, the S-wave shadow zone, compositional vs. mechanical layering, meteorite and density constraints |
| 5.2 | Earth History I: Hadean to Proterozoic | Tell the first four billion years from the rocks that record it | accretion and the Moon-forming impact, oldest zircons, first crust, banded iron formations and the Great Oxidation, supercontinent cycles, snowball Earth |
| 5.3 | Earth History II: The Phanerozoic | Trace the last half-billion years through its rock record | Pangaea assembly and breakup, the great orogenies, sea-level and climate history, the Pleistocene glaciations, the Anthropocene question |
| 5.4 | Resources & Geologic Hazards | Explain how a resource was concentrated and what threatens a site | ore-forming processes, hydrothermal and magmatic concentration, fossil-fuel formation and traps, aquifers as resources, hazard assessment and risk |

**Boss problem 5:** A drill core passes downward through: peat; then shale with marine shells; then cross-bedded sandstone with ripple marks; then a thick evaporite; then granite with a network of quartz veins carrying copper sulfides. (a) Name the depositional environment recorded by each sedimentary unit and reconstruct the sequence of environments over time. (b) Say which unit is oldest and give the principle. (c) Identify two economic resources in this core and explain the geologic process that concentrated each. (d) The granite gives a radiometric age of 1.1 billion years and the shale contains Cretaceous fossils — state what the gap between them represents and what it is called.

## Sources of truth

- Marshak, *Essentials of Geology* — organization, terminology, and the "big picture" framing.
- Grotzinger & Jordan, *Understanding Earth* — process-based explanations and figure conventions.
- U.S. Geological Survey public materials — hazard definitions, magnitude/intensity scales, and current nomenclature.
- International Commission on Stratigraphy timescale — period names, boundaries, and ages.
- Fetter, *Applied Hydrogeology* — groundwater conventions and Darcy's law usage in 3.6.

separated from surface processes into its own module. The Scope discipline table above
