# Geology · Lesson 2.1: The Evidence for Drift

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 2: Plate Tectonics & the Deforming Crust · Builds on: [1.6](01-06-metamorphic-rocks-rock-cycle.md), [1.5](01-05-sedimentary-rocks.md) · Unlocks: [2.2](02-02-plate-boundaries.md) (plate boundaries), [2.3](02-03-driving-mechanism.md) (the driving mechanism)

## Why this matters

Every remaining lesson in this course leans on plate tectonics. But the reason to teach 2.1 as *history* rather than as a list of evidence is that it is the cleanest case study in the earth sciences of a correct hypothesis, supported by genuinely good data, being rejected for half a century — **and the rejection was reasonable.**

Alfred Wegener assembled, by 1915, an evidential case that most geologists today would find persuasive. It was voted down. The usual telling makes his opponents into stubborn old men, and that telling is wrong and worth demolishing, because the actual reason is a general lesson: **a correlation without a viable mechanism is not enough to overturn a working paradigm.** What eventually won was not a better correlation. It was a hypothesis that made a *forced prediction* — one that could only come out one way if it were true, and could not plausibly come out that way if it were false — and then the prediction was tested at sea.

## The idea

The argument runs in three acts, and they are separated by decades.

**Act I (1912–1929) — the correlations.** Wegener noticed that if you slide the continents together, four independent classes of thing line up across the joins: coastline shapes, fossil species, mountain belts, and ancient climate indicators. Any one of these could be coincidence. Four of them, all agreeing on the *same* reconstruction, is a lot of coincidence. He called the assembled supercontinent **Pangaea**.

**Act II (1924–1950) — the refutation.** Wegener also proposed a mechanism: continents *ploughing* through the oceanic crust like icebreakers, driven by a rotational "pole-fleeing" force and by tidal forces. Harold Jeffreys, a first-rate geophysicist, did the arithmetic and showed the driving forces were smaller than the required forces by something like three or four orders of magnitude. The rock was far too strong. **This was correct.** And with the mechanism dead, the correlations had a competing explanation available — sunken land bridges — so the community, quite rationally, filed drift under "interesting but unmechanised."

**Act III (1953–1968) — the ocean floor.** Wegener's case was built entirely on continents, which is where the rocks are exposed. After the Second World War, sonar, magnetometers and drill ships turned the 71 percent of the planet nobody had looked at into a dataset. Everything decisive came from there — and none of it was evidence Wegener could have gathered. **The seafloor did not merely add support; it replaced the mechanism.** Continents do not plough through oceanic crust. The ocean floor itself is created at ridges, carried outward, and destroyed at trenches, with the continents riding along as passengers.

That reframing is why the theory that won is *plate tectonics* and not *continental drift*. They are not the same claim.

## The formal version

### Act I: what Wegener actually had

| Evidence | The observation | What it forces | Its weakness in 1915 |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Fit** | Africa and South America interlock; the fit is markedly *better* at the 1000 m depth contour (the continental shelf edge) than at the coastline | The true edge of continental crust is the shelf break, not the shore — so the good fit is at the geologically meaningful boundary | Shapes alone. Eyeballed, unquantified, and easy to dismiss as pattern-matching |
| **Fossils** | *Mesosaurus*, a freshwater reptile, in Permian rocks of Brazil and southwest Africa and nowhere else; *Glossopteris*, a seed fern with large heavy seeds, across South America, Africa, India, Antarctica and Australia | Neither organism could cross a wide ocean; the ranges are continuous only on the reconstruction | Land bridges explain it too |
| **Mountain belts and rock ages** | The Appalachians run out to sea in Newfoundland and reappear as the Caledonides of Ireland, Scotland and Norway — same age, same structural grain, same metamorphic history ([1.6](01-06-metamorphic-rocks-rock-cycle.md)) | A single orogen, later torn in two | Convergent-margin belts of similar age exist elsewhere; "similar" is a judgement call |
| **Palaeoclimate** | Permian **glacial striations** and tillites in India, southern Africa, Australia and South America, with the striation directions radiating outward from a single centre; Permian **coal swamps** in Antarctica; desert evaporites at latitudes that are now temperate | Those continents sat together over the South Pole; ice sheets do not grow in the tropics and coal swamps do not grow at the pole | Requires believing the climate belts stayed put while the land moved — a bigger ask in 1915 than it sounds now |

The striation evidence is the strongest of the four and the least often quoted. **Glacial striations are directional** ([3.4](03-04-glaciers-ice-ages.md) will use them again): they record which way the ice flowed. On the modern globe they point outward from four separate continents into what is now open ocean, which is impossible. On Pangaea they radiate from one ice centre, which is exactly what a single ice sheet does.

### Act II: why it failed, stated precisely

Wegener's mechanism required a continent to break oceanic crust ahead of it. Write the requirement as a stress comparison. Let $\tau_{\text{req}}$ be the shear stress the continent must apply to its leading edge to keep moving, and $\tau_{\text{rock}}$ the shear strength of oceanic lithosphere, of order $10^{8}\ \text{Pa}$ ([materials-science 4.4](../../materials-science/lessons/04-04-failure-fracture-fatigue-creep.md)). Then the hypothesis lives or dies on

$$\frac{F_{\text{available}}}{F_{\text{required}}} = \frac{F_{\text{available}}}{\tau_{\text{rock}}\, A_{\text{face}}} \;\gtrsim\; 1 \;?$$

*In words: does the force Wegener could name exceed the force needed to shear open a continent-sized channel through the seafloor?* Worked example 2 does this and gets a ratio near $2\times10^{-4}$. It is not close.

**Jeffreys was right and his conclusion was wrong, and both halves matter.** He was right that continents cannot plough through oceanic crust. He concluded that continents therefore do not move. The missing option — that the ocean floor is not a fixed medium to be ploughed through but is itself in motion, continuously created and destroyed — was not on anyone's list, because nobody had a map of it.

**The rival hypothesis was not "nothing."** Fixists explained the fossil matches with **land bridges**: isthmuses of continental crust that later foundered. Note that this rival was already in trouble on physical grounds, from an argument this course will develop in [2.6](02-06-mountain-building.md): continental crust is about $2.7\ \text{g/cm}^3$ and mantle is about $3.3\ \text{g/cm}^3$, so a slab of continental crust *floats*. It cannot sink into the mantle any more than an iceberg can sink into water. The land-bridge hypothesis violated isostasy. **Both sides had a fatal physical objection against them, which is precisely the condition under which a field stalls.**

### Act III: the ocean floor, and four facts that broke the tie

Notation first. $\text{Ma}$ is mega-annum, one million years (as an age: "80 Ma" means 80 million years ago); $\text{Ga}$ is giga-annum, one billion years. Earth is about $4.54\ \text{Ga}$ old.

1. **Bathymetry.** Echo sounding revealed a continuous 65,000 km mountain range running down the middle of every ocean basin, split by a central rift valley — and, separately, narrow trenches to 11 km depth hugging island arcs. Neither belongs on a static planet.
2. **Heat flow.** Measured heat flow is high at ridge crests and falls off systematically with distance. Something hot is arriving at the ridge.
3. **Sediment and age.** Pelagic sediment is thin at ridge crests and thickens outward, roughly in proportion to distance. And **no seafloor anywhere is older than about $180$–$200\ \text{Ma}$**, while continental rocks reach $4.0\ \text{Ga}$. The ocean floor records the last 4 percent of Earth history and nothing before it.
4. **Wadati–Benioff zones.** Earthquake foci beneath island arcs are not scattered; they define a planar surface dipping from the trench beneath the arc to depths of 700 km. That plane is a slab of something cold and brittle descending into the mantle — the *destruction* side of the budget.

Fact 3 is the one to sit with. **On a static Earth with permanent basins, ocean floor should be the oldest, most sediment-choked surface on the planet.** It is instead the youngest and the cleanest. Harry Hess in 1962 proposed the resolution — **seafloor spreading**: crust is made at ridges, moves outward, and is consumed at trenches, so the basins are conveyor belts with a finite transit time.

### The clincher: Vine–Matthews and why *symmetry* is the decisive word

Vine and Matthews (1963) combined three things, each independently established:

- Basalt cools through the **Curie temperature** and locks in the direction of the ambient magnetic field ([1.4](01-04-igneous-rocks-and-bodies.md) for the cooling; the rock-magnetism physics is `geophysics` 3.3).
- Earth's magnetic field **reverses polarity** at irregular intervals — established independently on land, from dated lava sequences, giving a reversal timescale that is a fixed global calendar.
- Hess's conveyor belt.

Conjoin them and you get a forced prediction. New crust at the axis records today's field; as it moves outward it carries that record with it; when the field reverses, the crust forming at the axis records the new polarity. The seafloor is a **tape recorder** running in both directions at once.

$$\text{age of crust at distance } x \text{ from the axis:} \qquad t(x) = \frac{x}{u}, \qquad u = \text{half-spreading rate}$$

*In words: distance from the ridge is a clock, and the reversal timescale is the dial it should read against.* Two consequences follow, and only one of them is quoted often enough:

$$\boxed{\;\text{(i) stripe widths} \;\propto\; \text{chron durations};\qquad \text{(ii) the pattern is a } \textbf{mirror image} \text{ about the axis.}\;}$$

**Why symmetry is decisive, and not just pretty.** Consequence (i) alone is suggestive but soft — a sceptic can invent a fixed-crust story where bands of differently magnetized rock happen to have widths in some ratio. Consequence (ii) has no such escape. There is no reason on a static Earth for the magnetic pattern 100 km east of a ridge to be the *bit-for-bit reflection* of the pattern 100 km west of it. Spreading forces it, because both flanks are cast from the same mould at the same instant and then carried apart.

That is the structure of a decisive observation: **not "consistent with the hypothesis," but "impossible without it."** The Eltanin-19 profile across the Pacific–Antarctic ridge in 1966 showed a symmetric pattern so clean it converted people on sight.

### Apparent polar wander: the argument that runs on continents alone

Rocks record the ancient field *direction*, from which you compute where the magnetic pole sat when they formed. Do this for a series of European rocks of different ages and you trace a path for the pole across the globe: an **apparent polar wander (APW) path**. Do it for North America and you get a *different* path.

The logic is a clean disjunction. There is only one magnetic pole at any time. So either:

- the pole moved — but then both continents must yield the *same* path, which they do not; or
- the continents moved relative to each other.

The word *apparent* is doing all the work: what wandered was the continent, not the pole. And the confirmation is quantitative — **rotating Europe and North America together to close the Atlantic superimposes the two paths**, and the age at which they merge is the age at which the ocean opened. Two completely independent methods, palaeomagnetism and magnetic stripes, agree on the same reconstruction. (The quantitative treatment is `geophysics` 3.3–3.4; see [its syllabus](../../geophysics/syllabus.md).)

By 1967–68 the pieces — spreading, subduction, transform faults, and rigid plates on a sphere — were assembled into plate tectonics. **Fifty-five years from Wegener's first paper; five years from Hess's.** The difference was not the quality of the correlations. It was having a mechanism that made a forced prediction.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (mechanical — reading the tape recorder).** A magnetometer profile east of a ridge crosses polarity boundaries at the following distances from the axis. The reversal timescale, established independently from dated lavas on land, gives the ages.

| Boundary | Distance from axis (km) | Age from the reversal timescale ($\text{Ma}$) |
|---|---|---|
| Brunhes normal / Matuyama reversed | 20.0 | 0.78 |
| Matuyama / Gauss normal | 66.0 | 2.58 |
| Gauss / Gilbert reversed | 92.0 | 3.60 |
| Base of Gilbert | 151.0 | 5.89 |

(a) Compute the half-spreading rate from each boundary. (b) What does the agreement between them establish? (c) The Atlantic half-rate measured the same way is about $15.6\ \text{km/Myr}$, and the Mid-Atlantic Ridge lies about 2,800 km from the North American continental margin. Check the implied age of the oldest Atlantic floor.

**(a)** Each boundary gives $u = x/t$:

$$\frac{20.0}{0.78} = 25.64, \qquad \frac{66.0}{2.58} = 25.58, \qquad \frac{92.0}{3.60} = 25.56, \qquad \frac{151.0}{5.89} = 25.64 \quad \text{all in } \text{km/Myr}.$$

$$u \approx 25.6\ \text{km/Myr} = 2.56\ \text{cm/yr} \ \text{(half-rate)}, \qquad \text{full spreading rate} \approx 5.1\ \text{cm/yr}.$$

Convert once and remember it: $1\ \text{km/Myr} = 10^{5}\ \text{cm} / 10^{6}\ \text{yr} = 0.1\ \text{cm/yr}$. **Geologic rates are fingernail-growth rates — a few centimetres a year — which is why the whole business was invisible until you could survey the seafloor.**

**(b)** Four independent boundaries give the same rate to within 0.3 percent. That is a **four-point consistency check on a hypothesis that was allowed to fail**: nothing forced those four ratios to agree. If the stripes were an unrelated pattern of magnetized rock, the widths would bear no fixed relation to a timescale derived from lavas on a different continent. **The agreement is the argument, not the rate.**

**(c)** $$t = \frac{2800\ \text{km}}{15.6\ \text{km/Myr}} = 179\ \text{Myr} \approx 180\ \text{Ma}.$$

Independently, the oldest sediments recovered by drilling on Atlantic abyssal crust are Middle Jurassic, about $180\ \text{Ma}$. **A rate measured over the first 6 Myr near the axis, extrapolated 2,800 km, predicts the age at the far margin correctly.** That is a thirty-fold extrapolation landing on the nose, and it is the reason the ocean-floor age structure is not seriously disputed by anyone.

**Example 2 (why you'd care — doing Jeffreys' calculation, and seeing what he missed).** Estimate whether Wegener's mechanism could work. Take a continent 3,000 km wide, 5,000 km long, and 35 km thick, of density $2800\ \text{kg/m}^3$, required to shear its way through oceanic crust of shear strength $\tau_{\text{rock}} \approx 10^{8}\ \text{Pa}$. Wegener's largest proposed driving force, the *Polflucht* or pole-fleeing force arising from Earth's rotational flattening, is of order $3\times10^{-7}$ of the continent's weight.

**Force required.** The continent must fail rock across its full leading face:

$$A_{\text{face}} = (5\times10^{6}\ \text{m})(3.5\times10^{4}\ \text{m}) = 1.75\times10^{11}\ \text{m}^2$$

$$F_{\text{req}} = \tau_{\text{rock}} A_{\text{face}} = (10^{8})(1.75\times10^{11}) = 1.75\times10^{19}\ \text{N}$$

**Force available.**

$$M = (3\times10^{6})(5\times10^{6})(3.5\times10^{4})(2800) = 1.47\times10^{21}\ \text{kg}, \qquad W = Mg = 1.44\times10^{22}\ \text{N}$$

$$F_{\text{avail}} \approx 3\times10^{-7} W = 4.3\times10^{15}\ \text{N}$$

**The verdict.**

$$\frac{F_{\text{avail}}}{F_{\text{req}}} = \frac{4.3\times10^{15}}{1.75\times10^{19}} \approx 2.5\times10^{-4}$$

**Short by a factor of about four thousand.** Every input here is generous to Wegener (a perfectly rigid continent, no basal friction at all, a low rock strength), and it still fails by nearly four orders of magnitude. This is an order-of-magnitude estimate, not Jeffreys' actual calculation, but it reproduces his conclusion and shows it was not a matter of taste. **Given his premises, refusing the hypothesis was the correct scientific judgement.**

**Now find the hidden premise.** The calculation assumes the continent moves *relative to* the ocean floor, so that oceanic crust must be broken. Delete that assumption and the required force collapses: if continent and ocean floor move together as one rigid plate over a weak asthenosphere, there is no face to shear, and the resisting force is viscous drag on the base rather than fracture at the front — smaller by orders of magnitude. Better still, [2.3](02-03-driving-mechanism.md) will show that the dominant force is not a push on the continent at all but **slab pull**, the weight of cold dense lithosphere sinking at a trench, a force of order $10^{13}\ \text{N}$ per metre of trench, which is ample.

**The lesson generalizes past geology.** Jeffreys' refutation was sound arithmetic applied to a premise nobody had noticed was a premise. **A refutation is only ever as strong as its least examined assumption**, and the way that assumption got examined was not argument — it was a ship with a magnetometer going somewhere nobody had been.

## Watch out

- **You might think geologists rejected drift out of dogmatism.** They rejected it because its only proposed mechanism was quantitatively impossible, and because a rival explanation (land bridges) existed. The failure was collective imagination about *what else could be moving*, not stubbornness. Treating it as a morality tale about closed minds gets the actual lesson exactly backwards.
- **You might think continental drift and plate tectonics are the same theory.** Wegener's continents ploughed through a static seafloor. In plate tectonics **the seafloor is the moving thing** and continents are passengers embedded in plates. Wegener was right about the reconstruction and wrong about the physics, and the correction is not a detail.
- **You might picture the magnetic stripes as bands of different rock.** They are the same basalt everywhere. What alternates is the direction of the frozen-in remanent magnetization: where it parallels today's field it *adds* to the measured total field, where it opposes it *subtracts*. **The stripes are anomalies in a measurement, not layers you could see in an outcrop.**
- **You might treat the coastline fit as the strong evidence.** It is the weakest of Wegener's four, because coastlines are erosional accidents at whatever sea level happens to be. The fit made at the shelf edge is far better, and in any case the force comes from **the things that match across the join**, not from the join itself.
- **You might quote stripe *widths* as the proof.** Widths alone are soft. **Symmetry about the axis is the observation with no fixist escape**, because both flanks being mirror images demands they were cast together and carried apart.
- **You might think Wegener was vindicated.** Partly. His reconstruction was close to right; his mechanism was wrong; and his rate was badly wrong — he claimed geodetic evidence for Greenland moving tens of metres per year, roughly a thousand times the true rate, which did his credibility no good at all.

## One-liner

> Wegener had four independent correlations and no workable mechanism, so a correct hypothesis was rightly voted down for fifty years; what overturned it was not better correlation but a forced prediction — that the seafloor's magnetic pattern must be a *mirror image* about the ridge axis, an observation with no fixist explanation at all.

**(b)** $s = \text{thickness}/\text{age}$:

The near-constant ratio says pelagic sediment has rained down at a steady rate and simply had **more time to do it further from the ridge**. Thickness is therefore a second, independent clock, and it agrees with the first. **A sediment layer that is thin at the ridge and thickens outward in direct proportion to distance is not something a static basin can produce** — a permanent basin would have a thick blanket everywhere, thickest where the supply is greatest, with no relation to distance from a mid-ocean mountain range.

**(c)** Over $4.0\ \text{Ga} = 4000\ \text{Myr}$ at $10.6\ \text{m/Myr}$:

$$\text{predicted thickness} = 4000 \times 10.6 = 42{,}400\ \text{m} \approx \mathbf{42\ \text{km}}.$$

$$\text{observed at Site D} = 850\ \text{m}. \qquad \text{Ratio} = \frac{42{,}400}{850} \approx \mathbf{50}.$$

**The permanent-basin view is wrong by a factor of fifty, and the discrepancy is in the impossible direction** — you can imagine reasons a basin has *more* sediment than expected, but not fifty times less, because the sediment has to go somewhere and there is no mechanism to remove it from the deep abyss. A 42 km sediment pile would also be thicker than the entire oceanic crust it sits on. **The seafloor is young. It is not an ancient surface that has been swept clean; there is nothing older than the sediment records.**

**(d)** $$x = u\,t = 16.1 \times 180 = \mathbf{2{,}900\ \text{km}}.$$

Which is about the distance from the Mid-Atlantic Ridge to the continental margin — the same consistency check as Worked example 1(c), reached from drill data instead of from magnetometry.

**P3 (a)**

$$A_0 = 4\pi R_0^{2} = 4\pi (6371)^{2} = 5.10\times10^{8}\ \text{km}^2.$$

$$A_{\text{young}} = 0.60\,A_0 = 3.06\times10^{8}\ \text{km}^2.$$

$$A_p = A_0 - A_{\text{young}} = 0.40\,A_0 = 2.04\times10^{8}\ \text{km}^2.$$

**(b)** $$R_p = \sqrt{\frac{A_p}{4\pi}} = R_0\sqrt{0.40} = 6371 \times 0.6325 = \mathbf{4{,}030\ \text{km}}, \qquad \frac{R_p}{R_0} = \mathbf{0.63}.$$

Earth would have been *smaller than Mars-plus-a-bit* — under two-thirds its present radius — as recently as the Jurassic.

**(c)** With $M$ fixed, density scales as $R^{-3}$ and surface gravity as $R^{-2}$:

$$\frac{\rho_p}{\rho_0} = \left(\frac{R_0}{R_p}\right)^{3} = (1.581)^{3} = \mathbf{3.95}, \qquad \rho_p = 3.95 \times 5.51 = \mathbf{21.8\ \text{g/cm}^3}.$$

$$\frac{g_p}{g_0} = \left(\frac{R_0}{R_p}\right)^{2} = (1.581)^{2} = \mathbf{2.50}, \qquad g_p = 2.50 \times 9.81 = 24.5\ \text{m/s}^2.$$

**(d)** Both numbers are fatal, and for different reasons.

**The density is impossible as chemistry.** A whole-planet mean density of $21.8\ \text{g/cm}^3$ exceeds osmium ($22.6$), the densest element at standard conditions, and vastly exceeds anything the solar system is made of — Earth's uncompressed density is about $4.1\ \text{g/cm}^3$ and its bulk composition is constrained independently by chondritic meteorites ([`planetary-science` syllabus](../../planetary-science/syllabus.md), 2.1). To rescue the hypothesis you must let Earth's *mass* grow too, which means inventing matter, or let $G$ vary in time, which is bounded by lunar laser ranging to parts in $10^{13}$ per year.

**The gravity is testable against the rock record.** Surface gravity 2.5 times present in the Mesozoic would show up in the fossils — sauropod limb-bone scaling, pterosaur flight, the settling velocities that sort sediment grains ([1.5](01-05-sedimentary-rocks.md)) — and it does not. It is also directly contradicted by palaeomagnetic determinations of Earth's ancient radius, which return the present value to within a few percent.

**The geologic observation that decides it: subduction.** An expanding Earth needs creation at ridges with *no destruction anywhere*. But **Wadati–Benioff zones** are exactly the missing sink — planar arrays of earthquake foci dipping from a trench to 700 km depth, tracing cold lithosphere descending into the mantle. Add the trenches themselves, the arc volcanism produced by dehydrating that slab ([1.3](01-03-how-the-earth-melts.md)), and blueschist — a high-pressure, low-temperature metamorphic assemblage that forms only where crust is pushed down fast enough to stay cold ([1.6](01-06-metamorphic-rocks-rock-cycle.md)) — and the destruction side of the ledger is documented three independent ways.

**Note the shape of this argument, which is the shape of the whole lesson.** The expanding Earth was not defeated by the evidence *for* spreading, which it shared. It was defeated by an observation that spreading predicts and expansion forbids. **Rival hypotheses are separated by the observations on which they disagree, and by nothing else.**

## Flashback

**From Lesson 1.5 (Sedimentary Rocks):** A road cut exposes three steeply tilted beds, stacked in the order 1–2–3 as you walk from the southeast end of the outcrop to the northwest end.

- **Bed 1** — poorly sorted, angular sandstone, roughly 30 percent fresh pink feldspar grains up to 3 mm, in a clay-rich matrix.
- **Bed 2** — well-sorted, well-rounded, almost pure quartz sandstone in cross-bedded sets; within each set the foresets are truncated at the northwest end and curve tangentially into the base at the southeast end.
- **Bed 3** — a single graded unit, coarse sand at its southeast side passing upward through fine sand to silt at its northwest side.

(a) What does bed 1's texture and mineralogy tell you about its transport history and source rock? (b) What does bed 2 tell you about maturity, and why does it differ so much from bed 1? (c) Using beds 2 and 3, determine which end of the outcrop is stratigraphically *up*, and hence whether bed 1 or bed 3 is the oldest. (d) State what the sequence as a whole records.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**(a) Short transport from a granitic source, buried fast.** Angularity means the grains were not abraded, so transport was short. Poor sorting means the flow never had time to separate grain sizes — a short, energetic, probably episodic transport. Most diagnostic is the **fresh feldspar**: feldspar hydrolyses to clay readily, so 30 percent of it surviving unaltered means the sediment was eroded, moved and buried faster than chemical weathering could work on it. Quartz plus abundant feldspar points to a **granitic or gneissic source** ([1.4](01-04-igneous-rocks-and-bodies.md)). A feldspar-rich sandstone of this kind is an **arkose**, and it implies steep relief close by — typically a fault-bounded basin next to uplifted crystalline basement.

**(b) Texturally and mineralogically mature; long or repeated transport.** Well-rounded grains have been abraded a long way. Good sorting means a persistently winnowing agent — a beach or a dune field, where waves or wind repeatedly rework the same sand. The absence of feldspar is the mineralogical half: **everything less durable than quartz has been destroyed**, either by long exposure to chemical weathering or by recycling through more than one sedimentary cycle.

The contrast with bed 1 is not about the source rock being different. **It is about time and distance.** Given the same granite, a short fast trip yields an arkose and a long slow one yields a quartz arenite.

**(c) Northwest is up; bed 1 is the oldest.**

Two independent way-up indicators, and they agree:

- **Cross-beds.** Foresets are deposited on the lee side of a migrating ripple or dune, curving tangentially into the set's base and being planed off by erosion at the top. So the **truncated end is the top and the tangential end is the bottom.** Truncation is at the northwest, so northwest is up.
- **Graded bedding.** A waning current drops its coarsest load first, so a graded unit **fines upward**. Coarse at the southeast, silt at the northwest: northwest is up.

Since the beds run 1–2–3 from southeast to northwest, and northwest is stratigraphically up, the sequence is **right-way-up, not overturned**, and **bed 1 is the oldest**.

Getting this right matters because the beds are steeply tilted, so the geometry alone cannot tell you which way is younger — a fold limb can be flipped past vertical ([2.5](02-05-folds-faults-structures.md)), and [4.1](04-01-relative-dating-unconformities.md) will make the point that superposition without a way-up check is an assumption, not an observation.

**(d) An upward-maturing sequence: the source area being worn down, or the site becoming more distal.** From immature arkose to mature quartz arenite to a graded turbidite-style unit is a progression from close-to-source and high-energy toward reworked and then deeper or quieter water. The most common reading is a basin whose bounding relief is being eroded away while it subsides — early coarse debris shed off nearby uplands, then mature sand delivered from further afield after the highlands are worn low. [4.4](04-04-stratigraphy-facies-correlation.md) will formalize reading a vertical sequence as a horizontal migration of environments.

</details>
