# Geology · Lesson 5.2: Earth History I — Hadean to Proterozoic

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 5: Earth's Interior, History & Resources · Builds on: [5.1](05-01-earths-internal-structure.md), [4.2](04-02-radiometric-dating.md), [4.3](04-03-geologic-timescale.md) · Unlocks: [5.3](05-03-earth-history-phanerozoic.md)

## Why this matters

From accretion at 4.567 Ga to the base of the Cambrian at 0.539 Ga is **88 percent of Earth history** — and it is covered by roughly one percent of the world's outcrop. ($\text{Ga}$ = giga-annum, billions of years before present; $\text{Ma}$ = mega-annum, millions.) For the first 500 million years there is no rock at all. Not "little rock" — none.

So this lesson cannot be a narrative. **It has to be organized around what the evidence *is***, because the whole intellectual content of Precambrian geology is the inference: how do you establish that the ocean existed at 4.4 Ga when nothing that old survives, or that the air had no oxygen when air does not fossilize?

Two answers recur, and they are the lesson's spine. The first is **a survivor**: one absurdly durable mineral outlives its parent rock and carries the record forward. The second is **a non-destruction**: a mineral that *should* have been destroyed sits there intact, and its survival puts a ceiling on the chemistry of the environment it passed through. Almost everything below is one of those two arguments.

The biology of this interval — the origin of life, the origin of the eukaryotic cell, the Ediacaran biota — belongs to [evolution-ecology 2.4](../../evolution-ecology/lessons/02-04-macroevolution-history-of-life.md). This lesson stays on the physical and geochemical planet.

## The idea

**The rock record thins backwards, and it thins unfairly.** Plate tectonics recycles ocean floor on a 200 Myr timescale ([2.1](02-01-evidence-for-drift.md)) and erosion strips continents. The processes that make Earth *Earth* are the same processes that destroy its memory. What survives is not a random sample of the past; it is the subset that was chemically inert, tectonically sheltered, or both.

**Three chapters, defined by what kind of evidence exists:**

| Eon | Span | The record is… |
|---|---|---|
| **Hadean** | 4.567–4.03 Ga | no rocks — **isolated mineral grains** recycled into younger sediment, plus meteorites and the Moon |
| **Archean** | 4.03–2.5 Ga | a few shielded **cratons** — patchy, metamorphosed, but real rock with real chemistry |
| **Proterozoic** | 2.5–0.539 Ga | a **global geochemical record** — enough sediment on enough continents to read planet-scale signals |

**And one story dominates: the Great Oxidation Event at about 2.4 Ga.** The reason it is believable is not that any one piece of evidence is decisive. It is that **three chemically unrelated records, in three different rock types, on different continents, all break at the same time.** Banded iron formations stop; red beds start; and a strange sulfur-isotope signature that can only be made in an ozone-free atmosphere vanishes. Nothing links these three except the oxygen content of the air.

**The other big idea is that the atmosphere and the solid Earth are one coupled system.** Oxygen destroys methane; methane was holding up the greenhouse against a Sun about 15 percent fainter than today's; so the oxidation of the air was followed within about 50 Myr by the first global glaciations. **Chemistry changed the climate, and the climate is recorded in the rocks.**

## The formal version

### Hadean: a mineral outlives its rock

**Accretion delivers absurd amounts of energy.** Assemble Earth from dispersed material and you release its gravitational binding energy. For a uniform sphere,

$$U = \frac{3}{5}\,\frac{GM^2}{R}$$

where $G = 6.674\times10^{-11}\ \text{N}\,\text{m}^2\text{/kg}^2$, $M = 5.97\times10^{24}\ \text{kg}$ and $R = 6.371\times10^{6}\ \text{m}$. That gives $U = 2.24\times10^{32}\ \text{J}$.

To melt the *entire* planet you must raise it about 1300 K (specific heat $c \approx 1000\ \text{J/(kg}\cdot\text{K)}$) and supply a latent heat of roughly $4\times10^{5}\ \text{J/kg}$ — about $1.7\times10^{6}\ \text{J/kg}$, so $1.0\times10^{31}\ \text{J}$ in total.

$$\frac{2.24\times10^{32}}{1.0\times10^{31}} \approx 22$$

*In words: **accretion supplies about twenty times the energy needed to melt the whole Earth**, before any radioactivity is counted.* The **magma ocean** is not a hypothesis that needs a special mechanism; it is the default, and the hard question is how Earth cooled fast enough to have a surface at all.

**Core formation** follows immediately: molten iron is denser than molten silicate, it drains to the centre, and *that* releases yet more gravitational energy. Hafnium–tungsten isotope systematics put core segregation within the first 30–50 Myr. The planetary mechanics is [`planetary-science`](../../planetary-science/syllabus.md) 2.1; the resulting layers are [5.1](05-01-earths-internal-structure.md).

**The Moon-forming giant impact**, at roughly 4.5 Ga, is supported by four independent observations that no other model reconciles: the Moon has a tiny core (about 2 percent of its mass, against Earth's 32), it is strongly depleted in volatile elements, the Earth–Moon system carries anomalously high angular momentum, and lunar and terrestrial oxygen isotopes are **identical to analytical precision**. That last one is a problem as well as a support — a Mars-sized impactor should have had its own isotopic signature, and the "isotopic crisis" is an active research area.

**The Jack Hills zircons.** In the Jack Hills of Western Australia, a roughly 3.0 Ga quartzite contains **detrital zircon grains** ($\text{ZrSiO}_4$) dated as old as **4.4 Ga**. The host rock is 1.4 billion years younger than the grains it contains. Why zircon and nothing else:

- **It takes up uranium and rejects lead.** $\text{U}^{4+}$ substitutes for $\text{Zr}^{4+}$ (similar charge and radius); $\text{Pb}^{2+}$ does not fit. So the initial daughter inventory is essentially zero — the ideal U–Pb chronometer ([4.2](04-02-radiometric-dating.md)).
- **It is nearly indestructible.** Hardness 7.5, chemically inert, and a Pb closure temperature above 900 °C. A zircon survives weathering, river transport, burial, metamorphism, and often survives the *melting* of its host rock.

$$\boxed{\;\text{A detrital age dates the } \textit{source}, \text{ not the sediment: it is a } \textbf{maximum} \text{ age for the host rock}\;}$$

$$\text{and a } \textbf{minimum} \text{ age for continental-type crust } \textit{somewhere} \text{ on Earth.}$$

**What the grains say about water.** Zircon in equilibrium with mantle-derived magma has $\delta^{18}\text{O} \approx 5.3$ per mil. Many Jack Hills grains give 6.5–7.4 per mil. **Heavy oxygen is a low-temperature signature** — you get it by weathering or hydrothermally altering rock at the surface and then remelting it. So the 4.4 Ga magmas assimilated material that had already met **liquid water**. Add Ti-in-zircon thermometry giving crystallization near 700 °C — granite-like, not basalt-like — and the picture is a cool, wet Earth with continental-type melts within 150 Myr of the Moon-forming impact.

**This is an inference, not an observation, and it is contested**: some grains have disturbed U–Pb systematics, and alternative explanations for heavy $\delta^{18}\text{O}$ exist. Hold it firmly but not tightly.

**The late heavy bombardment is genuinely open.** Apollo impact-melt ages cluster near 3.9 Ga, which was read as a spike in impacts 600 Myr after accretion. But the Apollo sites all sit within reach of Imbrium ejecta, so the cluster may date **one basin**, not an era. A monotonic decline in impact flux now fits the data at least as well.

### Archean: a hotter mantle, and air with no oxygen

**The oldest intact rock** is the Acasta gneiss of northwest Canada at 4.03 Ga. The **Isua supracrustals** of Greenland (3.7–3.8 Ga) are the oldest *sedimentary* rocks, and they matter because they include **pillow basalts** (erupted under water) and **banded iron formation** (deposited in water). Oceans existed.

**Komatiites are a thermometer for the mantle.** These are ultramafic lavas with over 25 weight percent MgO, common at 3.5–2.7 Ga and essentially absent since. To erupt a liquid that magnesium-rich you need eruption temperatures near 1600 °C against about 1200 °C for modern basalt, and a **degree of partial melting near 50 percent** against roughly 10 percent beneath a modern ridge ([1.3](01-03-how-the-earth-melts.md)). Their diagnostic **spinifex texture** — long skeletal olivine blades — records quenching of a superheated, low-viscosity melt.

*In words: **the Archean mantle was 200–300 K hotter**, and it melted much further when it decompressed.* That is expected, because radiogenic heat production decays. Relative to today, at 3.0 Ga:

$$^{40}\text{K}: \; 2^{3.0/1.25} = 5.3\times, \qquad ^{235}\text{U}: \; 2^{3.0/0.704} = 19\times, \qquad ^{238}\text{U}: \; 1.6\times$$

**The short-lived isotopes dominate the difference**, which is why the Archean Earth ran hot and why it cooled quickly.

**When did plate tectonics begin? This is unresolved, and proposals span 4.0 Ga to 0.8 Ga.** The honest statement is that the diagnostic markers appear at different times: andesites and possible arc signatures are early; ophiolites and passive margins appear around 2.0 Ga; and **blueschist**, which requires the cold subduction geotherm of [1.6](01-06-metamorphic-rocks-rock-cycle.md), appears only after about 0.8 Ga. A hotter mantle makes lithosphere weaker and more buoyant, which argues against clean subduction, and the alternatives — stagnant-lid convection, "drip" tectonics where dense lower crust founders vertically, a squishy-lid regime — are live candidates for the early Archean.

**The atmosphere had no free oxygen, and the argument is non-destruction.** Archean fluvial conglomerates (the Witwatersrand, 2.9–2.7 Ga; the Huronian, to 2.45 Ga) contain **rounded detrital grains of pyrite ($\text{FeS}_2$) and uraninite ($\text{UO}_2$)** — river gravels, transported and abraded. Both minerals are unstable in oxygenated water:

$$\text{pyrite} \rightarrow \text{iron oxide} + \text{sulfate}, \qquad \text{UO}_2 \rightarrow \text{soluble uranyl } \text{UO}_2^{2+}$$

At present-day oxygen levels those grains dissolve within a few kilometres of transport. **They are round, so they travelled; they exist, so the water and air were reducing.** These grains disappear from the record at about 2.4 Ga and never return.

**Cratons and why anything survives.** Archean crust persists where it sits on a thick (200 km or more), cold, **depleted mantle keel**. Extracting large melt fractions stripped iron and aluminium from that mantle, leaving low-density residual harzburgite that is permanently buoyant and too stiff to be entrained by convection. The Archean record exists because a few blocks became mechanically un-recyclable.

### Proterozoic: the Great Oxidation, in triplicate

Oxygenic photosynthesis is the source; the geology is the *timing*. Molecular and isotopic hints of it reach back to 3.0–2.7 Ga, yet oxygen does not accumulate until 2.45–2.32 Ga. **The delay is the puzzle**, and Worked Example 2 shows why the usual explanation is wrong.

| Record | What it is | Before | After | Why it tracks oxygen |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Banded iron formation** | mm-to-cm couplets of iron oxide and chert, in enormous sheets | abundant, peak 2.6–2.4 Ga | ends ~1.85 Ga | needs $\text{Fe}^{2+}$ dissolved and transported basin-wide, which needs an **anoxic deep ocean** |
| **Detrital pyrite, uraninite** | rounded grains in river gravels | present to 2.4 Ga | gone | survive only below roughly $10^{-3}$ of present oxygen |
| **Red beds** | continental sandstones with hematite grain coatings | absent | from ~2.3 Ga | need free $\text{O}_2$ in the *air* to rust iron in situ |
| **Sulfur MIF** ($\Delta^{33}\text{S}$) | mass-independent S-isotope anomaly in sediments | up to several per mil, to 2.45 Ga | zero after 2.32 Ga | needs an **ozone-free** atmosphere |

**The sulfur signal is the sharpest tool, so define it.** Ordinary isotope fractionation is *mass-dependent*: whatever a process does to $^{34}\text{S}$, it does about half as much to $^{33}\text{S}$. Departure from that line is measured by

$$\Delta^{33}\text{S} \;\approx\; \delta^{33}\text{S} - 0.515\,\delta^{34}\text{S}$$

*In words: $\Delta^{33}\text{S}$ is the part of the $^{33}\text{S}$ anomaly that ordinary chemistry cannot produce.* Values are in per mil, and modern sediments sit at $0.0 \pm 0.2$.

The only known way to generate a large $\Delta^{33}\text{S}$ is **ultraviolet photolysis of $\text{SO}_2$** in the upper atmosphere — and preserving it requires two further conditions: no ozone screen (or the UV never reaches the $\text{SO}_2$), and sulfur must leave the atmosphere by **two chemically distinct exit channels** (elemental sulfur to pyrite, sulfate to sulfate minerals) so the anomalies do not average back to zero. Both fail once oxygen exceeds roughly $10^{-5}$ of the present level.

$$\boxed{\;\Delta^{33}\text{S} \text{ detects } 10^{-5}\ \text{PAL}; \text{ red beds need } \sim10^{-2}\ \text{PAL} \;}$$

**Three orders of magnitude of sensitivity apart, and they agree on the timing anyway.** That is why the GOE is not seriously disputed even though no one has ever measured Archean air.

**What the GOE did not do:** it took oxygen to perhaps 1 percent of the present level, not to breathable air. The **mid-Proterozoic (1.8–0.8 Ga) is remarkably static** — low oxygen, sulfidic mid-depth oceans, little change in the sedimentary record. Its nickname, the "boring billion", is affectionate and slightly unfair.

**The climate consequence.** Methane is a strong greenhouse gas whose atmospheric lifetime in an anoxic atmosphere is on the order of $10^4$ years, against about a decade today. Oxygen destroys it. Meanwhile the **faint young Sun** was delivering only about 83 percent of present luminosity at 2.4 Ga. Removing the methane greenhouse from a planet already short of sunlight is dangerous — and the **Huronian glaciations (2.45–2.22 Ga, at least three of them)** follow the oxygen step immediately. On the figure, the oxygen rise and the first glacial bar share the same dashed guide line.

**Supercontinent cycles.** Continental blocks assemble and disperse repeatedly: **Kenorland** (~2.7–2.1 Ga), **Nuna/Columbia** (~1.8–1.35 Ga), **Rodinia** (~1.1–0.75 Ga), **Pannotia** (~0.63 Ga). The evidence is the same kind used in [2.1](02-01-evidence-for-drift.md) — matching orogenic belts across later ocean basins, and **apparent polar wander paths** that converge when blocks are reassembled — plus one Precambrian-specific tool: **detrital zircon age spectra**, whose peaks record continent-wide magmatic events at assembly. The **Wilson cycle** (open an ocean, close it, collide, rift again) is not a Phanerozoic invention; it is the Proterozoic's basic rhythm.

**Snowball Earth (Cryogenian, 717–635 Ma).** The evidence is a set of things that should not co-occur:

- **Glacial diamictites with dropstones deposited at sea level and, from palaeomagnetic inclination, at low latitude.** Ice at the equator, at sea level.
- **Cap carbonates**: metres of dolostone lying sharply, with no transition, directly on the glacial till, carrying strongly negative $\delta^{13}\text{C}$.
- **Banded iron formation returns** (the Rapitan) after a 1.1 Gyr absence — which requires the deep ocean to have gone anoxic again, exactly what an ice lid would do.

The mechanism is an **ice–albedo runaway**: ice raises albedo, which cools, which grows ice. Below about 30 degrees latitude the feedback becomes unstable and the ice reaches the equator. The escape is the reverse of [3.1](03-01-weathering-soils.md)'s carbon thermostat — under global ice, **silicate weathering shuts off**, so volcanic $\text{CO}_2$ accumulates with no sink until the greenhouse overwhelms the albedo (P3 computes how long). The cap carbonate is then the hangover: a $\text{CO}_2$-rich atmosphere attacking freshly ground glacial rock flour, delivering a colossal alkalinity flux to the ocean.

**Open question, and a real one: "hard snowball" versus "slushball".** Photosynthetic eukaryotes survived, which is hard to arrange under kilometres of ice everywhere; modellers who allow a thin-ice or open-water tropical belt can accommodate them. The abruptness of the cap carbonates is the strongest argument for the extreme version. **Both camps are still publishing.**

The **Ediacaran (635–539 Ma)** closes the eon: a further rise in oxygen, the Gaskiers glaciation, and the first large soft-bodied animals — whose story is [evolution-ecology 2.4](../../evolution-ecology/lessons/02-04-macroevolution-history-of-life.md)'s, not ours.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (mechanical — dating a Hadean zircon, and checking it).** A single detrital zircon from the Jack Hills quartzite is analysed by ion microprobe. Radiogenic ratios: $^{206}\text{Pb}/^{238}\text{U} = 0.980$ and $^{207}\text{Pb}/^{235}\text{U} = 75.5$. Decay constants: $\lambda_{238} = 1.55125\times10^{-10}\ \text{yr}^{-1}$, $\lambda_{235} = 9.8485\times10^{-10}\ \text{yr}^{-1}$. (a) Compute both ages. (b) What does the comparison test? (c) State precisely what event the age dates.

(a) The age equation from a daughter/parent ratio ([4.2](04-02-radiometric-dating.md)) is $t = \lambda^{-1}\ln(1 + D/P)$:

$$t_{206/238} = \frac{\ln(1.980)}{1.55125\times10^{-10}} = \frac{0.68310}{1.55125\times10^{-10}} = 4.404\times10^{9}\ \text{yr}$$

$$t_{207/235} = \frac{\ln(76.5)}{9.8485\times10^{-10}} = \frac{4.3373}{9.8485\times10^{-10}} = 4.404\times10^{9}\ \text{yr}$$

**Both give 4.40 Ga.** (Sanity check: the fraction of $^{238}\text{U}$ remaining is $e^{-\lambda t} = 1/1.980 = 0.505$ — almost exactly half, and the $^{238}\text{U}$ half-life is 4.468 Ga. Consistent.)

(b) **This is the concordia self-check, and it is why U–Pb on zircon is the gold standard.** Uranium has two decay chains running at very different rates in the same crystal, so you get **two independent clocks for free**. Agreement means the grain stayed a closed system. Disagreement — *discordance* — means lead was lost, usually by later heating or radiation damage; and because lead loss displaces the two ratios unequally, the $^{207}\text{Pb}/^{235}\text{U}$ age is degraded less than the $^{206}\text{Pb}/^{238}\text{U}$ age. **A discordant grain announces its own unreliability**, which no single-chronometer system can do.

(c) It dates **crystallization of that zircon grain from a melt** — strictly, its cooling below the Pb closure temperature, which for zircon is above 900 °C and so is essentially the crystallization moment.

It does **not** date the quartzite, which is 3.0 Ga: the grain is detrital, so 4.40 Ga is a *maximum* age for the sediment. And it does not prove there is 4.4 Ga rock beneath the Jack Hills. What it proves is that **at 4.4 Ga, somewhere on Earth, there was silica-rich magma cool enough to crystallize zircon** — 165 Myr after the planet formed.

**Example 2 (why you'd care — why oxygen was late, and why the usual answer fails).** The standard story is that banded iron formations were an oxygen sink: photosynthesis made $\text{O}_2$, dissolved $\text{Fe}^{2+}$ consumed it, and only when the iron ran out could oxygen accumulate. Test it. Assume global BIF holds $3\times10^{17}\ \text{kg}$ of iron ($3\times10^{14}$ tonnes), all oxidized from $\text{Fe}^{2+}$ to $\text{Fe}^{3+}$. Modern net $\text{O}_2$ production by organic-carbon burial is about $1\times10^{13}\ \text{mol/yr}$; take the Archean rate as ten times lower.

**Step 1 — how much oxygen the iron consumed.** Oxidation is one electron per iron; $\text{O}_2$ accepts four. So 4 mol Fe per mol $\text{O}_2$:

$$n_{\text{Fe}} = \frac{3\times10^{17}\ \text{kg}}{0.05585\ \text{kg/mol}} = 5.37\times10^{18}\ \text{mol}, \qquad n_{\text{O}_2} = \frac{5.37\times10^{18}}{4} = 1.34\times10^{18}\ \text{mol}$$

**Step 2 — compare with the present atmosphere.** Atmospheric mass $5.15\times10^{18}\ \text{kg}$, 23.1 weight percent $\text{O}_2$:

$$n_{\text{O}_2,\text{atm}} = \frac{0.231 \times 5.15\times10^{18}}{0.032} = 3.72\times10^{19}\ \text{mol}, \qquad \frac{1.34\times10^{18}}{3.72\times10^{19}} = 0.036$$

**All the banded iron on Earth absorbed about 3.6 percent of one present atmospheric inventory of oxygen.**

**Step 3 — how long that buys.** At $1\times10^{12}\ \text{mol/yr}$:

$$t = \frac{1.34\times10^{18}}{1\times10^{12}} = 1.3\times10^{6}\ \text{yr}$$

**About 1.3 million years.** The gap to be explained is 250–550 Myr.

**The explanation fails by two to three orders of magnitude, and it fails robustly.** Make the BIF estimate ten times larger and the production rate ten times smaller and you get 130 Myr — still short, and now using indefensible numbers.

**The reason it fails is structural, not arithmetical: BIF is a *reservoir*, and a reservoir of finite size cannot hold back a steady flux for long.** To suppress oxygen for hundreds of millions of years you need a **competing flux** — a continuous supply of reductant that consumes $\text{O}_2$ as fast as it is made. That is what the modern picture provides: reduced volcanic and metamorphic gases ($\text{H}_2$, $\text{H}_2\text{S}$, $\text{CO}$) plus continuous weathering of sulfide and ferrous minerals on land.

**And the GOE then becomes a crossover, not an exhaustion**: oxygen accumulates the moment the source flux overtakes the sink flux. Candidate causes for the crossover include a gradual oxidation of the mantle's volcanic output, the growth of continents changing what weathers, and **irreversible hydrogen escape to space** — every $\text{H}_2$ molecule lost from the top of the atmosphere leaves the planet a little more oxidized, permanently.

**Reframing a "how big is the tank" question as a "which flux is larger" question is the whole move**, and it is the same move that distinguishes a stock from a flow in any budget problem.

## Watch out

- **You might think the GOE gave Earth breathable air.** It reached roughly 1 percent of the present level and then *stalled for a billion years*. Oxygen approaching modern values is a Neoproterozoic-to-Palaeozoic development, half a billion years ago, not two and a half.
- **You might think banded iron formations prove there was no oxygen.** Backwards. BIF requires **both**: an anoxic deep ocean to dissolve and transport $\text{Fe}^{2+}$ across a basin, *and* an oxidant where it precipitated. It is a signature of a chemically **stratified** ocean, which is why it ends when the deep ocean finally ventilates.
- **You might read a 4.4 Ga detrital zircon as a 4.4 Ga rock at that locality.** It gives a maximum age for the host sediment and a minimum age for felsic crust *somewhere*. Provenance and outcrop are different claims.
- **You might conflate "oldest rock", "oldest mineral" and "age of Earth".** Acasta gneiss 4.03 Ga, Jack Hills zircon 4.40 Ga, meteorite-derived Earth age 4.567 Ga. Three different questions with three different answers, and quoting one for another is a common error.
- **You might take the late heavy bombardment as established.** The 3.9 Ga cluster may be an artifact of where Apollo landed. Treat it as a hypothesis under active revision.
- **You might picture Snowball Earth as an ocean frozen solid to the bottom.** Even the "hard" version freezes only the surface, and whether the tropics had thin ice or open water is unsettled.
- **You might expect glaciation to *cause* the oxygen rise.** The causal arrow runs the other way: oxygen destroyed the methane greenhouse, and the Huronian glaciations followed.

## One-liner

> Almost everything known about the first four billion years comes from two arguments — a mineral tough enough to outlive its own rock (zircon), and a mineral that survived when it should have dissolved (detrital pyrite) — and the Great Oxidation is believable because three chemically unrelated records break at the same moment.

**P1 (a)** Using $\Delta^{33}\text{S} \approx \delta^{33}\text{S} - 0.515\,\delta^{34}\text{S}$:

$$\text{A}: \; 5.60 - 0.515(3.20) = 5.60 - 1.648 = \mathbf{+3.95}\ \text{per mil}$$

$$\text{B}: \; 6.20 - 0.515(12.00) = 6.20 - 6.180 = \mathbf{+0.02}\ \text{per mil}$$

**(b)** B sits at zero within a tenth of the analytical precision — **mass-dependent, so post-GOE**, younger than about 2.32 Ga. A is nearly 20 standard deviations off the mass-dependent line: **mass-independent, so pre-GOE**, older than about 2.45 Ga. **A is older.**

The reasoning chain is worth restating: a large $\Delta^{33}\text{S}$ requires UV photolysis of $\text{SO}_2$, which requires no ozone shield, which requires oxygen below roughly $10^{-5}$ PAL — *and* it requires two separate sulfur exit channels to keep the anomaly from averaging away, which also fails once the ocean has a large oxidized sulfate reservoir.

**(c)** Because $\delta^{34}\text{S}$ is **mass-dependent**, so ordinary chemistry and biology produce it freely — microbial sulfate reduction alone spans tens of per mil, and its magnitude depends on sulfate availability, temperature and the organisms present. A large $\delta^{34}\text{S}$ tells you something interesting about the sulfur cycle but nothing unambiguous about oxygen.

**The general point: $\Delta^{33}\text{S}$ works as a proxy precisely because there is no ordinary way to make it.** A signal with exactly one known mechanism is worth far more than a large signal with many.

**P2 (a)** Bottom to top, so oldest first:

1. **Detrital pyrite–uraninite conglomerate** — a river gravel.
2. **Banded iron formation** — chemical precipitation in a marine basin.
3. **Red bed** — continental sandstone; cross-bedding and mud cracks mean a subaerial, periodically dry environment ([1.5](01-05-sedimentary-rocks.md)).
4. **Marine shale** — quiet deep water; the sequence is drowned.

Order: 1 → 2 → 3 → 4, by superposition ([4.1](04-01-relative-dating-unconformities.md)), and the beds are undeformed so superposition applies directly.

**(b)**
- Unit 1: **anoxic**. Rounded, transported pyrite and uraninite could not survive oxygenated river water — below roughly $10^{-3}$ PAL.
- Unit 2: **anoxic deep ocean, but an oxidant available where deposition happened.** BIF is the signature of a stratified ocean, not of zero oxygen everywhere.
- Unit 3: **free $\text{O}_2$ in the atmosphere.** Hematite coatings on grains in a subaerial sandstone means iron rusted in contact with air — at least $10^{-2}$ PAL.
- Unit 4: marine, and the pyrite is authigenic (see (d)), so it constrains the local sea floor, not the air.

**(c)** The core straddles the **Great Oxidation Event**. The detrital-pyrite conglomerate places unit 1 before about **2.45 Ga**; the red bed places unit 3 after about **2.3 Ga**. The BIF between them is consistent with the peak just before the transition. **The whole core spans roughly 2.5 to 2.2 Ga.**

**(d)** **Detrital and authigenic pyrite are different evidence.** Unit 1's pyrite is *rounded* — it was eroded from a source rock and carried down a river through oxygenated-or-not surface water, so its survival constrains the surface environment. Unit 4's pyrite grew **in place**, in anoxic pore water beneath the sediment–water interface, where sulfate-reducing bacteria produce sulfide regardless of what the atmosphere is doing. Authigenic pyrite forms happily today.

**The general skill: ask whether a mineral is telling you about where it formed or about where it travelled.** Only the travelled grain constrains the environment it crossed.

**(e)** Measure $\Delta^{33}\text{S}$ on the pyrite in unit 1 (or on sulfide in unit 2). **Expect a strongly non-zero value** — several per mil — confirming a pre-GOE, ozone-free atmosphere and independently checking the detrital-mineral argument. If it came back at zero, the detrital interpretation of unit 1 would need re-examination.

*(A U–Pb age on detrital zircons in unit 1 would give a maximum age but not a minimum, and a Re–Os or Pb–Pb age on unit 2 would date it directly — either is a reasonable alternative answer, but the sulfur measurement is the one that tests the same question with different chemistry.)*

**P3 (a)** Partial pressure is the weight of the $\text{CO}_2$ column per unit area, so the column mass is

$$m/A = \frac{p}{g} = \frac{1.2\times10^{4}\ \text{Pa}}{9.81\ \text{m/s}^2} = 1223\ \text{kg/m}^2$$

$$A = 4\pi R^2 = 4\pi(6.371\times10^{6})^2 = 5.10\times10^{14}\ \text{m}^2$$

$$M_{\text{CO}_2} = 1223 \times 5.10\times10^{14} = 6.24\times10^{17}\ \text{kg}, \qquad n = \frac{6.24\times10^{17}}{0.044} = \mathbf{1.42\times10^{19}\ \text{mol}}$$

*(Careful here: you cannot get the mole count by taking 12 percent of the present atmosphere's moles. Pressure fixes the column* mass*, and $\text{CO}_2$ is 52 percent heavier per mole than air, so the same pressure means fewer moles.)*

**(b)** $$t = \frac{1.42\times10^{19}}{7\times10^{12}} = 2.0\times10^{6}\ \text{yr}$$

**About 2 million years.** Published estimates range to a few tens of millions of years, mostly because Proterozoic outgassing rates are uncertain by a factor of a few — at $3\times10^{12}\ \text{mol/yr}$ you get 4.7 Myr. **The robust conclusion is the order of magnitude: escape takes millions of years, not thousands, and it is slow because the only source is volcanic.**

That timescale is itself testable, and it matches the observed thickness and duration of the Sturtian glaciation (roughly 57 Myr) only if the ice persisted well past the point at which $\text{CO}_2$ began accumulating — which is part of why the duration of these events is still argued about.

**(c)** At deglaciation the atmosphere holds roughly 0.12 bar of $\text{CO}_2$, more than 300 times the pre-industrial level, and the continents are blanketed in freshly ground, unweathered **glacial rock flour** — enormous mineral surface area ([3.1](03-01-weathering-soils.md)). Carbonic-acid weathering of silicates then runs at an extraordinary rate, delivering $\text{Ca}^{2+}$ and bicarbonate to an ocean that precipitates carbonate almost as fast as it arrives.

It is **sharp** because both the trigger and the exhaustion are fast relative to the glaciation. The ice sheets collapse in perhaps $10^3$–$10^4$ years once the runaway reverses, and the $\text{CO}_2$ excess is drawn down in $10^4$–$10^5$ years. **A metres-thick dolostone deposited in that window sits directly on till with no transitional bed — which is exactly what is observed, and which is the strongest argument that the deglaciation really was catastrophic.**

**(d)** **A continuous, well-dated record of photosynthetic eukaryotic life through the glacial interval, at low latitude, with an independent constraint on local ice cover.** Hard snowball must explain how obligate photosynthesizers survived kilometres of ice; slushball predicts open or thin-ice tropical water where they could persist.

Reasonable alternatives: sedimentary structures within the glacial units that require open water (wave ripples, ice-rafted debris needing calving into water) at demonstrated low palaeolatitude; or a proxy that constrains ice thickness directly. **What will not settle it is more diamictites** — both models predict those.

</details>

## Flashback

**From Lesson 4.2 (Radiometric Dating):** A gneiss from the core of an eroded orogen yields three ages on three minerals from the same hand sample: hornblende Ar–Ar at 48.0 Ma (closure 500 °C), biotite Ar–Ar at 42.0 Ma (closure 300 °C), and apatite fission track at 12.0 Ma (closure 110 °C). The regional geothermal gradient is 25 °C/km. (a) Explain in one sentence why three ages from one rock are all correct. (b) Compute the cooling rate over each interval. (c) Convert each to an exhumation rate in mm/yr. (d) Interpret the change.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**(a)** **A radiometric age dates cooling through that mineral's closure temperature, not "the rock's formation".** Above closure, the daughter isotope diffuses out as fast as it is made; below it, it is retained. Three minerals with three closure temperatures record three moments on one cooling path.

**(b)** $$500 \rightarrow 300\ °\text{C}: \quad \frac{200\ °\text{C}}{48.0 - 42.0\ \text{Myr}} = \frac{200}{6.0} = \mathbf{33.3\ °\text{C/Myr}}$$

$$300 \rightarrow 110\ °\text{C}: \quad \frac{190\ °\text{C}}{42.0 - 12.0\ \text{Myr}} = \frac{190}{30.0} = \mathbf{6.33\ °\text{C/Myr}}$$

**(c)** Dividing by the geothermal gradient converts a cooling rate into a rate of ascent through the crust ($1\ \text{km/Myr} = 1\ \text{mm/yr}$):

$$\frac{33.3\ °\text{C/Myr}}{25\ °\text{C/km}} = \mathbf{1.33\ \text{mm/yr}}, \qquad \frac{6.33\ °\text{C/Myr}}{25\ °\text{C/km}} = \mathbf{0.25\ \text{mm/yr}}$$

Total exhumation since the hornblende closed: $500/25 = \mathbf{20\ \text{km}}$ of overburden removed.

**(d)** **Exhumation slowed by a factor of about five at around 42 Ma.** The fast early phase is what [2.6](02-06-mountain-building.md) describes for an active orogen — tectonic denudation plus rapid erosion of high, steep topography while convergence continues. The slow later phase is the long decay: convergence has stopped, relief has been reduced, erosion rates fall with slope, and isostatic rebound is now doing most of the work of bringing rock upward.

**Two caveats worth stating.** The conversion assumes a *steady* geotherm, but rapid exhumation advects heat upward and compresses isotherms, which makes the geothermal gradient higher during fast phases — so 1.33 mm/yr is an **overestimate**. And 20 km is exhumation relative to the closure isotherm, not the height of any former mountain.

</details>
