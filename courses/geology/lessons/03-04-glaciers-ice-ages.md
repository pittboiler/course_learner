# Geology · Lesson 3.4: Glaciers & the Ice Ages

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 3: Surface Processes · Builds on: [3.3](03-03-rivers-landscape-evolution.md), [2.4](02-04-how-rock-deforms.md), [2.6](02-06-mountain-building.md) · Unlocks: [3.5](03-05-deserts-wind-coasts.md) (deserts, wind & coasts)

## Why this matters

Most of the landscape north of about 40 degrees latitude is not being made by the processes currently operating on it. It was made by ice that has been gone for ten thousand years and has not been replaced. The Great Lakes, the Finger Lakes, Long Island and Cape Cod, Puget Sound, the ten thousand lakes of Minnesota, the fjords of Norway and Chile, the boulder-strewn moorlands of northern Britain, and the sand-and-gravel aquifers that supply much of the American Midwest are all **inherited landforms**. A river reworks them; it did not build them.

That makes this lesson unusual: it is the one place in Module 3 where **the process you are reading is not the process you can watch.** You infer the ice from what it left.

Two more reasons it earns its space. First, ice is the surface agent with the cleanest quantitative handle — mass balance is literally bookkeeping, and it makes a testable prediction about the terminus every year. Second, **ice is the only erosional agent that can cut below base level.** [3.3](03-03-rivers-landscape-evolution.md) established that a river cannot erode below the level of the sea it drains into. A glacier can, and does, by more than a kilometre. The reason is a one-line argument, and it explains fjords, the Great Lakes, and every overdeepened basin on the map.

## The idea

**Ice is a mineral and a glacier is a metamorphic rock.** Ice satisfies every clause of [1.1](01-01-what-a-mineral-is.md)'s definition — naturally occurring, inorganic, solid, definite composition, ordered structure. And the way snow becomes glacier ice is exactly [1.6](01-06-metamorphic-rocks-rock-cycle.md)'s solid-state recrystallization: fresh snow (density around $100\ \text{kg/m}^3$) compacts, its delicate crystals sublime and regrow into rounded grains (**firn**, $400$–$830\ \text{kg/m}^3$), pore space closes off, and the result is **glacier ice** at about $917\ \text{kg/m}^3$. Nothing melts. It is metamorphism at temperatures a hiker walks around in, because ice sits at a very high fraction of its own melting temperature.

**A glacier is a conveyor belt, and this is the single most misunderstood thing about them.** Snow lands in the upper part, is buried, becomes ice, and flows down-valley. At the lower end it melts, sublimes or calves away. **The ice always moves toward the terminus. The terminus moves according to the arithmetic.**

Write the arithmetic as bookkeeping over a year:

- **Accumulation** — everything the glacier gains (snowfall, wind drift, avalanche).
- **Ablation** — everything it loses (melt, calving, sublimation).
- The line where the two exactly balance over a year is the **equilibrium line**; above it is the accumulation zone, below it the ablation zone.

If the gain over the whole glacier exceeds the loss, the snout pushes forward. If the loss exceeds the gain, the snout melts back — **while the ice inside it is still flowing forward the entire time.** A retreating glacier is a conveyor belt still running forward into a furnace that is burning faster than the belt delivers. This is why a retreating glacier keeps grinding striations into its bed, keeps carrying boulders down-valley, and keeps building moraines: retreat is a statement about the *edge*, not about the *motion*.

**How ice moves: two mechanisms, and one of them is [2.4](02-04-how-rock-deforms.md) all over again.**

- **Internal deformation.** Ice creeps. It is a solid deforming plastically, and — the crucial part — its response is strongly **nonlinear**: double the stress and you get roughly eight times the strain rate.
- **Basal sliding.** If meltwater is present at the bed, the glacier slides on it. A **warm-based** glacier (bed at the pressure-melting point) both slides and erodes hard; a **cold-based** glacier is frozen to its bed, barely slides, and barely erodes — which is why parts of the Canadian Shield still carry pre-glacial weathering surfaces that an ice sheet sat on top of and left alone.

And the depth structure is [2.4](02-04-how-rock-deforms.md)'s brittle–ductile transition, shrunk from 15 kilometres to about 30 metres. **Near the surface, confining pressure is too low to suppress fracture, so the ice cracks: that is what a crevasse is.** Below roughly 30 m the overburden closes cracks and the ice flows instead. Same argument, same physics, four hundred times smaller — and it is why crevasses have a maximum depth in dry ice, and why filling one with meltwater (which pushes the walls apart, exactly the effective-stress effect from [2.4](02-04-how-rock-deforms.md)) lets it drive far deeper.

**Why ice can erode below base level.** Ice flow is driven by the slope of the **ice surface**, not the slope of the bed. So a glacier can flow *uphill* over an adverse bed slope as long as its surface still slopes downward — and while it does, it keeps quarrying. Rivers have no equivalent trick: water needs a downhill water surface all the way to the sea. Hence **overdeepenings**: Sognefjord's floor is about 1300 m below sea level behind a sill only ~100 m deep, a bathtub no river could ever have cut.

**What ice leaves, and the one property that identifies it.** Ice is a terrible sorting machine — it carries a clay particle and a house-sized boulder at exactly the same speed, because it carries them rather than suspending them in a flow. So **ice-deposited sediment (till) is unsorted and unstratified**, and that is the diagnostic. Meltwater, on the other hand, sorts beautifully, so glacial *meltwater* deposits (outwash, eskers) look like the fluvial deposits of [3.3](03-03-rivers-landscape-evolution.md) — because that is what they are. **The contrast between till and outwash within a few metres of section is the cleanest fingerprint of a glacial environment there is**, and P2 asks you to use it.

**The crust remembers the ice.** An ice sheet is a load, and by [2.6](02-06-mountain-building.md)'s isostasy the lithosphere sinks under it and rises again after it melts — slowly, because the mantle is viscous. Hudson Bay and the Gulf of Bothnia are still rising about a centimetre a year, twelve thousand years after their ice left.

**And the ice came and went repeatedly.** The Pleistocene record shows dozens of glacial cycles. Their pacing matches Earth's orbital cycles, which is Milankovitch's insight — but the forcing is far too weak to do the job on its own, and the period that dominates the record is the one with the *weakest* direct effect. That mismatch is a live problem, and stating it honestly is part of the lesson.

## The formal version

**Mass balance.** Let $b$ be the **specific net balance** — the net gain or loss at a point over a year, quoted as a depth of **water equivalent** (m w.e.), so that snow, firn and ice are directly comparable. The glacier-wide net balance is

$$B = \int_{\text{glacier}} b\,dA \;\approx\; \bar{b}_c A_c \;+\; \bar{b}_a A_a$$

where $A_c$ and $A_a$ are the accumulation and ablation areas and $\bar b_c > 0$, $\bar b_a < 0$ their mean specific balances.

*In words: add up what the glacier gained and subtract what it lost. Positive means the glacier is growing and the snout will eventually advance; negative means it is shrinking and the snout will retreat.*

**The lag matters.** The terminus does not respond the same year. A useful estimate of the **response time** is

$$\tau_r \approx \frac{H_{\max}}{|b_{\text{terminus}}|}$$

*In words: it takes about as long as it would take the ablation rate at the snout to melt away the glacier's maximum thickness.* For $H_{\max} = 200\ \text{m}$ and terminal ablation of $4\ \text{m/yr}$, that is **50 years** — so a glacier's snout position today partly reflects the climate of the mid-twentieth century.

**Driving stress.** The shear stress the ice exerts on its bed is the lithostatic weight resolved along the slope:

$$\boxed{\;\tau_b = \rho_i\, g\, H \sin\alpha\;}$$

with $\rho_i \approx 917\ \text{kg/m}^3$, $H$ the ice thickness and $\alpha$ the **ice-surface** slope. *In words: thickness and surface slope, nothing else.* Real glaciers cluster in the range $50$–$150\ \text{kPa}$, which is the origin of the field rule of thumb that basal shear stress is "about one bar."

**Glen's flow law.** Ice creeps as a power-law solid:

$$\dot{\varepsilon} = A\,\tau^{\,n}, \qquad n \approx 3$$

*In words: strain rate goes as the cube of stress, so ice is far softer under high stress than a linear (Newtonian) fluid would be.* This is the same power-law creep as [materials-science 4.4](../../materials-science/lessons/04-04-failure-fracture-fatigue-creep.md); the contrast with a Newtonian viscous flow ([fluid-dynamics 3.3](../../fluid-dynamics/lessons/03-03-stokes-flow.md), where $\dot\varepsilon \propto \tau$) is what makes ice sheets behave the way they do. $A$ rises steeply with temperature, so warm ice is soft ice.

Integrating Glen's law through the thickness gives the surface velocity from internal deformation alone:

$$u_s = \frac{2A}{n+1}\,(\rho_i g \sin\alpha)^{n}\,H^{\,n+1} \;\;\propto\;\; H^{4}\sin^{3}\!\alpha \quad (n=3)$$

**That fourth power is the important line in this lesson.** *In words: a glacier that thins by a third does not slow by a third — it very nearly stops.* Ice-sheet thinning is strongly self-limiting for the deforming part of the flow, which is why the fast-moving parts of Greenland and Antarctica are the ones dominated by *sliding* rather than deformation.

**Pressure melting.** The melting point of ice falls by about $0.074\ \text{K}$ per $\text{MPa}$. Under $1000\ \text{m}$ of ice the overburden is $\rho_i g H = 9.0\ \text{MPa}$, depressing the melting point by $0.67\ \text{K}$ — small, but enough to keep a bed wet that would otherwise be frozen, and it is why the base of a thick ice sheet melts while its surface is far below freezing.

**Isostatic depression.** From [2.6](02-06-mountain-building.md)'s Airy relation with an ice load of thickness $H_i$ floating the lithosphere on mantle of density $\rho_m$:

$$\boxed{\;d = \frac{\rho_i}{\rho_m}\,H_i \;\approx\; 0.28\,H_i\;}$$

*In words: the crust sinks a little over a quarter of the ice thickness.* Recovery is a viscous relaxation, which to first order obeys

$$\frac{dd}{dt} = -\frac{d}{\tau}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad d(t) = d_0 e^{-t/\tau}$$

with a mantle relaxation time $\tau$ of a few thousand years — the same first-order decay you solved in [ode-refresher 1.3](../../ode-refresher/lessons/01-03-first-order-models.md). The quantitative treatment of the mantle viscosity that sets $\tau$ belongs to [`geophysics`](../../geophysics/syllabus.md) 4.2.

**The landform inventory, as inferences rather than names.** Every one of these is a measurement you can make in the field:

| You see | It is | It proves |
|---|---|---|
| Parallel scratches and polish on bedrock | striations | ice flowed along that **line** (not which way along it) |
| Asymmetric bedrock knob: smooth up-ice, ragged down-ice | roche moutonnée | abrasion on the stoss side, plucking on the lee — gives the **direction** |
| Streamlined hill, blunt end up-ice | drumlin | flow direction, and a deforming soft bed |
| Amphitheatre bowl at a valley head | cirque | a glacier nucleated here |
| Knife ridge / pyramidal peak between cirques | arête / horn | two or three cirques cut back into one another |
| Parabolic (U) cross-section, truncated spurs | glacial trough | ice, not a river, widened and deepened this valley |
| Tributary valley entering high on the wall | hanging valley | the trunk glacier was thicker, so it cut deeper |
| Trough floor below sea level behind a shallow sill | fjord / overdeepening | erosion below base level — **only ice does this** |
| Unsorted, unstratified, clay-to-boulder mix; faceted striated clasts | **till** | deposited directly by ice |
| Boulder of a rock type absent for tens of km | erratic | transport direction, traced to its source outcrop |
| Ridge of till at a former ice margin | moraine (terminal, recessional, lateral, medial) | the terminus **stood still** here long enough to build it |
| Sorted, cross-bedded sand and gravel in braided sheets | outwash | meltwater, not ice, did the depositing |
| Sinuous ridge of sorted gravel, sometimes running uphill | esker | a **pressurized** meltwater tunnel under the ice |
| Enclosed depression in outwash, often a pond | kettle | a buried ice block melted out |
| Rhythmic couplets of pale silt and dark clay | **varves** | one couplet = one year in a proglacial lake |

**Varves are a chronometer**, not just a texture: pale coarse silt settles in summer meltwater, dark fine clay settles under winter lake ice. Count the couplets and you have counted the years — the technique De Geer used to build a Swedish chronology thousands of years long, and one of the counting methods [4.1](04-01-relative-dating-unconformities.md) and [4.4](04-04-stratigraphy-facies-correlation.md) rely on.

**The orbital cycles.** Three geometric parameters vary quasi-periodically:

| Parameter | What varies | Range | Period |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Eccentricity** $e$ | shape of Earth's orbit | $0.000$–$0.058$ (now $0.0167$) | ~100 kyr and ~405 kyr |
| **Obliquity** | axial tilt | $22.1^\circ$–$24.5^\circ$ (now $23.44^\circ$) | ~41 kyr |
| **Precession** | direction of tilt relative to perihelion | full circuit | ~23 kyr and ~19 kyr |

Here $1\ \text{kyr} = 1000$ years. The **key quantitative fact**, which P3 makes you verify, is that these cycles change the *distribution* of sunlight in season and latitude enormously while changing the annual global total almost not at all: the annual mean insolation depends on eccentricity only through

$$\bar{S} \propto (1-e^2)^{-1/2},$$

which over the full eccentricity range is a change of under $0.2\ \text{percent}$.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (mechanical — a mass balance, and what it does to the snout).** A valley glacier has an accumulation area of $12\ \text{km}^2$ with mean net balance $+1.0\ \text{m w.e./yr}$, and an ablation area of $8\ \text{km}^2$ with mean net balance $-2.0\ \text{m w.e./yr}$. The snout is a wedge whose surface meets the bed at a taper angle of $6^\circ$, and ice at the terminus is flowing forward at $30\ \text{m/yr}$. (a) Compute the net balance. (b) Convert the deficit into a thinning rate over the ablation zone. (c) Estimate the retreat rate of the terminus. (d) Reconcile (c) with the $30\ \text{m/yr}$ of forward flow.

**(a)**

$$B = (12\times10^{6}\ \text{m}^2)(+1.0\ \text{m}) + (8\times10^{6}\ \text{m}^2)(-2.0\ \text{m}) = 12\times10^{6} - 16\times10^{6}$$

$$B = \mathbf{-4\times10^{6}\ \text{m}^3\ \text{w.e./yr}}$$

**The glacier is losing four million cubic metres of water equivalent per year.** Note that the accumulation area is 60 percent of the glacier, which *looks* healthy — the balance is still negative because the loss rate per unit area is twice the gain rate. **Area alone does not tell you the sign.**

**(b)** Spread that deficit over the ablation zone:

$$\text{thinning} = \frac{4\times10^{6}\ \text{m}^3}{8\times10^{6}\ \text{m}^2} = 0.50\ \text{m w.e./yr} \;\Rightarrow\; \frac{0.50}{0.917} = 0.55\ \text{m of ice per year}.$$

**(c)** The terminus is where the ice thickness reaches zero. Lower a wedge of taper angle $\theta$ by $\Delta h$ and that zero-crossing moves back by $\Delta h/\tan\theta$:

$$\text{retreat} = \frac{0.55\ \text{m/yr}}{\tan 6^\circ} = \frac{0.55}{0.1051} = \mathbf{5.2\ \text{m/yr}}$$

**(d)** Both are true at once. **Ice is arriving at the terminus at $30\ \text{m/yr}$ and the terminus is moving backwards at $5\ \text{m/yr}$**, because melting removes ice from the snout faster than flow delivers it. Over the same year, that glacier will grind fresh striations into its bed, transport boulders 30 m down-valley, and retreat. Nothing about the retreat says the ice slowed down.

**Example 2 (why you'd care — how much of the Laurentide is still leaving).** The Laurentide Ice Sheet was about $3.0\ \text{km}$ thick over Hudson Bay and disappeared from there roughly $8000$ years ago. Take $\rho_i = 917\ \text{kg/m}^3$, $\rho_m = 3300\ \text{kg/m}^3$, and a mantle relaxation time $\tau = 2500\ \text{yr}$. (a) How far did the crust sink? (b) How much depression remains, and how fast is it rising now? (c) Check against observation, and say what is wrong with the model.

**(a)**

$$d_0 = \frac{\rho_i}{\rho_m}H_i = \frac{917}{3300}\times 3000\ \text{m} = 0.278 \times 3000 = \mathbf{834\ \text{m}}$$

**Nearly a kilometre.** For scale: that is deeper than the topographic relief of most mountain ranges, produced by a load that then vanished.

**(b)** With $d(t) = d_0 e^{-t/\tau}$ and $t/\tau = 8000/2500 = 3.2$:

$$d(8\ \text{kyr}) = 834\,e^{-3.2} = 834 \times 0.0408 = \mathbf{34\ \text{m still down}}$$

$$\dot{d} = \frac{d}{\tau} = \frac{34\ \text{m}}{2500\ \text{yr}} = 0.0136\ \text{m/yr} = \mathbf{13.6\ \text{mm/yr}}$$

**(c)** GPS around Hudson Bay measures present uplift of roughly $10$–$13\ \text{mm/yr}$, and the Gulf of Bothnia about $9\ \text{mm/yr}$. **A one-parameter exponential reproduces the observed rate to within a few tens of percent** — which is a genuinely satisfying result for a model this crude, and it is the strongest everyday evidence that the mantle is a viscous fluid on long timescales ([2.3](02-03-driving-mechanism.md)).

What is wrong with it: the load did not vanish instantaneously (so the model badly overestimates the earliest uplift rates, giving $834/2500 = 334\ \text{mm/yr}$ at $t=0$), a real Earth has depth-varying viscosity so the relaxation is a sum of exponentials rather than one, and the load's *width* matters — broad loads relax more slowly because they deform deeper, stiffer mantle. That last effect is why [`geophysics`](../../geophysics/syllabus.md) 4.2 owns the quantitative version.

**Three things you can go and check.** The residual depression shows up as a **negative gravity anomaly** over Hudson Bay — a real mass deficit, because the mantle has not finished flowing back. Raised beaches ring Hudson Bay in staircases climbing over 200 m, each one a former shoreline stranded by uplift. And the region's rivers and lake outlets are being tilted, which is slowly changing the drainage of the Great Lakes.

## Watch out

- **You might think a retreating glacier is a glacier flowing backwards.** It never is. **Ice flow and terminus position are independent quantities**: the ice always moves toward the snout, and the snout advances or retreats purely on the sign of accumulation minus ablation. A retreating glacier still erodes, still transports, still builds moraines. The only thing "retreat" tells you is that melting is outrunning delivery.
- **You might read a nested set of moraines as a series of separate advances.** A moraine forms wherever the terminus **stands still** long enough for the conveyor to pile debris at one spot — which happens during a *pause* in retreat as readily as at a maximum. A staircase of recessional moraines usually records one stepwise retreat, not five advances. Only the outermost one marks the maximum extent.
- **You might expect everything glacial to be unsorted.** Till is; outwash, eskers, kames and varves are not, because **meltwater** deposited them and water sorts. The right question at an outcrop is never "glacial or fluvial" but "ice-laid or meltwater-laid" — and the two sit in the same section metres apart. An esker that runs uphill is the giveaway that the water was flowing under pressure inside the ice.
- **You might think orbital cycles change how much sunlight Earth gets.** They almost entirely **redistribute** it — by season and by latitude. The change in the annual global mean is under $0.2\ \text{percent}$, while high-latitude summer insolation swings by tens of watts per square metre. Milankovitch forcing is a seasonality argument, not an energy-budget argument, and the quantity that matters is summer melt at high northern latitude.
- **You might say the ice age ended.** We are *in* an ice age — the Quaternary — defined by the presence of permanent ice sheets. What ended around 11.7 kyr ago was the last **glacial**; the Holocene is an **interglacial** within an ongoing ice age. Greenland and Antarctica are the evidence sitting in plain sight.

## One-liner

> A glacier is a conveyor belt that only ever runs forward — the terminus advances or retreats purely on accumulation minus ablation — and everything it leaves is a readout: unsorted till where the ice dropped it, sorted outwash where meltwater did, overdeepened basins where ice cut below base level, and a crust still rising because the ice has not finished leaving.

**P2 (🟡)** A roadcut in Ohio exposes, from the bottom up:

1. Limestone bedrock, polished, with straight parallel grooves trending $025^\circ$–$205^\circ$.
2. $4\ \text{m}$ of unsorted, unstratified sediment: clay-rich matrix containing everything from granules to $1\ \text{m}$ boulders, many clasts faceted and striated. Several boulders are granite; the nearest granite outcrop is a pluton $80\ \text{km}$ to the north-northeast.
3. $3\ \text{m}$ of well-sorted, cross-bedded sand and gravel with rounded clasts.

(a) Name units 2, 3 and 4 and the agent that deposited each. (b) Give the ice-flow direction and say which observation fixes the *sense* rather than just the line. (c) Reconstruct the sequence of events. (d) How long did unit 4 take to accumulate, and what mean sedimentation rate does that imply?

**P3 (🔴, bridges to climate science)** (a) Using $\bar S \propto (1-e^2)^{-1/2}$ and a global mean insolation of $340\ \text{W/m}^2$, compute the change in annual mean insolation between a near-circular orbit ($e = 0.005$) and Earth's maximum ($e = 0.058$). (b) Summer insolation at $65^\circ\text{N}$ varies by roughly $100\ \text{W/m}^2$ over a precession cycle. Form the ratio. (c) Eccentricity has the weakest direct forcing of the three cycles, yet the ice record of the last 800 kyr is dominated by a ~100 kyr period. State the paradox precisely, explain the role eccentricity actually plays, and name the class of explanation that has to do the remaining work.

**P1 (a)**

$$\tau_b = \rho_i g H \sin\alpha = 917 \times 9.81 \times 220 \times \sin 4^\circ$$

$$917 \times 9.81 = 8996\ \text{N/m}^3; \quad 8996 \times 220 = 1.979\times10^{6}\ \text{Pa}; \quad \sin 4^\circ = 0.06976$$

$$\tau_b = 1.979\times10^{6} \times 0.06976 = 1.38\times10^{5}\ \text{Pa} = \mathbf{138\ \text{kPa}}$$

**Entirely typical** — glaciers cluster in the $50$–$150\ \text{kPa}$ band, and $138\ \text{kPa}$ is near the top of it, consistent with a thick, moderately steep valley glacier.

**(b)** At fixed slope, $u_s \propto H^4$:

$$\frac{u_{\text{new}}}{u_{\text{old}}} = \left(\frac{150}{220}\right)^{4} = (0.6818)^{4} = \mathbf{0.216}$$

**A 32 percent thinning cuts the deformational velocity to 22 percent of its former value — a factor of 4.6 slowdown.** This is Glen's law's non-linearity doing the work, and it matters: as an ice mass thins, its internal deformation collapses far faster than its thickness does. Fast-flowing outlet glaciers therefore have to be moving by **basal sliding**, not deformation, since sliding does not carry that fourth power.

**P2 (a)**

- **Unit 2 is till**, deposited directly by ice. Every diagnostic is present at once: unsorted (granules to boulders together), unstratified (no bedding), a clay-rich matrix supporting large clasts, and **faceted, striated clasts** — stones ground against the bed while frozen into the ice. No flow of water can produce this assemblage; water sorts.
- **Unit 3 is outwash**, deposited by glacial *meltwater*. Well sorted, cross-bedded and rounded means a current did the work — this is [3.3](03-03-rivers-landscape-evolution.md)'s braided-stream deposit, sitting on top of till.
- **Unit 4 is a varved glaciolacustrine silt and clay**, deposited in a still proglacial lake. The paired couplets are annual: pale coarse silt settling from summer meltwater inflow, dark fine clay settling out slowly under winter ice cover.

**(b)** The **striations give the line**: $025^\circ$–$205^\circ$, a north-northeast to south-southwest axis. A scratch is symmetric and cannot tell you which way along that line the ice travelled.

**The granite erratics fix the sense.** Granite is absent locally and its nearest source lies $80\ \text{km}$ **north-northeast** — along the striation line. Ice therefore flowed **from the NNE toward the SSW**, i.e. toward $205^\circ$. (Roche moutonnée asymmetry or drumlin shape would settle it equally well; provenance is what this outcrop offers.)

**(c)** Reading upward is reading forward in time ([4.1](04-01-relative-dating-unconformities.md)'s superposition):

1. **Ice advanced** over the limestone from the NNE, warm-based and sliding, abrading the bedrock into a polished, striated pavement and quarrying granite from the pluton on the way.
2. **Till was laid down** beneath or at the ice — unit 2, carrying the granite erratics.
3. **The ice retreated**, and meltwater streams built a braided outwash apron across the till — unit 3.
4. **The drainage was ponded** (an ice dam or a moraine dam), forming a proglacial lake in which varves accumulated for 240 years — unit 4.

**The section as a whole records a retreat**, from ice-contact at the base to increasingly ice-distal above.

**(d)** One couplet per year:

$$t = 240\ \text{couplets} \times 1\ \text{yr} = \mathbf{240\ \text{years}}$$

$$\text{rate} = \frac{1.2\ \text{m}}{240\ \text{yr}} = 5.0\times10^{-3}\ \text{m/yr} = \mathbf{5\ \text{mm/yr}}$$

**That is a very fast sedimentation rate** — hundreds of times faster than deep-marine mud — which is exactly what you expect from a lake being fed rock flour by a melting ice sheet. It also means the lake was short-lived: 240 years is a geological instant, and the whole unit is a snapshot of one stage of deglaciation.

$$\frac{\bar S_{\max}}{\bar S_{\min}} = \frac{1.0016862}{1.0000125} = 1.0016737 \;\Rightarrow\; +0.167\ \text{percent}$$

$$\Delta \bar S = 340\ \text{W/m}^2 \times 0.0016737 = \mathbf{0.57\ \text{W/m}^2}$$

**(b)**

$$\frac{100\ \text{W/m}^2}{0.57\ \text{W/m}^2} \approx \mathbf{175}$$

**The seasonal-latitudinal redistribution is about 175 times larger than the change in the global annual total.** This is the whole reason Milankovitch's argument is framed in terms of *high-latitude summer* insolation: the question is never how much energy Earth receives, it is whether last winter's snow at $65^\circ\text{N}$ survives the summer.

**(c) The paradox, stated precisely:** of the three orbital parameters, eccentricity produces by far the smallest change in insolation anywhere — a fraction of a watt per square metre in the global mean, and even its regional effects are small compared with obliquity and precession. Yet the benthic oxygen-isotope record of the last ~800 kyr is dominated by a ~100 kyr sawtooth that matches eccentricity's period, with obliquity (41 kyr) and precession (23 kyr) present but subordinate. **The record is loudest at the frequency where the forcing is quietest.**

**What eccentricity actually does** is act as an **amplitude envelope for precession**, and this part is not mysterious. Precession changes which season occurs at perihelion — but if the orbit is a circle, perihelion is meaningless and precession changes nothing at all. The strength of the precession signal is therefore proportional to $e$. That is what the dashed envelope in the figure's lower panel shows, and it explains why precession's amplitude waxes and wanes on 100 kyr and 405 kyr rhythms. It does **not** explain why ice volume tracks the envelope rather than the carrier.

**The class of explanation required is internal, nonlinear amplification** — the response has to supply the period, not the forcing. The leading candidates, which are complementary rather than competing:

- **Ice–albedo feedback**: more ice means a brighter planet means more cooling. Strongly nonlinear and strongly self-reinforcing.
- **Carbon dioxide**: ice cores show $\text{CO}_2$ falling from about 280 to 180 ppm into every glacial and rising again at every termination. It follows the initiation but amplifies the swing, so it is a feedback rather than a trigger.
- **Ice-sheet dynamics and isostasy**: a large ice sheet depresses its own bed (Example 2), lowering its surface toward warmer air, and its base warms and lubricates — building in a size threshold and a collapse mechanism. This is the most natural source of a *sawtooth* with slow growth and abrupt termination, which is the shape the record actually has and which no sinusoidal forcing can produce.

**The decisive evidence that the period comes from the response and not the forcing** is the **Mid-Pleistocene Transition**: between roughly 1.25 and 0.7 Ma the record switched from a 41 kyr (obliquity-paced) rhythm to a 100 kyr one — **with no corresponding change in the orbital cycles**, which have run unchanged throughout. Something inside the climate–ice–carbon system changed its own resonance. Naming that something is an open problem, and the machinery for attacking it belongs to [`climate-science`](../../climate-science/syllabus.md) rather than here. What geology contributes is the record being explained: the till sheets, the varve counts, the marine isotope stack and the erratics.

</details>

## Flashback

**From Lesson 3.3 (Rivers & Landscape Evolution):** A meandering river is eroding its cut banks at $0.8\ \text{m/yr}$. Two adjacent bends have grown until only a $120\ \text{m}$ neck separates them, and both outer banks are migrating toward each other at that rate. (a) How long until cutoff? (b) Name the resulting landform and describe what fills it. (c) A bridge is planned across one of the bends; on which bank should the engineers refuse to put a pier, and why?

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**(a)** Both banks advance on the neck, so it closes at $2 \times 0.8 = 1.6\ \text{m/yr}$:

$$t = \frac{120\ \text{m}}{1.6\ \text{m/yr}} = \mathbf{75\ \text{years}}$$

**(b)** Cutoff produces an **oxbow lake** — the abandoned bend, sealed off once the river takes the shorter route through the neck.

**What fills it is diagnostic**: cut off from the channel, the oxbow no longer receives bed load, so it fills with **fine silt and clay** dropped from overbank floodwater, plus organic muck from swamp vegetation. The result is a lens-shaped **clay plug** — fine-grained, organic-rich, and enclosed in the sands and gravels of the surrounding floodplain. Recognizing that plug in the subsurface is how a buried meander belt is mapped, and it matters in [3.6](03-06-groundwater-aquifers-karst.md) because a clay plug is an aquiclude sitting inside an aquifer.

**(c) No pier on the outer (cut) bank.** Helical flow in a bend drives surface water outward against the outer bank and returns along the bed toward the inner bank, so the outer bank gets the erosion and the deepest water (the thalweg sits against it) while the inner bank builds a **point bar** of sediment. That asymmetry is not a transient — it is the mechanism by which meanders migrate, so **the outer bank is guaranteed to keep retreating**, at $0.8\ \text{m/yr}$ here. A pier founded there is undermined by scour and eventually left in mid-channel.

The inner bank is depositional and therefore stable in the erosional sense, but it is aggrading and shoaling, which brings its own navigational problems. The right answer for a bridge is to cross at a **straight reach** between bends, where the flow is symmetric and the channel is not migrating.

</details>
