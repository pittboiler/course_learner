# Geology · Lesson 5.3: Earth History II — The Phanerozoic

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 5: Earth's Interior, History & Resources · Builds on: [5.2](05-02-earth-history-hadean-proterozoic.md), [4.3](04-03-geologic-timescale.md), [4.4](04-04-stratigraphy-facies-correlation.md) · Unlocks: [5.4](05-04-resources-geologic-hazards.md)

## Why this matters

[5.2](05-02-earth-history-hadean-proterozoic.md) had one problem: almost no record. The Phanerozoic — 538.8 million years, 12 percent of Earth history — has the opposite problem. (As in 5.2: $\text{Ma}$ is mega-annum, millions of years *before present*; $\text{Myr}$ is a *duration* in millions of years; $\text{Ga}$ is billions before present.) There is so much rock that the difficulty is no longer *finding* evidence but **deciding which records are independent of each other**, because a story assembled from five proxies that all respond to the same thing is one observation wearing five hats.

So this lesson has a different spine from 5.2. There, the recurring move was *how do you know anything at all*. Here it is: **one engine drives most of what you see, and the skill is tracing its consequences down to something you can measure in an outcrop.** That engine is the [Wilson cycle](02-01-evidence-for-drift.md) — continents assembling into a supercontinent and dispersing again — and it sets sea level, climate, ocean chemistry, where mountains are, and where coal, oil and salt end up ([5.4](05-04-resources-geologic-hazards.md)).

The biology — the Cambrian explosion, the Big Five as evolutionary events, the radiations that followed them — belongs to [evolution-ecology 2.4](../../evolution-ecology/lessons/02-04-macroevolution-history-of-life.md). **This lesson owns the physical planet and the physical evidence: the tectonics, the sediment, the isotopes, the impact debris.** Where the two meet, we supply the driver and hand over the consequence.

## The idea

**The supercontinent cycle is a machine with four outputs, and they move together.**

When continents are dispersed, the mid-ocean ridge system is long and much of the seafloor is young. Young seafloor is hot, thermally expanded and therefore *shallow* ([geophysics](../../geophysics/syllabus.md) 4.2 owns the cooling model; [heat-transfer 2.2](../../heat-transfer/lessons/02-02-semi-infinite-solid.md) is the same $\sqrt{t}$ half-space solution). A shallow ocean basin holds less water for its area, so **the water goes somewhere: onto the continents.** Sea level rises, shelves flood, and the continents grow warm shallow seas.

When a supercontinent exists, the reverse: fewer, shorter ridges, older and colder and deeper seafloor, a bigger basin, a lower sea level. Add high continentality (a huge landmass has a dry, extreme interior) and a large land area at high latitude and you have the recipe for an ice age.

$$\boxed{\;\text{dispersed continents} \Rightarrow \text{young seafloor} \Rightarrow \text{shallow basins} \Rightarrow \text{high sea level} \Rightarrow \text{greenhouse}\;}$$

*In words: **where the continents are controls how deep the ocean basin is, and that controls how much of the continents is under water.*** Look at the figure: track (a) and track (c) are not independent curves that happen to correlate. One causes the other.

**The Phanerozoic runs that cycle roughly once.** Gondwana and a set of northern blocks converge through the Palaeozoic into Pangaea at about 320 Ma, Pangaea holds for around 120 Myr, and then breaks apart from 200 Ma to now. Sea level tracks it: high in the Ordovician while the continents are scattered, at the eon's minimum in the Permian with Pangaea assembled, high again in the Cretaceous once dispersal is well underway.

**The second organizing fact is that the catastrophes are not random either.** Four of the Big Five coincide with a **large igneous province** ([2.8](02-08-volcanoes-volcanic-hazards.md)) — a continental flood basalt erupting a million cubic kilometres or more in under a million years — and one coincides with an impact. And a detail that turns out to matter more than the eruption size:

$$\textbf{twice, the severity was set by what the magma or the impactor hit, not by how big it was.}$$

The Siberian Traps intruded a basin stuffed with coal and evaporites; Chicxulub struck a platform of anhydrite and carbonate. **The target rock is part of the mechanism.** That is the single most transferable idea in this lesson.

## The formal version

### Palaeozoic: flooding a craton, then welding a supercontinent

**The Cambrian transgression is the largest continental flooding in the record, and it left a surface you can put your hand on.** Across most of North America a basal Cambrian sandstone rests directly on Precambrian crystalline rock with hundreds of millions of years missing — the **Great Unconformity**, a nonconformity ([4.1](04-01-relative-dating-unconformities.md)) of continental extent. Above it the classic transgressive triplet of [4.4](04-04-stratigraphy-facies-correlation.md): nearshore sandstone, then shale, then offshore carbonate, each facies migrating landward over the one before, exactly as Walther's law requires.

**North America records six such floodings, and they are the Palaeozoic–Cenozoic's coarsest useful subdivision.** Sloss's cratonic **sequences** — Sauk, Tippecanoe, Kaskaskia, Absaroka, Zuni, Tejas — are packages of strata separated by craton-wide unconformities. Each is one transgressive–regressive cycle lasting tens of millions of years. *In words: the interior of a continent is a very flat surface, so a small change in sea level moves the shoreline hundreds of kilometres, and the whole craton floods or drains together.*

**The orogenies build Pangaea from the outside in**, and each is a collision you can name the participants of:

| Orogeny | Age (Ma) | What collided |
|---|---|---|
| Taconic | ~470–440 | island arc onto eastern Laurentia |
| Caledonian | ~430–390 | Laurentia + Baltica; closes Iapetus |
| Acadian | ~390–360 | Avalonia onto Laurussia |
| Variscan / Hercynian | ~370–290 | Gondwana onto southern Europe |
| Alleghanian | ~325–260 | Gondwana onto Laurussia; **Pangaea complete** |

**And the Appalachians and the Caledonides are one belt, torn in half 200 Myr later.** The evidence is exactly [2.1](02-01-evidence-for-drift.md)'s: matching structural trends, matching metamorphic grades and ages, and matching stratigraphy from Alabama through Newfoundland to Ireland, Scotland and Scandinavia. But there is a sharper piece, and it is worth stating because it is fossil evidence used to locate a *suture* rather than to date a bed. **Cambro-Ordovician trilobites in Scotland belong to the Laurentian faunal province; those in England and Wales belong to the Avalonian one.** Two faunas that never mixed now sit 100 km apart, separated by the closed Iapetus suture, and the boundary runs across Britain at an angle to the modern coastline — which no static Earth explains.

**The Carboniferous coal, and an honest open question.** Between roughly 320 and 300 Ma, Euramerica sat on the equator with vast low-gradient coastal plains and a permanently wet tropical climate. Peat accumulates where plant production exceeds decay and where the water table keeps the peat anoxic — and those basins, subsiding rapidly in front of the rising Alleghanian mountains, had unlimited **accommodation** ([4.4](04-04-stratigraphy-facies-correlation.md)) to bury it in.

Whether that is the *whole* explanation is genuinely disputed. A molecular clock on the fungal enzymes that degrade lignin (class II peroxidases) places their origin near the Permo-Carboniferous boundary, which was read as: for 60 million years nothing could rot wood, so it piled up. **The counter-argument is stratigraphic and is strong**: coal abundance tracks tectonic and climatic setting rather than the fungal date, the dominant Carboniferous trees were lycopsids whose bulk was bark-like periderm rather than lignin-rich wood, and thick coals form in the Permian and again in the Cenozoic when the fungi are unambiguously present. **Treat the "lignin gap" as a live hypothesis, not a fact.**

One physical consequence is not disputed: burying that much organic carbon released oxygen, and **the Carboniferous–Permian atmosphere reached perhaps 30–35 percent $\text{O}_2$**. The rock evidence is charcoal — inertinite is abundant in these coals, and wildfire will not propagate in wet fuel below about 17 percent oxygen.

**The Late Palaeozoic Ice Age (~340–260 Ma) is Wegener's dataset.** Tillites, striated pavements and dropstones in South America, southern Africa, India, Australia and Antarctica, with striation directions that only make sense on a reassembled Gondwana centred on the pole. And it left a far-field record that is even better: **cyclothems**, the repeating coal–shale–limestone cycles of Kansas, Illinois and the English Pennines. Each cycle is one glacio-eustatic flooding and draining of the craton, driven by an ice sheet 8,000 km away. P1 reads one.

**The Permian: a supercontinent's climate.** With Pangaea assembled, the interior went arid and monsoonal. Enormous evaporites accumulate — the Zechstein of northwest Europe, the Castile Formation of the Delaware Basin — and the Castile is worth a sentence, because its calcite–anhydrite couplets have been counted, more than 200,000 of them, giving a directly counted duration in the same spirit as [3.4](03-04-glaciers-ice-ages.md)'s varves. Red beds spread. Sea level reaches its Phanerozoic minimum.

**The end-Permian, 251.9 Ma, at the physical level.** The [Siberian Traps](02-08-volcanoes-volcanic-hazards.md) erupted more than $3\times10^{6}\ \text{km}^3$ of basalt, and U–Pb zircon ages from the traps and from the extinction horizon at Meishan overlap within about 100 kyr — the two events are simultaneous to the limit of the best dating available. The geochemical record shows a negative $\delta^{13}\text{C}$ excursion of several per mil, widespread ocean anoxia and euxinia proxies, and carbonate-system evidence for acidification.

**But volume alone does not explain it, and this is where the target rock enters.** The Siberian magma intruded as sills into the Tunguska Basin, which is packed with coal, organic shale and evaporites. Heating those released thermogenic $\text{CO}_2$, methane and organohalogens on top of the magmatic gas load — so the eruption's effective carbon and ozone-destroying output was far larger than its basalt volume implies. **A comparable volume of basalt erupted through crystalline crust would have done far less.** The biological accounting is [evolution-ecology 2.4](../../evolution-ecology/lessons/02-04-macroevolution-history-of-life.md)'s.

### Mesozoic: taking Pangaea apart

**Rifting leaves a stereotyped stratigraphy, and you can read the stage of breakup off a drill core.** The Triassic **Newark Supergroup** of eastern North America is the type example of the first stage: a chain of half-grabens filled with red beds and lake sediments, the lacustrine cycles so regularly orbital (**Van Houten cycles**, precession-paced near 20 kyr) that they were used to build one of the first long astrochronologies. Then, at 201.4 Ma, the **Central Atlantic Magmatic Province** floods the same basins with basalt — the largest LIP by area known, and coincident with the end-Triassic extinction.

The sequence after rifting is: **rift-onset unconformity → synrift half-graben fill → salt → postrift thermal-subsidence wedge.** The salt stage matters because it is a matched-pair argument as clean as the Appalachian–Caledonide one. The Louann Salt of the Gulf of Mexico, and the Aptian salt of the Santos–Campos basins off Brazil and the Kwanza–Congo basins off Angola, are **conjugate deposits**: a young, narrow, restricted ocean basin in an arid latitude evaporates faster than it is resupplied ([1.5](01-05-sedimentary-rocks.md)), and when it later widens, the salt is split between the two margins. Find thick salt on one margin and you should predict it on the other. You do, and the oil industry drilled it.

Then the margin cools and sinks. The lithosphere thickens as $\sqrt{t}$, so subsidence does too — which is why passive margins carry a seaward-thickening wedge that accumulates fastest early and slowly forever after.

**The Atlantic opens in three stages**, and Worked Example 1 checks them against arithmetic: central Atlantic from about 190 Ma, South Atlantic from about 130 Ma, the North Atlantic between Greenland and Europe only at about 55 Ma.

**The western margin of North America does the opposite: it grows.** Subduction beneath the Cordillera through the Mesozoic produces arc magmatism (the Sierra Nevada batholith), the thin-skinned **Sevier** fold-and-thrust belt, and then the **Laramide** orogeny, which is the odd one — basement-cored uplifts 1,000 km inboard of the trench, best explained by the slab flattening and scraping along the base of the continent. And the whole margin is a collage of **terranes** ([2.6](02-06-mountain-building.md)): Wrangellia and its neighbours are crustal blocks with palaeomagnetic latitudes and faunas that do not belong where they now sit.

**The Cretaceous greenhouse is the eon's warm, high-water extreme.** No polar ice; forests within a few degrees of both poles; deep-ocean temperatures near 14 °C against today's 2 °C. Sea level was 170–250 m above present in the classic curves — recent compilations favour the lower half of that range, and the disagreement is itself instructive, since the number comes from stratigraphic backstripping and not from a measurement. The **Western Interior Seaway** split North America from the Arctic to the Gulf, and the flooded shelves accumulated **chalk** — coccolithophore ooze, the Niobrara and the cliffs of Dover — a rock essentially confined to the Late Cretaceous because it needs both the highstand and that particular plankton. Twice, in the Cenomanian–Turonian and earlier, the record shows **oceanic anoxic events**: globally correlatable black shales with a positive $\delta^{13}\text{C}$ excursion, deposited when a warm, sluggish, stratified ocean stopped ventilating. Those black shales are the source rock for a large share of the world's oil.

**The end-Cretaceous, 66.0 Ma: the physical evidence, and what each piece rules out.**

| Observation | What it is | What it excludes |
|---|---|---|
| **Iridium anomaly** | boundary clay carries ~9 parts per billion Ir against ~0.3 in the enclosing limestone | ordinary crustal sources: Ir is **siderophile**, so it went to the core during differentiation ([5.2](05-02-earth-history-hadean-proterozoic.md)) and crustal rock is depleted ~1000-fold relative to chondrites |
| **Shocked quartz** | grains with 2–4 sets of intersecting planar deformation features | volcanism — explosive eruptions reach roughly $1\ \text{GPa}$; multiple PDF sets need above $10\ \text{GPa}$ |
| **Ni-rich spinels, tektites, microkrystites** | quench products of a vapour plume | slow processes of any kind |
| **Thickness gradient** | millimetres in New Zealand, centimetres in Italy, **metres** of ejecta and disturbed beds around the Gulf of Mexico | a diffuse or multiple source: it points at a location |
| **Chicxulub structure** | ~180 km buried crater under Yucatán, found as a ring of gravity and magnetic anomalies; melt rock dated 66.05 Ma | coincidence — the age matches the boundary within analytical error |

**The thickness gradient is the piece to admire**, because it is a *ranging* argument: a global layer that systematically thickens toward one region names its own source, and it did so before the crater was recognized as an impact structure.

**And the honest complication.** The Deccan Traps erupted more than $10^{6}\ \text{km}^3$ across the boundary, with the main pulse straddling it. Current dating suggests a majority of the volume erupted *after* the impact, which some read as the impact having triggered or accelerated it. **What is not in doubt is that a large impact occurred at the boundary. What is argued is the partition of the kill mechanism**, and both drivers push the same environmental levers. Again, target rock: Chicxulub struck a thick anhydrite and carbonate platform, so the impact vaporized sulfate and injected sulfate aerosols along with the dust. **The severity was a function of the geology of Yucatán.**

### Cenozoic: collision, and the long cooling

**India hits Asia at about 55–50 Ma, and the collision is dated two independent ways.** Marine sedimentation ceases in the Indus–Tsangpo suture, and — more satisfyingly — **India's plate velocity drops abruptly from roughly 15 to 5 cm/yr**, recorded in the magnetic anomaly spacing of the Indian Ocean ([2.1](02-01-evidence-for-drift.md)). *In words: you can read the moment of a continental collision off the seafloor a thousand kilometres away, because the seafloor is a chart recorder of the plate's speed.* The crust beneath Tibet is now 70–80 km thick, roughly doubled, with the isostatic root that [2.6](02-06-mountain-building.md) requires.

**The climatic consequence is a real hypothesis with real problems.** Uplifting and shattering an enormous volume of silicate rock in a warm, wet monsoon exposes fresh mineral surface, and silicate weathering consumes $\text{CO}_2$ ([3.1](03-01-weathering-soils.md)). The supporting evidence is a steep Cenozoic rise in seawater $^{87}\text{Sr}/^{86}\text{Sr}$, from about 0.7077 at 40 Ma to 0.70918 today — radiogenic strontium delivered by weathering old continental crust. **The objection is the thermostat itself**: weathering is a *negative feedback* on $\text{CO}_2$, so it should adjust to the outgassing rate rather than run it down for 40 million years. Reduced volcanic outgassing, or enhanced organic-carbon burial, may be doing part of the work. Hold the Himalayan story as the leading candidate, not the answer, and hand the machinery to [`climate-science`](../../climate-science/syllabus.md).

**The temperature record itself comes from one proxy, and you must know what it measures.** The delta notation is a per mil deviation from a standard:

$$\delta^{18}\text{O} = \left(\frac{(^{18}\text{O}/^{16}\text{O})_{\text{sample}}}{(^{18}\text{O}/^{16}\text{O})_{\text{standard}}} - 1\right)\times 1000$$

Measured on the calcite of **benthic foraminifera** — bottom-dwelling single-celled protists — it responds to exactly two things:

1. **Temperature.** Oxygen-isotope fractionation between water and calcite is temperature-dependent: colder water puts more $^{18}\text{O}$ into the shell, by about $0.25$ per mil per degree Celsius.
2. **Ice volume.** Evaporation preferentially takes $^{16}\text{O}$, and snow is strongly depleted, so ice sheets are near $-40$ per mil. Storing that on land leaves the ocean **enriched**. The entire modern cryosphere accounts for about $0.55$ per mil of seawater $\delta^{18}\text{O}$, equivalent to roughly 65 m of sea level.

$$\boxed{\;\Delta\delta^{18}\text{O}_{\text{calcite}} \;=\; \underbrace{-0.25\,\Delta T}_{\text{thermometer}} \;+\; \underbrace{\Delta\delta^{18}\text{O}_{\text{seawater}}}_{\text{ice volume}}\;}$$

*In words: one number carries two signals, and you cannot separate them without a second measurement.* Worked Example 2 does the separation. **Benthic rather than planktonic** because deep water forms at high latitudes and sinks, so its temperature tracks the polar surface, and because the deep ocean is one well-mixed reservoir — a benthic record from a single core is a global signal, while a surface-dweller records local conditions.

**What the curve shows, with the physical evidence at each step:**

| Age | Event | Independent physical evidence |
|---|---|---|
| $56\ \text{Ma}$ | **PETM** — 5–8 °C in under 20 kyr | a $-3$ per mil carbon isotope excursion and an abrupt carbonate **dissolution horizon** in deep-sea cores, as the CCD shoaled |
| $50\ \text{Ma}$ | early Eocene climatic optimum, the eon's last hothouse | crocodilians and palms in the Arctic |
| $34\ \text{Ma}$ | **Eocene–Oligocene transition**: Antarctic ice sheet | $\delta^{18}\text{O}$ steps $+1.3$ per mil in ~300 kyr; ice-rafted debris appears in Southern Ocean cores; the CCD **deepens** by about 1 km |
| $23,\ 15\ \text{Ma}$ | Mi-1 glaciation; mid-Miocene optimum then cooling | Antarctic ice expands and stabilizes |
| $2.7\ \text{Ma}$ | Northern Hemisphere glaciation intensifies | ice-rafted debris in North Atlantic cores; loess in China |
| $0.0117\ \text{Ma}$ | the Holocene, our interglacial | [3.4](03-04-glaciers-ice-ages.md) owns the orbital pacing |

**Why Antarctica froze is contested, and the contest is worth knowing.** The classic answer is the **gateway** hypothesis: the opening of the Drake Passage and the Tasmanian Gateway let the Antarctic Circumpolar Current close around the continent and thermally isolate it. The competing answer is that atmospheric $\text{CO}_2$ crossed a glaciation threshold, with the gateways permissive rather than causal. **Recent proxy and modelling work has moved the weight toward $\text{CO}_2$**, and the gateway ages are themselves uncertain by several million years. Say "both are on the table" and mean it; the ocean-circulation side belongs to [`oceanography`](../../oceanography/syllabus.md).

### The Anthropocene: a stratigraphic question, answered stratigraphically

Ask it the way a stratigrapher must: **what would a geologist 10 million years from now actually find, and does it justify a formal unit?**

| Candidate marker | Signal | Survives $10\ \text{Myr}$? |
|---|---|---|
| **Bomb radionuclides** ($^{239}\text{Pu}$, $^{14}\text{C}$) | globally synchronous, sharp, 1952–1963 | **No** — see P3; the sharpest marker is the least durable |
| **Fly ash / spheroidal carbonaceous particles** | from high-temperature combustion; rises from ~1850, sharply after 1950 | **Yes** — chemically inert carbon, preserved like charcoal in coal |
| **Plastics** | over 10 gigatonnes produced; a distinctive organic component | **Yes**, in anoxic burial |
| **Novel minerals and technofossils** | over 200 anthropogenic mineral species; concrete; anthropogenic mass now exceeds living biomass | **Yes** |
| **Carbon isotope excursion** | fossil-fuel carbon is $^{13}\text{C}$-depleted, so atmospheric $\delta^{13}\text{C}$ has fallen ~2 per mil since 1850 | **Yes** — the PETM's excursion is still readable after 56 Myr |
| **Extinction and translocation horizon** | assemblage change; species moved between continents | **Yes** ([evolution-ecology 2.4](../../evolution-ecology/lessons/02-04-macroevolution-history-of-life.md)) |

**So the signal is real, large, and in several cases permanent. And the formal proposal was rejected anyway.** In March 2024 the Subcommission on Quaternary Stratigraphy voted down the proposal to ratify an Anthropocene Epoch with a GSSP in the varved sediments of Crawford Lake, Ontario, with its base at the 1952 plutonium rise; the ICS and IUGS upheld that vote. The objections were about **what a chronostratigraphic unit is for** ([4.3](04-03-geologic-timescale.md)), and they are good ones:

- **Rank and duration.** A 72-year epoch, against a Holocene of 11,700 years and Cenozoic epochs of $10^{6}$–$10^{7}$ years. Units at a given rank should be roughly commensurable, or the hierarchy stops meaning anything.
- **It is not yet a bed.** Most of the seafloor is bioturbated to 5–10 cm. At a typical shelf rate of $10\ \text{cm/kyr}$, 72 years is $0.7\ \text{cm}$ — entirely inside the mixed layer. **In most of the world there is no stratigraphic body to correlate.**
- **The base defines away the phenomenon.** Only bases are defined ([4.3](04-03-geologic-timescale.md)), so fixing the base at 1952 assigns agriculture, deforestation, the Columbian exchange and the entire Industrial Revolution to the Holocene. Human transformation of the surface is diachronous and millennia long; a chronostratigraphic unit needs one synchronous horizon. **Those two facts are in direct conflict, and the conflict is not the proposers' fault.**
- **There is a better-fitting box.** The counter-proposal that carried treats the Anthropocene as an ongoing geological **event** — the same status the Great Oxidation Event has ([5.2](05-02-earth-history-hadean-proterozoic.md)). Events may be diachronous, of any duration, and need no GSSP, and geologists use them constantly.

$$\boxed{\;\text{Rejecting the unit is a claim about stratigraphic housekeeping, not about the magnitude of human impact.}\;}$$

**That is the right note to end a course's history module on.** The timescale exists so that workers on different continents can agree which rocks correlate. A future geologist will find the fly ash, the plastics, the isotope excursion and the extinction horizon without difficulty — and will name the interval something. The argument was never about whether the signal is there.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (mechanical — integrating a spreading rate, and checking it against the map).** The conjugate continent–ocean boundaries of the central Atlantic are $5500\ \text{km}$ apart at 25 degrees north, and the oldest magnetic anomalies there give a rifting age of $190\ \text{Ma}$. The present full spreading rate at that latitude is $25\ \text{mm/yr}$. (a) Predict the width at constant present-day rate. (b) What mean rate does the observed width require? (c) Account for the difference. (d) Cross-check on the South Atlantic, which is $5440\ \text{km}$ wide at 20 degrees south, opened at $130\ \text{Ma}$, and spreads today at about $35\ \text{mm/yr}$.

**(a)** The convenient identity is $1\ \text{mm/yr} = 1\ \text{km/Myr}$, so no unit gymnastics are needed:

$$w = 25\ \text{km/Myr} \times 190\ \text{Myr} = 4750\ \text{km}.$$

$$\text{shortfall} = 5500 - 4750 = 750\ \text{km}, \qquad \frac{750}{5500} = 13.6\ \text{percent}.$$

**(b)** $$\bar{v} = \frac{5500\ \text{km}}{190\ \text{Myr}} = 28.9\ \text{km/Myr} = 28.9\ \text{mm/yr},$$

a half-rate of $14.5\ \text{mm/yr}$, and **16 percent faster than today.**

**(c) Two corrections, one of which is not spreading at all.** Continental crust stretches and thins for tens of millions of years before the first oceanic crust appears, so part of that $5500\ \text{km}$ is **rifted margin, not seafloor**. Allow a conservative $175\ \text{km}$ per margin:

$$\frac{5500 - 350}{190} = 27.1\ \text{mm/yr},$$

still **8 percent above the present rate.** The residual is real: magnetic anomaly spacing shows Cretaceous spreading was genuinely faster than Cenozoic spreading.

**(d)** $$\bar{v}_{\text{South}} = \frac{5440\ \text{km}}{130\ \text{Myr}} = 41.8\ \text{mm/yr} \quad\text{against}\quad 35\ \text{mm/yr} \text{ today} \;\Longrightarrow\; \text{19 percent faster}.$$

**Two things fall out, and the second is the payoff.**

First, an internal consistency check: **the South Atlantic is 60 Myr younger and almost exactly as wide, so it must have opened faster — and independently, it did.** The arithmetic and the geology agree without being made to.

Second, **both basins say the same thing: Cretaceous spreading was faster than today's, by roughly a fifth.** Faster spreading means more young, hot, thermally buoyant seafloor, which means less room in the ocean basins, which means the water goes onto the continents. That is the tectonic driver of the Cretaceous highstand on track (a) of the figure — **the sea-level curve and the spreading-rate arithmetic are not two facts, they are one fact measured twice.**

**Example 2 (why you would care — separating temperature from ice in one number).** A benthic foraminiferal $\delta^{18}\text{O}$ record across the Eocene–Oligocene transition steps from $+1.2$ to $+2.5$ per mil in about $300\ \text{kyr}$. Calibration: $0.25$ per mil per degree Celsius; the full modern cryosphere is worth $0.55$ per mil of seawater $\delta^{18}\text{O}$ and $65\ \text{m}$ of sea level. Take pre-transition deep water at $7\ \text{°C}$ and Antarctica as ice-free. (a) Interpret the step as pure temperature. (b) As pure ice. (c) Mg/Ca ratios measured on the *same shells* give a cooling of $2.5\ \text{°C}$ — redo the partition. (d) What does this say about the proxy?

$$\Delta\delta^{18}\text{O} = 2.5 - 1.2 = +1.3\ \text{per mil}.$$

**(a) All temperature:** $$\Delta T = -\frac{1.3}{0.25} = -5.2\ \text{°C} \;\Longrightarrow\; 7 - 5.2 = 1.8\ \text{°C}.$$

Physically possible — seawater freezes near $-1.9\ \text{°C}$ — but it sits close to the floor, and it requires Antarctica to have stayed ice-free while the deep ocean approached freezing, which no other evidence supports.

**(b) All ice:** $$\frac{1.3}{0.55} = 2.36 \times \text{the modern cryosphere} = 2.36 \times 65 = 153\ \text{m of sea level}.$$

**Reject.** That is more than twice today's global ice on a planet whose Northern Hemisphere had none. The end members bracket the answer and neither is it.

**(c) Partition using the independent thermometer.** Mg/Ca uptake in foraminiferal calcite depends on temperature and **not** on the isotopic composition of the water, so it breaks the degeneracy:

$$\text{temperature term} = 0.25 \times 2.5 = 0.625\ \text{per mil}$$

$$\text{ice term} = 1.3 - 0.625 = 0.675\ \text{per mil}$$

$$\text{ice volume} = \frac{0.675}{0.55} = 1.23 \times \text{modern} \;\Longrightarrow\; 1.23 \times 65 = \mathbf{80\ \text{m of sea-level equivalent}}.$$

**About 80 m — more ice than modern Antarctica holds (58 m).** That is a surprising number, and it is a real and current argument: published Eocene–Oligocene sea-level estimates from margin sequence stratigraphy span roughly 50–105 m, and the possibility of an ice "overshoot" larger than today's sheet is exactly what the spread is about. **Our answer lands inside the published range, and its uncertainty is dominated by the Mg/Ca cooling estimate, which different groups put anywhere between 0 and 2.5 °C.**

Cross-check with something not isotopic at all: the **CCD deepened by about 1 km** at this transition, and the first Southern Ocean **ice-rafted debris** appears. Both say a large ice sheet, independently of any calibration.

**(d) The general lesson, and it is the same one as [5.2](05-02-earth-history-hadean-proterozoic.md)'s banded-iron argument.** A proxy that responds to two variables is **one equation in two unknowns**, and no amount of precision on that one measurement fixes it — you need a second proxy with a *different* sensitivity. The famous Cenozoic $\delta^{18}\text{O}$ curve is often shown as "the Cenozoic temperature record"; it is not. **Before about 34 Ma, with little ice, it is close to a thermometer; after 34 Ma it is increasingly an ice gauge**, and reading the whole curve as temperature exaggerates the cooling substantially.

## Watch out

- **You might think Pangaea was the first supercontinent.** It is the most recent and by far the best recorded, which is not the same thing. Kenorland, Nuna and Rodinia preceded it ([5.2](05-02-earth-history-hadean-proterozoic.md)); the Wilson cycle is Proterozoic in origin and Pangaea is one turn of it.
- **You might read the Cretaceous sea-level high as more water.** The water budget barely changed. **The basin got shallower**, because fast spreading kept a large fraction of the seafloor young, hot and thermally expanded. Sea level is as much a tectonic variable as a climatic one.
- **You might treat the benthic $\delta^{18}\text{O}$ curve as a temperature record.** It is temperature plus ice volume, and after 34 Ma the ice term dominates the trend. One equation, two unknowns.
- **You might think the iridium anomaly proved the impact.** It made the case; **the shocked quartz closed it.** Iridium is also enriched in mantle-derived magmas, so a volcanic source was a serious competing explanation until multiple intersecting planar deformation features — needing shock pressures an order of magnitude above anything volcanic — ruled it out.
- **You might think impact versus Deccan is an open question about whether the impact happened.** It is not. The impact is established. What is argued is the **partition of the kill mechanism** between two drivers that push the same environmental levers at nearly the same time.
- **You might expect eruption volume to predict extinction severity.** The Siberian Traps and Chicxulub were both amplified by their **target rock** — coal and evaporites in one case, anhydrite and carbonate in the other. Comparable events in different geology did far less.
- **You might read the Anthropocene rejection as a judgement about human impact.** It is a judgement about rank, duration and the existence of a correlatable stratigraphic body. The signal is not disputed; the boxing of it is.
- **You might read a coincidence in age as a demonstration of cause.** Four of the Big Five sit near a large igneous province, which is a strong pattern — but the Central Atlantic and Deccan provinces bracket their boundaries rather than sitting on them, and precise dating has repeatedly moved these relationships. The argument always has to run through a mechanism.

## One-liner

> One engine runs the Phanerozoic — continents assemble and disperse, which sets how young the seafloor is, which sets how deep the ocean basin is, which sets how much of the continents is flooded — and the interruptions are not random either: four of the Big Five sit on a flood basalt and one on an impact crater, with the severity in both famous cases set by what the magma or the impactor happened to hit.

<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1 (a)** Reading the units as an environment sequence ([1.5](01-05-sedimentary-rocks.md), [4.4](04-04-stratigraphy-facies-correlation.md)):

| Unit | Environment | Depth rank |
|---|---|---|
| 1 coal on seat-earth | coastal peat swamp, at or above sea level | shallowest |
| 2 black shale, phosphate nodules, no benthos | **deepest**: anoxic bottom water, and a *condensed* interval — phosphate nodules mean almost no clastic input | deepest |
| 3 open-marine limestone | shallow marine, normal salinity, carbonate factory | intermediate |
| 4 grey silty shale with plant debris | nearshore, prograding, marine influence waning | shoaling |
| 5 palaeosol with desiccation cracks | subaerial exposure; the sharp top is a **sequence boundary** | emergent |

**The cycle is a transgression followed by a regression**: swamp drowned (1 → 2), maximum flooding at the black shale, then the shoreline builds back out (3 → 4 → 5) and the surface is exposed and soil-forming before the next flooding.

**Watch the counterintuitive one:** the black shale is thin and unimpressive and is the *deepest-water* unit in the cycle. Thinness records slow deposition, not shallow water — starving a basin of sediment is what a rapid transgression does.

**(b)** $$\frac{306 - 299}{60} = \frac{7\ \text{Myr}}{60} = 0.117\ \text{Myr} = \mathbf{117\ \text{kyr per cycle}}.$$

**That is the 100 kyr eccentricity band** ([3.4](03-04-glaciers-ice-ages.md)). Mean sedimentation rate $400/7 = 57\ \text{m/Myr}$, so a cycle is $400/60 = 6.7\ \text{m}$ thick — comfortably resolvable, which is why cyclothems are a standard astrochronologic target.

**(c) The chain has three links, and the middle one is the surprising part.**

1. **Orbital forcing changes Gondwanan ice volume** on a roughly 100 kyr beat.
2. **The ocean is one connected reservoir**, so storing or releasing that ice changes sea level *globally and essentially instantaneously* on geologic timescales. This is **glacio-eustasy**, and it is what makes a local ice sheet a global stratigraphic signal.
3. **The North American craton is nearly flat.** On a shelf sloping at $10^{-4}$, a 60 m eustatic change moves the shoreline $60/10^{-4} = 600\ \text{km}$. The recording device is not the sea-level change; it is the *gradient*.

**The Kansas section is a tide gauge for an ice sheet on another continent** — and the same logic is why [3.5](03-05-deserts-wind-coasts.md)'s marine terraces and drowned valleys record sea level rather than local uplift.

**(d)** **Cyclothems should be essentially absent before ~340 Ma and after ~260 Ma**, since without a large continental ice sheet there is no mechanism to swing sea level by tens of metres on orbital periods. That is what is observed: cyclothems appear as the Late Palaeozoic Ice Age begins and fade through the Permian as Gondwanan ice disappears.

**What would falsify it:** finding well-developed, orbitally paced, tens-of-metres cyclothems in an interval with independent evidence for an ice-free world. (Metre-scale carbonate cycles *do* occur in greenhouse intervals, driven by aquifer-eustasy or autocyclic shoreline migration — so the falsification requires matching the *amplitude*, not just the periodicity.)

**P2 (a)** Clay mass per unit area:

$$m_{\text{clay}}/A = 0.02\ \text{m} \times 2000\ \text{kg/m}^3 = 40\ \text{kg/m}^2 .$$

Excess iridium concentration is $9.1 - 0.3 = 8.8$ parts per billion $= 8.8\times10^{-9}$:

$$m_{\text{Ir}}/A = 40 \times 8.8\times10^{-9} = 3.52\times10^{-7}\ \text{kg/m}^2 .$$

Chondritic material carries $5\times10^{-7}$ kg Ir per kg, so

$$m_{\text{met}}/A = \frac{3.52\times10^{-7}}{5\times10^{-7}} = 0.704\ \text{kg/m}^2, \qquad M = 0.704 \times 5.1\times10^{14} = 3.59\times10^{14}\ \text{kg}.$$

$$V = \frac{3.59\times10^{14}}{3000} = 1.20\times10^{11}\ \text{m}^3, \qquad d = \left(\frac{6V}{\pi}\right)^{1/3} = \left(2.29\times10^{11}\right)^{1/3} = \mathbf{6.1\ \text{km}}.$$

**(b)** If only a fraction $f = 0.2$ was globally dispersed, the true mass is five times larger, and

$$d' = d \times 5^{1/3} = 6.1 \times 1.71 = \mathbf{10.4\ \text{km}}.$$

**The point is the cube root.** A factor of 5 in the most uncertain quantity in the calculation moves the diameter by a factor of 1.7. **You could be wrong about the dispersed fraction, the clay density and the boundary thickness by an order of magnitude in total and still be certain the object was several kilometres across and not several hundred metres** — which is the size range that matters, because that is the difference between a regional disaster and a global one. **A quantity buried under a cube root is a quantity you are allowed to estimate badly.**

**(c)** Iridium is strongly **siderophile**, so during core formation ([5.2](05-02-earth-history-hadean-proterozoic.md)) it followed the iron to the centre. Crustal rocks are depleted in it by about three orders of magnitude relative to chondrites, which have kept their solar-system-average complement. **A large chondritic Ir excess is therefore very hard to make on Earth's surface**, and the signal-to-background ratio here is about 30.

**The terrestrial source that could still be argued is volcanism.** Mantle-derived magmas are less depleted in Ir than crust, and Kīlauea's volcanic aerosols carry measurable iridium — so a "gigantic eruption" explanation was proposed seriously, and given that the Deccan Traps really were erupting, it was not unreasonable.

**(d) Shocked quartz with multiple intersecting sets of planar deformation features.** PDFs form only above roughly $10\ \text{GPa}$, and two or more intersecting sets require more still. **Explosive volcanic eruptions do not exceed about $1\ \text{GPa}$** — the gas pressures involved are simply an order of magnitude too low. The only natural process that reaches those pressures at the Earth's surface is hypervelocity impact.

**Note the structure of the argument, because it recurs.** The iridium is a *quantitative anomaly*, which invites a "big enough eruption" counter-argument. The shocked quartz is a *threshold* observation: it is not a matter of degree, and no amount of volcanism crosses it. **When you can, reach for the evidence that has a hard physical threshold rather than the evidence that has a large number.**

**P3 (a)** $$n = \frac{4\times10^{3}\ \text{kg}}{0.239\ \text{kg/mol}} = 1.67\times10^{4}\ \text{mol} \;\Longrightarrow\; N_0 = 1.67\times10^{4} \times 6.022\times10^{23} = 1.0\times10^{28}\ \text{atoms}.$$

Half-lives elapsed in $10\ \text{Myr}$ ([intro-nuclear-engineering 1.3](../../intro-nuclear-engineering/lessons/01-03-radioactivity-decay-law.md)):

$$\frac{10^{7}}{2.411\times10^{4}} = 414.8, \qquad \frac{N}{N_0} = 2^{-414.8} = 10^{-414.8 \times 0.30103} = 10^{-124.9}.$$

$$N = 1.0\times10^{28} \times 10^{-124.9} = 10^{-96.9} \approx \mathbf{0\ \text{atoms}}.$$

**Not "very few" — zero, by 97 orders of magnitude.** The last plutonium atom from the tests is gone long before 10 Myr.

**And this is the sharp irony of the proposal.** The markers were ranked partly on how *synchronous and sharp* they are, and the bomb spike is unbeatable on that criterion — it is a globally simultaneous horizon good to about a year. **But sharpness and durability are anti-correlated here: the signal is sharp precisely because it is a short-lived radionuclide.** The markers that will still be legible in 10 Myr — fly ash, plastics, the carbon isotope excursion, the biotic turnover — are exactly the diachronous, gradual ones. A golden spike must be *findable by a future worker*, and this one is not.

**(b)** $$10\ \text{cm/kyr} = 0.01\ \text{cm/yr} \;\Longrightarrow\; 72\ \text{yr} \times 0.01 = \mathbf{0.7\ \text{cm}}.$$

Against a bioturbated mixed layer of 5–10 cm. **The entire proposed epoch sits inside the depth that burrowing organisms stir, so over most of the world's seafloor it is not a bed at all — it is a smear.** A GSSP needs a section you can point at and correlate to; this one exists only in a handful of exceptional, unbioturbated settings such as varved anoxic lakes, ice cores and annually banded corals. That is a very thin basis for a global unit.

**(c) Two separate claims, and the vote was about the second.**

1. *"Human activity has left a large, globally distributed, permanently preservable signal in the sedimentary record."* — **Not disputed by anyone**, including those who voted no.
2. *"That signal should be formalized as an Epoch and Series with a base at 1952."* — **This is a question about rank, duration, the existence of a correlatable body, and where the base goes**, and it is the one the Subcommission answered.

**Conflating them is the standard error in the public coverage.** The stratigraphic community was not asked to rate the Anthropocene's importance; it was asked whether a particular formal object was well posed. Compare [4.3](04-03-geologic-timescale.md): the discipline's central design choice is to keep definitions boring and durable so that measurements can improve underneath them. Ratifying a 72-year epoch whose defining nuclide will be gone, whose deposits are mostly unlithified, and whose base excludes most of the process it names, cuts against every one of those instincts.

**(d) What "event" gains:** events may be **diachronous** (they need no synchronous base), may last any length of time, need no GSSP, and are already standard usage — the Great Oxidation Event ([5.2](05-02-earth-history-hadean-proterozoic.md)) has no rank and works fine. It also lets the Anthropocene include the agricultural and industrial transformations that a 1952 base would have assigned to the Holocene, which is arguably a *better* description of the phenomenon.

**What it gives up:** precision and enforceability. A formal unit fixes a level in rock that everyone must use identically; an event is a label whose boundaries each author may draw for themselves, so quantitative comparisons between studies become harder. It also gives up the rhetorical weight of formal ratification — which is not a scientific consideration, but it is honestly part of why the proposal was made, and worth naming as such.

**From Lesson 4.3 (The Geologic Timescale):** A 46 m continuous marine section is sampled for palaeomagnetic polarity. From the base upward: **normal** 0–7.5 m, **reversed** 7.5–19.5 m, **normal** 19.5–23.5 m, **reversed** 23.5–40.0 m, **normal** 40.0–46.0 m. The base of the section lies immediately above a reversal and the top immediately below one. The candidate part of the global polarity timescale is, from oldest to youngest:

| Chron | Polarity | Age range (Ma) | Duration (kyr) |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | normal | 5.235–4.997 | 238 |
| B | reversed | 4.997–4.631 | 366 |
| C | normal | 4.631–4.493 | 138 |
| D | reversed | 4.493–3.983 | 510 |
| E | normal | 3.983–3.800 | 183 |

(a) Match the section to the timescale, and say what feature of the pattern makes the match unique. (b) Compute the sedimentation rate implied for each chron, and say what a scatter in those numbers would mean. (c) An ash bed occurs at 31.0 m. Interpolate its age. (d) Ar–Ar dating of that ash gives $4.19 \pm 0.06\ \text{Ma}$. Are the two results consistent? (e) Give one advantage and one weakness of correlating by the polarity barcode rather than by a fossil first-appearance datum.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**(a)** The section reads N–R–N–R–N, matching chrons A–E in order, so it spans **5.235 to 3.800 Ma**.

**The unique feature is the thin normal interval in the middle.** Relative thicknesses are $7.5 : 12.0 : 4.0 : 16.5 : 6.0$ — the third interval is only a quarter the thickness of the fourth, and the timescale has exactly one short normal chron (C, 138 kyr) sandwiched between two long reversed ones. **A barcode is matched on the pattern of relative widths, not on the polarities alone**; N–R–N–R–N by itself would fit dozens of places in the Cenozoic.

**(b)**

| Chron | Thickness (m) | Duration (kyr) | Rate (m/Myr) |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | 7.5 | 238 | 31.5 |
| B | 12.0 | 366 | 32.8 |
| C | 4.0 | 138 | 29.0 |
| D | 16.5 | 510 | 32.4 |
| E | 6.0 | 183 | 32.8 |

Overall: $46.0\ \text{m} / 1.435\ \text{Myr} = 32.1\ \text{m/Myr}$, with the five values spanning 29–33, a scatter of about 6 percent.

**That tight scatter is itself the strongest evidence the match is right.** A wrong match would misassign durations and produce wildly inconsistent rates. And this doubles as a test of the section: a genuine outlier would flag either a **hiatus** (too little rock for the time, [4.1](04-01-relative-dating-unconformities.md)) or a real change in sedimentation rate — and either way you would not want to interpolate an age across it.

**(c)** 31.0 m lies inside chron D (23.5–40.0 m, 4.493–3.983 Ma):

$$\text{fraction from the base of D} = \frac{31.0 - 23.5}{40.0 - 23.5} = \frac{7.5}{16.5} = 0.4545$$

$$\Delta t = 0.4545 \times 510\ \text{kyr} = 232\ \text{kyr} \;\Longrightarrow\; \text{age} = 4.493 - 0.232 = \mathbf{4.261\ \text{Ma}}.$$

**(d)** $$4.261 - 4.19 = 0.071\ \text{Myr} = 71\ \text{kyr}, \qquad \frac{71}{60} = 1.2\sigma .$$

**Consistent.** And the analytical uncertainty is not the only term: the interpolation assumed a constant rate *within* chron D, and — exactly as in [4.3](04-03-geologic-timescale.md)'s Example 2 — a modest departure from that moves the answer by tens of thousands of years. Combining a rough $\pm 40\ \text{kyr}$ model term with the analytical one:

$$\sigma \approx \sqrt{60^2 + 40^2} = 72\ \text{kyr} \;\Longrightarrow\; \text{the discrepancy is under } 1\sigma .$$

**The right conclusion is that the two methods agree, and the ash is now the section's absolute tie-point** — which is what magnetostratigraphy needs, since the barcode gives pattern without an anchor.

**(e) Advantage: a reversal is globally synchronous and environment-independent.** The geomagnetic field flips for the whole planet within a few thousand years, and it does not care about latitude, water depth, facies or whether anything was living there. A FAD ([4.3](04-03-geologic-timescale.md)) is subject to Signor–Lipps truncation *and* to genuine diachroneity as a species spreads — so a reversal can correlate two sections in different oceans better than a fossil can.

**Weakness: the pattern is not unique on its own.** Polarity carries one bit per chron, so a short section could match many places on the timescale, and a hiatus that removes one chron can shift the whole match. **Magnetostratigraphy needs an independent anchor — an ash, a biostratigraphic assignment, an isotope excursion — and then it becomes the highest-resolution correlation tool available.** That is precisely why [4.3](04-03-geologic-timescale.md) lists "amenable to multiple correlation methods" as a GSSP ratification criterion: no single method is sufficient, and the combination is far stronger than the sum.
