## Why this matters

This is the lesson where the whole course gets used at once, in the two places geology actually touches people: **what we take out of the ground, and what the ground does to us.**

Both halves have the same structure, and it is worth seeing before the details. **A resource is a place where a geologic process concentrated something far above its ordinary abundance.** Copper is in every rock you have ever picked up — at about 28 parts per million, which is worth nothing. A porphyry deposit holds 5,000 parts per million, which is worth tens of billions of dollars. **Nothing about the copper changed. A process moved it.** Every ore-forming process in this lesson is a process you already learned for a different reason: fractional crystallization, hydrothermal fluids, fluvial sorting, extreme weathering, evaporite precipitation, an ocean's oxidation state.

The hazards half is the same trick run backwards. You already know the mechanisms — elastic rebound, pyroclastic currents, pore pressure on a failure plane, discharge distributions, dewatered clay. What you do not yet have is the framework that turns a mechanism into a decision, and it is one line: **risk is hazard times exposure times vulnerability.** Two of those three are things people choose.

## The idea

**Ore is a concentration anomaly.** Define the **concentration factor** as the ratio of minable grade to average crustal abundance:

$$\mathrm{CF} = \frac{\text{ore grade}}{\text{crustal abundance}}$$

*In words: how many times over a geologic process had to enrich ordinary rock before mining it made sense.* That single number explains most of the metal price list. Aluminium needs a factor of about 3 and iron about 14 — the ordinary crust is nearly ore already, and both metals are structural commodities sold by the tonne. Copper needs about 180, gold about 670, and those are not commodities you build bridges out of.

**The corollary is the one worth internalizing: an ore deposit is not where an element is. It is where a process put too much of it.** Exploration is therefore not a search for elements; it is a search for *the fingerprint of a process* — an alteration halo, a favourable structure, a particular unconformity, an ancient shoreline. That is why exploration geologists spend their time doing what you have spent this course learning: mapping, reading structures, dating events.

**Hazards invert the same logic.** A magnitude 7 earthquake in an empty desert is a geologic event, not a disaster. The same earthquake under a city of unreinforced masonry kills a hundred thousand people. **The hazard was identical; everything that differed was human.** So:

$$\text{Risk} = \text{Hazard} \times \text{Exposure} \times \text{Vulnerability}$$

*In words: expected loss is how often the ground does something, times how much is in the way, times how badly that something is damaged when it happens.* The practical consequence is sharp: **the hazard term is almost always fixed** — it is a property of the fault, the volcano, the slope, the coast — **while exposure and vulnerability are entirely policy**. Mitigation lives in the last two factors, and the exceptions where you *can* move the hazard term are worth naming, because they are exactly the anthropogenic ones.

**Two of these deserve a second look.**

**Hydrothermal deposits are where structural geology pays for itself.** A hydrothermal system needs a heat engine (a pluton), a fluid, a metal source, and — decisively — **permeability**, which in crystalline rock means fractures. So ore shoots sit at fault jogs, fold hinges, breccia pipes and lithological contacts, and finding them is a [2.5](02-05-folds-faults-structures.md) and [4.5](04-05-reading-a-geologic-map.md) problem. **The metal was never the hard part. The plumbing was.**

**Banded iron formation is a resource we cannot make more of.** It required a deep ocean carrying dissolved $\text{Fe}^{2+}$, which requires an anoxic ocean, which ended around 1.85 billion years ago ([5.2](05-02-earth-history-hadean-proterozoic.md)). **Essentially all of the world's iron ore is a one-off consequence of the oxygenation of the atmosphere.** No process operating on Earth today makes any.

**And "rare" earths are a process problem, not an abundance problem.** Cerium sits at 63 ppm in the upper crust — more than twice as abundant as copper. The trouble is that trivalent rare-earth ions substitute comfortably into the crystal sites of ordinary rock-forming minerals ([1.1](01-01-what-a-mineral-is.md), ionic substitution; [inorganic-chemistry 1.2](../../inorganic-chemistry/lessons/01-02-ionic-solids-lattice-energy.md) for why radius and charge govern that), so they stay diluted and camouflaged instead of forming minerals of their own. **Scarcity in the market reflects the scarcity of concentrating processes, not the scarcity of atoms.**

### The petroleum system: five conditions, all required

Fossil fuels are the same story told with carbon, and the framework is a checklist — **fail any one item and there is nothing there.**

1. **Source rock** — organic-rich mud (say 2–10 weight percent organic carbon) deposited where the water was stagnant enough that the carbon escaped oxidation.
2. **Maturation** — burial into the **oil window**, roughly $60$–$150\ ^\circ\text{C}$, where heat cracks kerogen into liquid hydrocarbons. Hotter, $150$–$220\ ^\circ\text{C}$, gives gas; hotter still and you have graphite and a dry hole. With a geothermal gradient of $27\ ^\circ\text{C}/\text{km}$ and a surface temperature of $15\ ^\circ\text{C}$, the oil window is a *depth* window from about $1.7$ to $5.0\ \text{km}$.
3. **Migration** — oil and gas are less dense than pore water, so they rise through connected pore space exactly as groundwater moves in [3.6](03-06-groundwater-aquifers-karst.md), driven by buoyancy instead of a head gradient.
4. **Reservoir** — a porous, permeable rock to hold it. Same property pair as an aquifer, same origin in sorting and cementation ([1.5](01-05-sedimentary-rocks.md)).
5. **Trap and seal** — a geometry that stops the upward migration, capped by a rock whose pore throats are too fine for the buoyancy to push through. **Anticlinal** traps (the crest of a fold, [2.5](02-05-folds-faults-structures.md)), **fault** traps (a seal juxtaposed against the reservoir), and **stratigraphic** traps (a pinch-out, a reef, an unconformity truncation — [4.4](04-04-stratigraphy-facies-correlation.md)).

**Timing is the sixth, unnumbered condition**: the trap has to exist *before* the source rock matures. A perfect anticline folded after the oil migrated past is a perfectly empty anticline — a relative-dating problem ([4.1](04-01-relative-dating-unconformities.md)) with a nine-figure price tag.

**Coal is simpler and different**: it is not a fluid that migrated, it is a rock made of plants in place, requiring a swamp with a water table high enough to keep the peat anoxic and a subsiding basin to bury it. That combination was spectacularly common in the Carboniferous ([5.3](05-03-earth-history-phanerozoic.md)). Unconventional resources — shale gas, tight oil, oil sands — are the cases where the source rock is produced directly, or the reservoir is too impermeable to flow, so the permeability is created by hydraulic fracturing rather than found.

### Reserves versus resources

**A resource is what is there. A reserve is what you can extract at a profit at today's price with today's technology.** The distinction is not pedantry; it is the reason "years of supply" numbers behave so strangely.

$$\text{reserve life (R/P)} = \frac{\text{reserves}}{\text{annual production}}$$

For copper the world reserve-to-production ratio has hovered around 30 to 40 years for a century, **without ever counting down**, because reserves are an inventory that price, technology and exploration keep restocking. Falling ore grades have been offset by bigger trucks and better flotation for a hundred years.

**But the binding constraint is energetic, and that one does not restock.** The rock you must move per tonne of metal goes as the reciprocal of grade:

$$\text{tonnes of ore per tonne of metal} = \frac{1}{g \cdot r}$$

where $g$ is the ore grade as a fraction and $r$ the metallurgical recovery. Halve the grade and you double the rock crushed, the energy, and the waste. **That is the real ceiling, and it is a physics ceiling, not a geology one.** The energy transition sharpens it: lithium, cobalt, nickel, copper and the rare earths are all needed in quantities that current deposits were not discovered to supply, and the new deposits will on average be lower grade and deeper. That is a statement about arithmetic, not a position on policy.

### Hazards, organized by the lesson each comes from

| Hazard | Lesson | What sets the hazard | Where the leverage is |
|---|---|---|---|
| Earthquake shaking | [2.7](02-07-earthquakes-seismic-hazard.md) | fault slip rate, distance, depth, **site soils** | vulnerability: building codes, retrofit |
| Pyroclastic currents, lahars, ash | [2.8](02-08-volcanoes-volcanic-hazards.md) | magma composition, valley geometry | exposure: zoning and evacuation, and monitoring works |
| Landslides | [3.2](03-02-mass-wasting-slope-stability.md) | slope angle against friction angle, **pore pressure** | **hazard** — drainage genuinely lowers it |
| Floods | [3.3](03-03-rivers-landscape-evolution.md) | discharge distribution, floodplain geometry | exposure: zoning; hazard: storage, permeable surfaces |
| Subsidence and sinkholes | [3.6](03-06-groundwater-aquifers-karst.md) | pumping against recharge, karst beneath | **hazard** — pumping limits lower it |
| Coastal erosion, sea-level rise | [3.5](03-05-deserts-wind-coasts.md) | sediment budget, relative sea level | exposure: managed retreat |

**Notice the two bold entries in the last column.** Landslides and subsidence are the hazards you can actually reduce, and it is not a coincidence — **they are the two that are partly anthropogenic to begin with.** You cannot slow a fault; you can absolutely stop over-pumping an aquifer and you can absolutely drain a slope.

**Rare events, formally.** For a hazard of intensity exceeded on average once every $T$ years (the **return period**), the annual exceedance rate is $\lambda = 1/T$, and treating exceedances as a Poisson process ([prob-stat-refresher 2.2](../../prob-stat-refresher/lessons/02-02-discrete-distributions.md)):

$$P(\text{at least one exceedance in } n \text{ years}) = 1 - e^{-n/T}$$

*In words: a return period is an average waiting time, not a schedule — the events are memoryless, and having had one last year tells you nothing.* The engineering standard for ordinary buildings, "10 percent probability of exceedance in 50 years", is exactly $T = 475$ years, since $1 - e^{-50/475} = 0.100$.

**And the geologic map is the siting instrument.** Active fault traces, liquefaction-susceptible young sediment, landslide scars, karst, floodplain deposits — every one of these is a *map unit*, and the skill of [4.5](04-05-reading-a-geologic-map.md) is what turns a hazard map into a "do not build here". **The most cost-effective mitigation ever performed is a line drawn on a map before anything was built.**

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (mechanical — concentration factors, and what they cost).** Using upper-continental-crust abundances, compute concentration factors for copper and gold, then work out how much rock each requires.

**(a) Copper.** Crustal abundance $28\ \text{ppm}$; a typical open-pit porphyry is minable at $0.50$ weight percent, which is $5{,}000\ \text{ppm}$:

$$\mathrm{CF}_{\text{Cu}} = \frac{5{,}000}{28} = 179 \approx \mathbf{180}$$

**(b) Gold.** Crustal abundance $1.5\ \text{ppb} = 0.0015\ \text{ppm}$; an open-pit deposit is minable at $1.0\ \text{g/t}$, which is $1\ \text{ppm}$:

$$\mathrm{CF}_{\text{Au}} = \frac{1.0}{0.0015} = 667 \approx \mathbf{670}$$

**(c) Now the physical meaning.** With a metallurgical recovery $r = 0.88$ for copper, the ore you must process per tonne of copper is

$$\frac{1}{g\,r} = \frac{1}{0.0050 \times 0.88} = 227\ \text{tonnes of ore}$$

and at a typical strip ratio of 2 tonnes of waste per tonne of ore, **about 680 tonnes of rock are moved for every tonne of copper.** For gold at $1\ \text{g/t}$ and $r = 0.90$:

$$\frac{1}{10^{-6} \times 0.90} = 1.1\times10^{6}\ \text{tonnes of ore per tonne of gold}$$

$$= \mathbf{1.11\ \text{tonnes of rock per gram}}.$$

**A five-gram wedding ring is 5.6 tonnes of processed rock.** That number, not the concentration factor, is what an open pit looks like from the air.

**(d) The grade sensitivity.** Halve the copper grade to $0.25$ percent and the ore per tonne of metal doubles to 455 tonnes. **Crushing and grinding dominate mine energy use, and both scale with tonnes processed, so halving the grade roughly doubles the energy per tonne of metal.** Reserves respond to price; energy per tonne responds only to grade, and grade only ever falls.

**Example 2 (why you'd care — the risk decomposition, and what a retrofit actually buys).** A town has 20,000 unreinforced masonry buildings with a total replacement value of 4.0 billion dollars. Probabilistic seismic hazard analysis gives shaking of Modified Mercalli intensity VIII a return period of 475 years. Fragility curves put the mean damage ratio for unretrofitted masonry at that intensity at $V = 0.35$.

**(a) Expected annual loss.**

$$\lambda = \frac{1}{475} = 2.105\times10^{-3}\ \text{per year}$$

$$\text{EAL} = \lambda \times E \times V = (2.105\times10^{-3})(4.0\times10^{9})(0.35) = \mathbf{2.95\ \text{million dollars per year}}$$

**(b) But nobody ever loses 2.95 million dollars.** The loss is zero in 474 years out of 475 and

$$E \times V = (4.0\times10^{9})(0.35) = 1.4\ \text{billion dollars}$$

in the one. **Expected annual loss is a budgeting device, not a forecast**, and the gap between the average and the realization is the entire political problem with hazard mitigation.

**(c) Retrofit.** Bracing and bolting cuts the damage ratio to $V' = 0.10$ at a cost of 3,000 dollars per building.

$$\text{EAL}' = (2.105\times10^{-3})(4.0\times10^{9})(0.10) = 0.842\ \text{million dollars per year}$$

$$\text{annual saving} = 2.947 - 0.842 = \mathbf{2.11\ \text{million dollars per year}}$$

$$\text{cost} = 20{,}000 \times 3{,}000 = 60\ \text{million dollars}, \qquad \text{simple payback} = \frac{60}{2.11} = \mathbf{28.5\ \text{years}}$$

At a 3 percent discount rate the saving in perpetuity is worth $2.11/0.03 = 70.2$ million dollars against a 60 million dollar cost — **net present value about plus 10 million, which is positive but not comfortably so.** That is an honest result, and it is why retrofit programmes are argued about rather than obviously adopted.

**(d) The point the decomposition makes, which nothing else does.** $\lambda$ did not move. **The fault does not know the buildings were retrofitted; the hazard is exactly what it was.** Every dollar of benefit came out of $V$. Had the town instead relocated a quarter of its building stock off the soft lake-bed sediment that amplifies the shaking, the benefit would have come out of $E$ — and that is the *only* other place it can come from.

**(e) And the honest caveat.** Over a 50-year horizon,

$$P(\text{at least one MMI VIII}) = 1 - e^{-50/475} = 1 - 0.900 = \mathbf{0.100}$$

**Nine times in ten, the town spends 60 million dollars and nothing happens.** A mitigation programme that works is indistinguishable, for a human lifetime, from one that was never needed.

## Watch out

- **You might think an ore deposit is a place where the metal occurs.** The metal occurs everywhere. A deposit is a place where a process concentrated it by a factor of a hundred to ten thousand, and **exploration hunts the process, not the metal** — alteration halos, favourable structures, ancient shorelines.
- **You might think rare earths are rare.** Cerium is more than twice as abundant in the crust as copper. Rare-earth ions substitute happily into ordinary minerals ([1.1](01-01-what-a-mineral-is.md)), so they resist being concentrated. **The scarcity is a scarcity of concentrating processes.**
- **You might read a 40-year reserve-to-production ratio as a countdown.** Copper's has sat near 40 years for a century. Reserves are an economic inventory restocked by price, technology and exploration. **The constraint that does bite is energy per tonne of metal, which scales as $1/g$ and only worsens.**
- **You might think oil sits in underground pools.** It occupies pore space in a reservoir rock, exactly as groundwater does in [3.6](03-06-groundwater-aquifers-karst.md), and a "trap" is a *geometry* that arrests buoyant migration, not a container.
- **You might think mitigation reduces the hazard.** Almost never. Hazard is a property of the fault, the volcano, the coast. **Codes and zoning act on vulnerability and exposure** — and the exceptions, slope drainage and pumping limits, are exactly the hazards people helped create.
- **You might think a 475-year event is due if it has been 500 years.** Exceedances are memoryless. The return period is a mean waiting time, and the last one carries no information about the next.

## One-liner

> An ore deposit is not where an element is but where a process put a hundred to ten thousand times too much of it — and a disaster is not where a hazard is but where the hazard found exposure and vulnerability waiting for it.

**P1 (a) Platinum.** $5.0\ \text{g/t} = 5.0\ \text{ppm}$, and $0.5\ \text{ppb} = 0.0005\ \text{ppm}$:

$$\mathrm{CF}_{\text{Pt}} = \frac{5.0}{0.0005} = \mathbf{10{,}000}$$

**Chromium.** The chromite ore's chromium content is

$$0.45 \times 0.684 = 0.3078 \ \text{by mass} = 307{,}800\ \text{ppm}$$

$$\mathrm{CF}_{\text{Cr}} = \frac{307{,}800}{92} = \mathbf{3{,}350}$$

**(b) Magmatic segregation by crystal settling** — [1.3](01-03-how-the-earth-melts.md). As the mafic magma cooled, chromite crystallized early and, being dense, accumulated on the chamber floor in layers; the platinum-group elements partitioned into an immiscible sulfide liquid that settled with them. This is fractional crystallization used as a sorting machine, and it is a phase-diagram result ([materials-science 3.1](../../materials-science/lessons/03-01-phase-diagrams-lever-rule.md)) with gravity added.

**(c) No contradiction, because the two numerators are set by different things.** The chromite layer is nearly *monomineralic* — chromite is a real mineral that crystallizes as its own phase, so the layer's grade is capped by the chromium content of chromite itself, around 30 weight percent, and no process can do better. Platinum forms no rock-forming mineral at all; it is scavenged in trace amounts by sulfide droplets, so its grade is a few parts per million no matter how efficient the scavenging.

**The concentration factor is set by the ratio of the two ends, and the ends are set by chemistry, not by how hard the process worked.** Chromium starts abundant and ends capped; platinum starts at half a part per billion and ends at five parts per million. Hence 3,350 against 10,000 in the same magma chamber. **A high concentration factor means the element was rare to start with, not that the deposit is better.**

**P2 (a)** $$\text{contained Cu} = 950\times10^{6} \times 0.0042 = 3.99\times10^{6}\ \text{t} = \mathbf{3.99\ \text{Mt}}$$

$$\text{recoverable} = 3.99 \times 0.87 = \mathbf{3.47\ \text{Mt}}$$

**(b)** $$\text{throughput} = 110{,}000 \times 350 = 38.5\times10^{6}\ \text{t/yr}$$

$$\text{mine life} = \frac{950}{38.5} = \mathbf{24.7\ \text{years}}$$

$$\text{annual Cu} = 38.5\times10^{6} \times 0.0042 \times 0.87 = \mathbf{141{,}000\ \text{t/yr}}$$

(Check: $3.47\ \text{Mt} / 24.7\ \text{yr} = 0.140\ \text{Mt/yr}$ ✓.)

**(c)** $$\frac{141{,}000}{22\times10^{6}} = 0.0064 = \mathbf{0.64\ \text{percent of world supply}}$$

$$\text{R/P} = \frac{890}{22} = \mathbf{40\ \text{years}}$$

**Note what (c) says about scale.** A genuinely world-class deposit — a billion tonnes of ore, a quarter-century of operation, tens of billions of dollars — supplies **two-thirds of one percent** of world copper. There is no single deposit anywhere that moves the global number. Supply is the sum of a few hundred of these.

**(d) Two reasons the inference fails, and the constraint that does not.**

1. **Reserves are not a physical stock.** A reserve is the subset of a resource that is economic at today's price with today's technology, after a feasibility study. Raise the price or cut the cost and material that was not a reserve yesterday becomes one today without a single new atom being discovered. Copper's R/P ratio has sat between 30 and 40 years for roughly a century **while cumulative production over that century exceeded every reserve estimate made during it.**

2. **Reserves are also an exploration inventory, and companies stop paying to find more once they hold a few decades' worth.** A 40-year R/P is partly a statement about the discount rate, not about the crust. Nobody funds drilling to prove up ore they would mine in 2140.

**The real constraint is energy and grade.** Ore per tonne of metal goes as $1/(g\,r)$, so as average mined grade declines the rock crushed, the energy consumed, the water used and the waste generated per tonne of copper all rise in proportion. **The world does not run out of copper; it runs out of cheap copper**, and "cheap" here is a statement about joules. Add to that the non-geological constraints that actually bind in practice — permitting, water rights, tailings storage, and the 10-to-20-year lag between discovery and first production.

**P3 (a) (i) is a hydrothermal porphyry system** — [1.6](01-06-metamorphic-rocks-rock-cycle.md) for the fluids, [2.5](02-05-folds-faults-structures.md) for the fracture plumbing, [2.2](02-02-plate-boundaries.md) for the setting (porphyries sit above subduction zones). Magmatic fluid exsolving from the cooling stock circulated through the stockwork and precipitated chalcopyrite as it cooled and reacted with the wall rock.

**Practically, the alteration halo is the exploration target.** It is bleached, pyritic and clay-altered over 400 m, whereas the ore shoot itself may be tens of metres across. **The halo is a hundred times bigger than the orebody and it is visible from the air and in satellite spectral data** — so you map alteration to find ore, which is the general rule in exploration: hunt the process footprint, not the payload.

**(ii) is a placer** — [3.3](03-03-rivers-landscape-evolution.md). Gold has a specific gravity near 19 against about 2.7 for quartz, so in a flow competent to transport quartz sand the gold cannot be lifted. It lags, works downward through the gravel framework, and accumulates where the flow decelerates: the inside of bends (point bars) and the base of the gravel against bedrock. **The concentration factor is delivered by the same sorting that made the sand well sorted in the first place.** And the placer is a downstream sample of (i) — placer gold is prospecting information about a bedrock source upstream.

**(b)** The source rock lies at $1{,}800 + 900 = 2{,}700\ \text{m} = 2.7\ \text{km}$:

$$T = 12 + 30 \times 2.7 = 12 + 81 = \mathbf{93\ ^\circ\text{C}}$$

**Squarely inside the oil window** ($60$–$150\ ^\circ\text{C}$), so it is generating liquid hydrocarbons now. The trap is **anticlinal** — a structural trap at the fold crest ([2.5](02-05-folds-faults-structures.md)) — with the **interbedded shale as the seal**, its pore throats too fine for the buoyancy of the oil column to overcome. The sandstone is the reservoir.

**One thing you cannot conclude:** that the anticline holds anything. **The fold must predate maturation.** If the shortening that made this anticline post-dates 93 °C burial, the oil migrated through a flat-lying sequence and left long ago. That is a relative-dating question ([4.1](04-01-relative-dating-unconformities.md)) and it is the question that decides whether the prospect is drilled.

**(c) Aggravated by (iv): sinkhole collapse**, and the mechanism is the one worked in [3.6](03-06-groundwater-aquifers-karst.md) — dewatering a karst cavity removes the buoyant support of the roof rock, adds downward seepage forces, and pipes fine sediment into the void. A 40 m drawdown is a large one. Secondary: well interference and dry shallow wells.

**Created by (v): a landslide.** Unconsolidated silt has a friction angle near 30 degrees, so a 28-degree slope has a factor of safety barely above 1 when dry and drops below 1 when pore pressure rises in a wet season ([3.2](03-02-mass-wasting-slope-stability.md)) — and the town is directly below it. Note that this hazard *is* reducible: drainage attacks the pore-pressure term directly.

**Neither created nor reducible: earthquake shaking.** The folded Palaeozoic sequence implies past convergence, and the valley may follow a structural weakness. Whatever the regional seismic hazard is, no local action changes $\lambda$ — only the town's exposure and its buildings' vulnerability can be changed.

**(d)** "An inferred resource of 640 Mt at 0.38 percent Cu" commits to a **geological estimate**: enough drilling and sampling to state, with the lowest of the three standard confidence levels, that a body of roughly that tonnage and grade is present.

**It does not commit to any of the following**, and each is a separate gate:

- that the copper can be extracted at a profit at any price — there is no mine plan, no pit shell, no cutoff-grade optimization;
- that metallurgical recovery is acceptable — sulfide mineralogy and impurities such as arsenic can render good grade unpayable;
- that the deposit can be permitted, watered, or its tailings stored;
- that "inferred" material will survive infill drilling — a substantial fraction routinely does not.

**Only a *reserve*, declared after a feasibility study at a stated metal price, commits to extractability.** The distinction is regulated precisely because the two sentences sound identical to a non-specialist and differ by everything.

</details>

## Flashback

**From Lesson 3.6 (Groundwater, Aquifers & Karst):** An unconfined alluvial aquifer underlies an irrigation district of $8{,}000\ \text{km}^{2}$. Saturated thickness is $120\ \text{m}$ and the specific yield (the drainable fraction of the pore space) is $S_y = 0.12$. Natural recharge averages $25\ \text{mm}$ per year over the district. Wells currently withdraw $1.4\ \text{km}^{3}$ per year.

(a) Volume of drainable water in storage. (b) Annual recharge volume and the annual deficit. (c) Average rate of water-table decline, and how long until the drainable storage is gone. (d) The district's water agency describes the aquifer as "a renewable resource under temporary stress". Evaluate that, quantitatively.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**(a)** $$A = 8{,}000\ \text{km}^{2} = 8.0\times10^{9}\ \text{m}^{2}$$

$$V_{\text{storage}} = A \, b \, S_y = (8.0\times10^{9})(120)(0.12) = 1.152\times10^{11}\ \text{m}^{3} = \mathbf{115\ \text{km}^{3}}$$

**(b)** $$V_{\text{recharge}} = (0.025)(8.0\times10^{9}) = 2.0\times10^{8}\ \text{m}^{3}\text{/yr} = \mathbf{0.20\ \text{km}^{3}\text{/yr}}$$

$$\text{deficit} = 1.4 - 0.20 = \mathbf{1.2\ \text{km}^{3}\text{/yr}}$$

**Pumping is seven times recharge.** Six-sevenths of every litre delivered is coming out of storage, not out of the water cycle.

**(c)** Each metre of water-table decline releases $A \, S_y = (8.0\times10^{9})(0.12) = 9.6\times10^{8}\ \text{m}^{3}$, so

$$\frac{dh}{dt} = \frac{1.2\times10^{9}}{9.6\times10^{8}} = \mathbf{1.25\ \text{m per year}}$$

$$t = \frac{115}{1.2} = \mathbf{96\ \text{years}} \quad\text{(check: } 120\ \text{m} / 1.25\ \text{m/yr} = 96\ \text{yr} \ \checkmark)$$

**(d) The description is wrong in the way that matters, and the arithmetic says so three separate times.**

1. **The refill time is not a human timescale.** Even if every pump were switched off tomorrow, refilling the drained storage at the natural recharge rate takes

$$\frac{115\ \text{km}^{3}}{0.20\ \text{km}^{3}\text{/yr}} = 575\ \text{years}.$$

A resource whose recovery time is six centuries is renewable only in the sense that granite is.

2. **"Temporary" would require the deficit to be temporary.** At the current balance the aquifer is not stressed, it is being **liquidated on a 96-year schedule**, and the schedule shortens as the last metres of saturated thickness become uneconomic to lift — wells go dry long before the aquifer does.

3. **Part of the loss is not recoverable at any timescale**, which is the point [3.6](03-06-groundwater-aquifers-karst.md) insisted on. The water can be replaced given centuries; the **pore space cannot**. Interbedded clays dewatered by the falling head compact irreversibly, so land subsides and the aquifer's storage capacity is permanently destroyed. **The reservoir gets smaller as you empty it**, and no future wet century restores it.

**The honest phrasing is the one the resources half of this lesson uses: this is mining, not harvesting.** The distinction is exactly the reserve-versus-flow distinction applied to water — and unlike a copper reserve, this one cannot be restocked by a higher price.

</details>

## Closing the course

Twenty-nine lessons, and they were all one idea. **Every rock is a record of the conditions that made it.** A mineral's structure records the temperature and pressure it grew at ([1.1](01-01-what-a-mineral-is.md), [1.6](01-06-metamorphic-rocks-rock-cycle.md)); a texture records a cooling rate ([1.4](01-04-igneous-rocks-and-bodies.md)); sorting and rounding record a transport history ([1.5](01-05-sedimentary-rocks.md)); a fold records a shortening direction ([2.5](02-05-folds-faults-structures.md)); a magnetic stripe records a reversal and a spreading rate ([2.1](02-01-evidence-for-drift.md)); a parent–daughter ratio records elapsed time ([4.2](04-02-radiometric-dating.md)); an unconformity records everything that is missing ([4.1](04-01-relative-dating-unconformities.md)). **Geology is the practice of reading those records backwards into a history** — and the reason it works is that the processes writing them are still running today, where they can be watched and calibrated.

**What the field has established, and should be believed:** plate tectonics, comprehensively and quantitatively, down to GPS velocities that match magnetic-stripe rates computed over millions of years. The age of the Earth. The layered interior and the evidence for each boundary. The oxygenation of the atmosphere, from three independent records that break at the same time. The physical mechanisms of every surface process in Module 3. These are not consensus positions; they are over-determined ones.

**What is genuinely open**, and worth knowing as open rather than settled: **when plate tectonics began** — estimates range across two billion years, and the rock record before 3 Ga is too sparse and too metamorphosed to settle it. **What the two continent-sized low-velocity provinces at the base of the mantle are** — piles of ancient subducted slab, primordial material never mixed in, or something else. **Short-term earthquake prediction**, which has failed for fifty years and may be impossible in principle if rupture nucleation is scale-invariant. And **whether the Anthropocene is a chronostratigraphic unit at all**, which is a real argument about what the geologic timescale is *for*.

**Where to go next in your library.** The quantitative Earth is [geophysics](../../geophysics/syllabus.md) — seismic wave physics, gravity and isostasy done properly, heat flow, palaeomagnetism, mantle convection; every place this course said "the numbers live there", they live there. The comparative view is [planetary-science](../../planetary-science/syllabus.md), which asks why Earth alone among the terrestrial planets has plate tectonics, and answers the differentiation and cratering questions Module 5 deferred. The fluid envelopes are [oceanography](../../oceanography/syllabus.md), [atmospheric-science](../../atmospheric-science/syllabus.md) and [climate-science](../../climate-science/syllabus.md) — the last of these takes over the silicate-weathering thermostat from [3.1](03-01-weathering-soils.md) and the Milankovitch machinery from [3.4](03-04-glaciers-ice-ages.md). **All five have syllabi on disk and no lessons yet**, so those are five `/new-course` or `/prep` runs away rather than ready to read. For biology in deep time, [evolution-ecology 2.4](../../evolution-ecology/lessons/02-04-macroevolution-history-of-life.md) is built and waiting, and it is the other half of [5.3](05-03-earth-history-phanerozoic.md).

**One last thing.** The skill this course was really teaching is not a vocabulary. It is the habit of looking at something — an outcrop, a landscape, a map, a core, a satellite image — and asking *what process would have to have run to produce this, and what else would that process have left behind?* That question is portable. It is the same question you were asking in [4.5](04-05-reading-a-geologic-map.md), and it is the same one an exploration geologist asks over an alteration halo and a hazard geologist asks over a 28-degree slope above a town.
