# Algebraic Geometry · Lesson 2.4: Projective varieties & the homogeneous Nullstellensatz

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 2: Projective varieties & morphisms · Builds on: [Lesson 2.3](02-03-projective-space.md) ($\mathbb{P}^n$, homogeneous coordinates, points at infinity), [Lesson 1.5](01-05-nullstellensatz.md) (the affine Nullstellensatz) · Unlocks: [Lesson 2.5](02-05-morphisms-projective-varieties.md) (morphisms of projective varieties)

## Why this matters

In [Lesson 2.3](02-03-projective-space.md) we built $\mathbb{P}^n$ so that closures behave and parallel lines meet. Now we need to *cut out shapes* inside it — the projective analogue of $V(S)\subseteq\mathbb{A}^n$. The catch is immediate and forces the whole theory: a point of $\mathbb{P}^n$ is only defined *up to scaling*, so "$f$ vanishes here" only makes sense for polynomials that don't care about scale. That single constraint funnels us into **homogeneous** polynomials, a **graded** ring, and a **homogeneous Nullstellensatz** with one surprising exception — an ideal that cuts out the empty set. Getting this right is what lets you take an affine curve and honestly complete it to its projective closure, the move behind Bézout's theorem and every degree count in the subject.

## The idea

An affine point is a *tuple*; a projective point is a *ray* — the whole line $\{\lambda a:\lambda\in k^\times\}$ collapsed to one point $[a_0:\dots:a_n]$. To ask whether a polynomial $f$ "vanishes at $[a]$" you have to get the same yes/no answer at $a$ and at $2a$ and at $17a$. A generic $f$ won't: $f(x)=y-x^2$ vanishes at $(1,1)$ but not at $(2,2)$, so "$f=0$" is not a property of the *point* $[1:1]$ at all.

What *is* scale-stable? A **homogeneous** polynomial of degree $d$ — one whose every term has total degree $d$ — because it pulls scaling straight out: $f(\lambda a)=\lambda^d f(a)$. So $f(a)=0$ forces $f(\lambda a)=0$ for every $\lambda$; vanishing becomes a property of the ray, i.e. of the projective point. That's the entire reason projective geometry is built out of homogeneous equations. Everything else in this lesson is bookkeeping to make that precise and to figure out which ideals cut out which projective sets.

## The formal version

**The graded ring.** Write $S=k[x_0,\dots,x_n]$ and split it by degree:
$$S=\bigoplus_{d\ge 0}S_d,\qquad S_d=\{\text{homogeneous polynomials of degree }d\}\cup\{0\},$$
with $S_d\cdot S_e\subseteq S_{d+e}$. An element $f\in S$ decomposes uniquely as $f=\sum_d f_d$ with $f_d\in S_d$ its **homogeneous components**.

*In words:* $S$ is graded — it's chopped into degree-slabs, and multiplying adds degrees.

**Why only homogeneous $f$ define zero sets.** For $f=\sum_d f_d$ and $\lambda\in k^\times$,
$$f(\lambda a)=\sum_d \lambda^d f_d(a).$$
If this is $0$ for *all* $\lambda$, then (viewing it as a polynomial in $\lambda$ with more than $\deg f$ roots, over the infinite field $k=\bar k$) every coefficient vanishes: $f_d(a)=0$ for each $d$. So "demand $f$ vanish at the projective point $[a]$" is *equivalent* to "demand each homogeneous piece $f_d$ vanish" — we lose nothing by working only with homogeneous polynomials.

**Homogeneous ideals.** An ideal $I\subseteq S$ is **homogeneous** if $f\in I\implies$ every component $f_d\in I$; equivalently, $I=\bigoplus_d(I\cap S_d)$; equivalently, $I$ can be generated by homogeneous elements.

*In words:* a homogeneous ideal is one you can describe purely with homogeneous generators — it respects the grading.

**The projective vanishing and ideal maps.** For a set $T$ of *homogeneous* polynomials,
$$V_+(T)=\{[a]\in\mathbb{P}^n:\ f(a)=0\ \text{for all }f\in T\}\subseteq\mathbb{P}^n,$$
which is well-defined by scale-invariance; these are the **projective algebraic sets** (**projective varieties** when irreducible). Going back, for $X\subseteq\mathbb{P}^n$,
$$I(X)=\big(\text{all homogeneous }f\ \text{with}\ f|_X=0\big)\subseteq S,$$
a homogeneous ideal.

*In words:* $V_+$ turns homogeneous equations into a shape in $\mathbb{P}^n$; $I$ turns a shape back into the homogeneous ideal of everything vanishing on it — the projective copy of the [affine dictionary](01-01-affine-dictionary.md).

**The irrelevant ideal.** The homogeneous ideal
$$\mathfrak{m}_0=(x_0,x_1,\dots,x_n)=S_+=\bigoplus_{d\ge 1}S_d$$
is the **irrelevant ideal**. Its affine zero set in $\mathbb{A}^{n+1}$ is just the origin $\{0\}$ — but $\mathbb{P}^n$ *has no origin* (the point $[0:\dots:0]$ is forbidden), so $V_+(\mathfrak{m}_0)=\varnothing$. Every homogeneous polynomial of positive degree vanishing simultaneously has no *projective* solution.

**Homogeneous Nullstellensatz.** Let $I\subseteq S$ be a homogeneous ideal, over $k=\bar k$. Then:

1. **(Emptiness criterion)** $\displaystyle V_+(I)=\varnothing\iff \sqrt{I}\supseteq\mathfrak{m}_0$ (equivalently $\sqrt I=\mathfrak m_0$ or $\sqrt I=S$).
2. **(Strong form)** If $V_+(I)\neq\varnothing$, then $\displaystyle I\big(V_+(I)\big)=\sqrt{I}.$

*In words:* the projective dictionary is exactly the affine one — radical homogeneous ideals match projective varieties — *except* for one glitch: an ideal whose radical swallows all the variables cuts out nothing at all.

**Why it's true (via the affine cone).** To a homogeneous ideal $I$ attach its affine zero set $C=V(I)\subseteq\mathbb{A}^{n+1}$, the **affine cone**: because $I$ is homogeneous, $C$ is a union of lines through the origin, and each such line (other than $\{0\}$) is one point of $V_+(I)$. So
$$V_+(I)=\varnothing\iff C\subseteq\{0\}\iff V(I)\subseteq V(\mathfrak m_0).$$
By the *affine* Nullstellensatz ([Lesson 1.5](01-05-nullstellensatz.md)), $V(I)\subseteq V(\mathfrak m_0)$ iff $\sqrt I\supseteq I(\{0\})=\mathfrak m_0$ — that's part 1. For part 2, when $C$ is bigger than the origin, $I(V_+(I))$ is exactly the homogeneous part of $I(C)=\sqrt I$ (affine Nullstellensatz again), and $\sqrt I$ of a homogeneous ideal is homogeneous. $\blacksquare$

**Projective closure.** Given an affine variety $X\subseteq\mathbb{A}^n$, embed $\mathbb{A}^n\hookrightarrow\mathbb{P}^n$ as the chart $U_0=\{x_0\neq 0\}$ via $(x_1,\dots,x_n)\mapsto[1:x_1:\dots:x_n]$. The **projective closure** $\overline X$ is the Zariski closure of $X$ in $\mathbb{P}^n$. Concretely you get it by **homogenizing the ideal** $I(X)$: for $f\in k[x_1,\dots,x_n]$ of degree $d$, its homogenization is
$$f^{h}(x_0,\dots,x_n)=x_0^{\,d}\,f\!\left(\tfrac{x_1}{x_0},\dots,\tfrac{x_n}{x_0}\right)\in S_d,$$
and $\overline X=V_+\big(\{f^{h}:f\in I(X)\}\big)$.

*In words:* fill in the missing variable $x_0$ to make each equation homogeneous, then read off the projective set. Dehomogenizing (set $x_0=1$) undoes it.

> ⚠ You must homogenize the **whole ideal**, not the generators one at a time — see *Watch out*.

## Picture

![An affine parabola y = x-squared and its projective closure V+(x0 x2 − x1²) in P², with the single added point at infinity [0:0:1] marked on the line at infinity where both arms of the parabola meet.](assets/02-04-fig1.svg)

The parabola $y=x^2$ lives in the affine chart $x_0\neq 0$. Both arms shoot upward, more and more vertical — in $\mathbb{P}^2$ they converge to the *single* point at infinity $[0:0:1]$, the vertical direction. The closure adds exactly that one point.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (homogenize the parabola, find its point at infinity).** Take $Y=V(y-x^2)\subseteq\mathbb{A}^2$, coordinates $(x,y)$ on the chart $x_0\neq 0$ with $x=x_1/x_0,\ y=x_2/x_0$. Here $f=y-x^2$ has degree $d=2$, so
$$f^{h}=x_0^{2}\Big(\tfrac{x_2}{x_0}-\tfrac{x_1^2}{x_0^2}\Big)=x_0x_2-x_1^2,\qquad \overline Y=V_+(x_0x_2-x_1^2)\subseteq\mathbb{P}^2.$$
Dehomogenize (set $x_0=1$, $x_1=x$, $x_2=y$): $x_2-x_1^2\rightsquigarrow y-x^2$ — the original curve, good. **Points at infinity** are where $x_0=0$: then $x_0x_2-x_1^2=-x_1^2=0$, so $x_1=0$, giving the single point $[0:0:1]$. So $\overline Y=Y\cup\{[0:0:1]\}$: the parabola plus *one* point at infinity, matching the picture. (Compare a hyperbola $xy=1$, whose closure $V_+(x_1x_2-x_0^2)$ meets $x_0=0$ at $x_1x_2=0$ — two points at infinity, the two asymptote directions.)

**Example 2 (the irrelevant ideal cuts out nothing).** Claim: $V_+(x_0,x_1,x_2)=\varnothing$ in $\mathbb{P}^2$. *Directly:* a point $[a_0:a_1:a_2]$ lying on it would satisfy $a_0=a_1=a_2=0$ — but $[0:0:0]$ is not a point of $\mathbb{P}^2$ by definition. So the solution set is empty. *Via the theorem:* $I=\mathfrak m_0$ is already radical ($\sqrt{\mathfrak m_0}=\mathfrak m_0$), so $\sqrt I=\mathfrak m_0\supseteq\mathfrak m_0$, and part 1 delivers $V_+(I)=\varnothing$. This is why $\mathfrak m_0$ earns the name "irrelevant": algebraically it looks like the biggest proper homogeneous ideal, but geometrically it carves out nothing — and it's precisely the exception the projective Nullstellensatz must carve back out.

## Watch out

- **You might think** you can homogenize an ideal by homogenizing its generators — **but** that can give a set strictly *larger* than the closure. Classic case: the twisted cubic $I=(y-x^2,\ z-x^3)$. Homogenizing the two generators gives $x_0x_2-x_1^2$ and $x_0^2x_3-x_1^3$, whose common zeros include the spurious line $\{x_0=x_1=0\}$ at infinity. The true closure also needs $x_1x_3-x_2^2$, which comes from homogenizing $xz-y^2\in I$ (a *combination* of the generators). In general you must homogenize a **Gröbner basis** of $I$ (with respect to a suitable term order) to be safe; for a single hypersurface it's never an issue.
- **You might think** $\mathfrak m_0=(x_0,\dots,x_n)$ cuts out a point (the "origin"), as it does in $\mathbb{A}^{n+1}$ — **but** $\mathbb{P}^n$ deletes the origin, so $V_+(\mathfrak m_0)=\varnothing$. The whole exception in the Nullstellensatz exists to handle this one missing point.
- **You might think** $I(V_+(I))=\sqrt I$ holds for every homogeneous $I$ — **but** it breaks exactly when $V_+(I)=\varnothing$. There $I(\varnothing)=S$ (everything vanishes vacuously), while $\sqrt I$ could be $\mathfrak m_0\neq S$. So $I=(x_0^2,x_1^2)\subseteq k[x_0,x_1]$ has $\sqrt I=(x_0,x_1)=\mathfrak m_0$, yet $I(V_+(I))=I(\varnothing)=S$. Always check emptiness (part 1) *before* invoking part 2.

## One-liner

> In $\mathbb{P}^n$ only homogeneous equations make sense; radical homogeneous ideals match projective varieties one-for-one — with the lone exception of the irrelevant ideal $\mathfrak m_0$, which cuts out nothing.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢)** Let $C=V(y-x^3)\subseteq\mathbb{A}^2$. (a) Compute its projective closure $\overline C\subseteq\mathbb{P}^2$ by homogenizing. (b) Find every point of $\overline C$ at infinity.

**P2 (🟡)** Work over $k=\mathbb{C}=\bar k$. Show that the closure of *any* circle $x^2+y^2+ax+by+c=0$ in $\mathbb{P}^2$ passes through the two points $[0:1:i]$ and $[0:1:-i]$ — the same two "circular points at infinity," independent of $a,b,c$. (This is why any two circles meet in the "extra" points needed for Bézout to come out even.)

**P3 (🔴, optional)** In $S=k[x_0,x_1]$ (so $\mathbb{P}^1$): (a) Let $I=(x_0^2,\ x_0x_1,\ x_1^2)$. Compute $\sqrt I$ and use the homogeneous Nullstellensatz to prove $V_+(I)=\varnothing$; confirm directly. (b) Show the two *distinct* homogeneous ideals $(x_1)$ and $(x_1^2)$ have the same projective variety $\{[1:0]\}$, and reconcile this with part 2 of the theorem by computing $I(V_+(-))$ for each.

<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** (a) $f=y-x^3$ has degree $d=3$. Homogenize: $f^{h}=x_0^{3}\big(\tfrac{x_2}{x_0}-\tfrac{x_1^3}{x_0^3}\big)=x_0^{2}x_2-x_1^{3}$. Since $I(C)=(f)$ is generated by one polynomial (a hypersurface), homogenizing the single generator is safe, so $\overline C=V_+(x_0^2x_2-x_1^3)\subseteq\mathbb{P}^2$.

(b) Points at infinity have $x_0=0$: then $x_0^2x_2-x_1^3=-x_1^3=0\Rightarrow x_1=0$, forcing the point $[0:0:1]$. So $\overline C$ meets the line at infinity in the single point $[0:0:1]$ (with multiplicity $3$, foreshadowing intersection theory). $\overline C=C\cup\{[0:0:1]\}$.

**P2** The circle has degree $2$, so its homogenization is
$$F=x_0^2\Big(\tfrac{x_1^2}{x_0^2}+\tfrac{x_2^2}{x_0^2}+a\tfrac{x_1}{x_0}+b\tfrac{x_2}{x_0}+c\Big)=x_1^2+x_2^2+ax_0x_1+bx_0x_2+cx_0^2.$$
At infinity, $x_0=0$, and $F$ collapses to $x_1^2+x_2^2=0$ — the linear terms in $x_0$ and the constant term all drop out, so the result is completely independent of $a,b,c$. Over $\mathbb{C}$, $x_1^2+x_2^2=(x_1-ix_2)(x_1+ix_2)=0$ gives $x_1=\pm i x_2$, i.e. the points $[0:1:i]$ and $[0:1:-i]$ (taking $x_2=1$). Every circle's closure passes through exactly these two fixed imaginary points at infinity. $\blacksquare$

**P3** (a) $I$ contains $x_0^2$ and $x_1^2$, so $x_0,x_1\in\sqrt I$; hence $\mathfrak m_0=(x_0,x_1)\subseteq\sqrt I$. Since $\sqrt I\subseteq S$ and $\mathfrak m_0$ is maximal among proper homogeneous ideals (and $1\notin I$, so $\sqrt I\neq S$), in fact $\sqrt I=\mathfrak m_0$. As $\sqrt I=\mathfrak m_0\supseteq\mathfrak m_0$, part 1 of the theorem gives $V_+(I)=\varnothing$. *Directly:* a point $[a_0:a_1]$ on $V_+(I)$ needs $a_0^2=a_0a_1=a_1^2=0$, forcing $a_0=a_1=0$ — not a point of $\mathbb{P}^1$. So $V_+(I)=\varnothing$. ✓ (Note $I\subsetneq\mathfrak m_0$ are distinct ideals with the same empty variety — the radical is what the dictionary sees.)

(b) $V_+((x_1))=\{[a_0:a_1]:a_1=0\}=\{[1:0]\}$, a single point. Likewise $V_+((x_1^2))=\{a_1^2=0\}=\{a_1=0\}=\{[1:0]\}$ — same set. Both varieties are nonempty, so part 2 applies and predicts $I(V_+(\cdot))=\sqrt{\cdot}$. Compute: $\sqrt{(x_1)}=(x_1)$ (already prime, hence radical), and $\sqrt{(x_1^2)}=(x_1)$ as well. So $I(V_+((x_1)))=I(\{[1:0]\})=(x_1)$ and $I(V_+((x_1^2)))=(x_1)$ too — both recover the *same radical* $(x_1)$, exactly as the theorem demands. The two ideals differ only by a non-radical factor, invisible to $V_+$. $\blacksquare$

## Flashback

**From [Lesson 2.3](02-03-projective-space.md) (points at infinity / parallel lines meet):** In $\mathbb{P}^2$ with affine chart $x_0\neq 0$ ($x=x_1/x_0,\ y=x_2/x_0$), consider the two parallel affine lines $\ell_1:\ y=2x+1$ and $\ell_2:\ y=2x-3$. Take the projective closure of each and find the point at infinity where they meet.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

Homogenize each degree-$1$ equation ($f^h=x_0\,f(x_1/x_0,x_2/x_0)$):
$$\ell_1:\ y-2x-1\ \rightsquigarrow\ x_2-2x_1-x_0=0,\qquad \ell_2:\ y-2x+3\ \rightsquigarrow\ x_2-2x_1+3x_0=0.$$
Points at infinity: set $x_0=0$. Both equations become $x_2-2x_1=0$, i.e. $x_2=2x_1$, giving the single point $[0:1:2]$. The two closures meet there — a *shared* point at infinity because both lines have slope $2$, i.e. the same direction. Parallel affine lines meet at exactly one point at infinity, determined by their common slope; the closures $\overline{\ell_1}$ and $\overline{\ell_2}$ are distinct projective lines through $[0:1:2]$. $\blacksquare$
