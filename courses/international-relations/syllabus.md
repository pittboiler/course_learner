# International Relations — Syllabus

> Politics & Society · Tier 1 · ~23 lessons · Prereqs: [`political-institutions`](../political-institutions/syllabus.md) · Roadmap id: `international-relations`

## Goal

This course is about politics where no one is in charge: how states behave when there is no government above them, and why they sometimes fight, sometimes cooperate, and sometimes bind themselves with law and organizations. It runs history and theory together. You get the modern state system from Westphalia to the present, at the level needed to argue theory: the Concert of Europe, the two world wars, the Cold War, and unipolarity and after. You also get the theories that argue over that history: classical and structural realism, liberal institutionalism and the democratic peace, constructivism, and, more briefly, Marxist and dependency views. You will finish able to say what a theory predicts about a historical case and which evidence would embarrass it, trace a crisis as a security dilemma or as a deterrence failure, say what the UN Charter permits and forbids in a use of force and where the law runs out, explain why states build international organizations and give them unequal votes, and name the single premise two schools actually divide on. The course is informal and verdict-neutral: realists, liberals, constructivists and their critics are each presented at the strength their best defenders would recognize, and no rubric rewards siding with any of them. It deliberately skips the formal models of war and cooperation (the bargaining model, commitment problems, signalling, alliances and sanctions as games, owned by `conflict-and-bargaining`), the ethics of war, the history of sovereign debt, how states are built from the inside, normative theories of global justice, and foreign-policy analysis of particular governments.

## How practice works in this course

Problems follow [HUMANITIES-BUILD-BRIEF.md](../../HUMANITIES-BUILD-BRIEF.md): every part declares its kind, and every solution is a rubric (must-hit moves, wrong turns) plus a model answer. Five archetypes carry the course:

- **Find the crux (archetype 5), exegetical.** The workhorse. IR's schools read the same history differently, and most lessons end with one historical case and two readings of it: was 1914 a spiral or a failure of deterrence? Did the Cold War end because Soviet power declined or because Soviet ideas changed? Does treaty compliance show that law binds, or only that states sign what they would do anyway? Name the premise one side must deny and the evidence that would move it.
- **Apply to a hard case (archetype 3), exegetical then evaluative.** Run a theory's prediction or a legal rule (Charter Article 2(4), Article 51, a Security Council authorization) on a case, and say where it stops determining an answer.
- **Diagnose (archetype 4), exegetical.** Invented speeches, op-eds and cables about a rising power, an alliance, a sanction or a tariff: name the theory or historical analogy doing the work, and say where it breaks. Invented quotations are never attributed to real people.
- **Close reading (archetype 7), exegetical.** Public-domain texts only: Thucydides' Melian Dialogue (Crawley, 1874), Hobbes's *Leviathan* ch. 13, Grotius (Whewell's translation, 1853), Kant's *Perpetual Peace* (Campbell Smith, 1903), Hobson's *Imperialism* (1902), Angell's *The Great Illusion* (1910), Wilson's Fourteen Points (1918), Daniel Webster's 1841–42 correspondence on the *Caroline*, and US government documents (Kennan's Long Telegram, 1946; NSC-68, 1950). Modern scholars (Carr, Morgenthau, Waltz, Keohane, Wendt, Mearsheimer) and modern translations are paraphrased and cited, never quoted at length. The UN Charter and later treaties are cited by article and paraphrased.
- **Formal model (archetype 8), formal, light.** Arithmetic only, where a claim is quantitative: a base-rate check on the democratic-peace correlation (3.2), counting blocking coalitions under weighted voting in the IMF or qualified majority in the EU Council (5.2, 5.5), and polarity from invented capability shares (2.2). Anything needing a game is cited to `conflict-and-bargaining`.

Evaluative parts (did bipolarity keep the peace? was Kosovo legitimate though unlawful? is the liberal order ending?) are verdict-neutral and graded on the moves: state the rival reading at full strength, use the evidence, and say what would change your mind. This holds for current conflicts too: the course reports positions and never takes a side on a live dispute.

## Dangerous Checklist

When you finish, you can:

- [ ] Sort an explanation of a war or a peace into Waltz's three images (individual, state, system), and say what evidence would separate them
- [ ] State what the Peace of Westphalia did and did not establish, and explain why many historians call "Westphalian sovereignty" a later myth
- [ ] Explain how the Concert of Europe managed great-power conflict after 1815, and compare it with the 1919 settlement and the League of Nations on the same criteria
- [ ] Place a historical period by polarity, and say what structural realism predicts about its stability and where the prediction has been embarrassed
- [ ] Trace a crisis as a security-dilemma spiral and as a deterrence failure, and name what the offense-defense balance predicts about it
- [ ] Explain second-strike capability, mutual assured destruction and the stability-instability paradox, and lay out the Waltz-Sagan dispute over whether nuclear spread stabilizes
- [ ] Explain how institutions make cooperation possible under anarchy on the liberal-institutionalist account, and state the realist objection from relative gains
- [ ] State the democratic-peace finding precisely, check its base rates, and weigh the normative, institutional and rival (capitalist-peace, selection) explanations
- [ ] Say what a constructivist means by "anarchy is what states make of it," and apply the norm life-cycle to a case such as the rise of human-rights norms
- [ ] State the sources of international law, and argue either side of the question whether international law binds, using the compliance debate
- [ ] Apply the UN Charter's rules on force (Article 2(4), Article 51, Chapter VII) to a new case, and say where anticipatory self-defence and humanitarian intervention leave the law
- [ ] Explain why states delegate to international organizations, compute who can block a decision under weighted or qualified-majority voting, and state the hegemonic-stability thesis and its critics

## Modules

### Module 1: The state system, 1648 to now

The history the theories fight over, told so that each era hands the next module a question.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1.1 | Anarchy and the levels of analysis | Say what IR explains and sort an explanation of war by where it locates the cause | anarchy vs hierarchy; the domestic analogy (Hobbes, ch. 13, cited to [`history-of-political-thought`](../history-of-political-thought/syllabus.md) 3.3); Waltz's three images; the system, the state, the leader; **find-the-crux** problems |
| 1.2 | Westphalia: myth and reality | State what the 1648 treaties did and weigh the claim that sovereignty began there | the Thirty Years' War (events cited to [`church-history`](../church-history/syllabus.md) 6.2); territorial and religious clauses; the Empire's survival; Osiander and Krasner on the "Westphalian myth"; sovereignty as a bundle (Krasner's four senses); Grotius and the law of nations |
| 1.3 | The balance of power and the Concert of Europe | Explain how the great powers managed Europe after Napoleon and why the system decayed | the Vienna settlement (1814–15); balance of power as policy vs as outcome; congresses and great-power management; legitimacy (Schroeder vs Kissinger's readings); the Crimean War; Bismarck's alliance system; the English School's "international society" (Bull) |
| 1.4 | Two world wars and the failed peace | Lay out the rival accounts of 1914 and of the interwar collapse | the July Crisis; German aims (Fischer) vs shared drift (Clark); the cult of the offensive; Wilson's Fourteen Points and the League Covenant; collective security vs balance of power; Manchuria, Abyssinia, Munich; Carr's critique of liberal "utopianism"; why war happens though it is costly (the puzzle, named for `conflict-and-bargaining`) |
| 1.5 | The Cold War | Trace containment from its founding documents to détente, and map the historians' schools | the Long Telegram (1946) and NSC-68 (1950), **close reading**; containment, the Truman Doctrine, NATO and the Warsaw Pact; Korea and the Cuban missile crisis; orthodox, revisionist and post-revisionist historiography; détente and Helsinki (1975) |
| 1.6 | The end of the Cold War and after | Weigh the rival explanations of 1989–91 and of the unipolar era, and state the live debate about what follows it | Soviet decline vs Gorbachev's "new thinking" vs Western pressure; the unipolar moment; Fukuyama's end-of-history thesis against Mearsheimer's prediction of returning rivalry; 9/11 and Iraq (2003); China's rise; Russia's 2022 invasion of Ukraine; power-transition arguments |

**Boss problem 1:** (a) Close-read the Long Telegram's section on the background of the Soviet outlook. Does Kennan attribute Soviet conduct chiefly to Marxist-Leninist ideology, or to a traditional Russian sense of insecurity that the ideology justifies? Say which words decide it, and state what policy follows on each reading. *(Exegetical.)* (b) Compare the 1815 and 1919 settlements on the same three criteria: the treatment of the defeated power, the mechanism for great-power management, and the principle of legitimacy each invoked. *(Exegetical.)* (c) Name the crux between historians who date modern sovereignty from 1648 and those who call it a nineteenth-century myth, and say what kind of evidence from the treaties or later practice would move it. *(Exegetical.)* (d) In 300–500 words: why did the Cold War never become a great-power war? Weigh bipolarity, nuclear weapons, and the ideas and domestic politics of the two sides. Any verdict passes if each explanation is stated at full strength and the answer says what evidence from 1.5–1.6 counts against its own view. *(Evaluative.)*

### Module 2: Realism, power and security

The oldest and most-attacked tradition: why anarchy makes states fear each other, and what fear does.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2.1 | Classical realism | Reconstruct the realist case from human nature and the pursuit of power, and read the Melian Dialogue closely | the Melian Dialogue (Thucydides V, Crawley), **close reading**; Carr's power and morality; Morgenthau's interest defined as power and his six principles (paraphrased); prudence vs morality in statecraft; Machiavelli cited to [`history-of-political-thought`](../history-of-political-thought/syllabus.md) 3.1 |
| 2.2 | Structural realism: anarchy, balance and polarity | Derive balancing from the structure of the system, and say what polarity predicts | Waltz's *Theory of International Politics*: ordering principle, like units, distribution of capabilities; self-help; balancing vs bandwagoning; balance of threat (Walt); measuring power (GDP, military spending, the Correlates of War index); bipolar vs multipolar stability (Waltz vs Deutsch and Singer); polarity from invented capability shares (**formal**, light) |
| 2.3 | The security dilemma | Trace how defensive moves produce spirals, and say when the dilemma is mild or severe | Herz and Butterfield; Jervis's spiral and deterrence models; offense-defense balance and distinguishability; the prisoner's dilemma and stag hunt as vocabulary (cited to [`game-theory-refresher`](../game-theory-refresher/syllabus.md) 1.1); defensive realism (Jervis, Glaser) vs offensive realism (Mearsheimer); relative vs absolute gains; **diagnose** problems |
| 2.4 | Nuclear deterrence | Explain why nuclear weapons changed strategy and the dispute over whether their spread stabilizes | Brodie; second-strike capability and Wohlstetter's delicate balance; mutual assured destruction; counterforce vs countervalue; the stability-instability paradox; Schelling's threat that leaves something to chance (credibility cited to [`game-theory-refresher`](../game-theory-refresher/syllabus.md) 2.2); the NPT (1968); the nuclear taboo (Tannenwald); Waltz vs Sagan on proliferation |

**Boss problem 2:** (a) Close-read the Melian Dialogue. Reconstruct in numbered premises the Athenians' argument that justice is not in question between them and Melos, and the Melians' reply that sparing Melos is in Athens's own interest. Mark which Melian premises appeal to justice and which to interest. *(Exegetical.)* (b) An invented intelligence cable describes a rising power building a navy and a status-quo power responding with new alliances. Diagnose the situation twice, as a Jervis spiral and as a deterrence problem, and say which facts in the cable each reading needs to be true. *(Exegetical.)* (c) Name the crux between offensive and defensive realists on whether a great power's security is best served by maximizing relative power, and say what historical evidence (1.3–1.4 or 1.6) would move it. *(Exegetical.)* (d) Steelman the Waltz position that the gradual spread of nuclear weapons stabilizes, then reply as Sagan would, 150 words each; say in one line which argument you think survives and why. *(Evaluative.)*

### Module 3: The challengers: liberalism, constructivism and radical views

Theories that deny realism's claim that anarchy dictates conflict, each for a different reason.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3.1 | Liberal institutionalism | Explain how institutions make cooperation possible without a world government, and state the realist rebuttal | Keohane's *After Hegemony*: transaction costs, information, reputation, iteration; the shadow of the future (cited to [`game-theory-refresher`](../game-theory-refresher/syllabus.md) 2.3); complex interdependence (Keohane and Nye); regimes; Grieco's relative-gains objection; Mearsheimer's "false promise" critique |
| 3.2 | Kant's peace and the democratic peace | State the democratic-peace finding precisely, and weigh its explanations and critics | *Perpetual Peace*'s three definitive articles, **close reading**; Doyle's liberal zone of peace; Moravcsik's liberal theory of preferences; normative vs institutional explanations; the capitalist-peace and selectorate alternatives; critics (Layne, Rosato); base rates and the rarity of democratic dyads before 1945 (**formal**, light); Angell's *Great Illusion* and commercial peace |
| 3.3 | Constructivism | Say how identities and norms constitute state interests, and apply the norm life-cycle to a case | Wendt's "anarchy is what states make of it" (paraphrased); Hobbesian, Lockean and Kantian cultures of anarchy; constitutive vs regulative norms; the logic of appropriateness vs of consequences; Finnemore and Sikkink's norm life-cycle (emergence, cascade, internalization); the end of the Cold War as constructivism's test case (1.6) |
| 3.4 | Marxist, dependency and world-systems views | Reconstruct the radical theories of the system and state what the evidence has done to them | Hobson's under-consumption thesis (**close reading**, public domain); Lenin on imperialism (paraphrased; Marx cited to [`social-theory`](../social-theory/syllabus.md) 2.1); Prebisch-Singer terms-of-trade thesis; Frank's development of underdevelopment; Cardoso and Faletto's dependent development; Wallerstein's core, periphery and semi-periphery; the East Asian growth record as the hard case; critical theory (Cox) in one paragraph |

**Boss problem 3:** (a) Close-read the three definitive articles of Kant's *Perpetual Peace* (Campbell Smith translation). State what each requires, and say whether Kant's argument that republics are cautious about war rests on who bears the costs, on public reason, or on both — citing the words that decide it. *(Exegetical.)* (b) An invented dataset reports the number of state pairs by regime type and the number of wars within each class, for 1816–1945 and 1946–2000. Compute war rates per dyad-year, and show whether "democracies rarely fight each other" is informative once the scarcity of democratic pairs before 1945 is taken into account. *(Formal.)* (c) An invented speech argues that trade with a rising autocracy will keep the peace because interdependence makes war unprofitable and will make the autocracy more like its trading partners. Diagnose which liberal and constructivist claims the speech runs together, and state what a structural realist and a dependency theorist would each object. *(Exegetical.)* (d) In 300–500 words: does the peaceful end of the Cold War count more strongly for constructivism or for structural realism? Any verdict passes if both theories' best accounts of 1989–91 are stated, and the answer says what each would have predicted beforehand. *(Evaluative.)*

### Module 4: International law: force and rights

Law without a sovereign to enforce it: what it says, whether it binds, and how it handles war and atrocity.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4.1 | What international law is and whether it binds | State the sources of international law and argue both sides of the bindingness question | Article 38 of the ICJ Statute: treaties, custom (practice plus *opinio juris*), general principles, subsidiary sources; *pacta sunt servanda*; *jus cogens*; Austin's claim that international law is positive morality, not law (public domain); Hart's reply cited to `philosophy-of-law`; the compliance debate: managerial (Chayes and Chayes) vs selection (Downs, Rocke and Barsoom) vs self-interest (Goldsmith and Posner) |
| 4.2 | The law of force | Apply the Charter's rules on the use of force to a new case and say where they run out | Article 2(4) and its two exceptions: Article 51 self-defence and Chapter VII authorization; the *Caroline* standard of necessity and proportionality (Webster's letters, **close reading**); anticipatory vs preventive self-defence; *Nicaragua v. United States* (1986); Iraq (2003); the Security Council veto as the rule's political limit; just war ethics cited to [`ethics`](../ethics/syllabus.md) and `catholic-social-teaching` |
| 4.3 | Human rights as institutions | Explain how human-rights law is built and what the evidence says about whether it changes state behavior | the Universal Declaration (1948); the two 1966 Covenants; the European Convention and its court; the Helsinki Final Act as a lever; Keck and Sikkink's boomerang; the spiral model; Hathaway's compliance findings against Simmons's domestic-mobilization account; Moyn's thesis that human rights became a mass movement only in the 1970s |
| 4.4 | Humanitarian intervention and the responsibility to protect | Trace how the intervention debate moved from Somalia to Libya, and state what R2P changed legally | Somalia, Rwanda (1994), Srebrenica (1995); Kosovo (1999) and "illegal but legitimate"; the ICISS report (2001) and sovereignty as responsibility; the 2005 World Summit endorsement; Libya and Resolution 1973 (2011), and the backlash over regime change; the ICC (Rome Statute, 1998) |

**Boss problem 4:** (a) Close-read Webster's letter on the *Caroline* incident. State the conditions he sets for lawful self-defence across a border, and say which later doctrines (anticipatory self-defence, proportionality) draw on which words. *(Exegetical.)* (b) An invented case: State A strikes a neighbor's weapons facility, citing an imminent attack; State B separately lands troops to stop a massacre after a Security Council resolution is vetoed. Apply Article 2(4), Article 51 and Chapter VII to each, and say where the law stops giving an answer. *(Exegetical, then evaluative.)* (c) Name the crux between the managerial account of compliance and the selection-effect critique, and say what evidence about treaty design or deep versus shallow commitments would move it. *(Exegetical.)* (d) In 300–500 words: when legality and legitimacy come apart, as many said they did over Kosovo, should states follow the law or the legitimacy judgment? Any verdict passes if the answer states the strongest case for the other side, and says what its own rule would permit in Iraq (2003) and Libya (2011). The ethics of war is cited, not argued. *(Evaluative.)*

## Notes

- **Where this goes next.** `conflict-and-bargaining` takes this course and [`grad-game-theory`](../grad-game-theory/syllabus.md) as prerequisites and formalizes what is informal here: 1.4's puzzle of costly war becomes the bargaining model, 2.3's security dilemma and 2.4's credible threats become commitment and signalling problems, 3.1's cooperation becomes repeated games with monitoring, and 5.4's sanctions and trade coalitions become games. When that syllabus is written, it should cite these lessons for the history and the informal theory and not retell them.
- **Bridges.** 1.2 ↔ [`church-history`](../church-history/syllabus.md) 6.2 (Westphalia as an event in church history). 2.1 and 1.1 ↔ [`history-of-political-thought`](../history-of-political-thought/syllabus.md) 3.1 and 3.3 (Machiavelli; Hobbes's state of nature, the domestic analogy for anarchy). 3.4 ↔ [`social-theory`](../social-theory/syllabus.md) Module 2 (Marx as social theorist). 4.2 and 4.4 ↔ [`ethics`](../ethics/syllabus.md) 3.2 and `catholic-social-teaching` (the moral and doctrinal case about war, which this course reports but does not argue). 5.3 and 5.5 ↔ [`history-of-debt`](../history-of-debt/syllabus.md) 5.3–5.5 (the IMF as crisis lender, Bretton Woods, HIPC); that syllabus cites this course for international organizations and theories of cooperation, and 5.5 here is that handoff.
- **A curriculum gap.** No course in the library teaches comparative advantage and the gains from trade; [`micro-refresher`](../micro-refresher/syllabus.md) and [`grad-micro`](../grad-micro/syllabus.md) do not, and there is no international-economics course on the roadmap. Lesson 5.4 states the intuition in a paragraph so the politics can proceed, and its reference card should list the gap under "Assumed, not taught here" until an owner exists. Normative global justice (cosmopolitanism, obligations across borders, immigration) likewise has no owner; [`political-philosophy`](../political-philosophy/syllabus.md) does not cover it.
- **Lesson count.** The roadmap estimated ~20; this runs 23 (+15%, inside the ±25% band). History gets six lessons because each era carries a crux the theory modules depend on, and merging the Cold War with its end, or the Concert with the world wars, would drop the historiographical dispute that makes the era useful. Human rights and humanitarian intervention need separate lessons because one is treaty law and compliance evidence and the other is the law of force meeting the Security Council.
- **Current events.** 1.6, 4.4 and 5.5 touch unsettled matters (the war in Ukraine, great-power rivalry with China, the WTO's dispute-settlement impasse, the future of the liberal order). Lessons state facts as of a named date, present the competing readings, and take no side.
- **Facts to verify at lesson-writing time; not asserted above.** Current IMF quota and vote shares; the EU Council's qualified-majority thresholds as currently in force; membership counts of the UN, NPT, ICC and WTO; the wording of paragraphs in the 2005 World Summit Outcome on the responsibility to protect; the exact dates of the Webster–Ashburton correspondence cited in 4.2; the dataset figures behind any democratic-peace claim (Boss problem 3 uses invented numbers deliberately).
