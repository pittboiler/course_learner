# Philosophy of Language and Logic — Syllabus

> Philosophy · Tier 2 · ~23 lessons · Prereqs: [`philosophical-method`](../philosophical-method/syllabus.md), [`mathematical-logic`](../mathematical-logic/syllabus.md) · Roadmap id: `philosophy-of-language-and-logic`

## Goal

This course owns the philosophy of language and philosophical logic for the whole library: how words reach the world, what meaning is, what we communicate beyond what we literally say, what truth is, and what the logic of necessity and possibility looks like as a formal system. It runs from Frege's puzzle about identity and Russell's theory of descriptions, through modal logic built properly (Kripke semantics, the systems from K to S5, quantified modal logic and the Barcan formula), to Kripke's case for direct reference and the necessary a posteriori, then to the big theories of meaning (Davidson's truth-conditional program, meaning as use, the rule-following paradox), Grice on implicature, Quine's attack on analyticity, and finally truth itself: Tarski, deflationism, the liar, and the sorites. You will finish able to translate a description at both scopes and say which reading an argument needs, build and check a countermodel in any of the standard modal systems, say exactly what rigid designation does and does not buy, derive an implicature and cancel it, and state what each theory of truth or vagueness says about a paradoxical sentence. The course takes no side between Fregeans and Millians, truth-conditional and use theorists, Quine and the defenders of analyticity, deflationists and correspondence theorists, or rival treatments of the liar and the sorites. It deliberately skips formal linguistic semantics (lambda-calculus type theory, generalized quantifiers, dynamic semantics), the history of analytic philosophy as history, completeness and incompleteness proofs (owned by [`mathematical-logic`](../mathematical-logic/syllabus.md)), non-classical logics beyond the three-valued and paraconsistent tools the paradoxes need, and the philosophy of mental content ([`philosophy-of-mind`](../philosophy-of-mind/syllabus.md)).

## How practice works in this course

Problems follow [HUMANITIES-BUILD-BRIEF.md](../../HUMANITIES-BUILD-BRIEF.md): every part declares its kind, and every solution is a rubric (must-hit moves, wrong turns) plus a model answer. This course has more formal work than any other in the field, and it plays out in four ways:

- **Formal parts are graded as in any math course.** Translating a description at both scopes, building a Kripke countermodel, deriving a theorem in a modal system, computing the stages of a Kripkean fixed point, checking whether a sentence is supertrue. Module 2 is almost entirely formal. **Every countermodel in a solution key is checked world by world** before it is published: the frame condition is verified pair by pair, and the formula's value is computed at every world it depends on. A countermodel that is not reflexive does not refute a T-theorem, and the key must say which frame class the model belongs to.
- **Exegetical parts are strict.** What Russell's theory says "the present King of France is bald" entails, what Strawson says it presupposes, whether a designator is rigid, what the Barcan formula claims, which Gricean maxim an implicature exploits, what Quine's circularity argument actually requires. These have right answers.
- **Evaluative parts are verdict-neutral.** No rubric rewards concluding that Millianism, the causal theory, meaning-as-use, analyticity, deflationism, the gap theory of the liar or epistemicism is correct. Must-hit moves are ones either side must make: state the view precisely, run it on the case, name the premise the objection attacks, and say what the reply costs.
- **Counterexample and find-the-crux dominate the non-formal parts.** The field's arguments are case-driven (Gödel and Schmidt, Madagascar, Pierre, plus and quus, Twin Earth), and the disagreements usually reduce to one premise about what a speaker must know or what a sentence must contribute. Close reading uses public-domain texts only: Aristotle, *Metaphysics* (Ross); Mill, *A System of Logic*; Russell, "On Denoting" (*Mind*, 1905). Frege (read in English translation), Strawson, Quine, Grice, Kripke, Davidson, Kaplan, Putnam, Tarski and Williamson are paraphrased and cited, never quoted at length.

## Dangerous Checklist

When you finish, you can:

- [ ] State Frege's puzzle about informative identities, and say what sense, Russellian descriptions, and Millian direct reference each make of it and of substitution failure in belief reports
- [ ] Translate a sentence containing a definite description into first-order logic at every scope, give each reading's truth value, and say which reading an argument needs
- [ ] Build a Kripke model, compute a modal formula's value at every world, and refute a formula in K, T, S4 or S5 with a countermodel whose frame condition you have checked
- [ ] Match the axioms T, 4, B and 5 to their frame conditions and say which system fits alethic, deontic, epistemic and provability readings of the box
- [ ] Say what the Barcan formula and its converse claim, show where each holds and fails under constant and varying domains, and state what necessitism and contingentism accept
- [ ] Derive the necessity of identity, state Quine's objections to quantifying into modal contexts, and give the scope reply
- [ ] Run Kripke's modal, epistemic and semantic arguments against descriptivism on a fresh case, and say what a causal-historical picture of reference must add to handle a reference shift
- [ ] Explain how rigidity produces the necessary a posteriori and the contingent a priori, and what two-dimensional semantics does with them
- [ ] Write a Davidsonian truth theory for a toy language, and say why extensional adequacy is not enough (Foster's problem)
- [ ] Derive a conversational implicature from the maxims, test it for cancellability, and tell it from a presupposition and an entailment
- [ ] Reconstruct Quine's case against analyticity and name the premise its defenders deny
- [ ] Say what Tarski's theory, deflationism, Kripke's fixed-point theory and dialetheism each make of the liar, and what supervaluationism, degree theories and epistemicism make of the sorites

## Modules

### Module 1: Sense, reference, and descriptions

How a name or a description reaches its object, starting from the two puzzles that founded the subject: why "Hesperus is Phosphorus" is informative when "Hesperus is Hesperus" is not, and what "the present King of France is bald" says when there is no king.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1.1 | Frege's puzzle: sense and reference | State the puzzle about informative identity and Frege's solution | cognitive value, *a = a* vs *a = b*, sense (*Sinn*) as mode of presentation vs reference (*Bedeutung*), compositionality of sense and of reference, the reference of a sentence as its truth value |
| 1.2 | Belief reports and empty names | Explain substitution failure in "believes that" and names with no bearer on Frege's account | substitutivity *salva veritate*, indirect (oblique) contexts, indirect reference as customary sense, sense without reference (Odysseus), the threatened hierarchy of senses |
| 1.3 | Russell's theory of descriptions | Translate a definite description into first-order logic and use scope to dissolve Russell's puzzles | the quantificational analysis (existence, uniqueness, predication), primary vs secondary occurrence (wide and narrow scope), the King of France, George IV and the author of *Waverley*, names as disguised descriptions, logically proper names |
| 1.4 | Strawson, Donnellan, and speaker reference | Tell presupposition from entailment, and a referential use from an attributive one | Strawson's "On Referring", sentence vs use of a sentence, truth-value gaps, Donnellan's referential/attributive distinction, Kripke's reply via speaker's reference vs semantic reference |

**Boss problem 1:** Take Russell's "On Denoting" (*Mind*, 1905; public domain), the passages that set out the three puzzles a theory of denoting must solve. (a) Close-read: state each puzzle (George IV and the author of *Waverley*, the excluded middle and the present King of France, and the non-subsistence of a difference) and say which feature of the theory — the quantificational analysis or the scope distinction — does the work on each. (b) Formal: translate "The present King of France is not bald" into first-order logic with the description at wide scope and at narrow scope, give each reading's truth value given that France has no king, and say which reading preserves the law of excluded middle. (c) Strawson holds that the sentence, as used today, is neither true nor false. Name the crux between him and Russell: the single claim about what the sentence says, as opposed to what its use takes for granted, that one side must deny. (d) An invented case: at a reception, Ana points and says "the woman holding the champagne is the new dean"; the woman she means is holding sparkling water and is the dean, while a woman across the room holding champagne is not. In 300–500 words, assess whether Donnellan's referential use refutes Russell's theory as semantics or is fully handled by the distinction between speaker's and semantic reference; any verdict, if you give the truth value of Ana's sentence on each account and say what each must say about what Ana communicated. Part (a) is exegetical; (b) is formal; (c) is exegetical; (d) is evaluative.

### Module 2: Modal logic

Necessity and possibility as a formal system: worlds, accessibility, the correspondence between axioms and frames, quantifiers across worlds, and the de re modality that Quine thought incoherent and Kripke made respectable.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2.1 | Kripke semantics and the system K | Build a Kripke model, evaluate a modal formula at a world, and refute a formula with a countermodel | frames and models, the accessibility relation, truth at a world for $\Box$ and $\Diamond$, validity on a frame vs in a model, the K axiom $\Box(p \to q) \to (\Box p \to \Box q)$, necessitation, duality $\Diamond = \neg\Box\neg$, countermodel construction |
| 2.2 | Axioms and frames: T, S4, S5 | Match T, 4, B and 5 to their frame conditions and prove the correspondence in one direction | reflexive, transitive, symmetric and Euclidean frames; T ($\Box p \to p$), 4 ($\Box p \to \Box\Box p$), B ($p \to \Box\Diamond p$), 5 ($\Diamond p \to \Box\Diamond p$); the systems K, T, S4, B, S5; S5 as equivalence (or universal) accessibility; soundness and completeness stated, cited to [`mathematical-logic`](../mathematical-logic/syllabus.md) 1.4 |
| 2.3 | Which logic for which modality | Choose a system for a reading of the box and justify the axioms it adds | iterated modalities and the S4 and S5 reduction laws, deontic D ($\Box p \to \Diamond p$) and why T fails for "ought", epistemic readings and positive introspection, provability logic GL named as a bridge to [`mathematical-logic`](../mathematical-logic/syllabus.md) 5.2, whether metaphysical necessity is S5 (Chandler's and Salmon's tolerance arguments against 4) |
| 2.4 | Quantified modal logic and the Barcan formula | State the Barcan formula and its converse, and prove or refute each under constant and varying domains | constant vs varying domains, the Barcan formula $\Diamond\exists x\,Fx \to \exists x\,\Diamond Fx$ and its converse, Ruth Barcan Marcus, BF as a theorem of standard quantified S5 (Prior), varying-domain countermodels, necessitism vs contingentism (Williamson) with necessary existence cited to [`metaphysics`](../metaphysics/syllabus.md) 3.3 |
| 2.5 | De re modality and Quine's challenge | Formalize de re and de dicto claims, derive the necessity of identity, and give the scope reply to Quine | quantifying in, referential opacity, Quine's objections (the number of planets, the mathematical cyclist, "Aristotelian essentialism"), Smullyan's reply using Russell's scope (1.3), the necessity of identity $\forall x \forall y\,(x = y \to \Box\, x = y)$ from Leibniz's law, counterpart theory's translation and why it lets identity be contingent |

**Boss problem 2:** (a) Formal: show that $\Diamond\Box p \to p$ is valid on every symmetric frame, and so a theorem of S5; then give a reflexive, transitive frame and valuation on which it fails, checking both frame conditions pair by pair and computing the formula at each world. (b) Formal: give a reflexive frame on which $\Box p \to \Box\Box p$ fails, and say which frame condition the countermodel lacks. (c) Formal: build a varying-domain S5 model with two worlds on which the Barcan formula $\Diamond\exists x\,Fx \to \exists x\,\Diamond Fx$ is false at a world, state each world's domain and the extension of $F$ at each, and say what the model shows about what "there could have been something that is F" commits one to. (d) Formal: write "Necessarily, the author of *Waverley* wrote *Waverley*" with the description inside and outside the scope of $\Box$, and say which reading is true given that Scott might not have written it. (e) Chandler and Salmon argue that metaphysical possibility is not transitive: an artefact could have come from slightly different material, and that different artefact could have come from slightly different material again, though the original could not have come from the doubly different material. In 300–500 words, assess whether the argument shows that the logic of metaphysical modality lacks axiom 4, or whether a defender of S5 can redescribe the case; any verdict, if you state exactly which inference the tolerance principle blocks and what giving up 4 would cost the S5 reasoning used in [`philosophy-of-religion`](../philosophy-of-religion/syllabus.md) 2.2. Parts (a)–(d) are formal; (e) is evaluative.

### Module 3: Names and necessity

Kripke's case that names are not descriptions, what rigid designation is, how a picture of reference-borrowing replaces a theory of sense, and the two things that fall out of it: necessary truths known by experiment, and a revived Frege puzzle about belief.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3.1 | Against descriptivism | Run Kripke's three arguments against the description theory of names on a fresh case | the classical and cluster theories (Russell, Searle), the modal argument, the epistemic argument, the semantic argument (Gödel and Schmidt; Feynman), descriptions that fix reference vs give meaning |
| 3.2 | Rigid designation and the causal picture | Say which designators are rigid, and what a causal-historical chain must supply | rigid vs non-rigid designation in a two-world model, de jure vs de facto rigidity, initial baptism and reference-borrowing, Evans's Madagascar reference shift, the qua problem, Mill's non-connotative names as the forerunner |
| 3.3 | The necessary a posteriori and natural kinds | Explain how rigidity plus an empirical identity yields a necessary truth, and what two-dimensional semantics does with it | necessity of identity (from 2.5) applied to Hesperus and Phosphorus, natural kind terms and water/H2O (metaphysical side at [`metaphysics`](../metaphysics/syllabus.md) 3.1), the contingent a priori (the standard metre), Putnam's Twin Earth and the division of linguistic labor, two-dimensionalism (Chalmers, Jackson) as a neo-Fregean reply; epistemic categories cited to [`epistemology`](../epistemology/syllabus.md) 4.1 |
| 3.4 | Indexicals and the return of Frege's puzzle | Give Kaplan's account of indexicals and state the Millian's problem with belief reports | Kaplan's character vs content, direct reference for "I", "here", "now", Perry's essential indexical, Kripke's puzzle about belief (Pierre and *Londres*), Millian responses (Salmon, Soames: guises and pragmatics) vs neo-Fregean and hidden-indexical accounts |

**Boss problem 3:** (a) Close-read the passage in Mill's *A System of Logic*, Book I, chapter 2 ("Of Names"; public domain) where he distinguishes connotative from non-connotative names and discusses the town of Dartmouth: what does Mill say a proper name contributes, and which words decide that the name would survive a change in the river? (b) Formal: build a two-world model in which "Franklin" is rigid and "the inventor of bifocals" is not, and show that "Franklin is the inventor of bifocals" is true at the actual world and false at the other; then show, using the necessity of identity from 2.5, that if "Hesperus" and "Phosphorus" are both rigid and co-refer at the actual world, "Hesperus is Phosphorus" is true at every world. (c) Name the crux between Frege and Mill on why "Hesperus is Phosphorus" is informative: the premise about what a competent speaker must grasp in understanding a name that one side must deny. (d) In 300–500 words, assess whether two-dimensional semantics recovers what was right in Frege's account of informativeness without giving up rigidity, or whether Kripke's puzzle about Pierre shows the problem is not one that senses of any kind can solve; any verdict, if you state the modal argument of 3.1 at full strength and say what each view makes of Pierre's two beliefs. Part (a) is exegetical; (b) is formal; (c) is exegetical; (d) is evaluative.

### Module 4: Meaning, use, and communication

What a theory of meaning is supposed to deliver, whether meaning is truth conditions or use, whether there is any fact about what we mean at all, how much of what we communicate is not said, and whether some sentences are true in virtue of meaning alone.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4.1 | Truth-conditional semantics and Davidson | Write a truth theory for a toy language and say what makes it a theory of meaning | compositionality and learnability, T-sentences, Convention T used in reverse, radical interpretation and the principle of charity, Foster's problem (true but non-interpretive theories), the Frege–Geach problem as a compositionality test (cited to [`ethics`](../ethics/syllabus.md) 6.2) |
| 4.2 | Meaning as use | State the later Wittgenstein's challenge to meaning-as-reference and truth conditions | language games, meaning as use in a practice, the builders, the Augustinian picture criticized, family resemblance as a claim about meaning (method at [`philosophical-method`](../philosophical-method/syllabus.md) 3.1), the private language argument in outline, inferentialism (Brandom) as a systematic heir |
| 4.3 | The rule-following paradox | Reconstruct Kripke's Wittgenstein and test the main replies | plus and quus, the skeptical challenge (no fact that fixes what I meant), the dispositional reply and its finitude and error objections, the skeptical solution (assertability conditions and community), meaning primitivism, the normativity of meaning |
| 4.4 | Speaker meaning and implicature | Derive a conversational implicature from the maxims and tell it from entailment and presupposition | Grice's non-natural meaning, what is said vs what is implicated, the cooperative principle and the maxims (quantity, quality, relation, manner), flouting, calculability and cancellability, scalar implicature, conventional implicature, speech acts and illocutionary force (Austin, Searle) in outline |
| 4.5 | Analyticity and "Two Dogmas" | Reconstruct Quine's case against the analytic/synthetic distinction and the best replies | Kant's and Frege's analyticity, Carnap's meaning postulates, Quine's circle of synonymy, analyticity and definition, reductionism and the web of belief, revisability of logic, Grice and Strawson's "In Defense of a Dogma", epistemic vs metaphysical analyticity (Boghossian) |

**Boss problem 4:** (a) Diagnose an invented exchange written for the problem: a hiring manager asks whether a candidate's coding test went well, and a colleague replies with a sentence praising only the candidate's punctuality and formatting. Derive the implicature step by step from the maxims, name the maxim exploited, and add a continuation that cancels it without contradiction. (b) Formal: for a toy language with two names, one predicate, negation and conjunction, write a Davidsonian truth theory (axioms for each expression plus recursive clauses) and derive the T-sentence for one conjunction; then replace one axiom with a Foster-style deviant axiom that yields only true T-sentences but wrong interpretations, and say what extra constraint Davidson added to exclude it. (c) Reconstruct Quine's argument in "Two Dogmas" that analyticity cannot be defined without circularity, in numbered premises (paraphrased, not quoted), and name the premise Grice and Strawson deny. (d) In 300–500 words, assess whether Kripke's Wittgenstein undermines the truth theory you wrote in (b): if there is no fact that I mean plus rather than quus, is there a fact about which T-sentences are correct for my language? Any verdict, if you state the skeptical challenge at full strength and say whether the skeptical solution leaves truth-conditional semantics standing. Part (a) is exegetical; (b) is formal; (c) is exegetical; (d) is evaluative.

### Module 5: Truth, paradox, and vagueness

What truth is, what Tarski's definition does and does not settle, what the deflationist thinks is left over, and two paradoxes that force a theory of truth to take a stand on gaps: the liar and the sorites.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5.1 | Tarski's theory as philosophy | Say what Tarski's definition of truth achieves and what philosophers dispute about it | Aristotle's formula (*Metaphysics* Γ 7), Convention T and material adequacy, object language vs metalanguage, satisfaction as the engine (cited to [`mathematical-logic`](../mathematical-logic/syllabus.md) 2.2), undefinability of truth derived from the diagonal lemma of 5.1 there, Field's physicalist complaint about list-like base clauses |
| 5.2 | Deflationism | State the deflationary theories and the objections they must survive | redundancy (Ramsey), disquotationalism (Quine, Field), minimalism (Horwich), prosententialism in outline, the generalization problem ("everything she said is true"), truth and meaning (can a deflationist be a Davidsonian?), correspondence and pragmatist theories as the rivals, the conservativeness debate named |
| 5.3 | The liar and theories of truth | Classify a paradoxical sentence and give each major theory's treatment | the liar, the strengthened liar, why Epimenides' Cretan is not a liar paradox, Tarski's hierarchy, Strong Kleene three-valued logic, Kripke's fixed-point construction and groundedness (the least fixed point as in [`programming-languages`](../programming-languages/syllabus.md) 2.3), the truth-teller, revenge, dialetheism and the logic LP (Priest) |
| 5.4 | The sorites and degrees of truth | Reconstruct the sorites in its two forms and assess many-valued treatments | the conditional and the universally quantified sorites, borderline cases, tolerance, three-valued and fuzzy (degree) logics, why "one step makes a small difference" is not a solution, Edgington's degrees, the problem of artificial precision |
| 5.5 | Supervaluationism and epistemicism | Compute supertruth on a sorites series and state the epistemicist's sharp-boundary claim | precisifications and supertruth (Fine), penumbral connections, the supertrue existential with no supertrue instance, what happens to excluded middle and bivalence, Williamson's epistemicism and margin-for-error principles, higher-order vagueness |

**Boss problem 5:** (a) Close-read the definition of truth in Aristotle's *Metaphysics* Γ 7 (Ross translation, public domain): state what the formula requires for a sentence to be true, and say whether its words support reading it as a correspondence theory, as a statement of the disquotation schema, or as neutral between them. (b) Formal: take a language containing "snow is white", a sentence $S$ asserting that "snow is white" is true, a liar sentence $L$ equivalent to "$L$ is not true", and a truth-teller $K$ equivalent to "$K$ is true". Compute the stages of Kripke's Strong Kleene construction from the empty extension and anti-extension to the least fixed point, classify each sentence as grounded or ungrounded, and prove that $L$ is in neither the extension nor the anti-extension of any fixed point, while $K$ is in the extension of some fixed point. (c) Formal: for a sorites series of 100 people ordered from tallest to shortest, with admissible precisifications drawing the cutoff for "tall" anywhere between person 40 and person 60, show that "there is an $n$ such that person $n$ is tall and person $n + 1$ is not" is supertrue, that no instance of it is supertrue, and that "person 50 is tall or person 50 is not tall" is supertrue though neither disjunct is. (d) In 300–500 words, assess whether the strengthened liar ("this sentence is not true") refutes the gap theory of (b), and whether a deflationist who accepts every instance of the T-schema is in a better or worse position; any verdict, if you state the revenge argument precisely and say what the dialetheist does with the same sentence. Part (a) is exegetical; (b)–(c) are formal; (d) is evaluative.

## Notes

- **Lesson count.** The roadmap estimated ~20; this runs 23 (+15%, inside the band). The steering added modal logic as a full formal system on top of the roadmap blurb, and it cannot be shared out: Kripke semantics, frame correspondence, choosing a system, quantified modal logic and de re modality are five separate 10-minute lessons, and Modules 3 and 5 depend on them. Frege's two lessons stay two because substitution failure in belief reports is what Module 3 returns to in 3.4.
- **Order.** Modal logic (Module 2) comes before Kripke on names (Module 3) so that rigid designation is defined in a model the student can compute with, and the necessity of identity is derived (2.5) before it is used (3.3). Russell's scope distinction (1.3) is the tool 2.5 uses against Quine.
- **Formal verification.** Every countermodel, fixed-point computation and supervaluation check in a lesson or boss key is verified before publishing: frame conditions pair by pair, formula values at every relevant world, stage-by-stage extensions. The Module 2 and Module 5 boss parts above were checked when this syllabus was written (e.g. the S4 countermodel to $\Diamond\Box p \to p$ needs two worlds with $p$ false at the world that sees a $\Box p$ world; the Barcan countermodel needs a world whose domain lacks the only $F$-thing of an accessible world).
- **What downstream courses can assume.** [`metaphysics`](../metaphysics/syllabus.md) (3.1–3.2, Boss 3(c)) and [`philosophy-of-religion`](../philosophy-of-religion/syllabus.md) (1.3, 2.2) are taught before this course and do their light formal steps without it; they should cite Module 2 for the system and 2.4–2.5 for de re modality and the Barcan formula. [`epistemology`](../epistemology/syllabus.md) 4.1 should cite 3.3 and 4.5.
- **Bridges.** Theological language — whether "good" and "wise" say the same thing of God and creatures, and Aquinas's answer that they are said analogically (ST I q.13) — is the natural next question after 4.1 and 4.5, and it belongs to `thomistic-synthesis`, with Scotus's univocity in [`ancient-medieval-philosophy`](../ancient-medieval-philosophy/syllabus.md) 8.2. Constitutional interpretation by "original public meaning" ([`constitutional-law`](../constitutional-law/syllabus.md) 8.1) is a live application of what is said vs what is implicated (4.4). Kripke's fixed-point theory of truth (5.3) is the same least-fixed-point construction as [`programming-languages`](../programming-languages/syllabus.md) 2.3, and Tarski's undefinability (5.1) is the diagonal argument of [`theory-of-computation`](../theory-of-computation/syllabus.md) 4.1 with "true" in place of "accepts".
