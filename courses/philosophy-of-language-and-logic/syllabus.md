# Philosophy of Language and Logic — Syllabus

> Philosophy · Tier 2 · ~23 lessons · Prereqs: [`philosophical-method`](../philosophical-method/syllabus.md), [`mathematical-logic`](../mathematical-logic/syllabus.md) · Roadmap id: `philosophy-of-language-and-logic`

## Goal

This course owns the philosophy of language and philosophical logic for the whole library: how words reach the world, what meaning is, what we communicate beyond what we literally say, what truth is, and what the logic of necessity and possibility looks like as a formal system. It runs from Frege's puzzle about identity and Russell's theory of descriptions, through modal logic built properly (Kripke semantics, the systems from K to S5, quantified modal logic and the Barcan formula), to Kripke's case for direct reference and the necessary a posteriori, then to the big theories of meaning (Davidson's truth-conditional program, meaning as use, the rule-following paradox), Grice on implicature, Quine's attack on analyticity, and finally truth itself: Tarski, deflationism, the liar, and the sorites. You will finish able to translate a description at both scopes and say which reading an argument needs, build and check a countermodel in any of the standard modal systems, say exactly what rigid designation does and does not buy, derive an implicature and cancel it, and state what each theory of truth or vagueness says about a paradoxical sentence. The course takes no side between Fregeans and Millians, truth-conditional and use theorists, Quine and the defenders of analyticity, deflationists and correspondence theorists, or rival treatments of the liar and the sorites. It deliberately skips formal linguistic semantics (lambda-calculus type theory, generalized quantifiers, dynamic semantics), the history of analytic philosophy as history, completeness and incompleteness proofs (owned by [`mathematical-logic`](../mathematical-logic/syllabus.md)), non-classical logics beyond the three-valued and paraconsistent tools the paradoxes need, and the philosophy of mental content ([`philosophy-of-mind`](../philosophy-of-mind/syllabus.md)).

## How practice works in this course

Problems follow [HUMANITIES-BUILD-BRIEF.md](../../HUMANITIES-BUILD-BRIEF.md): every part declares its kind, and every solution is a rubric (must-hit moves, wrong turns) plus a model answer. This course has more formal work than any other in the field, and it plays out in four ways:

- **Formal parts are graded as in any math course.** Translating a description at both scopes, building a Kripke countermodel, deriving a theorem in a modal system, computing the stages of a Kripkean fixed point, checking whether a sentence is supertrue. Module 2 is almost entirely formal. **Every countermodel in a solution key is checked world by world** before it is published: the frame condition is verified pair by pair, and the formula's value is computed at every world it depends on. A countermodel that is not reflexive does not refute a T-theorem, and the key must say which frame class the model belongs to.
- **Exegetical parts are strict.** What Russell's theory says "the present King of France is bald" entails, what Strawson says it presupposes, whether a designator is rigid, what the Barcan formula claims, which Gricean maxim an implicature exploits, what Quine's circularity argument actually requires. These have right answers.
- **Evaluative parts are verdict-neutral.** No rubric rewards concluding that Millianism, the causal theory, meaning-as-use, analyticity, deflationism, the gap theory of the liar or epistemicism is correct. Must-hit moves are ones either side must make: state the view precisely, run it on the case, name the premise the objection attacks, and say what the reply costs.
- **Counterexample and find-the-crux dominate the non-formal parts.** The field's arguments are case-driven (Gödel and Schmidt, Madagascar, Pierre, plus and quus, Twin Earth), and the disagreements usually reduce to one premise about what a speaker must know or what a sentence must contribute. Close reading uses public-domain texts only: Aristotle, *Metaphysics* (Ross); Mill, *A System of Logic* (8th edition); Russell, "On Denoting" (*Mind* 14, 1905); and, as the lesson's own translation, Frege's German originals. Frege (read in English translation), Strawson, Quine, Grice, Kripke, Davidson, Kaplan, Putnam, Tarski and Williamson are paraphrased and cited, never quoted at length.

## Dangerous Checklist

When you finish, you can:

- [ ] State Frege's puzzle about informative identities, and say what sense, Russellian descriptions, and Millian direct reference each make of it and of substitution failure in belief reports
- [ ] Translate a sentence containing a definite description into first-order logic at every scope, give each reading's truth value, and say which reading an argument needs
- [ ] Build a Kripke model, compute a modal formula's value at every world, and refute a formula in K, T, S4 or S5 with a countermodel whose frame condition you have checked
- [ ] Match the axioms T, 4, B and 5 to their frame conditions and say which system fits alethic, deontic, epistemic and provability readings of the box
- [ ] Say what the Barcan formula and its converse claim, show where each holds and fails under constant and varying domains, and state what necessitism and contingentism accept
- [ ] Derive the necessity of identity, state Quine's objections to quantifying into modal contexts, and give the scope reply
- [ ] Run Kripke's modal, epistemic and semantic arguments against descriptivism on a fresh case, and say what a causal-historical picture of reference must add to handle a reference shift
- [ ] Explain how rigidity produces the necessary a posteriori and the contingent a priori, and what two-dimensional semantics does with them
- [ ] Write a Davidsonian truth theory for a toy language, and say why extensional adequacy is not enough (Foster's problem)
- [ ] Derive a conversational implicature from the maxims, test it for cancellability, and tell it from a presupposition and an entailment
- [ ] Reconstruct Quine's case against analyticity and name the premise its defenders deny
- [ ] Say what Tarski's theory, deflationism, Kripke's fixed-point theory and dialetheism each make of the liar, and what supervaluationism, degree theories and epistemicism make of the sorites

## Modules

### Module 1: Sense, reference, and descriptions

How a name or a description reaches its object, starting from the two puzzles that founded the subject: why "Hesperus is Phosphorus" is informative when "Hesperus is Hesperus" is not, and what "the present King of France is bald" says when there is no king.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1.1 | Frege's puzzle: sense and reference | State the puzzle about informative identity and Frege's solution | cognitive value, *a = a* vs *a = b*, sense (*Sinn*) as mode of presentation vs reference (*Bedeutung*), compositionality of sense and of reference, the reference of a sentence as its truth value; predicates refer to concepts, not objects (universals cited to [`metaphysics`](../metaphysics/syllabus.md) 1.3) |
| 1.2 | Belief reports and empty names | Explain substitution failure in "believes that" and names with no bearer on Frege's account | substitutivity *salva veritate*, indirect (oblique) contexts, indirect reference as customary sense, sense without reference (Odysseus), the threatened hierarchy of senses |
| 1.3 | Russell's theory of descriptions | Translate a definite description into first-order logic and use scope to dissolve Russell's puzzles | the quantificational analysis (existence, uniqueness, predication), primary vs secondary occurrence (wide and narrow scope), the King of France, George IV and the author of *Waverley*, names as disguised descriptions, logically proper names; Russell's uniqueness clause as the expansion of $\exists!$; whether "exists" has one meaning (cited to [`metaphysics`](../metaphysics/syllabus.md) 1.1) |
| 1.4 | Strawson, Donnellan, and speaker reference | Tell presupposition from entailment, and a referential use from an attributive one | Strawson's "On Referring", sentence vs use of a sentence, truth-value gaps, Donnellan's referential/attributive distinction, Kripke's reply via speaker's reference vs semantic reference |

**Boss problem 1:** (a) Close-read the paragraph of Russell's "On Denoting" (*Mind* 14, 1905, p. 485) that states three puzzles a theory of denoting "ought to be able to solve", and the passages that solve them (pp. 488–490). State each puzzle in Russell's order: George IV and the author of *Waverley*; the excluded middle and the present King of France; and "the difference between A and B does not subsist". For each, say which feature or features of the theory Russell's solution uses: that a denoting phrase is not a constituent of the proposition, which "has been broken up" in the analysis, or the distinction between primary and secondary occurrences. Quote the words that show it. 150 words or fewer. (b) Formal: translate "The present King of France is not bald" into first-order logic with the description at wide scope (primary occurrence) and at narrow scope (secondary occurrence), using $Kx$ for "$x$ is now King of France" and $Bx$ for "$x$ is bald". Give each reading's truth value given that France has no king. Then say on which reading "the present King of France is bald or the present King of France is not bald" is an instance of the law of excluded middle, and what the disjunction's truth value is on the other reading. (c) Strawson ("On Referring", 1950) holds that someone who said "the present King of France is wise" today would make no true or false statement, though the sentence is perfectly meaningful: the question of truth would not arise. Name the single claim about the existence and uniqueness of a present King of France that Russell asserts and Strawson denies, say what Strawson puts in its place, and say what his distinction between a sentence and a use of a sentence contributes to the case against Russell. 150 words or fewer. (d) An invented case, built on Donnellan's party example: at a reception Maelis points and says "the woman holding the champagne is the new dean". The woman she points at is holding sparkling water and is the dean. Exactly one woman in the room is holding champagne; she is across the room and is not the dean. In 300–500 words, assess whether Donnellan's referential use shows that Russell's theory is wrong as semantics, whether Kripke's distinction between speaker's reference and semantic reference handles the case, or neither. Any verdict, if you give the truth value of what Maelis said on Russell's theory, on Donnellan's account and on Kripke's, name the speaker's referent and the semantic referent, say what each account takes Maelis to have communicated, and state the strongest consideration for the verdict you reject (for example, Kripke's test of a language in which Russell's theory is stipulated to hold). Part (a) is exegetical; (b) is formal; (c) is exegetical; (d) is evaluative.

### Module 2: Modal logic

Necessity and possibility as a formal system: worlds, accessibility, the correspondence between axioms and frames, quantifiers across worlds, and the de re modality that Quine thought incoherent and Kripke made respectable.

**Boss problem 2:** (a) Formal: show that $\Diamond\Box p \to p$ is valid on every symmetric frame, and so a theorem of S5; then give a reflexive, transitive frame and valuation on which it fails, checking both frame conditions pair by pair and computing the formula at each world. (b) Formal: model the tolerance case of (e). At $w_0$ an artefact originates from its actual matter, at $w_1$ from matter one step different, and at $w_2$ from matter two steps different; each world sees itself and each world whose matter differs from its own by at most one step. Check reflexivity, symmetry and transitivity pair by pair. With $p$ true at $w_2$ only, show that $\Diamond\Diamond p \wedge \neg\Diamond p$ is true at $w_0$, so that $\Diamond\Diamond p \to \Diamond p$, the dual of axiom 4, fails. Then show that $\Diamond\Box p \to p$ from (a) is valid on this frame while $\Diamond\Box p \to \Box p$ is not, giving a valuation and the world at which it fails. (The relation-level check is the pattern of [`discrete-mathematics`](../discrete-mathematics/syllabus.md) 2.2 P3; what is new here is what the frame does to the modal formulas.) (c) Formal: build a model with two worlds, universal accessibility (so an S5 frame) and varying domains, with $\exists$ at a world ranging over that world's domain only, on which the Barcan formula $\Diamond\exists x\,Fx \to \exists x\,\Diamond Fx$ is false at a world. State each world's domain and the extension of $F$ at each (each extension inside its world's domain), evaluate both sides of the formula at both worlds, and show that the converse Barcan formula $\exists x\,\Diamond Fx \to \Diamond\exists x\,Fx$ is true at both. In two sentences, say what the model shows about what "there could have been something that is F" commits one to, and whether the model is one a necessitist or a contingentist accepts. (d) Formal: write "Necessarily, the author of *Waverley* wrote *Waverley*" in Russell's notation with the description inside and outside the scope of $\Box$, using $Wx$ for "$x$ wrote *Waverley*". Evaluate both readings at $@$ in this model: constant domain {Scott, Hogg}, universal accessibility, three worlds; at $@$ Scott alone wrote *Waverley*, at $v$ Hogg alone did, at $u$ nobody did. Then evaluate $\Box\forall x\,(\forall y\,(Wy \leftrightarrow y = x) \to Wx)$ at $@$, and say what the three results show about the claim that the narrow-scope reading of "the F is F" is trivially necessary. (The de dicto/de re contrast for "the number of planets" is [`metaphysics`](../metaphysics/syllabus.md) 3.1 and its Boss 3(c), and is not re-set here.) (e) Chandler ("Plantinga and the Contingently Possible", 1976) and Salmon ("The Logic of What Might Have Been", 1989) argue that metaphysical possibility is not transitive. A bicycle could have originated with a few of its parts different; had it done so, that same bicycle could have originated with a few more different; but it could not have originated with that many of its original parts replaced. In 300–500 words, assess whether the argument shows that the logic of metaphysical modality lacks axiom 4 (and so 5), or whether a defender of S5 can resist it, for example by denying that the tolerance principle iterates, by treating the limit on variation as vague, or by moving to counterpart theory. Any verdict, including a mixed one, if you state the tolerance principle and the limit premise, show which instance of $\Diamond\Diamond p \to \Diamond p$ they jointly falsify, use the frame of (b), say whether the case also tells against axiom B, and say what follows for the claim in [`philosophy-of-religion`](../philosophy-of-religion/syllabus.md) 2.2 that the modal ontological argument goes through in B. Parts (a)–(d) are formal; (e) is evaluative.
