# Philosophy of Economics — Syllabus

> Philosophy · Tier 2 · ~21 lessons · Prereqs: [`ethics`](../ethics/syllabus.md), [`grad-micro`](../grad-micro/syllabus.md) · Roadmap id: `philosophy-of-economics`

## Goal

Economics makes normative claims in technical clothing: that a policy is "efficient", that welfare rose, that a future harm is worth less than a present cost, that people chose what they wanted. This course takes those claims apart. It asks what welfare is and how to measure it, what the Pareto principle and cost-benefit analysis actually license, how to discount the future, whether choices reveal preferences worth respecting, which things markets should not allocate and when a mutually beneficial deal still exploits someone, whether market outcomes can be just, and what a model built on false assumptions can explain. You will finish able to run the formal pieces precisely: a Scitovsky reversal, a revealed-preference violation, a value of a statistical life, the Ramsey discounting equation, a present-bias reversal, a capabilities index. You will also be able to say which value judgment each one smuggles in, and to name the premise two sides of the Stern–Nordhaus, nudge, commodification or Friedman debates actually disagree about. The course takes no side on any of them. It deliberately skips welfare functions and Harsanyi's theorem as decision theory ([`decision-theory`](../decision-theory/syllabus.md) 5.1-5.2), optimal taxation and welfare weights as economics ([`public-economics`](../public-economics/syllabus.md)), theories of distributive justice ([`political-philosophy`](../political-philosophy/syllabus.md) Module 2), the ethics of interest and debt (`philosophy-of-debt`), the choice axioms themselves ([`grad-micro`](../grad-micro/syllabus.md) 2.1, 2.6), and the methodology of causal inference and explanation in general ([`econometrics`](../econometrics/syllabus.md), [`philosophy-of-science`](../philosophy-of-science/syllabus.md)).

## How practice works in this course

Problems follow [HUMANITIES-BUILD-BRIEF.md](../../HUMANITIES-BUILD-BRIEF.md): every part declares its kind, and every solution is a rubric (must-hit moves, wrong turns) plus a model answer. This course sits on the border with economics, so the mix is:

- **Formal parts (archetype 8) run through every module except Module 5.** Test choices for a WARP violation, compute a present-bias reversal, build a Scitovsky reversal, derive a value of a statistical life, compute discount factors and a certainty-equivalent rate, compare arithmetic and geometric indices, and show the lemons market unravelling. They are graded as in any math course, and every number is checked before a key ships.
- **Exegetical parts are strict.** What the Kaldor–Hicks criterion requires, which of Musgrave's three kinds of assumption a given assumption is, what Sen means by commitment, which parameters of the Ramsey equation Stern and Nordhaus disagree about. These have right answers.
- **Evaluative parts are verdict-neutral.** No rubric rewards concluding that CBA is legitimate or illegitimate, that δ should be zero, that nudges respect autonomy, that kidney sales should be legal, that markets are just, or that Friedman was right. Must-hit moves are ones either side has to make: state the view precisely, run it on the case, name the premise the objection attacks, and say whether the objection generalizes.
- **Diagnose and find the crux carry the non-formal load**, because economic prose hides its value judgments. Invented op-eds, regulatory memos and policy briefs are written for each problem and never attributed to real people. Each module also uses reconstruct, counterexample, apply to a hard case and steelman-and-reply, and every module uses at least three archetypes. Close reading uses public-domain texts only: Mill's essay on the definition of political economy, Pigou's *The Economics of Welfare* (1920), J. B. Clark's *The Distribution of Wealth* (1899), Marx's *Capital* vol. 1 (Moore and Aveling). Modern authors (Sen, Hausman, Thaler and Sunstein, Kaldor, Hicks, Scitovsky, Adler, Sagoff, Anderson, Stern, Nordhaus, Weitzman, Satz, Roth, Wertheimer, Hayek, Friedman, Mäki, Sugden) are paraphrased and cited, never quoted at length.

## Dangerous Checklist

When you finish, you can:

- [ ] Say what welfarism and the preference-satisfaction view of welfare each assume, and run the adaptive-preference and laundering objections on a policy case
- [ ] Compare a money metric, a subjective well-being measure and a capabilities index as welfare metrics, and compute how aggregating by arithmetic vs geometric mean changes a ranking
- [ ] Test a set of observed choices for a revealed-preference violation, and say what Sen's commitment and menu-dependence objections do to the inference from choice to welfare
- [ ] Compute a present-bias preference reversal, and decide which of the conflicting choices a behavioural welfare economist, a libertarian paternalist and an anti-paternalist would respect
- [ ] State the Pareto principle and the Kaldor–Hicks criterion precisely, build a Scitovsky reversal, and say what the welfare theorems do and do not show about markets
- [ ] Compute a value of a statistical life from a wage-risk trade-off, run a cost-benefit test with it, and state the strongest incommensurability and citizen-vs-consumer objections and the welfarist reply
- [ ] Compute a social discount rate from the Ramsey equation under rival calibrations, and the present value of a century-distant harm under each
- [ ] Separate the descriptive and prescriptive approaches to discounting, name the parameter the Stern–Nordhaus dispute turns on, and compute a Weitzman certainty-equivalent rate
- [ ] Tell the corruption, coercion, fairness and noxious-market arguments against a market apart, and diagnose which one an argument about a contested exchange uses
- [ ] Say when a mutually advantageous transaction can still be exploitative, on Marxian and on Wertheimer-style accounts, and apply both to sweatshop labor or price gouging
- [ ] Reconstruct the desert, efficiency and Hayekian arguments about whether market rewards can be just or unjust, and name the crux between Hayek and a luck egalitarian
- [ ] Classify a model's assumptions (negligibility, domain, heuristic), and assess whether a model with false assumptions explains, using Friedman's instrumentalism, isolation and credible-worlds accounts

## Modules

### Module 1: Welfare and its measurement

Ethics asked what is good for a person. Economics has to put a number on it for policy, and each way of doing so takes a side in the ethics debate, often without saying so.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1.1 | Welfarism and preference satisfaction | Say what economics assumes when it equates welfare with satisfied preferences, and where that fails | welfarism, preference satisfaction as the economist's default (theories cited to [`ethics`](../ethics/syllabus.md) 1.2), informed and laundered preferences (Goodin), adaptive preferences (Elster's sour grapes, Sen), Hausman and McPherson on preference as evidence of welfare |
| 1.2 | Money and happiness as measures | Decide whether willingness to pay or reported happiness measures welfare, and what each misses | WTP and its dependence on wealth, EV and CV (cited to [`public-economics`](../public-economics/syllabus.md) 2.3), experienced vs remembered utility (Kahneman), life-satisfaction data and its scale problems, subjective well-being as a hedonist-leaning metric |
| 1.3 | Capabilities as a welfare metric | Build a capabilities index and say what the indexing choice decides | functionings and capabilities (Sen), Nussbaum's list (the justice debate cited to [`political-philosophy`](../political-philosophy/syllabus.md) 2.5), the Human Development Index and its switch to a geometric mean, the indexing problem, perfectionism worries |

**Boss problem 1:** (a) Country A has health, education and income indices of 0.9, 0.8 and 0.5; country B has 0.7, 0.7 and 0.8. Rank them by the arithmetic mean of the three (a tie at 0.733) and by the geometric mean (A about 0.711, B about 0.732). Then say what the geometric mean implies about substituting income for health that the arithmetic mean does not. *Formal.* (b) A case written for the problem: women in a region with sharply unequal access to schooling report life satisfaction as high as women elsewhere and express no preference for more schooling. State the verdict on their welfare given by a preference-satisfaction metric, a subjective well-being metric and a capabilities metric, each graded for fidelity to the view. *Exegetical.* (c) Name the crux between Sen's adaptive-preference argument and a defender of respecting stated preferences: the single premise about whose judgment of a life counts. *Exegetical.* (d) In 300-500 words, decide whether a development agency should rank projects by a capabilities index or by aggregate willingness to pay. Any verdict, if the paternalism objection to capabilities and the wealth-dependence objection to WTP are both stated at full strength. *Evaluative.*

### Module 2: Rationality, choice and nudges

Economics infers what people want from what they choose. What a preference is, whether choice reveals it, what the behavioural evidence does to the inference, and what a policymaker may do when choices conflict.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2.1 | Preference and revealed preference | Say whether a preference is a choice pattern or a mental state, and what revealed preference theory can and cannot show | Samuelson's program, revealed preference as behaviourism (WARP/GARP and Afriat cited to [`grad-micro`](../grad-micro/syllabus.md) 2.6), preferences as total comparative evaluations (Hausman), Sen's internal-consistency critique, commitment vs sympathy ("Rational Fools"), menu-dependent choice |
| 2.2 | The behavioural challenge | Decide whether framing, present bias and preference reversals show irrationality or a misspecified model | framing effects, the quasi-hyperbolic (beta-delta) model and time inconsistency, preference reversals, reference dependence (prospect theory cited to [`decision-theory`](../decision-theory/syllabus.md) 2.4), the normative vs descriptive status of the axioms ([`decision-theory`](../decision-theory/syllabus.md) 1.4) |
| 2.3 | Nudges and behavioural welfare economics | Decide whose preferences a policy should respect when a person's choices conflict | libertarian paternalism and choice architecture (Thaler and Sunstein), defaults and opt-outs, "as judged by themselves", Bernheim–Rangel choice-based welfare, the manipulation and autonomy objections, soft vs hard paternalism (cited to [`political-philosophy`](../political-philosophy/syllabus.md) 3.3) |

**Boss problem 2:** (a) At prices (1, 1) a consumer buys (3, 1); at prices (1, 3) she buys (1, 2). Show that each bundle was strictly affordable when the other was chosen, so the data violate WARP and GARP, and say what Afriat's theorem then implies. *Formal.* (b) Give two readings of (a) that do not convict her of irrationality, one using Sen's commitment and one using a change in the consumer's situation between observations, and say what each does to the welfare inference. *Exegetical.* (c) With quasi-hyperbolic discounting (β = 0.5, δ = 1, linear utility), an agent chooses between 10 at date 1 and 15 at date 2. Show that at date 0 she plans to wait for 15 (7.5 vs 5) but at date 1 takes the 10 (10 vs 7.5). *Formal.* (d) An invented regulatory memo proposes enrolling every employee in a retirement plan by default "because it is what they really want". Diagnose which of the date-0 and date-1 choices from (c) the memo treats as the true preference, and what Bernheim and Rangel's approach would say instead. *Exegetical.* (e) In 300-500 words, assess whether the default is justified. Any verdict, if the manipulation objection is stated at full strength and the answer says what would make a nudge objectionable. *Evaluative.*

### Module 3: Efficiency and cost-benefit analysis

"Efficient" is the most used and least examined normative word in economics. What the Pareto principle requires, what happens when economists relax it to potential compensation, and the fight over pricing lives and nature.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3.1 | The Pareto principle | State the Pareto principle, separate its normative from its technical uses, and say what the welfare theorems show | Pareto improvement vs Pareto optimality, the weak Pareto principle as a minimal value judgment, its welfarist and preference-satisfaction premises (from 1.1), silence on distribution, the welfare theorems' normative reading (cited to [`grad-micro`](../grad-micro/syllabus.md) 4.4) |
| 3.2 | The limits of Pareto and the compensation tests | Build a case where the Pareto principle misleads, and a reversal that breaks the Kaldor–Hicks criterion | Sen's liberal paradox (cited to [`social-choice`](../social-choice/syllabus.md) 4.1), ex ante vs ex post Pareto (cited to [`decision-theory`](../decision-theory/syllabus.md) 5.2), spurious unanimity (Mongin; Gilboa, Samet and Schmeidler), Kaldor and Hicks criteria, the Scitovsky reversal, potential vs actual compensation |
| 3.3 | Cost-benefit analysis and the value of a life | Run a cost-benefit test with a value of a statistical life, and say what each step assumes | CBA as summed willingness to pay, value of a statistical life from wage-risk trade-offs, WTP vs willingness to accept, contingent valuation and existence value, distributional weights (formulas cited to [`public-economics`](../public-economics/syllabus.md) 4.2, 5.1) |
| 3.4 | CBA's critics and defenders | Tell the objections to CBA apart and give the welfarist reply to each | incommensurability and plural valuation (Anderson), citizen vs consumer preferences (Sagoff), Kelman's ethical critique, CBA as decision procedure vs criterion of rightness (Adler and Posner), differentiated VSL by income, CBA as a check on regulatory intuition (Sunstein) |

**Boss problem 3:** (a) Two people, two goods. Person 1 has u₁ = 2x₁ + y₁; person 2 has u₂ = x₂ + 2y₂. State A has 2 units of x and none of y, split x₁ = 0.5, x₂ = 1.5, so utilities are (1, 1.5). State B has 2 units of y and none of x, split y₁ = 1.5, y₂ = 0.5, so utilities are (1.5, 1). Show that B passes the Kaldor test against A (redistributing B's goods can give (1.1, 1.8)) and that A passes it against B (redistributing A's goods can give (1.6, 1.2)). Draw both utility-possibility frontiers and say why they must cross for this to happen. *Formal.* (b) Workers accept an extra 1-in-10,000 annual risk of death for a wage premium of 900 dollars. Compute the value of a statistical life (9 million dollars). A safety rule costs 180 million dollars and is expected to prevent 15 deaths; say whether it passes (12 million dollars per life saved, so it fails). Then say how the verdict changes if the VSL is computed separately for workers whose premium is 1,800 dollars and for workers whose premium is 450 dollars. *Formal.* (c) Diagnose an invented op-ed that says the rule "puts a price on human life": does it make the incommensurability objection, Sagoff's citizen-consumer objection, or a distributional objection to differentiated VSLs? *Exegetical.* (d) In 300-500 words, decide whether (a) and (c) together undermine CBA as a decision procedure, as opposed to as a criterion of rightness. Any verdict, if Adler and Posner's distinction is stated precisely and applied to both parts. *Evaluative.*

### Module 4: Discounting the future

A harm a century away can be worth a quarter of its size today or less than half a percent of it, depending on one line of arithmetic. That line is where economics and ethics meet most sharply.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4.1 | Why discount? | Separate discounting utility from discounting consumption, and state the case against pure time preference | compounding and present value, pure time preference vs growth discounting, the opportunity-cost argument, Pigou's defective telescopic faculty, Ramsey and Sidgwick on impartiality across time, discounting vs the justice of interest (cited to `philosophy-of-debt`) |
| 4.2 | The Ramsey equation and the Stern–Nordhaus debate | Compute the social discount rate under rival calibrations and name the parameter the debate turns on | r = δ + ηg, δ as pure time preference, η as the elasticity of marginal utility and as inequality aversion (Dasgupta), the Keynes–Ramsey rule (cited to [`grad-macro`](../grad-macro/syllabus.md) 2.3), the prescriptive (Stern Review) vs descriptive (Nordhaus) approach, calibrating to market returns |
| 4.3 | Uncertainty, the long run, and future people | Compute a certainty-equivalent discount rate and say what discounting cannot settle about obligations to future generations | Weitzman's declining discount rates, gamma discounting, catastrophe and fat tails (named), the non-identity problem and population ethics (cited to [`decision-theory`](../decision-theory/syllabus.md) 5.3-5.4), discounting vs intergenerational justice |

**Boss problem 4:** (a) Using r = δ + ηg, compute the social discount rate for a Stern-style calibration (δ = 0.1 percent, η = 1, g = 1.3 percent, giving 1.4 percent) and a Nordhaus-style calibration (δ = 1.5 percent, η = 2, g = 2 percent, giving 5.5 percent). Compute the present value of 1,000 dollars of damage 100 years from now under each (about 249 and about 4.73 dollars), and the damage a century away that would justify spending 100 dollars today under each (about 402 and about 21,150 dollars). *Formal.* (b) The true rate is 1 percent or 7 percent with equal probability. Compute the certainty-equivalent discount factor and rate at 1 year and at 100 years (about 3.9 and about 1.7 percent), and say why the rate declines. *Formal.* (c) Close-read the passage in Pigou's *The Economics of Welfare* (1920, public domain) that attributes preference for present over future pleasures to a defective "telescopic faculty". Does Pigou condemn discounting future *consumption*, future *utility*, or both, and what does he think follows for the state's duty to the future? Point to the words that decide it. *Exegetical.* (d) Name the crux between Stern and Nordhaus: is it δ, η, or the choice between prescriptive and descriptive calibration? *Exegetical.* (e) In 300-500 words, decide whether the social discount rate should be read off market returns. Any verdict, if the democratic objection to philosopher-chosen parameters and the objection that markets register no votes from future people are both stated at full strength. *Evaluative.*

### Module 5: Markets: their moral limits and their justice

Some things money should not buy, some mutually beneficial deals still wrong someone, and market rewards may or may not be the kind of thing that can be just. The arguments on each side, at full strength.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5.1 | Commodification | Tell the arguments that markets corrupt a good from those that markets coerce or are unfair | the corruption and fairness arguments (Sandel), modes of valuation (Anderson), contested commodities (Radin), Polanyi's fictitious commodities (cited to [`social-theory`](../social-theory/syllabus.md) 5.3), the "markets without limits" reply (Brennan and Jaworski): whatever you may do for free, you may do for money |
| 5.2 | Repugnant markets and blocked exchanges | Apply the noxious-markets framework to a contested exchange and say what repugnance is evidence of | blocked exchanges (Walzer), repugnance as a constraint (Roth), kidney exchange and kidney sales, noxious markets (Satz: vulnerability, weak agency, harm to individuals and to society), vote-buying and surrogacy, prohibition vs regulation |
| 5.3 | Exploitation | Say when a voluntary, mutually advantageous exchange can exploit, and apply rival accounts to a case | Marx on surplus value and the sphere of exchange (*Capital* vol. 1), Roemer's property-relations account, exploitation as unfair advantage-taking (Wertheimer), mutually advantageous exploitation, the non-worseness claim (Wertheimer, Zwolinski), sweatshops, price gouging |
| 5.4 | Markets and desert | Decide whether market income can be deserved, and what marginal productivity has to do with it | desert bases (Feinberg, Miller), marginal productivity theory as a claim of justice (J. B. Clark), brute luck in talent and demand (luck egalitarianism cited to [`political-philosophy`](../political-philosophy/syllabus.md) 2.6), entitlement without desert (Nozick cited to [`political-philosophy`](../political-philosophy/syllabus.md) 2.4), the just wage as one position (cited to `catholic-social-teaching`) |
| 5.5 | Hayek: knowledge, order, and the mirage of social justice | Reconstruct Hayek's case that market outcomes are neither just nor unjust, and state the strongest replies | the knowledge problem and prices as signals, spontaneous order, value vs merit, social justice as a category mistake, efficiency as a justification of markets (from 3.1), replies from structural injustice and the basic structure (Rawls, Young, cited to [`political-philosophy`](../political-philosophy/syllabus.md) 2.2, 4.4) |

**Boss problem 5:** (a) Close-read the closing paragraphs of *Capital* vol. 1 ch. 6, "The Buying and Selling of Labour-Power" (Moore and Aveling, public domain), where Marx describes the sphere of circulation as a realm of freedom, equality and property. What does he claim this sphere conceals, and which words show the description is ironic rather than endorsed? *Exegetical.* (b) After a hurricane, a seller drives generators into the disaster zone and sells them at four times the usual price; buyers line up. An invented op-ed calls it "exploitation of desperate people"; a second invented op-ed calls anti-gouging laws "a ban on bringing generators". Diagnose which argument each op-ed runs: exploitation as unfair advantage-taking, the noxious-markets framework, the non-worseness claim, or Hayek's argument from prices as signals. *Exegetical.* (c) Build a variant of the case, changing one factor only, in which Wertheimer's account and a Marxian account give different verdicts on whether the sale exploits. *Evaluative.* (d) In 300-500 words, decide whether the seller's profit is deserved, unjust, or neither. Any verdict, if a desert-based view, a luck-egalitarian view and Hayek's category-mistake view are each stated at full strength and the crux between Hayek and the luck egalitarian is named. *Evaluative.*

### Module 6: What economic models explain

Economic models assume agents who never existed in worlds that never could. How such models could explain anything, and the long argument over whether it matters that their assumptions are false.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 6.1 | Idealization and isolation | Say what a model isolates and what it distorts, and when an idealization is harmless | Mill's economic man as abstraction (*Essays on Some Unsettled Questions*, the essay on definition and method), tendency laws, isolation (Mäki), Galilean idealization and capacities (Cartwright), the representative agent as an idealization ("as-if" aggregation cited to [`grad-micro`](../grad-micro/syllabus.md) 3.4) |
| 6.2 | Friedman's "as if" and its critics | Reconstruct the F-twist, classify an assumption, and say what instrumentalism must give up | Friedman's methodology essay: prediction as the test, the billiard player and the leaves, the F-twist (Samuelson's name for it), Musgrave's negligibility, domain and heuristic assumptions, instrumentalism vs realism (in general, cited to [`philosophy-of-science`](../philosophy-of-science/syllabus.md) 5.1-5.4), the behavioural challenge from 2.2 revisited |
| 6.3 | How-possibly models and credible worlds | Decide whether a model with false assumptions can explain a real phenomenon | how-possibly vs how-actually explanation, minimal models (Grüne-Yanoff), credible worlds (Sugden), Schelling's segregation model, the lemons model as a worked case (cited to [`grad-micro`](../grad-micro/syllabus.md) 5.1), Reiss's explanation paradox, accounts of explanation (cited to [`philosophy-of-science`](../philosophy-of-science/syllabus.md) 4.1-4.3), causal inference as the complement (cited to [`econometrics`](../econometrics/syllabus.md) 3.1) |

**Boss problem 6:** (a) Car quality q is uniform on [0, 1]. A seller values a car at q and a buyer at 1.5q; only sellers know q. Show that at any price p > 0 the cars offered have average quality p/2, so buyers value the average car offered at 0.75p < p, and the market unravels. *Formal.* (b) Classify three of the model's assumptions (uniform quality, buyers who cannot inspect, risk-neutral buyers) as negligibility, domain or heuristic assumptions in Musgrave's sense, and say which one Friedman's methodology would treat as irrelevant to the model's worth. *Exegetical.* (c) Close-read the passage in Mill's essay on the definition of political economy (public domain) where he says the science considers man only as a being who desires wealth. Is Mill an instrumentalist in Friedman's sense, or does he think the abstraction is true of a real tendency? Point to the words that decide it. *Exegetical.* (d) In 300-500 words, decide whether the lemons model explains why used cars sell at a discount, or shows only how that could happen. Any verdict, if the answer states Reiss's explanation paradox, says which of its three claims it gives up, and runs the answer against one credible-worlds and one isolation account. *Evaluative.*
