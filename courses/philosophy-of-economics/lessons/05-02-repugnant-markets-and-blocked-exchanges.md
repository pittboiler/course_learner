# Philosophy of Economics · Lesson 5.2: Repugnant markets and blocked exchanges

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 5: Markets: their moral limits and their justice · Builds on: [5.1 Commodification](05-01-commodification.md), [3.3 Cost-benefit analysis and the value of a life](03-03-cost-benefit-analysis-and-the-value-of-a-life.md) · Unlocks: [5.3 Exploitation in labour markets](05-03-exploitation-in-labour-markets.md)

## Why this matters

In the United States it has been a federal crime since the National Organ Transplant Act of 1984 to give "valuable consideration" for a kidney. Patients die waiting, and an economist sees a price ceiling at zero and a shortage. Yet economists also built the institution that works inside the ban: kidney exchange, a market with no money in it. [5.1](05-01-commodification.md) asked whether selling a good corrupts it. This lesson asks three narrower questions. Which exchanges does a society block? What is the widespread revulsion at a trade evidence of? And when a market really is bad, should it be banned or regulated?

## The idea

Three thinkers give three different kinds of answer, and the lesson's main job is to keep them apart.

**Walzer: the wrong is in the sphere.** In *Spheres of Justice* (1983), Michael Walzer argues that each social good has its own distributive criterion, fixed by what the good means to the community. Offices go by qualification, punishment by guilt, votes by citizenship. Money is legitimate in the market sphere and tyrannical when it converts into goods of other spheres. His list of [blocked exchanges](../reference.md#blocked-exchanges), things money may not buy, is long. It includes human beings, political power, criminal justice, exemption from military service, and what he calls *desperate exchanges*, deals so driven by need that the community sets a floor under them (limits on hours, safety rules). The claim is **conceptual and normative**: the good's social meaning settles who should get it.

**Roth: the wrong is a fact about us.** In "Repugnance as a Constraint on Markets" (*Journal of Economic Perspectives*, 2007), Alvin Roth treats [repugnance](../reference.md#repugnance), people's objection to a transaction even when its parties are willing, as a constraint on market design as real as technology or incentives. His examples run from organ sales to lending at interest, which was repugnant for centuries and then was not. The claim is **empirical**. Roth does not say repugnance is right or wrong; he says a designer who ignores it will build a market no one adopts.

**Satz: the wrong is in the conditions and effects.** In *Why Some Things Should Not Be for Sale* (2010), Debra Satz looks for the wrong somewhere other than the good's nature or social meaning. A market is [noxious](../reference.md#noxious-markets) when it scores high on one or more of four parameters. Two concern its **sources**: *vulnerability* (one side trades from desperate need or faces a monopolist) and *weak agency* (a party lacks information, or someone else decides for them). Two concern its **outcomes**: *extreme harm to individuals* (death, destitution, servitude) and *extreme harm to society* (the market undermines people's standing to relate as equals). The claim is **normative**, but it runs on facts about the particular market.

**The value judgment in the technique.** An economist evaluating a ban counts each blocked trade as lost surplus: two parties would both have gained. That count is [welfarism](../reference.md#welfarism) at work: only the traders' preferences enter. Satz's fourth parameter, and Walzer's whole view, name a cost that no party's willingness to pay records, a change in relations among people who never traded. Whether that cost exists is the dispute; the surplus count decides it by leaving it out.

**Designing inside the constraint.** Roth, with Tayfun Sönmez and Utku Ünver ("Kidney Exchange", *Quarterly Journal of Economics*, 2004), took the ban as given. A patient whose willing donor is incompatible can swap: donor 1 gives to patient 2, donor 2 to patient 1. Take five invented patient-donor pairs, where an arrow means the donor on the left can give to the patient on the right: 1→2, 2→1, 2→3, 3→2, 3→4, 4→5, 5→3. With two-way swaps only, at most one swap runs, either {1,2} or {2,3}: **2 transplants**. Allow three-way cycles and {1,2} plus 3→4→5→3 gives **5**. The catch is that a 3-cycle needs six operations at once. A promise to give a kidney cannot be enforced, so all the surgeries in a cycle run together lest a donor walk away once his own patient is treated. That is [inalienability](../../philosophy-of-debt/lessons/03-03-what-may-be-pledged.md) as a hard engineering constraint. Choosing {2,3} first would block the 3-cycle, so how a [kidney exchange](../reference.md#kidney-exchange) searches for cycles matters. (The matching theory is [`grad-game-theory` 6.3](../../grad-game-theory/lessons/06-03-stable-matching-market-design.md), which names kidney exchange as an application.)

## The argument

**The noxious-markets argument** (Satz's, reconstructed).

1. **The standard case for markets presupposes parties who understand what they trade, have tolerable alternatives, and come out as equals able to make claims on each other** *(conceptual)*. *In words:* efficiency and freedom justify a market only where trading is a real choice among equals.
2. **Where a market's sources or outcomes depart sharply from these conditions, the standard case does not cover it** (from 1).
3. **Such departures are measured on four parameters: vulnerability, weak agency, extreme harm to individuals, extreme harm to society** *(conceptual)*.
4. **A market high on one or more parameters is noxious and warrants intervention** *(normative)*.
5. **The intervention should fit the parameter.** If the harm lies in the background (poverty, missing information), fix the background or regulate; ban only where the harm is in the exchange itself *(normative)*.

∴ **C.** Some markets should be blocked, many noxious markets should be regulated, and which is which depends on facts about the market, not on the good's nature.

*In words:* the four parameters say where to look, and premise 5 says the answer is often not prohibition.

**Where the argument is weakest.** Premises 4 and 5 together. "High" has no threshold and the parameters have no weights, so two readers can score the same market and disagree. And a critic in the tradition of the [non-worseness claim](../reference.md#non-worseness-claim) ([`philosophy-of-debt` 3.2](../../philosophy-of-debt/lessons/03-02-predatory-lending-and-usury-caps.md)) presses vulnerability: if desperate need makes a sale noxious, a ban leaves the desperate person with the need and one option fewer. Satz's reply is premise 5 itself (fix the need, not the trade), but where the need will not be fixed, the critic asks what the ban buys.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean): vote sales.** An invented town lets residents sell their council-election vote to a licensed exchange for 150 dollars an election. Every sale is voluntary and both sides gain.

- *Vulnerability:* medium. The poor sell more, but no one is desperate for 150 dollars.
- *Weak agency:* low. A vote is simple to understand.
- *Harm to individuals:* low. No one is injured.
- *Harm to society:* high. Votes flow to whoever pays most, so political power tracks wealth, and the equal standing that a vote expresses is gone.

The market is noxious on one parameter. Premise 5 then asks where the harm lies. Here it is in the exchange itself: a regulated vote market still turns money into votes. So the framework points to prohibition, and Walzer agrees for his own reason, since political power is a blocked good. Roth's question is different again: is vote-selling repugnant here? That a ban is popular explains why it is stable; it does not justify it.

**Example 2 (hard): kidney sales.** Compare the figure's first two rows. In an unregulated market, brokers recruit the poor, misstate the risks and skip the after-care: high on both sources and on harm to individuals. A state buyer at a fixed price, with screening, counselling and lifetime follow-up, can lower weak agency and individual harm. Iran has run a government-regulated scheme of payment to unrelated living donors since 1988, the case defenders and critics of sales both cite. What regulation cannot obviously touch is vulnerability: sellers will still mostly be poor.

Here the framework strains. Premise 5 says to fix the background. The defender of a regulated market answers that the background will not be fixed before the patient dies, and that a ban protects the poor seller from an option she wants, at the cost of a stranger's life. The critic answers with the fourth parameter: a society where the poor are a standing reserve of organs for the rich has changed how its members stand to each other, even if every sale was chosen. Both sides accept Satz's parameters. They disagree on an empirical question (would regulation in fact protect sellers?) and on a normative one (how much harm to society outweighs lives saved?). The parameters frame the dispute but do not settle it.

## Watch out

- **You might think Roth argues that repugnant markets should be legalized, but actually his central claim is empirical.** Repugnance constrains what can be built, as kidney exchange shows by working within the ban. Reading Roth as a verdict on kidney sales mixes an empirical claim with a normative one.
- **You might think "noxious" means "ban it", but actually premise 5 often points to regulation or to fixing the background.** Satz's framework is a diagnosis that locates the problem, and the remedy follows the location.
- **You might think a good that may not be sold may not be transferred at all, but actually blocking comes in degrees.** A kidney may be given and, through exchange, swapped in kind; only payment is blocked. [5.1](05-01-commodification.md)'s question of whether money itself corrupts is exactly what the gift-yes, sale-no line assumes.

## One-liner

> Walzer blocks an exchange because of what the good means, Roth treats revulsion at it as a fact designers must work around, and Satz asks who trades and with what effects; the economist's count of blocked trades as lost surplus leaves out the one harm, to relations among equals, that the case against a market most often rests on.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢) *(Exegetical (a)–(b).)*** An **invented** scheme. A country lets people facing eviction enrol in first-in-human drug trials for 3,000 dollars, paid straight to their landlords. Four features:

- (i) Recruiters advertise in the waiting room of the housing court.
- (ii) The risks of a first dose in humans are unknown even to the trial's designers, and the 40-page consent form is signed the same day.
- (iii) There is a small chance of permanent organ damage.
- (iv) If the scheme spreads, courts begin offering trial enrolment in place of an eviction hearing, so that one class of citizens comes to pay its debts with its bodies.

(a) Assign each feature to one of Satz's four parameters. **One line each.** (b) Which of the four could a rule short of prohibition address, and which could it not? **Two sentences.**

**P2 (🟡) *(Exegetical.)*** Find the crux. Two **invented** positions, not the words of any real person:

> **A:** "Repugnance is a fact about a population, like a transport cost. A designer must respect it, but it is no evidence that a trade is wrong. Lending at interest was repugnant for centuries."
>
> **B:** "Widely shared revulsion at a willing exchange is defeasible evidence that it wrongs someone, as a strong sense that a division is unfair is evidence of unfairness."

(a) Name the single premise one side must deny, and say which kind of claim it is. (b) Say what evidence or argument would move it. **120 words or fewer in all.**

**P3 (🔴, optional) *(Evaluative.)*** Steelman and reply. An **invented** proposal: a national health service buys kidneys from living donors at a fixed 40,000 dollars, allocates them by the existing medical waiting list, bans private sales and brokers, and gives every donor lifetime follow-up care. State the strongest objection to it, then the best reply. **150 words or fewer.** Any verdict; you are graded on the moves.

<details>
<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical (a)–(b))*

**Must hit, strict (a):**

- (i) **Vulnerability:** recruitment targets people trading from desperate need.
- (ii) **Weak agency:** the information is missing (unknown risks) and there is no time to absorb what there is.
- (iii) **Extreme harm to individuals.**
- (iv) **Extreme harm to society:** a class answering for debts with its bodies no longer stands as equals with the rest.

**Must hit, strict (b):**

- Weak agency, and to a degree vulnerability at the point of recruitment, can be addressed short of a ban: a waiting period, independent counselling, no recruiting at the court, the fee paid to the participant rather than the landlord.
- The individual harm cannot be regulated away, since the risk of a first dose in humans is unknown by its nature; and the deeper vulnerability (people facing eviction) is in the background, which premise 5 says to fix directly.

**Wrong turns:** filing (ii) under vulnerability because the participants are poor: vulnerability concerns bargaining position, weak agency concerns information and decision. Filing (iv) under harm to individuals because each debtor is harmed: the feature is about how a class stands to everyone else. Saying regulation can fix everything, or nothing.

**Model answer:** (a) (i) vulnerability; (ii) weak agency; (iii) extreme harm to individuals; (iv) extreme harm to society. (b) Rules on recruitment, a cooling-off period, independent advice and paying the participant directly could lower weak agency and the recruiting side of vulnerability. They cannot remove the unknown risk of a first human dose, nor the eviction-driven need behind every enrolment, which premise 5 sends to housing policy rather than to the trial.

---

**P2** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict:**

- (a) The crux is whether **repugnance has evidential weight about wrongness**. A must deny it, B asserts it. It is an **epistemological claim about moral evidence** (whether a reaction is evidence of a normative fact), not a disagreement about what people in fact feel, which both accept.
- (b) What would move it: a **debunking** story showing the revulsion tracks something irrelevant to wrongness (unfamiliarity, disgust at the body, a fading taboo, as with interest), which favours A; or evidence that the revulsion survives reflection and full information and lines up with an independently identifiable harm (Satz's parameters), which favours B. This is the general question of when [intuitions count as evidence](../../philosophical-method/lessons/03-04-intuitions-and-reflective-equilibrium.md).

**Wrong turns:** calling the dispute empirical, as if a survey of how many people object could settle it: both sides grant the revulsion exists. Treating A's interest example as decisive: one revulsion that faded shows repugnance is fallible, which B already concedes with "defeasible".

**Model answer:** (a) The crux is whether a widely shared revulsion at a willing exchange is evidence that the exchange wrongs someone. A denies it and B asserts it. It is an epistemic claim about moral evidence, since both agree on the empirical fact that people object. (b) A debunking explanation would move it toward A: if the revulsion tracks unfamiliarity or bodily disgust and fades with exposure, as with interest. Toward B: if it survives full information and reflection and coincides with a harm identifiable without it, such as harm to people's standing as equals.

---

**P3** *(Evaluative)*

**Accept:** any objection that is the strongest available against *this* design (not against an unregulated market), and a reply that engages it directly.

**Must hit, any verdict:**

- The objection aimed at what the design leaves intact. Weak agency and individual harm are largely addressed by screening and follow-up care, so the strong objections run through **vulnerability** (sellers will be overwhelmingly poor; a fixed price paid to people in need is a desperate exchange, in Walzer's term) or **harm to society** (the poor become a reserve of organs, which changes how citizens stand to each other).
- A reply that meets that objection: the non-worseness point (the ban leaves the poor seller with the need and one option fewer, and costs a patient's life); or that allocation by medical need, not wealth, blocks the "organs for the rich" form of the societal harm; or premise 5 turned round (fix the poverty, but do not make the patient wait for it).
- Some recognition of what remains open: whether regulation in fact protects sellers is empirical.

**Wrong turns:** objecting with risks the design has removed (brokers, fraud, no after-care). Replying "it is their body, their choice", which ignores the vulnerability parameter rather than answering it. Treating the repugnance of kidney sales as itself the objection, without saying what it is evidence of.

**Model answer (one of several):** The strongest objection is from vulnerability and harm to society. At a fixed 40,000 dollars, the people who sell will be the people who need 40,000 dollars, so the scheme turns poverty into a supply of organs. Even if each sale is chosen, a society that meets its transplant needs from the bodies of its poorest members has made their standing as equals depend on what they can give up. The reply: allocation by the medical waiting list means poor patients receive kidneys as often as rich ones, so the organs do not flow upward; and the alternative is not a world without poverty but the same poverty plus deaths on the waiting list. What decides between them is partly empirical: whether sellers in such schemes end up better or worse off.

</details>

## Flashback

**From Lesson [4.3](04-03-uncertainty-the-long-run-and-future-people.md) (Uncertainty, the long run, and future people):** *(Formal (a)–(b) · Exegetical (c).)* Illustrative numbers. A planner holds $\delta$ fixed and is uncertain only about the persistent rate: 4 percent with probability 0.9, or 0.5 percent with probability 0.1 (annual compounding). (a) Compute the certainty-equivalent discount factor $A(t)$ and rate $R(t)$ at 1 and 300 years, and the share of $A(300)$ supplied by the 0.5 percent scenario. (b) Value 1 million dollars of damage 300 years away at $A(300)$, then at the mean rate. (c) In two sentences: has the planner become more patient at long horizons, and which premise of Weitzman's argument must a critic attack to make the long-run rate *rise* instead?

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

(a) $t=1$: $A=0.9/1.04+0.1/1.005=0.865385+0.099502=0.964887$, so $R=0.964887^{-1}-1\approx 3.64$ percent (the mean rate is $0.9(4)+0.1(0.5)=3.65$).

$t=300$: $1.04^{-300}=7.76\times10^{-6}$ and $1.005^{-300}=0.223966$, so $A=0.9(7.76\times10^{-6})+0.1(0.223966)=0.000007+0.022397=0.022404$ and $R=0.022404^{-1/300}-1\approx 1.27$ percent. The 0.5 percent scenario supplies $0.022397/0.022404\approx 99.97$ percent of $A$.

(b) At $A(300)$: $0.022404\times10^{6}\approx 22{,}404$ dollars. At the mean rate: $1.0365^{-300}\times10^{6}\approx 21$ dollars, about a thousand times less.

**Must hit, strict (a)–(b):** $R$ about 3.64 percent at 1 year and 1.27 percent at 300; share about 99.97 percent; about 22,400 dollars against about 21.

**Must hit, strict (c):**

- No: $\delta$ is fixed. The decline comes from averaging discount factors, which are convex in the rate, so the scenario with the lowest possible rate dominates the far future even at probability 0.1.
- The premise to attack is premise 2, valuing at expected *present* value. Averaging *future* values (Gollier) makes the 4 percent world dominate, so the implied rate rises toward 4 percent; which date's dollar counts as safe is a welfare choice.

**Wrong turns:** discounting at the mean rate of 3.65 percent (averaging rates, not factors). Attacking premise 3 instead: convexity is arithmetic, not a contestable premise.

**Model answer (c):** The planner's impatience is unchanged; the rate falls because averaging factors lets a 10 percent chance of a 0.5 percent world carry almost all the weight at 300 years. A critic who wants a rising rate must deny premise 2, averaging future rather than present values, which makes the 4 percent world dominant instead.
