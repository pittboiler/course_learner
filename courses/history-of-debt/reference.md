# A History of Debt · Reference Card

> Open book. This card is meant to be open while you work — including during
> quizzes. Nothing here is something you're expected to recall cold.

This course follows debt as a **political institution**: not a contract between two private parties but an arrangement that states write, enforce, suspend and rewrite. The same three questions come back in every era. *Who may bind whom through debt* — a body, a child, a field, a nation's customs revenue, a successor government? *What happens when debtors cannot pay* — a royal edict, a calendar release, a discharge, a rescheduling, an injunction? And *who decides who takes the loss* — a king, a mediator, a bankruptcy commission, a bondholders' committee, a gunboat, the IMF, a New York judge, a legislature? The standing discipline is that **contested episodes are taught as contested**: for the disputes listed under Historiographical cruxes the card gives both sides at full strength, the premise they divide on and the evidence that would move it, and no verdict. Numbers the lessons hedged ("about", "contested", "illustrative", "a modern reconstruction") are hedged here in the same words; do not harden them. Use the card mid-problem to look up a term, an episode's facts and dates, a formula and a worked instance of it, which public-domain translation a passage comes from, and where a ceded topic is actually taught.

## Episodes and institutions

One entry per episode or institution: a plain-English line, then the facts and dates as the lessons give them. Hedges are the lessons' own.

### Mesopotamian credit before coinage

Loans at interest recorded on clay for roughly two millennia before the first coins.

- Earliest proto-cuneiform tablets at Uruk, c. 3300–3000 BC, nearly all administrative accounts of goods owed and delivered. Interest-bearing loans attested in Sumerian cities in the third millennium.
- The Laws of Eshnunna price one *gur* of barley at one shekel of silver (§1) and fix conventional interest at 20 percent on silver and 33 and a third percent on barley (§18A); the same maxima appear in a restored section of Hammurabi's laws.
- Hammurabi §48: in a year when storm or drought destroys the crop, the debtor owes no grain and no interest — King renders it "he washes his debt-tablet in water."
- First coins in Lydia, late seventh or early sixth century BC.
- An Old Babylonian school tablet in the Louvre (AO 6770, as usually read) asks how long silver takes to double at 20 percent compounded, and answers a little under 3.79 years by linear interpolation between year 3 and year 4.

*Introduced:* [1.1](lessons/01-01-credit-before-coins.md)

### The merchants of Kanesh

The case where credit and weighed-metal money plainly work side by side.

Old Assyrian traders, from c. 1900 BC, shipped tin and textiles by donkey caravan from Assur to Kanesh in Anatolia, sold them for silver and gold and sent the metal home. Their archives show goods sold on terms, debts among merchants at a commonly reported rate of about 30 percent a year, and *naruqqum* investment pools. Silver here physically travels, which is why "credit before coins" survives the case easily and "money was originally credit *rather than* a medium of exchange" does not.

*Introduced:* [1.1](lessons/01-01-credit-before-coins.md)

### Hammurabi on debt servitude

A standing rule, running case by case with no royal act needed.

§117 (King, 1910): a person handed over or sold for debt works three years in the buyer's or proprietor's house and is set free in the fourth. It names two routes — outright sale and handing over to work off — and caps the service; it says nothing about whether the debt is thereby cleared. "Himself" is King's rendering; Johns (1903) and most later translations list only wife, son and daughter. §118 treats a *slave* handed over differently: the merchant may sublease or sell on, and "no objection can be raised."

*Introduced:* [1.2](lessons/01-02-debt-bondage-and-the-royal-clean-slate.md)

### Misharum edicts and the edict of Ammi-saduqa

The occasional royal act that broke the consumption-debt tablets while leaving the trading economy running.

- The fullest surviving text is the edict of Ammi-saduqa, issued at his accession, c. 1646 BC on the conventional middle chronology. J. J. Finkelstein's translation (*ANET*, 1969) is the standard English version; the lesson paraphrases it and never quotes it.
- What it does: cancels arrears to the palace and interest-bearing barley and silver loans owed by the protected population (Akkadians and Amorites); frees persons held in debt service; leaves commercial ventures standing; voids loans disguised as sales or deposits, orders refunds of debts collected by pressure in breach of it, and threatens offenders with death.
- Hudson's list, built on F. R. Kraus's collection (1984), gives sixteen acts by Babylon's kings from Sumulael (c. 1880 BC) to Ammi-saduqa's second (c. 1636) — roughly one every 12–13 years on the count used in 1.2 — but that list and its reading of year-names are contested.
- Dates depend on the middle chronology; other chronologies shift the dynasty by decades.

*Introduced:* [1.2](lessons/01-02-debt-bondage-and-the-royal-clean-slate.md)

### The biblical release laws

The Near Eastern clean slate put on a calendar, so that every claim runs out on a date fixed in advance.

- A Hebrew servant serves six years and goes out free in the seventh (Exodus 21:2); Deuteronomy 15:12–14 extends this to women and requires provisions on release. Hammurabi §117 set three years; this sets six.
- In the seventh year a creditor may not demand repayment from a fellow Israelite, though he may from a foreigner (Deuteronomy 15:1–3).
- The law foresees its own side effect and forbids it: the lender must not say "The seventh year of remission draweth nigh" and refuse (15:7–10). The only sanction named is that the poor man may cry against him to the Lord.
- In the fiftieth year *deror* is proclaimed and every man returns to his holding and family (Leviticus 25:10, 13); land cannot be sold in perpetuity, and a sale is priced by the harvests left before the Jubilee (25:14–16, 23).
- Interest on a loan to a poor brother is forbidden (Exodus 22:25; Leviticus 25:35–37; Deuteronomy 23:19–20, which permits it from a foreigner).
- Outside evidence for the seventh year: Zedekiah's release and its revocation during the siege of 588–587 (Jeremiah 34:8–16); Nehemiah's covenant pledge (Nehemiah 10:31; Douay 2 Esdras 10:31); Beth-zur surrendering for lack of food "because it was the seventh year" (1 Maccabees 6:49, 53, c. 163 BC); a loan note from Wadi Murabba'at (Mur 18, usually dated AD 55/56) drafting around a release year. For the Jubilee there is no narrative evidence outside the law itself; later rabbinic tradition held it lapsed with the exile of the tribes east of the Jordan (*Arakhin* 32b).

*Introduced:* [1.3](lessons/01-03-release-laws-in-ancient-israel.md)

### The prosbul

The device that made the release waivable loan by loan.

Mishnah Shevi'it 10:3 reports Hillel seeing people refrain from lending, in breach of Deuteronomy 15:9. His remedy used a rule in the same chapter: debts whose documents have been handed to a court are not released. The *prosbul* (the name is usually derived from Greek, "before the council") is a declaration made before judges delivering the debts to them, so the creditor may collect whenever he wishes (10:4). The Talmud later asked how Hillel could set aside a biblical law; one answer was that once the Jubilee had lapsed, the seventh-year release held only by rabbinic enactment (*Gittin* 36a–b). Whether it was fidelity to 15:9 or evasion of 15:2 is ceded to `theology-of-debt` 1.2; what the historian can use is that nobody invents a device to get around a release nobody observes.

*Introduced:* [1.3](lessons/01-03-release-laws-in-ancient-israel.md)

### The seisachtheia

Solon paired a one-time release with a permanent rule about what could be collateral.

- Solon made archon and mediator, 594/3 BC by the traditional date; some modern scholars put the legislation a couple of decades later.
- He **releases**: debts cancelled, the *horoi* pulled up, citizens enslaved in Attica freed and some sold abroad brought home. He **forbids**: no loan secured on a person, which the *Constitution of Athens* (ch. 9) ranks first among his democratic measures. He **refuses**: no redistribution of land, and no restoration of the old order (*Const. Ath.* 11).
- A law the orator Lysias quotes as ancient (10.18), often taken to be Solon's, let lenders set whatever rate they chose. The reform changed what could be **collateral**, not whether credit existed.
- Two separate disputes cross here: who the *hektemoroi* were (defaulted debtors, or dependent cultivators owing a share by status), and what the *seisachtheia* did to debts (outright cancellation, or Androtion's relief by lowering interest and revaluing the currency — outline; full reconstruction is Boss 1).
- Athens probably struck no coins until the mid-sixth century, and the surviving inscribed mortgage stones are overwhelmingly fourth-century and later (Finley, 1952), so the sources may be reading their own world back onto Solon.

*Introduced:* [1.4](lessons/01-04-solons-shaking-off-of-burdens.md)

### Nexum and the Twelve Tables procedure

Rome's two routes into bondage for debt, and the most frightening execution law in antiquity.

- **By contract**, *nexum*: a free man bound himself, by bronze and scales, to a lender until he paid. He stayed a citizen and legally free, and could be chained by his creditor. **By judgment**, *addictio*: a debtor who lost in court and did not pay was seized and assigned to the creditor by the magistrate.
- The Twelve Tables procedure (c. 451–450 BC), surviving only in Gellius's quotation (*Attic Nights* XX.1.45–49): 30 days of grace; *manus iniectio* and, if no one pays or stands surety, the creditor leads him off in fetters of fifteen pounds (the texts disagree whether that is a maximum or a minimum), owing him a pound of spelt a day; 60 days chained, with three appearances on successive market days and the sum proclaimed; on the third, sale "across the Tiber", death, or division of "shares" (*partis secanto*). Gellius's jurist reads *partis* literally and defends the cruelty as a terror meant never to be used, adding that he never read or heard of anyone being cut up (XX.1.52); a minority of modern scholars read it as a division of the debtor's estate. The Latin does not settle it.
- Almost every step is publicity and delay: the law squeezes the debtor's network far more than it plans to use the knife. Nothing in it requires a *nexum* contract — this is execution after judgment.
- Livy's narrative (II.23–33): debt riots and the scarred centurion in the Forum (495), the consul Servilius's edict suspending seizure of soldiers, the first secession of the plebs and the creation of the tribunate (494). Licinian–Sextian proposals of the 370s–367 deduct interest already paid from the principal and give three annual installments (Livy VI.35); interest capped in 357 and halved in 347, with "some authors" reporting a ban in 342 (VII.16, 27, 42).

*Introduced:* [1.5](lessons/01-05-nexum-and-the-roman-plebs.md)

### The Lex Poetelia Papiria

Goods, not the person, answer for the debt — and the question whether bondage ended or moved into a courtroom.

Livy VIII.28 (Roberts, 1905) gives the senate's proposal after the Publilius scandal: no man kept in irons or the stocks except for a crime, and "the goods and not the person of the debtor shall be the security for the debt." The *nexi* were released and *nexum* forbidden in future. Livy dates it 326; Varro (*De Lingua Latina* VII.105) puts it under the dictator C. Poetelius, usually dated 313. It did not end debt enforcement: judgment debtors could still be *addicti*, and Gellius, some five centuries later, says he sees many debtors adjudged and chained in his own day (XX.1.51).

*Introduced:* [1.5](lessons/01-05-nexum-and-the-roman-plebs.md)

### The Caesarian debt settlement of 49 BC

Relief that rewrote contracts retroactively without cancelling anything.

Credit froze across Italy in 49; debtors expected *tabulae novae* and creditors hoarded. Caesar refused cancellation. He appointed arbitrators to value debtors' land and goods at pre-war prices and hand them to creditors (*Civil War* III.1). Suetonius (*Julius* 42) adds that anything already paid as interest, in cash or by written transfer, was deducted from the principal, and estimates creditors lost about a quarter. Relief was general but its size depended on each debtor's history. Caelius, as praetor in 48, pushed for more and was removed; Dolabella agitated for cancellation in 47. Catiline had promised *tabulae novae* in 63 (Sallust, *Catiline* 21).

*Introduced:* [1.5](lessons/01-05-nexum-and-the-roman-plebs.md)

### The commenda

Don't lend to the ship captain; go into business with him.

Genoa and Venice from the twelfth century. A sedentary investor hands capital or goods to a travelling merchant for one voyage; on return the capital goes back to the investor and profit is split, customarily three quarters to the investor and one quarter to the traveller. If the cargo is lost, the investor loses his capital and the traveller his labour, and the traveller owes nothing more. In the bilateral version the investor puts up two thirds and the traveller one third, with profit split half and half. Historians debate its roots in Byzantine and Islamic forms (the *qirad*). Aquinas's sorting test (*ST* II-II q.78 a.2 ad 5) turns on two variables: who owns the money during the venture, and at whose risk it is used.

*Introduced:* [2.1](lessons/02-01-commerce-around-the-prohibition.md)

### The bill of exchange

Don't lend florins in Florence; buy money payable in Bruges.

The deliverer gives local money to the taker, who writes a letter ordering his correspondent, the payer, to pay the deliverer's agent, the payee, a sum in another currency after the usance. The deliverer has given credit for the usance *and* bought foreign money at a rate agreed today, so his gain is not certain in advance — the feature on which contemporaries distinguished it from a loan. De Roover, reading the Medici ledgers (*The Rise and Decline of the Medici Bank*, 1963), argued that in each market the price of foreign money built in a reward for whoever advanced cash there, so round trips usually came back ahead; they did not always. He also argued that the prohibition steered Italian banking toward exchange and away from discounting bills. The bill moved real money — wool payments, papal revenues, army pay — which is what made the credit riding on it defensible.

*Introduced:* [2.1](lessons/02-01-commerce-around-the-prohibition.md)

### The rent charge

Don't lend to a landowner; buy a yearly income secured on his vineyard.

A buyer pays a lump sum; the seller owes a yearly rent charged on land or a house, redeemable by repaying the sum — at the seller's option only. Two features make it a sale rather than a loan: the buyer cannot call it, so if yields rise he takes a capital loss when he sells on, and on the standard account the charge lapses if the property is destroyed. A **personal** census, secured on the seller's person or general assets and redeemable at the buyer's demand, has neither feature and is a loan in all but name. Northern European towns sold rent charges on their tax revenues, one root of the civic debts in 2.3 and of Holland's *renten* in 3.1.

*Introduced:* [2.1](lessons/02-01-commerce-around-the-prohibition.md)

### Jewish lending in England, 1190 to 1290

A license, a tax and a threat as one institution.

- 1190: massacre of the Jews of York, with the records of debts burned. From 1194 the Crown sets up *archae* holding copies of every bond, administered by an Exchequer of the Jews by about 1200 — the king protects the paperwork because the paperwork is his tax base.
- 1215: Fourth Lateran, canon 67 against Jewish *graves et immoderatas usuras*, "heavy and immoderate" usury (the lesson's translation), and canon 68 on distinctive dress. Because *usura* meant any charge on a loan, this did not legalize moderate Christian interest.
- Mid-1200s: heavy royal tallages under Henry III (hedged; totals debated). 1275: Edward I's Statute of the Jewry forbids Jewish usury, requires a badge and allows trade, crafts and farming for a limited term — narrowly and temporarily. 1278–79: coin-clipping prosecutions and many hanged (numbers debated). 1290: expulsion of roughly 2,000–3,000 people; Parliament granted Edward a large tax in the same year, and historians read the expulsion as part of that bargain.
- Edward had meanwhile found the Riccardi of Lucca, who advanced money against the customs revenue. Christian credit went on; only its supplier and its legal form changed.
- Most medieval Jews were not moneylenders and most medieval credit was Christian. Why Jewish lending concentrated where it did has rival explanations: exclusion from land and guilds; Botticini and Eckstein's literacy account (*The Chosen Few*, 2012); Botticini (2000) on Italian towns actively recruiting lenders.

*Introduced:* [2.2](lessons/02-02-jewish-lenders-and-the-monti-di-pieta.md)

### The Monti di Pieta

A charitable pawnshop that needed a fee to survive, founded amid both real concern for poor borrowers and open anti-Jewish preaching.

First at Perugia in 1462, after the preaching of Michele Carcano and planning by Observant Franciscans; Bernardino da Feltre promoted them at Mantua and across northern and central Italy from 1484, and by the standard accounts insisted a fee was necessary if the monti were to last. Dominican critics, including the young Tommaso de Vio (later Cajetan) in the 1490s, held that any required charge beyond the principal made the loan usurious whatever it was called. The Fifth Lateran Council's bull *Inter multiplices* (1515) approved moderate charges for expenses; what it defined is ceded to `theology-of-debt` 5.1. Monti charged a few percent up to about 10, varying by town and period. A monte's capital was limited and it lent only a share of a pledge's value, so many towns kept renewing the *condotte* alongside it; some early monti borrowed their starting capital from Jewish lenders. Venice founded no monte in the city itself and capped its Jewish pawnbanks' rates (Pullan, 1971). Capped *condotta* rates ran commonly somewhere around 15–30 percent a year and higher in some places; figures vary by town and decade, and the preachers' charge that they were ruinous is evidence about the preachers, not a measurement.

*Introduced:* [2.2](lessons/02-02-jewish-lenders-and-the-monti-di-pieta.md)

### Venice, Florence and Genoa: the first funded public debts

Forced loans turned into consolidated, tradable, perpetual credits — and the city's creditors were the city's rulers.

| | Venice | Florence | Genoa |
|---|---|---|---|
| How money was raised | *Prestiti* assessed on *estimo* | *Prestanze* assessed on wealth; *catasto* of 1427 reformed the assessment | *Compere*, each secured on a named revenue |
| Consolidation | Monte, 1262 (later the Monte Vecchio) | Monte Comune, 1345 | Casa di San Giorgio, 1407 |
| Nominal interest | 5 percent, paid in two instalments; cut later in bad times | 5 percent on face, face sometimes a multiple of cash paid | Varied by *compera*; dividends on *luoghi* depended on revenues |
| Tradable | Yes | Yes, often far below par | Yes, *luoghi* |
| Creditors' political position | The same patriciate as the Great Council | The same families as the ruling councils; tax debtors could be barred from office | A separate corporation with its own council, revenues and territories |

The three moves that make this public debt in the modern sense: consolidation into one fund at one rate, transferability (credits could be sold, inherited, pledged, given as dowries), and perpetuity (the principal was rarely repaid; a holder who wanted out sold). Whether a given forced loan was mostly loan or mostly tax depended on one number the council did not set: the price at which the credit could be sold. Theologians argued for decades over whether taking Monte interest was usury (studied by Julius Kirshner); a common defence of the original lender was that he had been compelled, which was harder to extend to a buyer who purchased at a deep discount voluntarily.

*Introduced:* [2.3](lessons/02-03-the-public-debts-of-city-states.md)

### The Casa di San Giorgio

Creditors who did not merely lobby the government that owed them, but ran a parallel one.

Genoa financed itself through *compere*, loans each secured on a specific tax, with claims divided into *luoghi*. In 1407 these were consolidated in the Casa di San Giorgio, a corporation of the state's creditors that collected the pledged revenues itself and, from the mid-fifteenth century, administered territories such as Corsica. Machiavelli (*Florentine Histories* VIII.29, anonymous translation, W. Walter Dunne edition, 1901; Book VIII, ch. VI in that edition) says the Bank governs and protects these towns "and every year sends its deputies, appointed by vote, without any interference on the part of the republic," calls the arrangement something no philosopher's republic imagined, exhibiting "liberty and tyranny, integrity and corruption, justice and injustice" in one community, and predicts that if San Giorgio came to possess the whole city "the republic will become more distinguished than that of Venice." His origin story compresses a long history of *compere* into one founding after a war with Venice.

### Edward III and the Bardi and Peruzzi

The classic cautionary tale of royal lending, and the case against it.

The Bardi and Peruzzi financed Edward III through the 1320s–1340s, largely against assignments on the English wool customs. Around 1339–40 Edward, deep in war costs, stopped paying his Florentine lenders in full; the Peruzzi and then the Bardi failed in 1343–46 (dates given vary slightly in the literature), amid a Florentine political and fiscal crisis under Walter of Brienne (overthrown 1343) and withdrawals by Neapolitan depositors. Giovanni Villani reported that Edward owed the Bardi 900,000 florins and the Peruzzi 600,000; Villani had been a Peruzzi partner, and Edwin Hunt (1990; *The Medieval Super-Companies*, 1994) argued the companies lacked the capital to lend on that scale, that the sums likely include promised interest and rewards, and that some recovery came through channels outside the English ledgers. The causal role of the default is contested, not settled.

*Introduced:* [2.4](lessons/02-04-lending-to-kings.md)

### The Fuggers and Charles V

Collateral that is itself a royal grant.

Charles V was elected emperor in 1519 with payments to the electors of roughly 850,000 florins, about 543,000 of it from Jakob Fugger. The Fuggers took repayment largely in Spanish assets they administered themselves, including the lease of the military orders' estates (*maestrazgos*) and the Almadén mercury mines — the purest form of "grab the cash flow before it reaches him." It strained twice. The suspension of 1557 reached the German bankers, and by most accounts the settlement of the inherited debts dragged on for years, partly paid by leasing them further Crown revenues: the collateral bought priority in a renegotiation, not immunity from one. And whether the 1519 money bought anything is itself disputed — H. J. Cohn (2001) argued that payments to electors were customary and did not decide the vote.

*Introduced:* [2.4](lessons/02-04-lending-to-kings.md)

### Philip II and the Genoese asientos

A serial defaulter whose debts, on one reconstruction, were sustainable and whose lenders made money.

- Suspensions in 1557 and 1560, mostly on debts inherited from Charles V; Genoese-led asiento lending resumes in earnest from 1566; suspension on about 14.6 million ducats in 1575, settled by the *medio general* of 1577; suspension on about 7 million ducats in 1596, settled within a year with a 20 percent cut. Philip III and Philip IV suspend again in 1607, 1627 and later.
- Drelichman and Voth (*Lending to the Borrower from Hell*, 2014): most of Castile's debt — the long-term *juros* — was never defaulted; asientos were contingent contracts specifying what happened if the silver fleet arrived late or a tax fell short; suspensions were liquidity crises settled fast, with large primary surpluses and debt service averaging about half of revenue in 1566–96, peaking near three quarters in 1574; lenders' benchmark annualized return across 1566–1600 is about 15 percent after settlements, against 7.14 percent on juros, and stays positive even assuming total repudiation; the Genoese acted as a coalition, and a banker who broke a lending moratorium could be cheated in turn, while the king's fear of losing transfer services to his armies made him settle.
- Their estimates are reconstructions from contract archives, built on assumptions about discount rates, revenue and output, and not every historian of Castilian finance accepts them. The 60 percent debt-to-GDP figure is a modern reconstruction; nobody in 1575 measured GDP.
- The 1577 settlement repaid bankers on average about 62 percent of outstanding asientos, mostly in juros backed by new taxes, and the bankers lent a fresh 5 million ducats. Recoveries tracked collateral: 70 percent on loans secured on ordinary juros, 55 percent on juros on the Casa de la Contratación, 42 percent unsecured.

### Holland and the Amsterdam Exchange Bank

The republic of creditors that borrowed at rates no king could get.

The province of Holland issued *losrenten* (perpetual annuities, redeemable at the issuer's option) and *lijfrenten* (life annuities), mostly to its own burghers, secured on provincial excises; on the usual reconstructions its rate fell to about 4 per cent by the 1650s. Johan de Witt published a treatise in 1671 pricing life annuities against redeemable ones. The Amsterdam *Wisselbank* (1609) gave merchants a stable bank money to settle bills in. "Dutch finance" was not simply imported with William in 1688: say "on the Dutch model" only for the features actually copied, perpetual redeemable annuities and excise-backed funds.

### The Stop of the Exchequer

A claim on a tax is only as good as the will of the person who controls the tax.

Restoration England borrowed by *anticipation* through the goldsmith bankers, using Sir George Downing's registered, assignable Treasury Orders (Additional Aid, 1665). On 2 January 1672, with war against the Dutch coming (declared in March), Charles II suspended repayment on Orders and warrants totalling about 1,211,000 pounds, roughly a year of his ordinary revenue. Interest at 6 per cent was promised instead; it began only in 1675, grew erratic after 1680, then stopped. Edward Backwell and Sir Robert Vyner were ruined. The Bankers' Case ran through the Exchequer courts in the 1690s and reached the Lords by 1700; Parliament legislated in 1701, and from December 1705 the claims, reckoned at 1,328,526 pounds, carried 3 per cent and were redeemable at half their face value — which is the promised 6 per cent kept on a principal cut by half, before counting interest missed from the early 1680s and never made up. Whether the Stop was fiscal necessity or a political choice made under the Treaty of Dover is a live dispute (see Historiographical cruxes).

*Introduced:* [3.1](lessons/03-01-from-the-stop-of-the-exchequer-to-the-bank-of-england.md)

### The Bank of England

A corporation of lenders with a charter, a vote on the next loan, and a statute behind their interest.

The Million Act of 1693 raised money on a tontine and life annuities. The Bank of England Act of 1694 raised 1.2 million pounds at 8 per cent from 1,268 subscribers in twelve days, secured on tonnage and liquor duties; the Crown paid 100,000 pounds a year, the 96,000 of interest plus a 4,000-pound management fee, an all-in 8.33 per cent. The subscriber's rate was not low; what had changed was the *structure* of the claim — longer, collective, secured on a parliamentary tax, and revocable only by repaying the loan. On the standard figures the debt went from about 1 million pounds in 1688 to roughly 17 million by the peace of 1697. In 1694 the Bank was a private joint-stock company whose capital was a loan to the government: no monopoly of note issue in England, and no lender-of-last-resort doctrine, both of which are nineteenth-century.

### The credible-commitment thesis

North and Weingast, "Constitutions and Commitment" (*Journal of Economic History*, 1989): tie the sovereign's hands and lenders will lend.

Premises, as 3.2 reconstructs them: (1) before 1688 the Crown altered its obligations unilaterally, from the early Stuarts' forced loans to the Stop; (2) wealth holders priced this in — on their figures the Crown owed only about 1 million pounds on the eve of the Revolution, borrowed short, and paid anywhere from 6 to 30 per cent, while Holland borrowed long at 4; (3) the Revolution settlement moved taxation and appropriation to Parliament, abolished prerogative courts, and the Act of Settlement (1701) gave judges tenure during good behaviour; (4) changing a debt's terms now needed several independent vetoes, with the Bank as a further check; (5) Parliament represented the wealth holders who lent; (6) so the promise became credible and the state could borrow far more, far cheaper. Their predicted evidence: debt from about 1 million (1688) to 16.7 million (1697), roughly 40 per cent of national income on their estimate, and 54 million by 1720; long-term loans from 14 per cent in the early 1690s to 6–8 per cent by the decade's end and about 3 per cent by the 1730s. They add a learning clause: credibility grew as society gained experience with the new institutions. The critics and their targets are in Historiographical cruxes. Note what the Bill of Rights art. 4 actually says — it limits how the Crown may *raise* money and says nothing about lenders, repayment or interest, so any protection for creditors has to be inferred.

*Introduced:* [3.2](lessons/03-02-did-1688-make-britain-creditworthy.md)

### The South Sea scheme of 1720

A debt conversion priced in shares.

- By 1719 Britain owed roughly 50 million pounds, about 15 million of it irredeemable. The South Sea Company, founded by Harley in 1711 to absorb about 9 to 10 million pounds of short-term war debt for 6 per cent and a trading monopoly on Spanish America, offered annuitants its stock. The state would pay the Company 5 per cent on the debt taken in, falling to 4 per cent after 1727 and redeemable thereafter.
- The trick was the exchange rate. Parliament let the Company add stock worth, at par, 20 years' purchase per long annuity (2,000 pounds of stock per 100 a year), but the Company set the terms offered and valued its stock at the *market* price. The higher the share price, the more stock it kept. It lent money on its own shares and sold new shares for small deposits.
- Prices per 100 pounds of nominal stock, approximate, from the *Course of the Exchange* as reported by Neal (1990) and Velde (2025): about 130 in January 1720; first money subscription at 300 in April, when annuitants deposited their annuities *before terms were announced*; terms for irredeemables set 19 May with stock about 350; near 1,000 in June–July; last money subscription at 1,000 on 24 August; the Sword Blade Company, the South Sea Company's bankers, stopped payment on 24 September; around 200 late in the year. Converted annuitants' stock was not issued until October.
- The Bubble Act (June 1720) forbade unchartered joint-stock companies. Walpole managed the settlement in 1721–23: the inquiry contained, directors' estates confiscated, subscribers' terms equalized, and the Company's payment to the state remitted.
- What the state kept: Velde (2025) finds the cash payment was remitted, partly in 1721 and the rest soon after, so the government made little or nothing in cash, and the immediate interest saving was trivial. What stuck was structural — about 12 million pounds of formerly irredeemable annuities became part of a redeemable South Sea annuity, cut to 4 per cent in 1727 and later to 3.5 and 3. Velde puts the ex post gain at about 1.8 million pounds in present value, and follows Dickson (1967) in calling the 1720–21 crisis the closest Britain came that century to repudiating its obligations.

*Introduced:* [3.3](lessons/03-03-the-south-sea-bubble.md)

### The Mississippi System

The same experiment in Paris, at the same moment.

John Law's bank (1716, made royal at the end of 1718) issued paper money, and his Mississippi Company, merged into the Compagnie des Indes in 1719, took over the French royal debt in exchange for shares. Shares rose from 500 livres to about 10,000 near the peak in early 1720, when Law became controller-general; a decree scaling down notes and shares broke confidence in May; shares were about 2,000 by August–September; Law left France in December. The debt was later written down through a *visa*.

### Hume on public credit

Either the nation must destroy public credit, or public credit will destroy the nation.

*(outline; stating the mechanism and the alternatives he considers is Boss 3(a))* *Of Public Credit* (1752); paragraph numbers follow the davidhume.org edition of the 1777 text. Hume's target is the modern practice of mortgaging revenues instead of saving in peacetime (PC 1-2); he doubts ministerial frugality (PC 5, PC 28), weighs and dismisses the benefits (PC 8-10), attacks the "we owe it to ourselves" reply as loose reasoning about who is taxed (PC 17-21), and ends with two ways the story can close, which he names but which you should read in his own words (PC 29-32). Note that a later addition concedes his fathers' prophecies of ruin had already failed, and declines to set a date.

### Smith on public debts

*Wealth of Nations* V.3, hedged more than Hume.

Funding lets governments avoid imposing the full cost of a war while it is fought, so wars last longer and begin more lightly. Borrowed capital is turned from productive to unproductive uses. "The practice of funding has gradually enfeebled every state which has adopted it" — his examples are Genoa, Venice, Spain, France and the Dutch Republic. The liberation of the public revenue, where it has happened at all, "has always been brought about by a bankruptcy; sometimes by an avowed one, though frequently by a pretended payment", the usual form of the second being a debasement of the coin. But he also writes that "Great Britain seems to support with ease, a burden which, half a century ago, nobody believed her capable of supporting," while warning that this does not mean she can bear any burden. He also supplies the course's vocabulary for *anticipation* versus *funding* (V.iii) and calls the Bank "a great engine of state" (II.ii).

### The British debt after 1815

Britain carried close to twice its income and paid it down through ninety years of surpluses, not growth.

Official and academic series agree on the shape but not the peak: the Office for Budget Responsibility's long-run data put the debt at about 155 per cent of GDP on the eve of the French wars and close to 180 per cent after Waterloo, while Eichengreen, El-Ganainy, Esteves and Mitchener ("Public Debt through the Ages", IMF conference paper, 2018), using the Bank of England's millennium dataset, date the peak to 1822 at 194 per cent. Postwar deflation raised the ratio after the fighting stopped, because Britain returned to gold at the prewar parity in 1821; prices had risen roughly 90 per cent between 1791 and 1813 on the same paper's figures. The ratio fell to 28 per cent by 1913. Their decomposition of the fall, 1822–1913: primary balance a surplus averaging 1.6 per cent of GDP, contributing +180.5 per cent of the fall; growth minus interest (real growth 1.9, real interest 3.5) contributing −95.6 per cent; stock-flow adjustment +15.1 per cent. Growth did not beat interest. The averages do not reproduce the history — plugged into the identity they give a ratio of about 4.8 rather than 0.28 — so the surpluses, or a narrower interest-growth gap, must have been concentrated in the years when the debt was largest.

### The First Report on Public Credit

Honour the transferable contract at face value, in whoever's hands it is found.

Hamilton's Report is dated 9 January 1790. His case, with his own debt figures: nations must borrow in emergencies, and a country with little monied capital more urgently than most; a nation of doubtful credit pays a premium on every loan; credit is kept by the punctual performance of contracts, and this debt above all — "It was the price of liberty"; a properly funded debt "answers most of the purposes of money", which benefits people who hold none of it; the debt is about 54.1 million dollars owed by the Union (about 11.7 million foreign, 40.4 million domestic, of which 13 million unpaid interest, plus about 2 million unliquidated) and about 25 million owed by the states, so full nominal terms are unaffordable and creditors should accept voluntary modified terms; and state creditors would otherwise be worse off than federal ones, dividing the creditor class against the Union. He added that creating any debt should always come with the means of paying it off. His answer on discrimination rests on the contract — "The nature of the contract, in its origin, is, that the public will pay the sum expressed in the security, to the first holder or his assignee" — on the value transferability gives the paper, on Article VI of the Constitution keeping the old debts valid, and on the practical problem that many sales passed through several hands. (Outline; the full numbered reconstruction is Boss 4.)

### The Compromise of 1790

The political settlement that did not decide the principle.

Madison's discrimination proposal — pay present holders the highest market price the securities had reached, and the balance to the original holders — was made on 11 February 1790 and defeated 36 to 13 on 22 February. Assumption was narrowly defeated in the House in April. Around 20 June, in Jefferson's later account, he hosted Hamilton and Madison and brokered votes for assumption in exchange for a Potomac capital; the Residence Act passed 16 July, assumption 26 July, the Funding Act 4 August. How decisive the dinner was is disputed, and Jefferson wrote after he had turned against Hamilton. The settlement of state accounts with the Union was eased for Virginia (historians differ on how much that mattered). The Funding Act terms: for each 100 dollars of principal, 66 and two thirds in stock paying 6 per cent from 1791 and 33 and a third in "deferred" 6 per cent stock paying nothing until 1801, with unpaid interest funded in 3 per cent stock; assumption capped at 21.5 million dollars. Two readings of the statute remain in play — the nation-building one (Sylla and others on the financial revolution) and the redistribution one (running from the Jeffersonian critics through Beard, 1913, and E. James Ferguson, *The Power of the Purse*, 1961) — and both can be true of the same act.

### The state defaults of 1841 to 1842

The sequel people cite as a precedent, which is not a reversal of 1790.

After a canal-and-bank borrowing boom, eight states and the Florida Territory defaulted; several repudiated some or all of their debt, Mississippi most notoriously. Congress did not assume. Assumption in 1790 was a founding bargain; refusing in the 1840s left states responsible for their own borrowing. The episode is the standard precedent invoked in the euro crisis, with the cautions set out in 6.2.

*Introduced:* [4.1](lessons/04-01-hamiltons-assumption-plan.md)

### The debtors prison

Jail as a screening device run on the debtor's body.

Two writs: arrest on *mesne process*, before judgment, on a creditor's sworn claim; and the *capias ad satisfaciendum* after judgment, with no fixed term. Its defenders held that coercion reveals ability and that imprisonment was enforcement, not punishment — which is why the prisoner owed fees to the keeper of the Fleet, the Marshalsea or the King's Bench, and why, as Dickens's narrator complains (*Pickwick*, ch. XLII), the law fed a convicted felon but left a destitute debtor on the poor side to charity. Robert Morris, financier of the Revolution and Hamilton's ally on public credit, spent 1798–1801 in Philadelphia's Prune Street prison. Dates: Court for the Relief of Insolvent Debtors, 1813; John Dickens in the Marshalsea for some three months in 1824; New York's Stilwell Act, 1831, with other states abolishing imprisonment for debt from the 1820s to the 1840s; mesne-process arrest abolished in England in 1838 except for debtors about to leave the country; a federal statute of 1839 barring imprisonment for debt on federal process in states that had abolished it; Marshalsea and Fleet closed 1842; Debtors Act 1869, which abolished imprisonment for debt while keeping a committal of up to six weeks for a debtor found to have had the means to pay an ordered instalment.

### English and American bankruptcy statutes

The discharge began as a bribe for disclosure and became something closer to a right.

- England: the Tudor statute of 1542 set up commissioners to seize and distribute a fleeing debtor's goods; the Elizabethan act of 1571 confined bankruptcy to merchants and traders, a restriction that lasted until 1861. The Act of 1705 (4 Anne c.17) gave a conforming bankrupt an allowance and a discharge from the remaining debts and made fraudulent concealment a capital felony; a 1706 amendment required the certificate to be signed by four-fifths of creditors in number and value. John Perrott was hanged in 1761 for concealing assets, often called the last bankrupt executed (Kadens, 2010).
- United States: the Constitution (1787) gives Congress the power to pass uniform bankruptcy laws. The first federal Act (1800–03) covered merchants only and was creditor-initiated. The second (1841–43), after the Panic of 1837, opened voluntary petitions to non-merchants; the third ran 1867–78; the permanent Act came in 1898.
- Bruce Mann (*Republic of Debtors*, 2002) reads the era as a contest in which failure was slowly recast from moral fault toward economic misfortune; Edward Balleisen (*Navigating Failure*, 2001) shows the same double edge in the short-lived 1841 Act, which critics charged with favouring merchants and speculators.

### Sharecropping and the crop lien

What replaced slavery when freedpeople wanted land and planters wanted gang labour, and neither got its way.

A family worked its own plot; the landowner supplied land, housing, a mule, tools and seed, and the crop was divided at harvest, often half and half. Nobody had cash until the crop was sold, so the landlord or a furnishing merchant supplied the family from spring to settlement, and southern legislatures passed crop lien laws from the late 1860s giving the creditor a claim on the growing crop. Goods on credit carried a credit price above the cash price. Ransom and Sutch (*One Kind of Freedom*, 1977) estimated implicit annual rates on the order of 60 percent, argued that each merchant held a territorial monopoly since a farmer with an outstanding lien could not take his crop to a rival, and argued that merchants insisted on cotton as collateral, pushing farmers away from growing their own food — the **lock-in** thesis. Alongside it ran the criminal layer: the Black Codes of 1865–66 (Mississippi's and South Carolina's first) with vagrancy laws, penalties for breaking a labour contract and for enticement; then, after Reconstruction swept the explicit codes away, race-neutral successors — false-pretenses statutes aimed at labour advances, enticement and vagrancy laws, and criminal-surety systems. Behind both sat convict leasing, made possible by the Thirteenth Amendment's exception for punishment for crime (Alabama ended its state system in 1928; Douglas Blackmon, *Slavery by Another Name*, 2008).

*Introduced:* [4.3](lessons/04-03-debt-peonage-after-slavery.md)

### Bailey v. Alabama

A state cannot reach a forbidden end by a statutory presumption.

*Bailey v. Alabama*, 219 U.S. 219 (1911), Hughes for the Court. Alonzo Bailey signed a one-year contract as a farm hand, took a 15-dollar advance to be deducted from wages, and quit after about a month; Alabama's statute made that refusal, without repayment, prima facie evidence of intent to defraud, and its evidence rules barred him from testifying about his own uncommunicated intent. He was fined 30 dollars and, in default, sentenced to hard labour. The reasoning: the Thirteenth Amendment aimed to make labour free; the Peonage Act of 1867 (passed with New Mexico in view) abolishes service performed as peons to pay off a debt and voids state laws maintaining it; peonage is compulsory service based on indebtedness however it began (*Clyatt*, 1905), so a voluntary start does not cure a compelled continuation; a state cannot jail a worker simply for quitting while he owes an advance; Alabama's presumption reaches that result indirectly. Holmes, dissenting with Lurton, denied the last inference: a state may make breach of contract a crime, the presumption only lets a jury decide, and working in a jail for no private master is not peonage. Hughes expressly declined to decide on equal protection grounds; historians have documented that Booker T. Washington quietly helped support the litigation.

*Introduced:* [4.3](lessons/04-03-debt-peonage-after-slavery.md)

### United States v. Reynolds

The criminal-surety system, struck down in 1914.

Ed Rivers was convicted of petit larceny in Alabama and fined 15 dollars plus 43.75 in costs. J. A. Reynolds acted as surety and paid, and Rivers signed a contract to work the debt off as a farm hand for nine months and twenty-four days at 6 dollars a month — 58.75 owed against 58.80 of labour, so the contract simply converts the debt into months. Alabama law made breaking it a crime. Rivers quit, was prosecuted at Reynolds's instance, fined one cent plus 87.05 in costs, and signed with a new surety for over fourteen months. Justice Day noted he could have served at most about 68 days at county hard labour for the original offence, and described the system as keeping the convict "chained to an everturning wheel of servitude." Holmes concurred. *Bailey* did not end peonage: Florida's version of the statute was struck only in *Pollock v. Williams* (1944), and prosecutions under the federal act continued long after (Pete Daniel, *The Shadow of Slavery*, 1972).

*Introduced:* [4.3](lessons/04-03-debt-peonage-after-slavery.md)

### The ordinance of 1825 and the double debt

Recognition converted into a financial claim, the claim into a bond, and the bond into a lasting lever.

- Haiti declared independence on 1 January 1804 after the French expedition of 1802–03 failed, but no great power would recognize the new state. The ordinance of Charles X, 17 April 1825 (verified against the French text reprinted in *Outre-Mers* 90, 2003; the translations in 4.4 are the lesson's own), requires "the present inhabitants of the French part of Saint-Domingue" to pay 150 million francs in five equal annual instalments to indemnify the former colonists, halves Haitian port duties for the French flag (Art. 1), and *grants* (*concédons*) full and entire independence "on these conditions" (Art. 3). Haiti is never named. The first instalment fell due 31 December 1825 and went to the Caisse des dépôts et consignations.
- Baron de Mackau delivered the ordinance in July 1825 backed by a squadron reportedly of about fourteen warships; Boyer accepted. A 30-million-franc loan was floated in Paris, bids run by Ternaux and underwriting by a Laffitte-led syndicate. The bonds carried a 6 per cent coupon but sold at 80 per cent of par, so only about 24 million reached the Caisse against a 30-million instalment, and Haiti owed the full 30 million plus interest.
- Haiti paid the first instalment but missed the second; the loan was serviced briefly, reportedly with French Treasury help, then a moratorium followed. The treaty of 12 February 1838 cut the unpaid 120 million to 60, making 90 million in all, and Louis-Philippe recognized Haiti as a free, sovereign and independent state. Payments were suspended in 1843, resumed in 1849, stopped again in 1867–69; the indemnity itself was paid off around 1883. New Paris loans with heavy fees followed in 1875–1910, with a Paris-controlled Banque Nationale d'Haïti (1880–81) handling the treasury and a 1910 reorganization bringing in French, German and American capital, National City Bank among the owners. In December 1914 US Marines carried about half a million dollars in gold from the bank to New York; the US occupation (1915–34) took over the customs. New York bonds refinanced the French debts in the 1920s; debt service took more than 30 per cent of revenue in 1925–36, and by the *New York Times*'s count the last payments on this chain came in 1947.
- Modern cost estimates: the *Times* counted about 112 million francs actually paid on indemnity and 1825 loan together, roughly 560 million of today's dollars, and estimated the loss to Haiti's economy at at least 21 billion dollars and up to 115 billion. Oosterlinck, Panizza, Weidemaier and Gulati ("A Debt of Dishonor", *Boston University Law Review*, 2022) ran counterfactuals on the 90 million francs: about 0.45 billion 2020 dollars at a 0 per cent real return, 2.9 billion at 1 per cent, 22 billion at 2.1 per cent (Haiti's own long-run growth) and 125 billion at 3 per cent. The spread is pure compounding; the dispute is entirely about the counterfactual, and both sets of authors flag it. The 90 million francs is the hard figure; the 21–115 billion range is a model-dependent estimate and contested.

### Egypt from loans to occupation

A borrower who paid 7 per cent on money it never received, and whose creditors climbed all five rungs in about twenty years.

Tunçer's table of Egypt's foreign loans (*Sovereign Debt and International Financial Control*, 2015, drawing on the Council of Foreign Bondholders' reports) lists nine issues from 1862 to 1873 with face values summing to about 68.8 million pounds, which at issue prices would have raised about 55.8 million, or 81 per cent of face; commissions and syndicate discounts came off that, and critical accounts put net receipts near 46 million, a figure the lesson marks as less secure. The 1873 loan was 32 million nominal, 7 per cent, issued at 79: 25.28 million raised, 2.24 million a year of interest owed on face, an effective 8.86 per cent on proceeds. Across the list the coupons came to about 4.86 million pounds a year, 8.7 per cent of issue-price proceeds. Timeline: Said Pasha's first state loan 1862; the cotton boom and bust of 1861–65; Ismail rules 1863–79 with loans 1864–73, railways, canals and Cairo rebuilt; the Suez Canal opens 1869; November 1875 Ismail sells his 176,602 Canal shares to Britain for about 4 million pounds (Disraeli, financed by Rothschild; the share count is from the Suez Canal (Shares) Act 1876); April 1876 suspension; 1878 Commission of Inquiry and a "European ministry" with an English finance minister; 1879 Ismail dismisses it, the Sultan deposes him under Anglo-French pressure, Tewfik succeeds and Dual Control is restored; 1880 Law of Liquidation, unified debt at 4 per cent; 1881 the Urabi movement; July 1882 bombardment of Alexandria and September the British occupation.

*Introduced:* [4.5](lessons/04-05-egypt-the-ottomans-and-debt-administration.md)

### The Caisse de la Dette and Dual Control

The first international agencies whose job was to run part of a borrowing state for its creditors.

May 1876: the Caisse de la Dette and unification of the debt at 91 million. November 1876: the Goschen–Joubert decree setting the unified debt at 59 million at 6 per cent plus a 1 per cent sinking fund, and creating Dual Control — controllers over the whole budget, which is rung 3, followed by ministers at rung 4. The case for creditor administration, in its architects' terms: the debts are obligations of the Egyptian *state*; Egypt cannot currently pay in full; the Khedive's administration cannot be trusted to keep a settlement, since earlier arrangements had been broken, accounts hidden and taxes taken years in advance; so the pledged revenues must be collected by agents answerable to creditors and the whole budget watched; and honest administration serves the taxpayer too (Cromer's "lever"). Blunt denies the first premise, holding that Ismail's debts were personal, not public. The Caisse was not an early IMF: set up by decree of a debtor ruler, staffed by creditor governments' nominees, serving bondholders, lending nothing and holding no mandate for the debtor's economy.

*Introduced:* [4.5](lessons/04-05-egypt-the-ottomans-and-debt-administration.md)

### The Ottoman Public Debt Administration

The same instrument, stopped at rung 2.

The Ottoman state had borrowed abroad since 1854; coupons were cut in half in October 1875 and payments stopped in 1876, on a nominal debt of roughly 200 million pounds. The Decree of Muharrem (December 1881) cut it from about 191 million to about 106 million, a nominal reduction of 44.5 per cent, and assigned specified revenues to a council of creditors' representatives: the salt and tobacco monopolies, stamps, spirits, fisheries, the silk tithe, and tributes and surpluses from territories the empire no longer governed directly. The Administration hired its own tax staff and paid bondholders directly, with no voice over the rest of the budget, and ran to the First World War while the empire stayed sovereign. Three explanations for the divergence from Egypt, to be kept apart: revenue base (a council can hold a few monopolies without running a state, whereas Egypt's loans were secured on railways, land tax, the Khedive's estates and nearly all general revenue); great-power rivalry (Egypt had the Canal and two contesting powers, while Constantinople's creditors were spread across five); and legal status (Egypt was an autonomous Ottoman province whose ruler the Sultan could depose). Tunçer treats Egypt as the exception among his cases.

*Introduced:* [4.5](lessons/04-05-egypt-the-ottomans-and-debt-administration.md)

### The Latin American loans of the 1820s

A boom, a crash, and creditors with no effective lever.

The new republics borrowed heavily in London in 1822–25, on the order of 20 million pounds nominal. After the crash of 1825–26 nearly all were in default by about 1828, Brazil the main exception. The sidelight is Poyais: Gregor MacGregor sold bonds and land for a Central American "country" that did not exist, and emigrants who sailed there in 1822–23 died in numbers. British bondholders organized as the Council of Foreign Bondholders in 1868, licensed as the Corporation of Foreign Bondholders in 1873 — a private body, not an arm of the state, whose leverage came from organization, publicity and access to listings. Palmerston's circular of 1848 said that whether Britain took up bondholders' claims was a matter for the government's discretion, not a right bondholders could invoke, and the reserved power was rarely used.

*Introduced:* [4.6](lessons/04-06-bondholders-gunboats-and-receiverships.md)

### The Venezuelan blockade and the Drago Doctrine

Force rewarded, then restricted but not banned.

Britain, Germany and Italy blockaded Venezuela in 1902–03 over claims that mixed injuries to their nationals during the civil war under Cipriano Castro with unpaid debts. In February 1904 a Hague arbitral tribunal gave the blockading powers priority over the claims of non-blockading states. Argentina's foreign minister Luis María Drago had argued in December 1902 that public debt could not justify armed intervention or occupation of an American state; the Hague Convention of 1907 (the Porter Convention) adopted the narrower rule that states would not use armed force to recover contract debts *unless* the debtor refused arbitration or ignored the award. Coding the episode is itself the dispute: the same facts count as a supersanction on one reading and as a tort dispute coinciding with a default on the other.

*Introduced:* [4.6](lessons/04-06-bondholders-gunboats-and-receiverships.md)

### The Dominican customs receivership

The middle path between doing nothing and annexation.

Theodore Roosevelt's annual message of 6 December 1904 — the Roosevelt Corollary — puts paying obligations in the *safe-harbor* sentence, alongside keeping order, and names "chronic wrongdoing, or an impotence which results in a general loosening of the ties of civilized society" in the *trigger* sentence, which may require "intervention by some civilized nation" and could force the United States "however reluctantly, in flagrant cases of such wrongdoing or impotence, to the exercise of an international police power." Under a *modus vivendi* of 1905, US officials collected Dominican customs and paid 55 per cent toward the debts and 45 per cent to the government; a convention of 1907 formalized the receivership. By then claims of over 30 million dollars had been scaled down to about 17 million and refinanced with a 20-million-dollar New York loan. Nicaragua (1911) and Haiti (1915, under occupation) followed with variants.

*Introduced:* [4.6](lessons/04-06-bondholders-gunboats-and-receiverships.md)

### Newfoundland, 1934

Self-government lost without a gunboat.

A British dominion near default was carried by British and Canadian loans. After a royal commission (1933), its own legislature asked for the constitution to be suspended, and an appointed Commission of Government ruled from 1934 to 1949.

*Introduced:* [4.6](lessons/04-06-bondholders-gunboats-and-receiverships.md)

### Article 231 and the London Schedule of Payments

Liability established first, the sum turned into an administrative process, then a headline in three tiers.

Article 231 of the Treaty of Versailles opens Part VIII, "Reparation": the Allied and Associated Governments affirm and Germany accepts the responsibility of Germany and her allies for causing all the loss and damage suffered as a consequence of the war imposed upon them by their aggression. It asserts *responsibility for loss and damage* and names no sum. Article 232 concedes that Germany's resources are not adequate for complete reparation and limits the demand to listed civilian damage, including pensions; Articles 232–233 hand the amount to a Reparation Commission to fix by 1 May 1921, with 20 billion gold marks due meanwhile (Art. 235). The London Schedule of Payments (5 May 1921) fixed 132 billion gold marks in A bonds (12 billion), B bonds (38 billion) and C bonds (82 billion), with Germany paying 2 billion gold marks a year plus 26 per cent of the value of its exports. The C bonds became an active obligation only by a further decision, once payments were shown to be enough to service them, so Sally Marks ("The Myths of Reparations", 1978) reads the operative demand as about 50 billion. "War guilt clause" is not the treaty's language: Keynes read 231 as drafted so that Wilson could take it as a moral admission and Lloyd George as a financial one (*Economic Consequences*, ch. V), and the label comes from the German reading, not the text. (Outline; the reconstruction of Keynes's capacity argument in the reparation chapter, and the present value of a reparations schedule against a headline that rests on bonds never scheduled for service, are Boss 5.)

*Introduced:* [5.1](lessons/05-01-war-debts-and-reparations.md)

### The inter-Allied war debts

One debt or two — the political fight of the 1920s.

Keynes's 1919 table of wartime loans to governments, in millions of dollars: the United States lent 4,210 to Britain, 2,750 to France, 1,625 to Italy; Britain lent 2,540 to France, 2,335 to Italy, 2,840 to Russia; France lent 800 to Russia. His total for US loans was about 9.5 billion; postwar credits and accrued interest raised the principal the commission worked from to roughly 11 billion. Russia repudiated in 1918, which wiped out Britain's and France's largest claims. The reparation shares agreed among the Allies at Spa in 1920 were about 52 per cent to France, 22 to Britain, 10 to Italy (approximate; Belgium's and the smaller shares omitted). **US position:** the advances were loans to sovereign governments; Congress created a World War Foreign Debt Commission (1922) and forbade it to cancel any part, set a rate below 4 and a quarter per cent, or run past 1947. **British position** (the Balfour Note, August 1922): the debts were "a connected series of transactions" for a common purpose; Britain was owed about 3.4 billion pounds by its allies and owed the US about 850 million, proposed to write off everything including reparations, and failing that would collect only what it needed to pay Washington — "if our undoubted obligations as a debtor are to be enforced, our not less undoubted rights as a creditor cannot be left wholly in abeyance." **Keynes** (1919, ch. VII): without cancellation the war would end with the Allies paying "indemnities to one another instead of receiving them from the enemy", and the inter-Allied debts are what make the reparation demand politically impossible to lower. Settlements: Britain 1923, 4.6 billion dollars over 62 years at 3 per cent for ten years and 3 and a half thereafter, which broke the 1922 statute's limits and passed as an amendment; Italy 1925 (2.0 billion) and France 1926 (4.0 billion) at still lower average rates.

*Introduced:* [5.1](lessons/05-01-war-debts-and-reparations.md)

### The occupation of the Ruhr

A creditor state using troops against a great power.

Germany accepted the 1921 schedule under an Allied ultimatum threatening occupation of the Ruhr. In late 1922 the Reparation Commission declared Germany in default on deliveries in kind (timber, then coal). French and Belgian troops occupied the Ruhr in January 1923; the German government backed passive resistance and financed it largely by printing money, and prices already rising fast went into hyperinflation, a dollar costing about 4.2 trillion marks by November 1923. How much the occupation, rather than prior policy, caused the collapse is disputed. Two readings of French motives fit the same facts: a creditor enforcing a claim on which the French budget depended, or a power weakening Germany and seeking leverage to revise the settlement, the Rhineland included. The web of claims does not decide between them, and this is the same cause-or-pretext crux as Egypt in 1882.

*Introduced:* [5.1](lessons/05-01-war-debts-and-reparations.md)

### The Dawes and Young plans

The transfer argument turned into machinery, and then taken apart.

**Dawes (1924):** graduated annuities beginning around 1 billion gold marks and rising to a "standard" 2.5 billion by 1928–29, paid *in marks* into an account in Berlin, with an Agent-General for Reparation Payments transferring abroad only so far as it could be done without wrecking the exchange rate. A loan of 800 million marks started the system, much of it placed in New York. It conceded Keynes's distinction and kept the sum: the risk that the annuity could not be transferred was carried by the people waiting to be paid. It worked, narrowly, for five years, because American lending supplied the foreign exchange the export adjustment was supposed to earn. **Young (1929, in force 1930):** reduced and stretched the schedule to 59 annuities running to 1988, on the order of 112 billion marks including interest, created the Bank for International Settlements to handle payments, and split the annuity into a postponable part and an unconditional part — commercializing the debt and removing most of the transfer protection, months before the American lending boom ended.

*Introduced:* [5.2](lessons/05-02-could-germany-pay.md)

### The end of reparations, 1931 to 1932

The same country called able to pay in 1929 and unable in 1931, with no change in its productive capacity.

When the lending stopped, Germany's foreign exchange had to come from a trade surplus, which meant deflation: Brüning's emergency decrees, falling wages, mass unemployment. In June 1931 Hoover announced a one-year moratorium on intergovernmental war debts and reparations alike, and a "standstill" froze Germany's short-term bank credits. At Lausanne in July 1932 the Allies effectively ended reparations, asking only that Germany deposit 3 billion marks of bonds to be sold later, conditional on relief on their own war debts from Washington. The relief never came, the bonds were never marketed, and in 1933–34 the European debtors defaulted on the United States; the Johnson Act (April 1934) barred further American lending to governments in default. "Capacity to pay" turned out to be a property of Germany *plus* the capital market.

*Introduced:* [5.2](lessons/05-02-could-germany-pay.md)

### The London Agreement on German External Debts

Reparations settled by being set aside.

Signed 27 February 1953 (English is one of its authentic texts). Article 5, "Claims excluded from the Agreement", defers consideration of governmental claims arising out of the first World War "until a final general settlement of this matter", and of claims arising out of the second "until the final settlement of the problem of reparation" — understood to wait on a peace treaty with a reunified Germany. It does not decide whether the claims are good; it parks them. What it did settle was the ordinary debts, prewar and postwar, including the Dawes and Young loans that had financed reparations, cutting them by roughly half (about 29.7 billion Deutschmarks of claims reduced to something near 14.5 billion; historians quote slightly different totals depending on what is counted). Article 34 provides for consultation if Germany has difficulty, with attention to "all relevant economic, financial and monetary considerations which relate to the ability to transfer of the Federal Republic of Germany, as influenced by both internal and external factors" — while opening "without derogating from the obligations which the Federal Republic of Germany has assumed."

*Introduced:* [5.2](lessons/05-02-could-germany-pay.md)

### Bretton Woods

The creditor's plan won, as creditors' plans do.

Delegates from forty-four nations met in July 1944. **Keynes's Clearing Union (1943):** *bancor* as a new unit of account; overdrafts scaled to pre-war trade, several times larger than White's fund; both sides adjust by design, with a charge to the Union's reserve fund when a member's average balance, credit *or* debit, exceeds a quarter of its quota and a further charge above a half, and a large persistent creditor also obliged to discuss corrective measures — revaluation, domestic expansion, lower tariffs, foreign lending — with the governing board; financing close to automatic within quota. **White's Stabilization Fund (1943):** gold and subscribed national currencies, roughly 5 billion dollars, the deficit country adjusts, no creditor obligation, access at the Fund's discretion. **The Articles (1944):** gold and the dollar, par values in gold or the 1944 dollar; about 8.8 billion dollars of quotas; the deficit country adjusts; no creditor obligation except the scarce-currency clause; purchases capped at 25 per cent of quota in twelve months and Fund holdings of a member's currency capped at 200 per cent of quota. Article I(v) carries the design in two phrases — "under adequate safeguards", the seed of conditionality, and "correct maladjustments in *their* balance of payments", which names the drawing member as the one corrected. The World Bank (IBRD), with authorized capital of 10 billion dollars, was the conference's other creation: long-term project lending, not balance-of-payments finance. The Fund's own machinery came later: the stand-by arrangement from 1952, binding performance criteria from the late 1950s, the Paris Club out of a 1956 meeting on Argentina.

*Introduced:* [5.3](lessons/05-03-bretton-woods-and-the-lending-boom.md)

### Petrodollar recycling and the syndicated loan

Deficit countries financed lavishly and asked for almost nothing in return.

On 15 August 1971 Nixon suspended the dollar's convertibility into gold, and by 1973 the major currencies floated. Oil prices roughly quadrupled over 1973–74; exporters' surpluses were deposited in banks, largely in the offshore Eurodollar market, and lent on to oil-importing governments and state enterprises through syndicated loans at LIBOR plus a spread, reset every six months. Non-oil developing countries' external debt rose from roughly 130 billion dollars in 1973 to something like 470 billion by 1980 on IMF-derived figures (round numbers; the series are revised), and through much of the decade inflation left real interest rates near zero or negative. Two readings, both held by serious people: recycling as success, since private banks did in months what no official institution had the resources to do and the world avoided a 1931-style trade collapse; and recycling as the fuse, since the borrower now carried the interest-rate risk, maturities were short, no conditionality slowed the borrowing, and bankers reasoned — in a remark usually attributed to Citibank's Walter Wriston — that sovereigns do not go bankrupt.

*Introduced:* [5.3](lessons/05-03-bretton-woods-and-the-lending-boom.md)

### Mexico, August 1982

The rate shock, and a creditor structure that made writing the loans down unthinkable.

The Volcker disinflation drove the effective federal funds rate to a monthly average of 19.1 per cent in June 1981, the highest on record, roughly doubling the cost of debt already contracted while world recession cut commodity prices and new lending stopped. In mid-August 1982 Mexico's finance minister Jesús Silva Herzog told the US Treasury, the Federal Reserve and the IMF that Mexico could not meet payments on some 80 billion dollars of debt (accounts differ on the exact day). Within days came a standstill on principal and bridge financing from the US government and the BIS; within months the same script was running in Brazil, Argentina, Chile and beyond Latin America. By 1982 the nine largest US money-center banks held Latin American debt equal to about 176 per cent of their capital, and total developing-country debt near 290 per cent (FDIC, *History of the Eighties*, 1997). The first Mexican package (November 1982 – March 1983) made Fund money conditional on the banks putting in their share: roughly 5 billion dollars of new money from more than 500 banks alongside about 1.3 billion from the Fund and 2 billion from governments, with some 19 billion of existing obligations rescheduled — the Fund, in its own history's words, bailing the banks *in* rather than out.

*Introduced:* [5.4](lessons/05-04-from-mexico-1982-to-the-brady-plan.md)

### The Baker Plan and the years of waiting

Growth, not debt reduction — until the banks could afford to book a loss.

Peru's García capped debt service at 10 per cent of exports in July 1985. The Baker Plan (Seoul, October 1985) promised roughly 20 billion dollars of new bank lending over three years to fifteen debtors plus about 9 billion from the development banks. Brazil suspended interest to commercial banks on 20 February 1987, the largest debtor defecting. On 19 May 1987 Citicorp added 3 billion dollars to loan-loss reserves and other banks followed, which changed the bargaining. The US International Lending Supervision Act (1983) had already given regulators a formal tool to force reserves against impaired country loans. Mexico's 1988 exchange of loans for bonds backed by US Treasury zeros (the "Aztec bonds") rehearsed the collateral technique with limited take-up. Regional income per head at the end of the 1980s was roughly back where it started — the *década perdida* — with deep cuts in investment and public spending; what caused it is contested, with the 1987 UNICEF study *Adjustment with a Human Face* the best-known critique of the programs' design and defenders arguing the alternative was disorderly default.

*Introduced:* [5.4](lessons/05-04-from-mexico-1982-to-the-brady-plan.md)

### The Brady Plan

From more lending to less debt.

Announced by Treasury Secretary Nicholas Brady on 10 March 1989. The case: debt ratios had not fallen after years of surpluses and slashed imports, and the loans traded at deep discounts; an overhang taxes adjustment; so reducing face value or coupon can *raise* what creditors collect; and since 1987 the banks had provisioned, so a write-down no longer threatened their solvency, while official money could guarantee the residual principal. Mexico's agreement came July 1989 and closed March 1990, covering about 49 billion dollars of bank claims, with each bank choosing a *discount bond* (face cut 35 per cent, floating rate of LIBOR plus thirteen sixteenths of a point), a *par bond* (full face, fixed 6.25 per cent, well below market) or new money. Principal was collateralized by 30-year US Treasury zero-coupon bonds bought with loans from the IMF, the World Bank and Japan, with a rolling guarantee covering roughly a year and a half of interest. Take-up was roughly half discount bonds, somewhat over 40 per cent par bonds, about a tenth new money. Costa Rica, Venezuela, Uruguay, Argentina, Brazil and others followed, roughly 18 countries in all; across all the deals private lenders wrote off on the order of 60 billion dollars, about a third of the debt involved (Federal Reserve History). Both the liquidity and the overhang arguments take repayment as the goal and adjustment as the price; neither is a forgiveness argument.

*Introduced:* [5.4](lessons/05-04-from-mexico-1982-to-the-brady-plan.md)

### The HIPC Initiative

Forgiveness made computable.

Launched by the IMF and World Bank in 1996 and enhanced at the Cologne G8 in June 1999. (1) Debt is unsustainable when its net present value exceeds 150 per cent of annual exports, or, through a fiscal window, 250 per cent of government revenue, open only to countries whose exports exceed 30 per cent of GDP and whose revenue exceeds 15 per cent of GDP; the 1996 thresholds were higher, 200–250 per cent of exports, and lowering them is most of what "enhanced" means. (2) The test applies to what is left after full use of traditional Paris Club terms. (3) Multilateral claims are included — the preferred creditors taking losses on themselves is the genuinely new thing in the design. (4) Relief is staged: at the *decision point* the amount is fixed, interim relief begins and a poverty reduction strategy paper (PRSP) and triggers are agreed; at the floating *completion point*, when the triggers are met, relief becomes irrevocable. (5) Relief was to be additional to aid — a promise later disputed. Somalia became the 37th country to reach completion point in 2023. The IMF and World Bank put the cost of HIPC relief to creditors at about 76 billion dollars and MDRI at about 43 billion, both in end-2017 present-value terms. Because the target is set by exports, a commodity price collapse after the decision point pushes a country back above the line through no act of its own.

*Introduced:* [5.5](lessons/05-05-jubilee-2000-and-hipc.md)

### Jubilee 2000

The political force that made creditors run the computation, including on themselves.

A coalition of churches, aid agencies and unions that borrowed a name from Leviticus 25, collected a petition signed by more than 20 million people, and on 16 May 1998 ringed the G8 summit in Birmingham with a human chain of roughly 70,000. John Paul II's appeal for relief in the approaching Jubilee year gave it a second pulpit; the theology is ceded to `theology-of-debt`. Its argument: these debts will not be paid in full on any schedule anyone can name, so the question is who books the loss and when; debt service is paid out of budgets that also fund clinics and schools; and much of the stock was lent to unaccountable governments by creditors who knew it. Its demand was cancellation of the unpayable debts of the poorest countries *by the year 2000*, on a deadline rather than a case-by-case timetable; the deadline passed with a handful of countries at completion point. Creditors' standing objection was moral hazard — forgive once and you teach every future borrower and lender that debts can be escaped. The actors argued need against moral hazard; the doctrine is ceded to `philosophy-of-debt`. The ancient release the campaign named itself after was periodic, automatic and internal to one society; HIPC was case-by-case, conditional, negotiated between states, and available once.

*Introduced:* [5.5](lessons/05-05-jubilee-2000-and-hipc.md)

### The MDRI

Relief on top of relief, from the institutions that had never written anything down.

Proposed at the Gleneagles G8 in July 2005: on top of HIPC, the IMF, IDA and the African Development Fund cancel 100 per cent of their eligible claims on completion-point countries. Note that "X billion cancelled" can mean face value, present value, debt *service* over some horizon, or the cost booked by creditors, and those differ by large factors; always ask which, and in which year's present-value terms.

*Introduced:* [5.5](lessons/05-05-jubilee-2000-and-hipc.md)

### The Cuban debt of 1898 and odious debt

Sovereignty passed; the debt did not.

Negotiating the Treaty of Paris in 1898, Spain proposed to hand over Cuba together with the "Cuban debt" run up largely to suppress Cuban revolts. The American Commissioners replied (Annex 1 to Protocol No. 4, conference of 11 October 1898): "It is difficult to perceive by what logic an indebtedness contracted for any purpose can be deemed part of the sovereignty of Spain over the Island of Cuba." In the reply of 14 October (Annex to Protocol No. 5) they add that "the thing done in the exercise of sovereignty is not a part of the sovereignty itself; the power to create is not the thing created", and that these "are debts created by the Government of Spain, for its own purposes and through its own agents, in whose creation Cuba had no voice", imposed "upon the people of Cuba without their consent and by force of arms." Spain kept the debt, and the bondholders were left with the debtor they had actually lent to. Alexander Sack systematized *odious debt* in 1927 partly out of episodes like this one (his text is not public domain in English, so 5.5 paraphrases): a debt does not bind a successor state when it was contracted without the consent of the people, was not spent for their benefit, and the creditors knew this when they lent. Reading the doctrine back into 1898 is an anachronism of vocabulary.

*Introduced:* [5.5](lessons/05-05-jubilee-2000-and-hipc.md)

### Iraq, 2004

Where the doctrine was argued loudly and used not at all.

After 2003, American officials and outside lawyers argued publicly that Saddam Hussein's debts were odious in Sack's sense. What happened was a Paris Club negotiation: on 21 November 2004 the creditors cut Iraq's debt by 80 per cent in net present value, in three stages tied to IMF programs — deeper than HIPC's own average, but delivered as a discretionary concession, not as a finding that the debt had never been owed. The doctrine says the loss was always the creditors', so no conditions attach and no precedent can be refused to other debtors; the negotiation says the loss is a concession, so it can be priced, staged, tied to a program and confined to this case.

*Introduced:* [5.5](lessons/05-05-jubilee-2000-and-hipc.md)

### The New Deal mortgage

The long fixed-rate amortizing loan is a policy artifact, not a market invention.

Before the 1930s an American mortgage was short and unamortized: five years was common, half the purchase price a common maximum, and many loans were interest-only with the whole principal falling due at the end, to be refinanced. After 1930 lenders stopped rolling loans over and foreclosure became a mass event. The **Home Owners' Loan Corporation** (1933) bought distressed mortgages from lenders and rewrote them as long, fully amortizing loans, making roughly a million loans in three years, around a tenth of non-farm owner-occupied homes, and standardizing the fifteen-year amortized loan. The **Federal Housing Administration** (1934) insured private loans on similar terms with small down payments and twenty- to thirty-year maturities, and **Fannie Mae** (1938) bought FHA-insured loans so lenders could sell them on. The settlement came with a federal underwriting apparatus that graded neighborhoods, and both HOLC maps and FHA practice treated Black and mixed neighborhoods as bad risks; how far each agency's *own lending* discriminated is itself disputed (Fishback and co-authors, 2022), but the discriminatory framework is not. A secondary market was built into the design from the start.

*Introduced:* [6.1](lessons/06-01-the-american-mortgage-and-2008.md)

### Securitization and the subprime boom

The machinery that carried household losses into banks around the world, and could not be unwound to grant relief.

Fannie was split in 1968 into a shareholder-owned company and Ginnie Mae; Freddie Mac followed in 1970; pooled mortgages became securities. By the 2000s a private-label channel had grown beside the government-sponsored one, pooling loans the agencies would not buy, slicing the cash flows into tranches of different seniority and selling them worldwide. Subprime originations rose from roughly 160 billion dollars in 2001 to roughly 600 billion in 2006, around a fifth of the market, and most of it was securitized. Homeownership peaked near 69 per cent in 2004, national house prices in 2006, and mortgage debt roughly doubled between 2001 and 2007. Why 2009 could not repeat 1933: HOLC dealt with one creditor and bought the loan; a securitized loan sits in a trust whose junior tranches gain from modification while senior holders may do better from foreclosure, administered by a servicer who owns none of it, is paid a fee, and whose contract constrains what it may agree to. HAMP (2009) therefore worked through incentive payments to servicers rather than by purchase, and produced roughly 1.7 million permanent modifications against an announced reach of three to four million homeowners.

*Introduced:* [6.1](lessons/06-01-the-american-mortgage-and-2008.md)

### The rescues of 2008

The chain running backwards, fast.

Rising delinquencies in 2007; Bear Stearns rescued in March 2008 with about 30 billion dollars of its assets financed by the Federal Reserve; Fannie and Freddie into federal conservatorship on 7 September; Lehman Brothers' bankruptcy on 15 September; an 85 billion dollar Fed credit line for AIG the next day; TARP, authorizing up to 700 billion dollars, signed 3 October. A headline rescue number is not a cost: less than the TARP authorization was disbursed, most of the bank money was repaid, and the conservatorship drew roughly 190 billion dollars of Treasury injections while later returning large sums in dividends. The Financial Crisis Inquiry Commission, created by Congress in 2009, reported in January 2011; six of ten members signed the majority report and four dissented in two statements. Its first conclusion — "We conclude this financial crisis was avoidable. The crisis was the result of human action and inaction, not of Mother Nature or computer models gone haywire" — asserts a counterfactual, not a single cause. On the GSEs the majority concluded that "these two entities contributed to the crisis, but were not a primary cause", and that HUD's affordable-housing goals "only contributed marginally" to their participation in those mortgages; Peter Wallison's dissent makes federal housing policy the primary cause. The commission is a source *about* the dispute as well as evidence in it.

*Introduced:* [6.1](lessons/06-01-the-american-mortgage-and-2008.md)

### The euro without a fiscal union

A treaty with a prohibition where it needed a procedure.

Monetary policy union-wide, fiscal policy national, disciplined by the Stability and Growth Pact's reference values of 3 per cent of GDP for the deficit and 60 per cent for the debt; the central bank may not finance governments (Art. 123) and no member assumes another's commitments (Art. 125). Discipline was to come from the bond market plus the Pact, but from 1999 to 2008 Greek and German ten-year yields traded within a fraction of a percentage point of each other — either investors did not believe Art. 125 or they thought the risk negligible, and both readings make market discipline arrive as a shock rather than a signal. Two strains follow. A member cannot devalue, so adjustment runs through wages, prices and output, and therefore through the *nominal* denominator of its own debt ratio. And a member's default is a union-wide banking event, because its bonds sit on banks' balance sheets, including banks in other member states, where the union's rules let them be held against almost no capital — the sovereign–bank doom loop. Art. 125(1) forbids being *liable for* and *assuming* another member's commitments and says nothing about lending, and the Court of Justice leaned on exactly that gap in *Pringle* (Case C-370/12, 2012), holding that the aim is that members "remain subject to the logic of the market when they enter into debt", so assistance is compatible where the borrower stays responsible to its own creditors and the attached conditions push it toward sound budgets.

*Introduced:* [6.2](lessons/06-02-the-eurozone-and-greece.md)

### The Greek programs and the 2012 PSI

The largest sovereign restructuring on record, which barely moved the ratio.

October 2009: a new government revises the 2009 deficit to about 12.7 per cent of GDP against roughly 6 per cent projected earlier (an earlier notification had said 3.7); Eurostat's figure, published November 2010, was 15.4 per cent. 2 May 2010: a first program of about 110 billion euros, roughly 80 billion in bilateral loans from euro-area states and about 30 billion from the IMF at exceptional access far beyond normal quota limits — loans, not assumption. May–June 2010: the EFSM (on Art. 122(2)) and the temporary EFSF created, and the ECB begins buying member bonds in the secondary market. October 2010, Deauville: the German and French leaders say future rescues should involve private creditors taking losses, and peripheral spreads widen. Ireland and Portugal enter programs, November 2010 to May 2011. February–April 2012: the PSI exchange, about 199 billion euros of private bonds, 96.9 per cent of eligible tendered, face value cut 53.5 per cent — 15 per cent of old face in short-term EFSF notes, 31.5 per cent in new long bonds, plus a GDP-linked warrant — with Greek law retrofitting collective action clauses onto domestic-law bonds, and a second program of about 130 billion euros. About 107 billion euros of face value disappeared, and Zettelmeyer, Trebesch and Gulati (2013) put the loss in present value at 59–65 per cent depending on the discount rate, the largest in the modern record. Greece's debt ratio still ended 2012 at about 160 per cent of GDP and was higher two years later: the cut applied only to privately held bonds, the state borrowed tens of billions more to recapitalize the banks just written down, and output fell about a quarter from its 2008 peak. June–August 2015: capital controls, a 5 July referendum rejecting the creditors' terms by about 61 per cent No, and a third program of up to 86 billion euros within weeks. The lasting effect was the creditor list: after 2012 most of Greece's debt was owed to other governments, the ESM and the IMF, on long maturities at low rates.

### Whatever it takes

A central-bank backstop the treaty never wrote.

Draghi in London, 26 July 2012: "Within our mandate, the ECB is ready to do whatever it takes to preserve the euro. And believe me, it will be enough." Outright Monetary Transactions were announced in September–October 2012, conditional on an ESM program, and have never been used; the permanent ESM began work at the same time. Conditionality became the price of protection.

*Introduced:* [6.2](lessons/06-02-the-eurozone-and-greece.md)

### NML Capital v. Argentina

Fifteen years in which a New York courtroom, not a treaty, decided what a holdout could extract.

Argentina's 1994 Fiscal Agency Agreement contained a *pari passu* clause in two sentences; the court called the second the Equal Treatment Provision: "The payment obligations of the Republic under the Securities shall at all times rank at least equally with all its other present and future unsecured and unsubordinated External Indebtedness" (quoted in *NML Capital, Ltd. v. Republic of Argentina*, 699 F.3d 246, 2d Cir. 2012). *(outline; the court's reading, the alternative Argentina offered and the words each relies on are Boss 6(a))* The Second Circuit affirmed in October 2012 and again in August 2013, treating the clause as an ordinary question of New York contract law on a record where practitioners and scholars disagreed about any settled meaning. What the two sentences each do, and which words carry the result, are what the boss problem asks you to read out of the opinion itself.

Sequence: default December 2001 on roughly 81.8 billion dollars of bonds; 2005 exchange with 76 per cent participation, 62.3 billion of par value, and the Lock Law (Law 26,017); 2010 reopening, cumulatively over 91 per cent restructured; February 2012 Griesa's injunction; November 2012 amended injunctions requiring the plaintiffs' roughly 1.33 billion dollars to be paid alongside the exchange bonds; August 2013 affirmance of the formula and its reach to intermediary banks; June 2014 the Supreme Court declines the *pari passu* appeal and, separately, allows worldwide discovery of Argentina's assets; July 2014 the payment is blocked and Argentina defaults on bonds it can pay and wants to pay, the money sitting in a New York bank; settlement delayed because the exchange bonds' RUFO clause would have let every consenting holder claim better holdout terms until 31 December 2014; 2016 the Lock Law repealed, about 4.65 billion dollars paid to the lead holdouts, and a 16.5 billion dollar return to the market. Elliott Associates' 2000 Brussels order freezing Peru's Brady payments, settled for about 58 million dollars, is where the strategy was born.

*Introduced:* [6.3](lessons/06-03-holdouts-and-the-law-of-sovereign-restructuring.md)

### Collective action clauses

Writing the majority rule into the bond, because no court can supply it.

English-law sovereign bonds had carried such clauses for a century; New York-law bonds generally had not until Mexico issued one with CACs in February 2003, after which the practice spread. Greece's Bondholder Act retrofitted them into Greek-law bonds in 2012, and euro-area bonds have had to carry standard CACs since January 2013. In August 2014 the ICMA published model single-limb aggregated CACs and a clarified *pari passu* clause. What they do not reach: the stock of older bonds, judgments already won, and non-bond claims such as bilateral and policy-bank loans.

*Introduced:* [6.3](lessons/06-03-holdouts-and-the-law-of-sovereign-restructuring.md)

### The SDRM

Bankruptcy by treaty, proposed and shelved.

In 2001 the IMF's Anne Krueger proposed a Sovereign Debt Restructuring Mechanism letting a supermajority of creditors bind the rest, with a stay on litigation. It needed an amendment of the IMF's Articles, the United States would not support it, and it was shelved in 2003. The statutory and contractual camps agree that holdouts are a real problem; what they divide on is whether contract terms alone can reach the whole stock of claims.

*Introduced:* [6.3](lessons/06-03-holdouts-and-the-law-of-sovereign-restructuring.md)

### Chinese policy-bank lending

Claims you cannot count, cannot classify, and cannot make wait.

By 2020 the largest bilateral creditor to low-income countries was China, which is not a Paris Club member. Horn, Reinhart and Trebesch (2021) estimated that around half of China's lending to developing countries was missing from IMF and World Bank statistics; AidData's *Banking on the Belt and Road* (2021), working from 13,427 projects worth about 843 billion dollars, put "hidden" exposure at roughly 385 billion. Both are estimates built from project records with different methods, not audited totals. Classification is contested too: China Exim Bank extends concessional export credits, China Development Bank lends at market-linked rates, and both are state-owned, so whether a CDB loan is "official" is the thing being argued about rather than a fact to look up. Gelpern, Horn, Morris, Parks and Trebesch, *How China Lends* (2021), read 100 contracts with 24 governments and found recurring confidentiality obligations, lender-controlled revenue accounts, cross-default clauses, and provisions excluding the debt from collective restructurings — "no Paris Club" clauses. The G20's Debt Service Suspension Initiative (April 2020 to December 2021) deferred roughly 13 billion dollars for about 48 of 73 eligible countries; the Common Framework (November 2020) was built to supply the room.

*Introduced:* [6.4](lessons/06-04-new-creditors.md)

### Hambantota

Three claims the "trap" story runs together.

The port was built in Sri Lanka's south, on a coast its promoters had wanted developed for decades. China Exim Bank lent about 307 million dollars for the first phase under a 2007 buyer's credit, at a rate later fixed at 6.3 per cent — high for official lending, and much argued over. The port ran far below capacity. In 2017 the government leased it for 99 years to China Merchants Port Holdings, which paid about 1.12 billion dollars for an 85 per cent stake in the operating company. Sri Lanka defaulted in 2022, on an external debt spread across bondholders, multilateral lenders, Japan, India and China. Separate the claims: (i) the loan was made knowing it could not be repaid; (ii) default on that loan caused the transfer; (iii) the asset was seized. On (ii) and (iii) Brautigam and Rithmire's point is documentary — the port loan was not in default, and the transaction was a lease sold for cash whose proceeds went to reserves and other obligations, with no creditor foreclosing. Claim (i) turns on what the lender expected in 2007 and is not settled by that. And none of it makes the outcome politically weightless: a strategic deep-water port sits under a 99-year lease to a Chinese state company.

*Introduced:* [6.4](lessons/06-04-new-creditors.md)

### Zambia and the Common Framework

Comparability enforced, and what enforcing it costs in time.

Zambia missed a 42.5-million-dollar coupon in November 2020, the first pandemic-era sovereign default in Africa, and applied to the Common Framework in early 2021. Its Official Creditor Committee was co-chaired by China and France, the first time Beijing sat at the head of such a table. An agreement in principle covering about 6.3 billion dollars of official bilateral claims came in June 2023, with a memorandum of understanding that October. A deal struck with bondholders in October 2023 was then blocked because the official creditors judged it *not comparable* to their own concession; a revised bondholder agreement followed in March 2024. Roughly three and a half years passed between default and settlement. Who conceded more is contested: the campaign group Debt Justice calculated that bondholders would recover about 73 cents per dollar lent against about 55 for official creditors — an estimate from an advocacy organization, not an agreed figure.

*Introduced:* [6.4](lessons/06-04-new-creditors.md)

### Marquette and rate exportation

State usury statutes were never repealed; they were routed around.

*Marquette National Bank of Minneapolis v. First of Omaha Service Corp.*, 439 U.S. 299 (1978). A Nebraska bank mailed BankAmericards to Minnesota residents and charged them Nebraska's rate, above Minnesota's usury limit. Brennan, for a unanimous Court: section 85 of the National Bank Act lets a national bank charge the rate allowed where the bank is *located*; a bank is located in the state named in its organization certificate, where it approves the credit, issues the cards and receives the payments; so the Nebraska rate applies. Any resulting impairment of state usury laws "has always been implicit in the structure of the National Bank Act", and "the protection of state usury laws is an issue of legislative policy, and any plea to alter § 85 to further that end is better addressed to the wisdom of Congress than to the judgment of this Court." The political step the opinion does not take: if the lender's home state sets the price, states can bid for the lending business. South Dakota removed its ceiling in 1980 and Citibank moved its card operation there the next year; Delaware's Financial Center Development Act followed in 1981. Dates on the consumer side: Diners Club charge card 1950; BankAmericard launched in 1958 with an unsolicited mailing to Fresno households; Truth in Lending amended in 1970 to ban unsolicited cards; bankruptcy reform tightening consumer filing in 2005; the CARD Act of 2009–10 imposing fee and repricing limits and minimum-payment disclosure.

*Introduced:* [6.5](lessons/06-05-student-and-consumer-debt.md)

### The student loan and nondischargeability

A debt with a ceiling set by Congress and almost no exit.

Built in four moves. (1) Federal student lending — the National Defense Education Act (1958), then the guaranteed loans of the Higher Education Act (1965), with Sallie Mae created in 1972 to buy and finance them — lends without collateral and without underwriting. (2) The thing bought cannot be repossessed. (3) So enforcement was written into statute: the Education Amendments of 1976 made government-backed student loans nondischargeable absent "undue hardship", entering the Bankruptcy Code as 11 U.S.C. § 523(a)(8); the waiting period after which loans became dischargeable, five years and then seven from 1990, was abolished in 1998; the bar was extended to private education loans in 2005. (4) "Undue hardship" was given a hard reading under *Brunner v. New York State Higher Education Services Corp.* (2d Cir. 1987), proved in a separate adversary proceeding — not impossible, but a lawsuit most debtors never file; guidance issued in 2022 tried to make the process more usable. Federal lending moved entirely to direct loans in 2009–10. Because the exit is closed, relief arrives as a political program rather than a courtroom proceeding: income-driven repayment, targeted cancellations, and in 2022 a general one. Scale, as 6.5 gives it: Americans owe roughly 1.26 trillion dollars on credit cards (Federal Reserve Bank of New York, household debt report, mid-2026) and about 1.7 trillion in federal student loans over some 43 million borrowers (Federal Student Aid portfolio data; the Supreme Court used 1.6 trillion and 43 million in 2023).

*Introduced:* [6.5](lessons/06-05-student-and-consumer-debt.md)

### Biden v. Nebraska

A holding about authority and standing, not about forgiveness.

The Secretary of Education invoked the HEROES Act of 2003, which lets him "waive or modify" provisions of the student-aid statutes as necessary in connection with a national emergency, and cancelled up to 10,000 dollars of federal loans for borrowers with incomes below 125,000 dollars, and up to 20,000 for former Pell Grant recipients. The Court's opinion put the scale at roughly 430 billion dollars of principal, erasing about 20 million borrowers' balances entirely and lowering the median balance of the other 23 million from about 29,400 to 13,600 dollars. In *Biden v. Nebraska*, 600 U.S. 477 (2023), the Court held 6–3 that the Act did not authorize it: Roberts read "modify" as carrying a connotation of increment or limitation, and held that a program of this size and significance required clear congressional authorization. Missouri had standing because the plan would cost MOHELA, a state-created loan servicer, revenue. Kagan's dissent argued the states had no standing at all and that "waive or modify" in an emergency statute means what it says. Subsequent plans have had a similar history; income-driven repayment has been repeatedly revised and litigated, and the SAVE plan adopted in 2023 was being wound down by 2026 in favour of a successor plan enacted by Congress.

*Introduced:* [6.5](lessons/06-05-student-and-consumer-debt.md)

## Historiographical cruxes

Each entry gives the two sides, the premise they divide on, and the evidence that would move it, as the lessons state them. The course takes no side, and neither does this card.

### How general were the ancient cancellations

**Routine clean slates** (Michael Hudson, from essays of the 1990s to *...and forgive them their debts*, 2018): regular acts, especially at accession, with real enforced restorations; Finkelstein (1965), as Hudson reports, found commissions set up to review sales after an edict; the anti-evasion clauses show edicts were enforced and expected. **Limited, discretionary measures** (a more cautious reading found among many Assyriologists; Van De Mieroop has urged care in reading royal proclamations as descriptions of practice; hedged): many attestations are year-names whose content is unknown, frequency and scope varied by king, effect is hard to measure, relief could be partial, local or mostly a remission of the palace's own arrears, and the anti-evasion clauses show evasion was common enough to need repeated prohibition. Both agree the edicts existed, that the Ammi-saduqa text cancels consumption and not commercial debts, and that bondservants of free status were released. **They divide on** whether the surviving proclamations describe practice or royal self-presentation. **The same evidence sits on both sides**, which is the mark of a live crux. **Would move it:** documentary evidence of enforcement — the composition of claims named in edicts and in post-edict lawsuits, dated loan tablets in the years around accessions, and counts of sale or deposit documents later voided as disguised loans.

*Live in:* [1.2](lessons/01-02-debt-bondage-and-the-royal-clean-slate.md), [1.4](lessons/01-04-solons-shaking-off-of-burdens.md)

### Was the Jubilee ever practised

**Utopian legislation** that no community carried out, against **a codification of a real release practice** of the kind the Babylonian edicts attest. Note the asymmetry in the record: the seventh year leaves outside traces — Zedekiah's release and revocation, Nehemiah's pledge, Beth-zur in 1 Maccabees, the Murabba'at clause, the *prosbul* itself — while the Jubilee leaves none outside the law. **Would move it:** a document recording a Jubilee being held, or a contract priced by the harvests left before one.

*Live in:* [1.3](lessons/01-03-release-laws-in-ancient-israel.md)

### What the seisachtheia did

**Debts cancelled outright** (*Const. Ath.* 6, and Plutarch's "most writers"), against **relief short of cancellation** (Androtion, in Plutarch 15.4: interest lowered and the currency revalued, so that a debt fixed in drachmas got lighter without being cancelled — the figures and the arithmetic of the relief are Boss 1). Crossing it is a second dispute about *who the hektemoroi were*: on the **debtor** reading the sixth was interest or instalments on a defaulted loan and the *horoi* were mortgage markers; on the **dependency** reading it was rent or tribute owed by status and the stones marked lordly claims. Each combination makes different trouble — cancellation plus dependency needs "debts" and "sixth-parting" to be two different wrongs both ended by the reform; Androtion plus debtor leaves sixth-parters as debtors on easier terms, hard to square with the poem's claim that Earth was set free. **Would move it:** for the *hektemoroi*, evidence about the nature of the obligation (was it a loan, and what did the stones record); for the cancellation dispute, which words in Plutarch and the *Constitution of Athens* rest on Solon's own poems and which on later reconstruction. (Outline; the full reconstruction of the two accounts is Boss 1.)

*Live in:* [1.4](lessons/01-04-solons-shaking-off-of-burdens.md)

### Did the Lex Poetelia end debt bondage or relabel it

**Ended it:** the 326 principle made goods and not the person the security, the *nexi* were released and *nexum* forbidden. **Relabelled it:** judgment debtors could still be *addicti*, and Gellius reports seeing debtors adjudged and chained five centuries later (XX.1.51). The reading of Livy VIII.28 turns on whether the clause is read broadly, as abolishing all personal execution for debt, or narrowly, as abolishing *nexum* only — and Example 1 of 1.5 shows why it matters, since the Twelve Tables procedure needs no *nexum* contract at all. **Would move it:** evidence on how often personal execution after judgment was actually used, and on what happened to debtors who lost in court after 326.

*Live in:* [1.5](lessons/01-05-nexum-and-the-roman-plebs.md)

### Were the monti founded for cheap credit or against Jewish lenders

**Historian X:** the monti answered a real demand for cheaper credit among the urban poor, and anti-Jewish rhetoric was the idiom of the preachers, not the purpose of the institutions. **Historian Y:** they were instruments of a campaign to drive out Jewish lenders, and cheap credit was the justification. Both can point to documented cases: the monti's own arithmetic forced a fee on them, and the 1475 Trent episode followed Bernardino da Feltre's Lenten preaching. **Would move it:** town records — what the founding deliberations say, whether *condotte* were cancelled or renewed alongside the monte, who the monte actually lent to and on what terms.

*Live in:* [2.2](lessons/02-02-jewish-lenders-and-the-monti-di-pieta.md)

### Was a forced loan a tax or a loan

The same instrument is mostly one or the other depending on a price the council did not set. **Would move it:** market prices for Monte credits over time, the multiplier applied to cash paid, whether interest was actually paid in bad years, and whether holders could and did sell. Keep this separate from the question why cities borrowed more cheaply than kings — that is Boss 2's crux, between an explanation from creditors' control of government (Stasavage's *States of Credit*, 2011: lenders who control the institutions that decide on repayment do not vote to stiff themselves, which compact city-states made feasible and scattered territorial assemblies did not) and one from revenue base and scale (dense, easily pledged customs and excise duties in a small polity). **That one would be moved by** a comparison holding one factor fixed while the other varies.

*Live in:* [2.3](lessons/02-03-the-public-debts-of-city-states.md)

### Did Edward III break the Bardi and Peruzzi

**The traditional account** (Sapori's classic reading): the royal default destroyed the great Florentine companies. **Hunt** (1990; *The Medieval Super-Companies*, 1994): the failures had other principal causes, the companies lacked the capital to lend on Villani's scale, his sums likely include promised interest and rewards, and some recovery came through channels outside the English ledgers. **They divide on** whether the English claims were large enough, and recovered little enough, to be the cause. **Would move it:** company account books showing actual exposure and recoveries, and evidence on the Florentine deposit withdrawals and political crisis of 1342–43.

*Live in:* [2.4](lessons/02-04-lending-to-kings.md)

### Was Philip II a reckless borrower or a sustainable one

**The traditional account** (Sapori, Braudel on Philip's bankers): kings borrowed for war on revenues they could not forecast, stopped paying when war went badly, and lenders who kept lending were reckless or trapped, so great banking houses died of royal debt. **Drelichman and Voth** (2014): the juros were never defaulted, asientos were contingent contracts that priced many "missed payments" in advance, suspensions were liquidity crises settled fast, lenders earned a premium in normal times that more than covered the haircuts, and the Genoese coalition, not any court, did the enforcing. **They divide on** whether profit should be measured over the whole relationship or at the moment of suspension — and on whether the reconstructions' assumptions about discount rates, revenue and output hold. **Would move it:** loan-by-loan realized returns from the contract archives. (Outline; the full comparison is Boss 2.)

*Live in:* [2.4](lessons/02-04-lending-to-kings.md)

### Why did Charles II stop the Exchequer

**A:** fiscal necessity — ordinary revenue could not cover the Orders falling due, the goldsmiths' credit was exhausted, and a fleet had to be fitted out for a war already agreed on. **B:** a political choice — committed by the Treaty of Dover to fight the Dutch alongside France, Charles took the goldsmiths' money rather than recall a Parliament that would have attached conditions to supply. **Would move it:** the Treasury's own accounts of what was due and what was available, and evidence of whether recalling Parliament was considered and rejected, and on what grounds.

*Live in:* [3.1](lessons/03-01-from-the-stop-of-the-exchequer-to-the-bank-of-england.md)

### Did 1688 make Britain creditworthy

| Position | What made Britain creditworthy | When should rates have fallen | Key evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| North and Weingast (1989) | Constitutional rules: parliamentary supremacy, veto players, independent courts | Soon after 1688, then further with experience | Debt growth plus falling rates on government loans, 1690s–1730s |
| Clark (1996) | Secure property long predates 1688; the Revolution changed little for private wealth | No break at 1688 | Returns on land and rent charges show no shift around 1688 |
| Sussman and Yafeh (2006) | Not the rules, at least not soon: war, peace and financial development | Not for decades | Government yields stayed high and volatile for about forty years, moved with war and with Dutch rates, and were no lower than those of less constitutional states |
| Stasavage (2003) | Who governed under the rules: a Whig coalition that included the creditors | When and while the Whigs held power | Borrowing costs rose when the Tories took office in 1710 and fell under Whig control |
| Quinn (2001) | A complication, not a rival cause | — | A goldsmith-banker's private lending rates *rose* in the 1690s as government borrowing crowded out private credit |

The rows are not symmetrical. Clark and Sussman–Yafeh attack the **timing**, and the learning clause is a possible reply to them. Stasavage accepts that politics made the debt credible and disputes the **mechanism**, which the learning clause does not touch: supremacy moved the power to default from the king to Parliament without abolishing it, so what protected creditors was that the majority did not want to default. A defender's replies at full strength: Tory governments did not in fact repudiate, the veto structure made outright default slow and visible and so costly even for a hostile majority, and a party system in which creditors can win power at all is itself a product of 1688. **They divide on** whether "institutions" names the rules alone or the rules plus the coalitions they allow. **Would move it:** for the timing dispute, series of prices and rates — private returns, government yields, Dutch yields — around 1688 and across the following decades, with instruments held comparable (the early-1690s 14 per cent loans were annuity and lottery schemes, not directly comparable with later perpetual loans); for the mechanism dispute, a comparison holding the rules fixed while the governing coalition varies, or the same coalition under different rules. (Outline; the full crux statement is Boss 3.)

*Live in:* [3.2](lessons/03-02-did-1688-make-britain-creditworthy.md)

### Was 1720 mania or rational riding

**Mania** (Mackay 1841; in modern form Kindleberger's *Manias, Panics and Crashes*, 1978): buyers bid shares far above any value the Company's trade or its government annuity could support, carried by the crowd. Dale, Johnson and Tang (2005) compare South Sea shares with linked instruments such as subscription receipts and argue the pricing is hard to reconcile with rational behaviour; Shea (2007) disputes their method. **Rational, or rationally ridden** (Garber, *Famous First Bubbles*, 2000; Temin and Voth, 2004): buyers were betting on a scheme that looked plausible at the time, and Hoare's Bank's trading records show it knew a bubble was under way, bought anyway and profited. **They divide on** whether the marginal buyer expected to sell to someone else before the fall, and could reasonably expect to — not on whether prices were too high, which both accept. **What counts as evidence follows:** anecdotes about foolish buyers fit both readings, since a rider needs fools to sell to; records of identifiable investors show that *some* buyers were riders, not that the market was; price comparisons across linked securities are the most direct test, because a rational market should not price the same claim two ways, which is why the dispute is over method. Note also that the April 1720 debt subscribers handed over their annuities without knowing the terms and received stock only in October: their losses came from the *contract*, whatever their psychology.

*Live in:* [3.3](lessons/03-03-the-south-sea-bubble.md)

### Was Hume wrong, or right about a world that changed

Britain's premise-6 failure is not in doubt: governments were frugal for ninety years, and premise 4 bent as the tax base grew. The live question is what did the work. **Growth made a century of surpluses politically bearable** against **political choice did it — who sat in Parliament, and the creed of sound finance** (Peel, Gladstone), which is what the 2018 decomposition credits when it assigns +180.5 per cent of the fall to the primary balance and −95.6 per cent to the interest–growth gap. **Would move it:** the timing of the surpluses against the timing of growth, since the averages plugged into the identity cannot reproduce the history; and evidence on what made surpluses politically sustainable in the decades when the debt was largest. (Outline; the full comparison is Boss 3.)

### Why did the discharge spread

**Creditors' bargain:** it bought disclosure and a larger, faster dividend, and creditors kept control of it — the 1705 Act's allowance and discharge sat beside a capital felony for concealment and, from 1706, a four-fifths creditor veto. **Moral change:** failure was recast as misfortune in a commercial society (Mann's reading). **Would move it:** evidence from the English and American record on who initiated proceedings and why, what dividends conforming bankrupts produced, and how the arguments for and against each statute were actually made. Note the double edge: the 1800 Act that freed Robert Morris was a creditors' remedy limited to merchants, repealed within three years amid charges that it favoured merchants and speculators.

*Live in:* [4.2](lessons/04-02-debtors-prisons-and-the-invention-of-bankruptcy.md)

### Crop-lien lock-in

**Ransom and Sutch** (*One Kind of Freedom*, 1977): high credit prices reflected merchants' territorial monopoly, and debt plus monopoly credit plus racial exclusion locked farmers into debt and into cotton regardless of formal freedom. **Critics** (Robert Higgs, *Competition and Coercion*, 1977; Gavin Wright, *Old South, New South*, 1986): credit prices reflected risk and cost, tenants moved heavily at year end, landlords competed for labour, and where coercion existed it came from violence and the criminal law rather than from the credit market. **They divide on** whether the furnishing merchant faced competition. **Would move it:** county records — how often tenants changed landlords or merchants, whether liens actually prevented switching, default rates and how long goods were outstanding (which is what the 60 per cent figure depends on). The court cases cannot settle it: they reached statutes and prosecutions, not the everyday settlement of accounts.

*Live in:* [4.3](lessons/04-03-debt-peonage-after-slavery.md)

### How much of Haitian poverty the indemnity explains

**The *Times*'s "The Ransom" and the economists it consulted:** the indemnity and its loans were a major cause of long underdevelopment. **Critics, including some economists of Haiti:** the causes operated before 1825 or after the debt ended — coups and instability, heavy military spending, the breakup of plantations into smallholdings, predatory rulers. **They divide on** the counterfactual: would the money have been invested and left untouched, and would Haiti have grown like its neighbours? **It cuts both ways:** a counterfactual fund would probably have been raided by later rulers, which overstates the cost, but the debt may itself have fed the instability that held growth down, which understates it. **Would move it:** a theory of Haitian growth that both sides accept — which is exactly what is contested.

*Live in:* [4.4](lessons/04-04-haitis-independence-debt.md)

### The causes of 1882 in Egypt

**Bondholders at the centre** (Hopkins) against **strategy and a local political crisis at the centre** (Robinson and Gallagher). The same debt-as-cause against debt-as-pretext question returns for the Ruhr in 1923. A related pair is in 4.5's P3: the **stabilizer** reading, that creditor administration made Egypt's promises believable again (effective rates near 4 per cent after 1885 against roughly 7.5–10 per cent before 1876), against the **wedge** reading, that it transferred fiscal sovereignty and built the machinery a later occupation took over. **Would move it:** archival evidence of what ministers wrote to one another about their aims, set against what they said in public; and, for the stabilizer/wedge pair, comparative evidence from states that accepted control and did not lose sovereignty. (Outline; the full crux statement is Boss 4.)

### Gunboats or reputation

**Supersanctions** (Mitchener and Weidenmier, "Supersanctions and Sovereign Debt Repayment", 2005): between 1870 and 1913 creditor powers answered a number of defaults with military pressure or external fiscal control; afterwards the country's new bonds traded at much lower spreads, estimated default risk fell and time spent in default shrank; countries that gave up fiscal sovereignty for a long period could borrow large sums again; so the threat was an important enforcement mechanism. **Reputation** (Tomz, *Reputation and International Cooperation*, 2007): lenders sort governments by type, learning from conduct in good and bad times; unexcused default was punished through exclusion or dearer credit while defaults matching visible bad luck were forgiven; and the military disputes that coincided with default were mostly about torts against nationals, civil wars and territory, so the correlation is largely spurious. **They divide on what the events were** — enforcement actions, or coincidences the data count as one. Venezuela 1902 codes both ways on the same facts. **Would move it:** the archives, on what ministers said they were doing; bond spreads alone cannot, because both sides accept the price evidence and disagree about the coding. Map onto `economics-of-debt`: reputation resembles Eaton–Gersovitz, sanctions resemble Bulow–Rogoff. (Outline; the full comparison is Boss 4.)

*Live in:* [4.6](lessons/04-06-bondholders-gunboats-and-receiverships.md)

### Could Germany pay

| Position | Could Germany pay | Who ultimately bore it | Leans on |
|---|---|---|---|
| Keynes (1919, 1929) | Not the sums demanded; the transfer, not the budget, is binding | Whoever forced the deflation — German wage earners, then everyone, via the slump | Export elasticities, creditor tariffs, the fixed gold parity |
| Ohlin (1929) | Yes, with modest adjustment | Germans, through a transfer of purchasing power, not a collapse | The income effects of paying and receiving |
| Mantoux (1946) | Yes; the capacity estimates were falsified by what Germany later mobilized for rearmament | Nobody, in the end — the schedule was evaded | Comparison of 1920s payments with 1930s German military output |
| Marks (1978) | Yes; the real obligation was far below the headline, and German governments treated evasion as policy | The Allies, who collected far less than the public figures suggested | The A and B bonds (50 billion gold marks) as the genuine claim, the C bonds largely a device for public opinion; German fiscal and diplomatic conduct |
| Schuker (1988) | The question is mis-set: on net, capital moved *toward* Germany | American lenders, through loans never repaid and mark assets wiped out by the inflation | Net capital flows 1919–31: on his accounting the inflow exceeded reparations by roughly 2 per cent of German national income a year |

Albrecht Ritschl has argued, in a sovereign-debt reading of the period, that the seniority given to the Dawes loan made reparations effectively junior to commercial debt, so Germany's eventual default fell on private lenders rather than Allied governments — a mechanism for Schuker's ledger rather than a rival to it. **The Keynes–Mantoux/Marks crux:** is the disagreement about Germany's economic *capacity* or its political *will*? Evidence from 1924–31 bears on it. **The Schuker crux:** his critics must deny that net capital flows are the right measure of who paid; **would move it** by an accounting decision about what counts — gross transfers against net flows, and whether inflation losses on mark assets belong in the ledger. Note also that "who paid" and "who suffered" are different questions, and that Schuker's ledger and Keynes's account of German deflation can both be true.

### Symmetric adjustment or limited, conditional finance

**Keynes:** persistent surpluses cause world imbalance as much as persistent deficits and should be charged accordingly, and denying a country finance does not produce graceful adjustment but trade and payments restrictions, as the 1930s showed. **The American negotiators:** close-to-automatic financing gives debtors an open line of credit backed largely by one country's exports, which Congress would never appropriate, and credit without conditions invites postponement of adjustment rather than adjustment. **Would move it:** the record of 1945–1975 on whether conditional, limited finance produced adjustment or restriction, and on whether large creditors adjusted at all. Note that the 1944 compromise conceded Keynes's first premise in exactly one clause, which has never been invoked.

*Live in:* [5.3](lessons/05-03-bretton-woods-and-the-lending-boom.md)

### Liquidity or solvency in 1982

**The official strategy, 1982–85** (its best-known public defence is William Cline's, 1983): the shock — a rate spike, a commodity collapse, a sudden stop — was temporary, so the borrowers were solvent and cash-starved; a coordinated non-run beats an individually rational run; adjustment under conditionality restores capacity; given time the debt is repaid at face value and no loss is booked. **The overhang case, 1987–89** (Jeffrey Sachs; Paul Krugman's 1988 formalization): ratios had not fallen after years of surpluses, the loans traded at deep discounts, an overhang taxes adjustment, so writing debt down can raise what creditors collect, and provisioning since 1987 had removed the constraint. **They divide on** whether the shortfall was temporary. **Evidence available at the time** bears on it; the fall in secondary-market prices is not by itself decisive, since a price reflects what creditors expect to collect and not the country's capacity. And note what changed by 1989 was not the debtors' arithmetic but the banks' ability to absorb a loss.

*Live in:* [5.4](lessons/05-04-from-mexico-1982-to-the-brady-plan.md)

### Did HIPC relief raise social spending

**Official reports** treat HIPC and MDRI as having freed budget room that went into poverty-reducing spending; the IMF and World Bank report such expenditure in post-decision-point countries far exceeding debt service after relief, about 7 per cent of GDP against 2 per cent in 2017. **Sceptical researchers** — Depetris Chauvin and Kraay's study of the 2000s is the usual reference — find little robust effect of relief on growth or on the quality of policy. **They divide on** the counterfactual, and on fungibility: money is not labelled. **Would move it:** a research design that identifies the counterfactual spending path, and expenditure data that can distinguish reallocation from addition.

*Live in:* [5.5](lessons/05-05-jubilee-2000-and-hipc.md)

### Subprime or middle class in 2008

| | Extra borrowing came from marginal borrowers | Extra borrowing was spread across the distribution |
|---|---|---|
| **Driver: credit supply loosened** | Mian and Sufi: subprime ZIP codes, securitization, a negative income–credit correlation in 2002–05 | FCIC majority: a general credit bubble, weak regulation, toxic products at every level |
| **Driver: beliefs about house prices** | Wallison's FCIC dissent: federal affordable-housing mandates pushed high-risk loans; his loan counts are contested | Adelino, Schoar and Severino; Albanesi, De Giorgi and Nosal: middle-class and prime borrowers and real-estate investors levering up on expected price growth |

The columns disagree about a **measurement** (who took on the debt, and at what unit — ZIP code or individual borrower); the rows disagree about a **mechanism** (lenders pushing, or borrowers and investors pulling). A position can be wrong on one axis and right on the other, which is why the dispute has outlived a decade of new data. **They divide on** whether the individual-level income measure is trustworthy: Mian and Sufi reply (2017) that incomes reported on applications in high-growth areas were systematically overstated. **Would move it:** income data independent of the loan application — tax records matched to borrowers — and evidence on how much of the growth came from investors holding several mortgages.

### Fiscal crisis or balance-of-payments crisis in the euro area

**A:** states borrowed beyond their means and hid it, and the treaty's discipline failed to bind. **B:** it was a balance-of-payments crisis in a fixed-exchange-rate system — a decade of capital flowing from core to periphery stopped suddenly, and members without their own currency or a lender of last resort could not absorb the reversal. **Would move it:** evidence from 2000–2008 on where the borrowing actually happened, and the crisis countries other than Greece — Ireland and Spain entered 2008 with low debt and budget surpluses, and their crises came from private and bank borrowing, so any explanation that works only through government profligacy explains Greece and little else.

*Live in:* [6.2](lessons/06-02-the-eurozone-and-greece.md)

### Statutory or contractual

Both camps agree holdouts are a real problem. **The SDRM camp** holds that only a statutory mechanism can bind every claim and stay litigation; **the CAC camp** holds that contract terms, once standard, do the same work without a treaty. **They divide on** whether clauses that bind only the bondholders of the issues containing them can reach the whole stock of claims — older bonds, judgments already won, and non-bond claims such as bilateral and policy-bank loans. **Would move it:** the record of restructurings since 2014, including whether aggregated clauses have actually been used and whether unbound claims have blocked deals. (Outline; the full *pari passu* reading is Boss 6.)

*Live in:* [6.3](lessons/06-03-holdouts-and-the-law-of-sovereign-restructuring.md)

### The debt-trap thesis

Two questions run underneath, and they are independent: is the lending *directed* (a state strategy) or *decentralized* (banks, ministries and firms with their own incentives)? And is the pay-off *capture* (assets or political leverage) or an ordinary *workout* problem (getting repaid, badly)?

| | Pay-off is capture / leverage | Pay-off is a workout problem |
|---|---|---|
| **Directed** | Debt-trap diplomacy (Chellaney, 2017): loans extended where repayment is doubtful, because the default is the point; Hambantota is the exhibit | Rescue lending: Beijing acts deliberately, but to protect claims already made — swap lines and emergency loans to distressed borrowers (Horn, Reinhart, Trebesch and Parks, 2023; hedge the details) |
| **Decentralized** | Contract design (Gelpern and co-authors, 2021): no master plan is needed, since confidentiality, revenue accounts and cross-defaults generate creditor advantage on their own | Commercial lending, opacity and coordination failure (Brautigam, 2020; Brautigam and Rithmire, 2021): policy banks lent commercially, sometimes unwisely, and nobody can see the claims or agree on terms |

The cells are not mutually exclusive. **The evidence that would move you differs by cell:** internal decision documents for the top row, contract texts and loan-by-loan terms for the bottom. **Chellaney against Brautigam–Rithmire** accept the same documented facts about Hambantota and divide on whether the loan was made in the expectation that it would not be repaid — which is not a question about the 2017 lease, since no creditor foreclosed. **"Debt trap" is three claims, not one:** designed-to-fail lending, default-triggered transfer, and asset seizure; evidence for one is not evidence for the others.

*Live in:* [6.4](lessons/06-04-new-creditors.md)

### The 1970s bankruptcy bar on student loans

**Supporters** of the new bar said graduates were shedding loans they could have repaid on the eve of well-paid careers. **Opponents** said such filings were rare and that one class of debtor should not lose the fresh start. **They divide on** how common opportunistic filing actually was. **Would move it:** filing data from the years before 1976 — how many recent graduates discharged student loans, at what balances, and what their subsequent earnings were.

## Formulas and arithmetic

Every formula the course uses, what it is for, and a worked instance lifted from a lesson. Figures marked illustrative in a lesson stay illustrative here.

### Simple and compound interest

What a debt grows to. $P$ is the principal, $r$ the annual rate, $t$ the time in years.

$$A_{\text{simple}} = P(1 + rt), \qquad A_{\text{compound}} = P(1+r)^t.$$

*In words:* simple interest adds the same amount each year; compound interest charges interest on the interest. Mesopotamian loans were normally simple.

**Worked (1.1):** 60 shekels at 20 percent for 3 years gives $60(1+0.6)=96$ simple and $60 \times 1.2^3 = 103.68$ compound. 300 *sila* of barley at 33 and a third percent gives $300 \times 2 = 600$ simple and $300 \times (4/3)^3 = 711.11$ compound.

*From* [1.1](lessons/01-01-credit-before-coins.md)

### Doubling time and the rule of 72

How long before a debt is twice what was borrowed. Set $A = 2P$:

$$t_{\text{simple}} = \frac{1}{r}, \qquad t_{\text{compound}} = \frac{\ln 2}{\ln(1+r)} \approx \frac{72}{100r}.$$

**Worked (1.1):** at 20 percent, 5 years simple, $\ln 2/\ln 1.2 = 3.80$ years compound, and 3.60 by the rule of 72 — about 5 percent short. At 33 and a third percent, 3 years simple, 2.41 compound, 2.16 by the rule — about 10 percent short. The approximation works only for low rates, because $\ln(1+r) \approx r$ only for small $r$. **Also used (4.4)** to show why modern estimates of Haiti's loss range so widely: over roughly 195 years a real rate of 1, 2.1 and 3 percent gives about 2.8, 5.9 and 8.3 doublings, or factors of about 7, 57 and 320.

*From* [1.1](lessons/01-01-credit-before-coins.md), [4.4](lessons/04-04-haitis-independence-debt.md)

### Annualizing a short-period return

For comparing a 60-day exchange round trip with a yearly rate. With a return $x$ over a period that fits $k$ times into a year:

$$\text{simple annual} = kx, \qquad \text{compounded annual} = (1+x)^k - 1.$$

**Worked (2.1, rates and route invented for illustration):** deliver 1,000 lire in Genoa at 16.3 sous per lira, giving 16,300 sous in Barcelona after 30 days; buy a return bill at 15.9, giving $16{,}300/15.9 = 1{,}025.16$ lire after another 30 days. That is 2.516 percent in 60 days, about 15.1 percent a year simple and 16.1 percent compounded. If the Barcelona rate had moved to 16.5, the round trip returns 987.88 lire, a loss of 1.2 percent; the break-even Barcelona rate is exactly 16.3.

*From* [2.1](lessons/02-01-commerce-around-the-prohibition.md)

### Current yield

What a buyer of a credit at market price collects each year, as a rate on what he paid.

$$y_{\text{current}} = \frac{C}{P_{\text{market}}}$$

where $C$ is the annual coupon and $P_{\text{market}}$ the price.

**Worked (2.3, illustrative figures):** a Florentine pays 100 lire cash and is credited 300 of face at a nominal 5 percent, so the interest is 15 lire a year — 15 percent on the cash he paid. At a market price of 25 per 100 of face his credit is worth 75, and a buyer who pays 75 collects 15 a year, a current yield of 20 percent, which is the same as $5/25$.

*From* [2.3](lessons/02-03-the-public-debts-of-city-states.md)

### Effective rate on money received

What a loan really costs when the bond sells below face but interest is owed on the face. With coupon rate $c$ and issue price $p$ per unit of face:

$$r_{\text{effective}} = \frac{c}{p}.$$

**Worked (4.5):** Egypt's 1873 loan — 32 million pounds nominal, 7 per cent coupon, issued at 79 — raised $32 \times 0.79 = 25.28$ million, owed $32 \times 0.07 = 2.24$ million a year, and so cost $7/79 = 8.86$ per cent on the money the issue price delivered, before commissions, with the 6.72 million gap between face and proceeds still to be repaid. Across Egypt's nine issues of 1862–73 the coupons came to about 4.86 million pounds a year, 8.7 per cent of issue-price proceeds. **Same trap (4.4):** Haiti's 1825 bonds, 30 million of face at a 6 per cent coupon sold at 80 per cent of par, delivered about 24 million while the coupon cost $0.06 \times 30 = 1.8$ million a year. Yield to maturity is a further step and is higher still, because the discount is also repaid at maturity; it is ceded to `mathematical-finance` 4.1.

*From* [4.5](lessons/04-05-egypt-the-ottomans-and-debt-administration.md), [4.4](lessons/04-04-haitis-independence-debt.md)

### Present value and the annuity factor

What a stream of payments is worth today. With a payment $A$ at the end of each of $n$ years and a discount rate $r$:

$$PV = A \cdot a(n,r), \qquad a(n,r) = \frac{1-(1+r)^{-n}}{r}, \qquad PV_{\text{perpetuity}} = \frac{C}{r}.$$

*In words:* the lump sum today that is worth the same as the stream.

**Worked (4.4):** the 150 million francs of 1825, as five annual instalments of 30 million with the first due at once, is worth $30(1 + 1.06^{-1} + 1.06^{-2} + 1.06^{-3} + 1.06^{-4}) = 30 \times 4.4651 \approx 134.0$ million francs at 6 per cent — spreading the payments takes only about 11 per cent off the headline. **Perpetuity (2.1):** a rent charge of 80 florins a year bought for 1,000 is worth $80/0.10 = 800$ if going yields rise to 10 per cent, a 20 per cent capital loss the buyer cannot escape, because only the seller may redeem. **Perpetuity again (2.4):** settlement juros paying 5 per cent, valued at the 7.14 per cent ordinary-juro yield, are worth $0.05/0.0714 \approx 0.700$ per ducat of face, so a 70 per cent recovery of face is worth about 0.49 of the original claim — a 30 per cent nominal haircut and, on that illustrative valuation, about 51 per cent in present value.

*From* [4.4](lessons/04-04-haitis-independence-debt.md), [2.1](lessons/02-01-commerce-around-the-prohibition.md), [2.4](lessons/02-04-lending-to-kings.md)

### The level payment on an amortizing loan

The payment whose stream, discounted at the loan rate, is worth exactly the amount borrowed.

$$A = P\,\frac{r}{1-(1+r)^{-n}}$$

with $r$ the rate per period and $n$ the number of periods.

**Worked (6.1):** 200,000 dollars at 5 per cent nominal, compounded monthly, so $r = 0.05/12$. Over the HOLC's fifteen years ($n=180$), $A \approx 1{,}581.59$ a month; over thirty years ($n=360$), $A \approx 1{,}073.64$ — a third less for the same loan at the same rate, which is the lever the New Deal pulled. The cost is at the other end: about 386,500 dollars paid in total against about 284,700, and equity built slowly at first. **Same formula, sovereign scale (5.1):** a 1 billion dollar debt funded over 62 years at 3.5 per cent gives $A = 1{,}000 \times 0.035/(1-1.035^{-62}) \approx 39.7$ million a year, about 2.46 billion in total. **And again (6.4)** for an instalment loan whose service is taken out of a pledged export flow.

*From* [6.1](lessons/06-01-the-american-mortgage-and-2008.md), [5.1](lessons/05-01-war-debts-and-reparations.md), [6.4](lessons/06-04-new-creditors.md)

### What stretching a debt forgives

Value the new stream at the rate the *old* claim carried, and the difference is the concession the face value hides.

$$PV = A \cdot a(n,i), \qquad \text{concession} = 1 - \frac{PV}{P}.$$

**Worked (5.1):** the 1 billion dollars above, refunded at 3.5 per cent over 62 years, throws off 39.7 million a year; discounted at the 5 per cent the original demand notes carried, $PV \approx 39.7 \times 19.03 \approx 755$ million. The face value is untouched and about 24 per cent of the debt's value has been forgiven — which is why "the US forgave nothing" and "the US forgave a lot" can both be true. Always ask whether a claim is nominal or present-value, and at what discount rate.

*From* [5.1](lessons/05-01-war-debts-and-reparations.md)

### Nominal and present-value haircuts, and the exit yield

The two numbers a debtor and a creditor quote about the same swap.

$$h_{\text{nominal}} = 1 - \frac{F_{\text{new}}}{F_{\text{old}}}, \qquad h_{PV} = 1 - \frac{PV_{\text{new}}}{F_{\text{old}}}$$

where $F$ is face value and $PV_{\text{new}}$ discounts the new bond's coupons at the **exit yield**, the rate the market applies to the restructured claim.

**Worked (5.4, illustrative):** a Brady *par bond* keeps 100 million dollars of face, 30-year bullet, fixed 6.25 per cent, with principal collateralized by US Treasury zeros and so discounted at 8 per cent while the coupons carry the sovereign's risk at a 12 per cent exit yield. Using $a(30,0.12) = 8.0552$ and $1.08^{-30} = 0.09938$, the bond is worth $6.25 \times 8.0552 + 100 \times 0.09938 \approx 60.28$ million. The nominal haircut is zero; the present-value haircut is about 40 per cent. A *discount bond* with face cut 35 per cent and a market-tracking coupon can be worth more, which reverses the ranking the nominal haircuts give. **Same distinction (6.2):** Greece 2012, a 53.5 per cent face-value cut and a 59–65 per cent present-value loss (Zettelmeyer, Trebesch and Gulati, 2013), with a third number — the change in what the state owed overall — much smaller again. **And (4.5):** the Decree of Muharrem's nominal reduction of $1 - 106/191 = 44.5$ per cent. These are answers to different questions, not competing estimates.

*From* [5.4](lessons/05-04-from-mexico-1982-to-the-brady-plan.md), [6.2](lessons/06-02-the-eurozone-and-greece.md), [4.5](lessons/04-05-egypt-the-ottomans-and-debt-administration.md)

### The debt-ratio identity

The whole of Module 3's arithmetic in one line. $d_t$ is debt over GDP in year $t$, $r$ the interest rate on the debt, $g$ the growth rate of GDP (nominal growth when the debt is nominal), $s$ the primary surplus as a share of GDP.

$$d_{t+1} = \frac{1+r}{1+g}\,d_t - s, \qquad d_{t+1} - d_t = \frac{r-g}{1+g}\,d_t - s.$$

*In words:* interest scales the debt up, growth scales the economy up and so the ratio down, and the surplus pays some of it off; the ratio falls only if the surplus beats the interest–growth gap applied to the existing debt. It is an accounting identity, not a theory of sustainability — that is `economics-of-debt`'s subject.

**Worked (3.4, illustrative):** $d_0 = 1.8$, $r = 4\%$, $g = 3\%$, $s = 1\%$. Interest adds $1.8 \times 0.04/1.03 = 0.0699$; growth removes $1.8 \times 0.03/1.03 = 0.0524$; the surplus removes 0.0100; net $+0.0075$, so $d_1 = 1.8075$. The ratio rises even though the budget is in surplus. Over 20 years the ratio comes out at about 2.18 with no surplus, 1.96 with a 1 per cent surplus, 1.53 with a 3 per cent surplus: when $r > g$, a big debt eats small surpluses.

*From* [3.4](lessons/03-04-humes-prophecy-carrying-a-great-debt.md)

### The surplus that holds a ratio constant

Set $d_{t+1} = d_t = d$ in the identity:

$$s^{*} = d\,\frac{r-g}{1+g}.$$

**Worked (6.2, illustrative, Greek-like):** with $d = 1.30$ and $r = 4.5\%$, nominal growth of $g = 3\%$ needs $s^{*} = 1.30 \times 0.015/1.03 \approx 1.9\%$ of GDP — demanding but ordinary. Nominal GDP *falling* at 5 per cent needs $s^{*} = 1.30 \times 0.095/0.95 = 13.0\%$ of GDP, which no democracy has sustained. The parameter that moved was the sign of $g$, which is why internal devaluation and the debt ratio pull against each other, and why a one-off haircut lowers $d_t$ once while a negative $g$ works on it every year. (Outline for the surplus that holds a debt of twice GDP constant, and the years to halve it, is Boss 3.)

*From* [6.2](lessons/06-02-the-eurozone-and-greece.md), [3.4](lessons/03-04-humes-prophecy-carrying-a-great-debt.md)

### Pricing a loan under cancellation risk

For asking what a scheduled release or a suspension does to the terms a lender offers. If a one-year loan is wiped out with probability $p$ and the safe alternative pays $r_0$, the rate $r$ that leaves the lender indifferent solves

$$(1-p)(1+r) = 1 + r_0.$$

**Worked (1.2, a stylization):** against a 10 per cent safe alternative, a 20 per cent loan is fair when $p = 1 - 1.10/1.20 = 1/12 \approx 8.3$ per cent a year; at $p = 0.10$ the loan would need $1.10/0.90 - 1 \approx 22.2$ per cent, above the conventional 20. What the stylization leaves out: edicts were not random draws, accessions were partly foreseeable, and lenders could exit to commercial lending or disguise the loan. **The same shape (6.3)** prices a holdout's choice between an exchange worth 30 today and a judgment of 150 at year 10 with probability $p$, discounted at 10 per cent.

*From* [1.2](lessons/01-02-debt-bondage-and-the-royal-clean-slate.md), [6.3](lessons/06-03-holdouts-and-the-law-of-sovereign-restructuring.md)

### The HIPC relief calculation

Turning unpayability into arithmetic. With $V$ the present value of debt after traditional relief and $X$ three-year average exports:

$$\text{ratio} = \frac{V}{X}, \qquad \text{target} = 1.5X, \qquad \text{relief} = V - 1.5X, \qquad \text{common reduction factor} = \frac{V - 1.5X}{V}.$$

**Worked (5.5):** Bantara has exports averaging 900 million dollars and a post-traditional-relief debt present value of 2,250 million. The ratio is 250 per cent, above the 150 threshold; the target stock is 1,350 million; relief required is 900 million in present value; the common reduction factor is 40 per cent, which each creditor applies to its own claims. Two features drive the disputes: the relief is a present-value number, so the face amount written off is a different and usually larger figure, and the target is set by exports, so a commodity price collapse after the decision point pushes a country back above the line through no act of its own.

*From* [5.5](lessons/05-05-jubilee-2000-and-hipc.md)

### Minimum payments and payoff time

Why a card balance can be designed never to end, and what a rate ceiling is worth.

- **Minimum as a flat percentage of the balance** (say 2 per cent) at a 2 per cent monthly rate: the payment exactly equals the interest, so the balance never moves.
- **Minimum as interest plus 1 per cent of the balance:** $B_{m+1} = B_m(1+r) - (rB_m + 0.01B_m) = 0.99\,B_m$, so $B_m = B_0 (0.99)^m$ — a fixed proportion each month, and a 5,000-dollar balance first falls below 1,000 after 161 months. Real minimums carry a floor, which is what actually ends the debt.
- **Fixed payment $P$** on balance $B$ at monthly rate $r$:

$$n = -\frac{\ln\!\left(1 - \frac{rB}{P}\right)}{\ln(1+r)}.$$

**Worked (6.5):** 5,000 dollars paid down at 150 dollars a month takes about 47 months and roughly 1,984 dollars of interest at an 18 per cent ceiling, and about 56 months and roughly 3,322 dollars at the 24 per cent an issuer in a state with no ceiling may charge. Nothing about the borrower, the loan or the payment changed — only which state's law the contract points at.

*From* [6.5](lessons/06-05-student-and-consumer-debt.md)

### Loss allocation in a tranched structure

Losses are absorbed from the bottom up: equity first, then mezzanine, then senior. This is what makes a servicer's choice between modification and foreclosure a conflict among holders of the same pool rather than a judgment about the borrower, and it is why no single creditor had standing to grant relief in 2009.

*From* [6.1](lessons/06-01-the-american-mortgage-and-2008.md), [6.3](lessons/06-03-holdouts-and-the-law-of-sovereign-restructuring.md)

### Blocking stakes under two-limb and single-limb clauses

What aggregation buys. With a 75 per cent amendment threshold, a blocking stake is just over 25 per cent of whatever the vote is taken over.

**Worked (6.3):** ten equal series of 1 billion dollars. Series by series, a fund needs about 251 million — 2.5 per cent of the stock — to block one series and force full payment or litigation. With a single limb across all series it needs just over 25 per cent of 10 billion, about 2.5 billion: ten times the capital for the same veto. The same arithmetic is the case for aggregation and the worry about it, since one vote also lets a majority impose terms on a series that voted no — hence the *uniformly applicable* condition.

## Pitfalls

From every lesson's "Watch out", one line each.

### Module 1: Ancient debt

- "Before coins" is not "before money": weighed silver was money for accounting and for many payments, and Graeber's claim is about credit and units of account. *([1.1](lessons/01-01-credit-before-coins.md))*
- A Mesopotamian "interest rate" is not a market price: 20 and 33 and a third percent were conventions fixed by custom and royal law, sticky over centuries. *([1.1](lessons/01-01-credit-before-coins.md))*
- Refuting barter-as-history does not refute Smith: read as conjectural history, I.4 explains why a widely accepted commodity is *useful*, and that logic needs no barter era. *([1.1](lessons/01-01-credit-before-coins.md))*
- The rule of 72 fails at ancient rates: it says 2.16 years at 33 and a third percent where the true compound figure is 2.41. *([1.1](lessons/01-01-credit-before-coins.md))*
- A "clean slate" is not modern debt forgiveness or a bankruptcy discharge: it restored a *prior order*, freed the free-born but not slaves bought from outside, spared commercial creditors, and was royal discretion, not a right a debtor could file for. *([1.2](lessons/01-02-debt-bondage-and-the-royal-clean-slate.md))*
- The Laws of Hammurabi are not a statute book courts applied: many Assyriologists read them as model rulings and royal self-presentation. *([1.2](lessons/01-02-debt-bondage-and-the-royal-clean-slate.md))*
- Debt service was not chattel slavery: §117's family member was time-limited and belonged to a free household, and §118 treats slaves differently. *([1.2](lessons/01-02-debt-bondage-and-the-royal-clean-slate.md))*
- The dates are not firm: say "c." and name the chronology, since other chronologies shift the whole dynasty by decades. *([1.2](lessons/01-02-debt-bondage-and-the-royal-clean-slate.md))*
- The Jubilee does not cancel debts: Leviticus 25 returns land and persons and never mentions loans; the debt release is Deuteronomy's seventh year. *([1.3](lessons/01-03-release-laws-in-ancient-israel.md))*
- "Liberty" in Leviticus 25:10 is not political freedom: *deror* is a legal release of persons and land, like *andurarum*, not a civic right. *([1.3](lessons/01-03-release-laws-in-ancient-israel.md))*
- The release did not cover everyone: the debt release excludes the foreigner (Deut. 15:3), so does the interest ban (23:20), and Leviticus 25:44–46 permits permanent bondmen from surrounding nations. *([1.3](lessons/01-03-release-laws-in-ancient-israel.md))*
- A law in the Pentateuch does not prove a practice: the seventh year has outside evidence, the Jubilee has none, and the two must be treated separately. *([1.3](lessons/01-03-release-laws-in-ancient-israel.md))*
- Coins and mortgages are anachronisms for Solon's Athens: talk of drachmas and minas projects later money back onto him, and the surviving inscribed *horoi* are overwhelmingly fourth-century and later. *([1.4](lessons/01-04-solons-shaking-off-of-burdens.md))*
- The ban did not end slavery or bondage in Athens: it protected *Athenians* from enslavement for debt, in a city that remained slave-owning. *([1.4](lessons/01-04-solons-shaking-off-of-burdens.md))*
- Whether the sixth was paid or kept is not certain: Plutarch says paid, some later lexicographers reportedly took it the other way, and the *Constitution of Athens* says only "the rent thus indicated". *([1.4](lessons/01-04-solons-shaking-off-of-burdens.md))*
- The *seisachtheia* did not redistribute land: both sources say Solon refused, and that the poor were angry about it. *([1.4](lessons/01-04-solons-shaking-off-of-burdens.md))*
- "Debt slavery" misdescribes Rome: a *nexus* or *addictus* kept his citizenship, and sale into slavery had to happen abroad — which is why abolition could be framed as restoring *liberty* to citizens. *([1.5](lessons/01-05-nexum-and-the-roman-plebs.md))*
- Livy is not an eyewitness: he wrote under Augustus, after Catiline and Caesar had made debt the hottest issue in politics. *([1.5](lessons/01-05-nexum-and-the-roman-plebs.md))*
- The *Lex Poetelia* did not end debt enforcement: judgment debtors could still be *addicti*, and Gellius saw them chained five centuries later. *([1.5](lessons/01-05-nexum-and-the-roman-plebs.md))*
- *Tabulae novae* is not a technical term for total cancellation: it covered anything from full cancellation to a partial write-down, so check what each proposal actually did. *([1.5](lessons/01-05-nexum-and-the-roman-plebs.md))*

### Module 2: Credit under the usury ban

- *Usura* is not "excessive interest": in medieval law any gain beyond the principal on a *mutuum* was usury, however small, so a 5 per cent contract is not "not usurious" in thirteenth-century terms. *([2.1](lessons/02-01-commerce-around-the-prohibition.md))*
- "Loophole" is a conclusion, not a description: one reading treats these forms as disguises, another treats their risks as real, and the exchange loss and the personal census are both real arithmetic. *([2.1](lessons/02-01-commerce-around-the-prohibition.md))*
- The bill of exchange was not mainly a credit trick: it moved real money, and the credit rode on a genuine transfer. *([2.1](lessons/02-01-commerce-around-the-prohibition.md))*
- The commenda traveller is not a borrower: he owed nothing back if the cargo sank, so calling the investor his "creditor" imports the very contract the form avoided. *([2.1](lessons/02-01-commerce-around-the-prohibition.md))*
- Do not read "usury" as loan-sharking: Lateran IV's *graves et immoderatas* was aimed at Jewish rates specifically and did not legalize moderate Christian interest. *([2.2](lessons/02-02-jewish-lenders-and-the-monti-di-pieta.md))*
- "Jews were forced into moneylending" is one explanation, not the settled one: exclusion from land and guilds, the literacy account and active recruitment by Italian towns all sit side by side. *([2.2](lessons/02-02-jewish-lenders-and-the-monti-di-pieta.md))*
- Do not let the preachers' voice become the lesson's: their charge that Jewish rates were ruinous is evidence about the preachers, not a measurement. *([2.2](lessons/02-02-jewish-lenders-and-the-monti-di-pieta.md))*
- The Lombards and Cahorsins were Christians: most medieval Jews were not moneylenders, and most medieval credit was Christian. *([2.2](lessons/02-02-jewish-lenders-and-the-monti-di-pieta.md))*
- Monte credits were not government bonds bought by investors: the original holders were assessed, and a secondary market of willing buyers grew on top of a compulsory base. *([2.3](lessons/02-03-the-public-debts-of-city-states.md))*
- "Creditors in government" is not democracy: the mechanism is representation of *merchants*, usually a narrow patriciate, and the same feature excluded most residents from power. *([2.3](lessons/02-03-the-public-debts-of-city-states.md))*
- Five per cent nominal is not 5 per cent cost: with multipliers on face and prices far below par, the rate on cash paid and the yield to a buyer could each be several times the nominal. *([2.3](lessons/02-03-the-public-debts-of-city-states.md))*
- Machiavelli's origin story for San Giorgio is not chronology: the corporation of 1407 consolidated arrangements that were already old. *([2.3](lessons/02-03-the-public-debts-of-city-states.md))*
- A king's "bankruptcy" was no bankruptcy: no court, trustee or discharge existed; Philip issued a decree and then negotiated. *([2.4](lessons/02-04-lending-to-kings.md))*
- A default does not mean lenders lost money: profit is measured over the whole relationship, and a 20 per cent cut can leave a lender ahead. *([2.4](lessons/02-04-lending-to-kings.md))*
- Villani's figures do not settle the Bardi and Peruzzi story: he had been a Peruzzi partner, and Hunt contests the scale, the composition and the recoveries. *([2.4](lessons/02-04-lending-to-kings.md))*
- The 60 per cent debt-to-GDP figure is a modern reconstruction, not a sixteenth-century fact. *([2.4](lessons/02-04-lending-to-kings.md))*

### Module 3: The financial revolutions

- The Bank of England was not a central bank in 1694: a private joint-stock company whose capital was a loan, with no monopoly of note issue in England and no lender-of-last-resort doctrine. *([3.1](lessons/03-01-from-the-stop-of-the-exchequer-to-the-bank-of-england.md))*
- "Dutch finance" was not simply imported with William: Downing's Orders and parliamentary appropriation predate 1688, and Holland's debt was provincial. *([3.1](lessons/03-01-from-the-stop-of-the-exchequer-to-the-bank-of-england.md))*
- Funding is not repayment: a funded debt was designed never to be paid off, only serviced and occasionally converted to a lower rate. *([3.1](lessons/03-01-from-the-stop-of-the-exchequer-to-the-bank-of-england.md))*
- Do not write that the Stop happened because the Crown was broke: the cause is disputed. *([3.1](lessons/03-01-from-the-stop-of-the-exchequer-to-the-bank-of-england.md))*
- "Rates fell after 1688" is not "rates fell because of 1688": the decline spans forty years that include peace after 1713, a growing Bank and secondary market, and falling Dutch rates. *([3.2](lessons/03-02-did-1688-make-britain-creditworthy.md))*
- Watch the 14 per cent: the early-1690s loans were annuity and lottery-style schemes whose yields are not directly comparable with later perpetual loans, so part of the "fall" may be a change in instruments. *([3.2](lessons/03-02-did-1688-make-britain-creditworthy.md))*
- "Constitution" does not mean a written document: the 1689 settlement was built from statutes and practice, and England had no court that could strike down Parliament's acts. *([3.2](lessons/03-02-did-1688-make-britain-creditworthy.md))*
- Stasavage is not saying institutions do not matter: his claim is that the same rules yield different credibility under different coalitions. *([3.2](lessons/03-02-did-1688-make-britain-creditworthy.md))*
- A South Sea price of 1,000 is a price per 100 pounds of nominal stock, not a share count: converted annuitants were paid in *nominal* stock at a stated valuation, so always ask "valued at what price?" *([3.3](lessons/03-03-the-south-sea-bubble.md))*
- The annuitants did not get rich at the peak: the first debt subscribers took stock valued at 375 and were issued it in October, when the price had collapsed. *([3.3](lessons/03-03-the-south-sea-bubble.md))*
- "Ponzi scheme", "bailout" and "irrational exuberance" are modern labels: Velde's Ponzi-like finding is about who paid whom *ex post*, not a claim that the directors planned a fraud. *([3.3](lessons/03-03-the-south-sea-bubble.md))*
- Newton's line about calculating the heavens but not the madness of people is not found in anything he wrote; that he lost money in 1720 is better attested. *([3.3](lessons/03-03-the-south-sea-bubble.md))*
- Hume and Smith did not misjudge a debt-to-GDP ratio: national-income accounting did not exist, they measured the burden by mortgaged taxes and taxable capacity, and the modern series are reconstructions. *([3.4](lessons/03-04-humes-prophecy-carrying-a-great-debt.md))*
- "Britain grew out of its debt" is wrong on the 2018 decomposition: the growth–interest gap worked *against* Britain after 1822 and surpluses did the reducing, with growth keeping the needed surplus a modest share of a rising income. *([3.4](lessons/03-04-humes-prophecy-carrying-a-great-debt.md))*
- Inflation was not the exit: Britain restored gold at the old parity, and reversing the wartime inflation raised the real debt. *([3.4](lessons/03-04-humes-prophecy-carrying-a-great-debt.md))*
- Hume's forecast was not only fiscal: premise 5 is a claim about the constitution, so grading it only on whether Britain defaulted misses half of it. *([3.4](lessons/03-04-humes-prophecy-carrying-a-great-debt.md))*

### Module 4: Debt, nation and empire

- "Vulture fund" is an anachronism for 1790: contemporaries did say "speculators", but mapping them onto modern distressed-debt funds imports a sovereign-bankruptcy setting and a litigation strategy that did not exist. *([4.1](lessons/04-01-hamiltons-assumption-plan.md))*
- "Funding at par" is not "paid in full in present value": the deferred stock and the 3 per cent interest stock cut the claim's value, and what was protected was each holder's *nominal* claim. *([4.1](lessons/04-01-hamiltons-assumption-plan.md))*
- Madison was not proposing a partial default: his plan paid the whole sum and divided it differently. *([4.1](lessons/04-01-hamiltons-assumption-plan.md))*
- The 1840s sequel is not a reversal of 1790: assumption was a founding bargain, and refusing to assume left states responsible for their own borrowing. *([4.1](lessons/04-01-hamiltons-assumption-plan.md))*
- Bankruptcy was not a debtor's right: before 1841 in the US and for most of the period in England it was something a creditor did *to* a trader, with discharge a reward creditors could veto. *([4.2](lessons/04-02-debtors-prisons-and-the-invention-of-bankruptcy.md))*
- Imprisonment for debt was civil, not criminal: no court sentenced a debtor to a term, which is why jails charged him fees and why 1869 could "abolish" it while keeping short committals. *([4.2](lessons/04-02-debtors-prisons-and-the-invention-of-bankruptcy.md))*
- Do not collapse bankruptcy and insolvency: separate tracks for separate classes until 1861 — insolvency relief freed the body, bankruptcy freed the future income. *([4.2](lessons/04-02-debtors-prisons-and-the-invention-of-bankruptcy.md))*
- Modern "debtors' prisons" are a different institution: criminal-justice debts and contempt, not private creditors' writs, and the analogy is contested. *([4.2](lessons/04-02-debtors-prisons-and-the-invention-of-bankruptcy.md))*
- *Bailey* did not end peonage: it voided a presumption, Florida's similar statute was still enforced in the 1940s, and federal prosecutions continued long after. *([4.3](lessons/04-03-debt-peonage-after-slavery.md))*
- "Peonage" is not any heavy debt: legally it is *compelled service* tied to a debt, which is why the lock-in debate is about economics, not law. *([4.3](lessons/04-03-debt-peonage-after-slavery.md))*
- *Bailey* is not a race-discrimination holding: Hughes expressly declined to decide on equal protection grounds, and the case rests on the Thirteenth Amendment and the statute. *([4.3](lessons/04-03-debt-peonage-after-slavery.md))*
- The 60 per cent credit-price figure is not settled: it depends on how long goods were actually outstanding and on default risk, which is what critics contest. *([4.3](lessons/04-03-debt-peonage-after-slavery.md))*
- "Haiti paid for its independence" needs care: Haiti had won independence in 1804, the ordinance *says* it grants independence, and many historians describe the payment as buying recognition. *([4.4](lessons/04-04-haitis-independence-debt.md))*
- "Double debt" does not mean 300 million: the loan financed the indemnity rather than adding to it, and the doubling is in the creditors and the costs. *([4.4](lessons/04-04-haitis-independence-debt.md))*
- Do not read 1826–28 as a modern sovereign restructuring: no Paris Club, no IMF, and no receivership machinery yet. *([4.4](lessons/04-04-haitis-independence-debt.md))*
- The 21–115 billion dollar range is a model-dependent estimate, not a measured loss; the 90 million francs is the hard figure. *([4.4](lessons/04-04-haitis-independence-debt.md))*
- "Odious debt" is an anachronism for Blunt: he argued about justice and Egyptian law in 1907, and Sack systematized the doctrine only in 1927. *([4.5](lessons/04-05-egypt-the-ottomans-and-debt-administration.md))*
- The Caisse was not an early IMF: set up by a debtor ruler's decree, staffed by creditor governments' nominees, serving bondholders, lending nothing; the resemblance is conditionality, not design. *([4.5](lessons/04-05-egypt-the-ottomans-and-debt-administration.md))*
- Both Egyptian witnesses are parties: Cromer ran the system he describes, and Blunt was the nationalists' ally with an antisemitic picture of the Rothschilds — label that as prejudice, not evidence. *([4.5](lessons/04-05-egypt-the-ottomans-and-debt-administration.md))*
- "Ismail spent too much" is not the whole story: the cotton bust after 1865, the Panic of 1873 and contagion from the Ottoman default hit a borrower already exposed. *([4.5](lessons/04-05-egypt-the-ottomans-and-debt-administration.md))*
- The Council of Foreign Bondholders was not British policy: a private association whose leverage came from organization, publicity and listings, lobbying a Foreign Office that usually declined. *([4.6](lessons/04-06-bondholders-gunboats-and-receiverships.md))*
- The Porter Convention did not outlaw debt-collecting force: it restricted it, and force stayed lawful against a debtor that refused arbitration or defied an award. *([4.6](lessons/04-06-bondholders-gunboats-and-receiverships.md))*
- "Supersanctions" are not modern trade embargoes: it is a category the authors built — gunboats plus fiscal control — and contemporaries spoke of intervention, protection and control. *([4.6](lessons/04-06-bondholders-gunboats-and-receiverships.md))*
- Roosevelt did not announce that the US would collect Europeans' debts: disorder and "impotence" are the trigger, and paying obligations appears only as a sign of good conduct. *([4.6](lessons/04-06-bondholders-gunboats-and-receiverships.md))*

### Module 5: Reparations to Jubilee

- "War guilt clause" is not the treaty's language: Article 231 speaks of *responsibility for loss and damage*, and parallel articles appear in the treaties with Germany's allies. *([5.1](lessons/05-01-war-debts-and-reparations.md))*
- The 132 billion is a headline, not the effective demand: the C bonds were contingent, so any figure for what Germany "owed" has to say whether it includes them. *([5.1](lessons/05-01-war-debts-and-reparations.md))*
- "The US forgave nothing" and "the US forgave a lot" can both be true: one is nominal and one present-value, so always ask which, and at what discount rate. *([5.1](lessons/05-01-war-debts-and-reparations.md))*
- Keynes is not a neutral recorder: he resigned from the delegation and wrote as an advocate, with figures estimated for an argument. *([5.1](lessons/05-01-war-debts-and-reparations.md))*
- Arguing about capacity against the headline number argues about the wrong number, on either side. *([5.2](lessons/05-02-could-germany-pay.md))*
- "Who paid" and "who suffered" are different questions: Schuker's ledger of net capital flows does not say German deflation was painless, and Keynes's account of that pain does not answer the ledger. *([5.2](lessons/05-02-could-germany-pay.md))*
- The 1923 hyperinflation was not reparations being paid: at most it was reparations *not* being paid, and it destroyed domestic mark claims rather than transferring resources to the Allies. *([5.2](lessons/05-02-could-germany-pay.md))*
- There was no floating exchange rate and no IMF in 1929: devaluation and an official lender of last resort are post-1944 furniture, which is why the argument took the political form it did. *([5.2](lessons/05-02-could-germany-pay.md))*
- Bretton Woods was not a gold standard: members pegged to the dollar or gold, only foreign official holders could convert dollars into US gold, and par values were adjustable for a "fundamental disequilibrium" the Articles never define. *([5.3](lessons/05-03-bretton-woods-and-the-lending-boom.md))*
- "Conditionality" is not in the 1944 text, and neither are performance criteria or structural adjustment: the Articles say "adequate safeguards", and the machinery was built by board decisions from 1952. *([5.3](lessons/05-03-bretton-woods-and-the-lending-boom.md))*
- The Fund is not the Bank: short-term balance-of-payments credit is not a long-term development loan, and blurring them makes the 1970s look like a failure of the official institutions when the lending that mattered was private. *([5.3](lessons/05-03-bretton-woods-and-the-lending-boom.md))*
- "Petrodollar recycling" does not mean oil exporters lent to oil importers: banks stood in the middle, borrowing short and lending long, and passing the interest-rate risk to the borrower. *([5.3](lessons/05-03-bretton-woods-and-the-lending-boom.md))*
- The par bond was not a gift because the face value was untouched: its nominal haircut is zero and its present-value haircut is not. *([5.4](lessons/05-04-from-mexico-1982-to-the-brady-plan.md))*
- "Bailout" in 1982 does not mean what it meant in 2008: no government injected capital into a US bank, and the support was forbearance, bridge loans to *debtors*, and IMF programs. *([5.4](lessons/05-04-from-mexico-1982-to-the-brady-plan.md))*
- The crisis was not only Latin American: Poland, Yugoslavia, the Philippines, Nigeria and much of sub-Saharan Africa were caught too, and the poorest debtors got nothing from a plan aimed at commercial bank debt. *([5.4](lessons/05-04-from-mexico-1982-to-the-brady-plan.md))*
- "Lost decade" does not settle the argument about adjustment: the outcome is real and its cause is contested, with critics of conditionality and its defenders both on the record. *([5.4](lessons/05-04-from-mexico-1982-to-the-brady-plan.md))*
- HIPC cancelled debts because a ratio was exceeded, not because they were unjust: every step is capacity arithmetic plus conditionality, which is why the two sides could agree on relief and disagree about what it meant. *([5.5](lessons/05-05-jubilee-2000-and-hipc.md))*
- 1898 was not an application of the odious-debt doctrine: Sack's 1927 formulation was built partly out of episodes like it, and the Commissioners argued from the protocol and from who had contracted the debt. *([5.5](lessons/05-05-jubilee-2000-and-hipc.md))*
- Jubilee 2000 was not applying Leviticus 25: the ancient release was periodic, automatic and internal to one society, while HIPC was case-by-case, conditional, negotiated between states and available once. *([5.5](lessons/05-05-jubilee-2000-and-hipc.md))*
- "X billion cancelled" is not one number: face value, present value, debt *service* over some horizon and the cost booked by creditors differ by large factors. *([5.5](lessons/05-05-jubilee-2000-and-hipc.md))*
- The effect of relief on social spending is not documented but contested, partly over the counterfactual and partly over fungibility. *([5.5](lessons/05-05-jubilee-2000-and-hipc.md))*

### Module 6: Debt in the twenty-first century

- "Subprime" does not mean "poor": it classifies the *loan and the credit record*, and a high earner with a bad payment history qualifies. *([6.1](lessons/06-01-the-american-mortgage-and-2008.md))*
- Do not call the pre-1930s balloon mortgage "predatory" or "subprime": it was the ordinary product of its time, and its danger was structural refinancing risk, not a hidden term. *([6.1](lessons/06-01-the-american-mortgage-and-2008.md))*
- A headline rescue number is not a cost: TARP's authorization was not its disbursement, most bank money was repaid, and the GSE conservatorship later returned large sums in dividends. *([6.1](lessons/06-01-the-american-mortgage-and-2008.md))*
- The FCIC is a commission, not a verdict: its majority, its two dissents and the academic literature are three separate bodies of argument. *([6.1](lessons/06-01-the-american-mortgage-and-2008.md))*
- "Bailout" in the euro crisis is doing argumentative work: the programs were repayable conditional loans, and they also financed payments to creditor-country banks, so say which description you are using. *([6.2](lessons/06-02-the-eurozone-and-greece.md))*
- Do not say the no-bailout clause was simply broken: compatibility with Art. 125 was litigated and decided in the funds' favour, so the claim to argue is about the reading. *([6.2](lessons/06-02-the-eurozone-and-greece.md))*
- Three different haircut numbers describe the same swap — face cut, present-value loss, and the change in what the state owed overall — and they answer different questions. *([6.2](lessons/06-02-the-eurozone-and-greece.md))*
- The 1841–42 American precedent is not a clean analogy: the federal government had already assumed state debts once, had its own tariff revenue and currency, and the defaulting states adopted balanced-budget rules themselves. *([6.2](lessons/06-02-the-eurozone-and-greece.md))*
- Greece is not the euro area: Ireland and Spain entered 2008 with low debt and surpluses, so an explanation running only through government profligacy explains Greece and little else. *([6.2](lessons/06-02-the-eurozone-and-greece.md))*
- "No bankruptcy court" does not mean "no law": the binding constraint is *which* law — Greece could rewrite its own-law bonds by statute, and Argentina's Lock Law bought nothing against New York-law bonds. *([6.3](lessons/06-03-holdouts-and-the-law-of-sovereign-restructuring.md))*
- Do not read "ratable payment" back into the clause's history: research on the nineteenth-century origins (Flandreau) argues *pari passu* addressed involuntary subordination, and the court claimed only that no settled meaning had been shown. *([6.3](lessons/06-03-holdouts-and-the-law-of-sovereign-restructuring.md))*
- Argentina's 2014 default was not an inability to pay: the money was in the trustee's hands, which is why the case is about remedies rather than capacity. *([6.3](lessons/06-03-holdouts-and-the-law-of-sovereign-restructuring.md))*
- CACs did not close the problem: they bind holders of the issues that contain them, not older bonds, judgments already won, or non-bond claims. *([6.3](lessons/06-03-holdouts-and-the-law-of-sovereign-restructuring.md))*
- "Debt trap" is three claims, not one: designed-to-fail lending, default-triggered transfer and asset seizure, and evidence for one is not evidence for the others. *([6.4](lessons/06-04-new-creditors.md))*
- The nineteenth-century analogy has an edge and a break: escrow accounts do resemble revenue capture, but the Caisse and the customs receiverships were multilateral administrations installed inside the debtor state and backed by force, so importing the word "receivership" carries the gunboat with it. *([6.4](lessons/06-04-new-creditors.md))*
- "China" is not a creditor: Exim and CDB lend on different terms, and provincial banks, state firms and central-bank swap lines differ again, which is what makes comparability hard to define. *([6.4](lessons/06-04-new-creditors.md))*
- Hidden-debt figures are estimates with methods: say which reconstruction a claim rests on, and treat the totals as contested. *([6.4](lessons/06-04-new-creditors.md))*
- *Marquette* repealed nothing: state ceilings remain on the books and still bind lenders who cannot export, and it was *states* that then dropped their own caps to attract issuers. *([6.5](lessons/06-05-student-and-consumer-debt.md))*
- Modern "usury" is not the medieval prohibition: a rate cap concedes the thing the scholastic doctrine denied, that a loan may yield a return at all. *([6.5](lessons/06-05-student-and-consumer-debt.md))*
- Student loans are not literally nondischargeable: they are dischargeable on a showing of undue hardship, made hard and put behind a separate lawsuit — "impossible" hides where the discretion sits. *([6.5](lessons/06-05-student-and-consumer-debt.md))*
- Do not read *Biden v. Nebraska* as a ruling about forgiveness: it holds on statutory authority and standing, not on whether debts should be cancelled. *([6.5](lessons/06-05-student-and-consumer-debt.md))*

## Conventions

- **One card per course**, covering every lesson. The linter checks that every lesson file is cited somewhere on this card.
- **Intuition first**, same as lessons: a plain-English line before any formula.
- **Headings are anchors.** Renaming a `###` breaks inbound lesson links; the linter catches it, but prefer not to rename.
- **Hedges are load-bearing.** Where a lesson said "about", "contested", "illustrative" or "a modern reconstruction", the card says it too. Never harden a hedged number to make a problem come out cleanly.
- **Verdict-neutral.** The cruxes above are maps, not answers. The card takes no position on forgiveness, austerity, bailouts or Chinese lending, and neither do the lessons.
- **Boss problems stay unsolved here.** Where an entry is marked "(outline; full reconstruction is Boss N)", the card gives the terrain and stops.
- **No prose dollar signs** — write "150 million francs", not the symbol (see CLAUDE.md).
- Length is not capped the way a lesson's is: this is a lookup surface, not a read-through. But every line still has to earn its place.
