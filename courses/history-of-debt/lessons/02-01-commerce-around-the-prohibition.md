# A History of Debt · Lesson 2.1: Commerce around the prohibition

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 2: Credit under the usury ban · Builds on: [1.5 *Nexum* and the Roman plebs](01-05-nexum-and-the-roman-plebs.md) · Unlocks: [2.2 Jewish lenders and the Monti di Pietà](02-02-jewish-lenders-and-the-monti-di-pieta.md)

## Why this matters

Medieval Christian Europe treated lending money at interest as a sin and, in canon law, a crime. It was also where Genoese and Venetian shipping, Florentine banking and the fairs of Champagne and Bruges grew into the densest credit network the West had yet seen. Both facts are true at once. This lesson is about the contracts that made them compatible: partnerships, bills of exchange and rent charges. Each let a person with money be paid for putting it in someone else's hands without making the one contract the Church condemned. Whether the moralists were right to draw the lines where they did is `theology-of-debt`'s question. Here the question is what the contracts were, how the money moved, and where the return hid.

## The idea

The prohibition did not forbid profit, or even profit on money. It forbade one contract: the *mutuum*, a loan of something used up in the using (grain, wine, coin), where the borrower owes back the same quantity. Demand anything beyond the principal (*sors*) *because of the loan*, and that surplus was *usura*.

So the test a merchant's contract had to pass was about **form and risk**, not about whether money earned money. Three families passed it.

- **Partnership.** Don't lend to the ship captain; go into business with him. If the voyage fails, your capital is lost with it. You were an owner, not a creditor.
- **Exchange.** Don't lend florins in Florence; *buy* money payable in Bruges. The price is an exchange rate, and nobody knows what the rate will be when your agent converts back.
- **Rent charge.** Don't lend to a landowner; *buy* a yearly income secured on his vineyard. You own a stream of payments, and you can't demand your lump sum back.

Each moves money from someone who has it to someone who needs it, and each usually paid. What each changes is **who carries which risk, and whether the investor can demand a fixed sum back on a fixed date**.

## Source

Aquinas, *Summa Theologiae* II-II q.78 a.2 ad 5 (English Dominican Province translation, public domain). He first describes the loan:

> He who lends money transfers the ownership of the money to the borrower. Hence the borrower holds the money at his own risk and is bound to pay it all back …

Then the man who entrusts his money to a merchant or craftsman to form a partnership:

> does not transfer the ownership of his money to them, for it remains his, so that at his risk the merchant speculates with it …

and so he may lawfully claim a share of the profits his money earns.

Read it as a source on how contracts were *sorted*, not as doctrine to evaluate. Two variables do all the work: **who owns the money** during the venture, and **at whose risk** it is used. In a loan both pass to the borrower. In a partnership both stay with the investor. Every contract in this lesson can be placed by asking those two questions.

## The argument

The sorting reasoning merchants and their lawyers relied on, reconstructed from how the contracts were drafted and defended. The doctrine behind step 1 is taught in `theology-of-debt`.

1. **Usury is a gain beyond the principal exacted by reason of a *mutuum*.** *In words:* the offence attaches to a particular contract, not to profit as such.
2. **In a *mutuum* ownership and risk pass to the borrower, who must repay in full whatever happens.** *In words:* the lender is owed a certain sum, so anything extra is pay for the loan itself.
3. **In a partnership the investor keeps ownership and bears the risk of loss, so a share of profit is his own money's fruit.** *In words:* if you can lose your capital, your gain is a return on a venture, not a price for lending.
4. **An exchange (*cambium*) is a purchase of one money, payable in one place, for another money payable elsewhere. The rate at which it will convert back is unknown in advance.** *In words:* the buyer's gain is not certain, so it was argued that no loan occurs.
5. **A rent charge (*census*) is a purchase of a right to income from fruitful property, redeemable at the seller's option only.** *In words:* the buyer has bought a thing and cannot demand the price back, so he is not a lender.
6. **Therefore a supplier of capital could be paid if the contract took one of these forms *and the risk it named was real*.** *In words:* the live arguments moved from "may money earn?" to "is this form genuine or a disguise?"

Step 6 is where every later fight happens. It was also where contracts failed. Gregory IX's decretal *Naviganti*, collected in his Decretals of 1234, was read as condemning the old **sea loan**, where the lender took the risk of shipwreck in return for a premium above the principal. A loan with a risk premium was still a loan. Historians commonly connect this to the rise of the commenda for capital and, later, to separate insurance contracts for sea risk; treat the causal link as plausible rather than proven.

## The case

**The partnership (*commenda*).** Genoa and Venice, twelfth century onward. A sedentary investor (the *stans* in Genoese notarial usage) hands capital or goods to a travelling merchant (*tractator*) for one voyage. On return: capital back to the investor, and profit split, customarily **three-quarters to the investor, one-quarter to the traveller**. If the cargo is lost, the investor loses his capital and the traveller loses his labour; the traveller owes nothing more. In the bilateral version (Genoa's *societas maris*; Venice used *colleganza* for both forms) the investor puts up two-thirds, the traveller one-third, and profit is split **half and half**. Historians debate its roots in Byzantine and Islamic partnership forms (the *qirad*). On land, the Florentine *compagnia* was a longer partnership with pooled capital (*corpo*) and unlimited liability. It also took deposits that earned a return "at discretion" (*a discrezione*) rather than a contracted rate.

**The bill of exchange.** Four parties, two cities:

```mermaid
graph LR
    D["Deliverer, in Genoa<br/>pays 1,000 lire now"] -->|"lire"| T["Taker, in Genoa<br/>receives lire, writes the bill"]
    T -->|"bill ordering payment"| P["Payer, in Barcelona<br/>the taker's correspondent"]
    P -->|"16,300 sous after usance"| B["Payee, in Barcelona<br/>the deliverer's agent"]
    D -->|"sends the bill"| B
```

The **deliverer** (*datore*) gives local money to the **taker** (*prenditore*). The taker writes a letter ordering his correspondent, the **payer** (*pagatore*), to pay the deliverer's agent, the **payee** (*beneficiario*), a sum in the other currency after a customary interval, the **usance**. The usance was set by courier time and custom: about two months Florence–Bruges, longer for Venice–London. The deliverer has given credit for the usance *and* bought foreign money at a rate agreed today. The taker has raised cash now against funds abroad.

**The rent charge (*census*).** A buyer pays a lump sum. The seller owes a yearly rent charged on land or a house, which he may redeem by repaying the sum. Northern European towns sold them on their tax revenues, one root of the civic debts in 2.3. Papal bulls of 1425 and 1455 (Martin V, Callixtus III) accepted redeemable rent charges on property.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean: where the interest hides in an exchange round trip).** Rates, route and usance are invented for illustration. In Genoa, bills on Barcelona sell at **16.3 sous per lira**. In Barcelona, bills on Genoa sell at **15.9 sous per lira**. Usance is **30 days** each way.

- Day 0, Genoa: deliver 1,000 lire. The bill pays $1{,}000 \times 16.3 = 16{,}300$ sous in Barcelona.
- Day 30, Barcelona: the agent collects and buys a bill on Genoa at 15.9: $16{,}300 / 15.9 = 1{,}025.16$ lire.
- Day 60, Genoa: collect 1,025.16 lire. Return $= 2.516\%$ in 60 days, or about $15.1\%$ a year simple ($\times 6$), $16.1\%$ compounded ($1.02516^6 - 1$).

No loan was written, and no rate of interest appears anywhere. The return comes from the **gap between the two cities' rates**. De Roover, reading the Medici ledgers (*The Rise and Decline of the Medici Bank*, 1963), argued that such gaps were systematic: in each market the price of foreign money built in a reward for whoever advanced cash there, so round trips usually came back ahead. They did not *always*. Suppose money is plentiful in Barcelona by day 30 and the rate there is 16.5. Then $16{,}300 / 16.5 = 987.88$ lire, a loss of $1.2\%$. The break-even Barcelona rate is exactly 16.3. De Roover also argued that the prohibition steered Italian banking toward exchange and away from discounting bills, which spread only later.

**Example 2 (hard: is a rent charge a loan?).** A buyer pays 1,000 florins for 80 florins a year charged on a vineyard. While it runs, the cash flows match a loan of 1,000 at 8%. What makes it a sale?

- **He cannot call it.** Only the seller may redeem. If the buyer needs cash, he must sell the rent to someone else at the market price. If going yields rise to 10%, a perpetual 80 a year is worth $80/0.10 = 800$ florins, a 20% capital loss. The lender in a *mutuum* never faces that.
- **It rests on the property.** On the standard account, if the vineyard is destroyed the charge lapses.

Now strain it. A **personal** census, secured on the seller's person or general assets rather than a specific property, and **redeemable at the buyer's demand**, has neither feature. It is a loan in all but name. Contemporaries saw exactly this. Later papal rules (Pius V, 1569) insisted on real, fruit-bearing property. The line between sale and loan was fought over case by case, clause by clause. The historian's job is to see which clause carried the risk, not to decide who was right.

## Watch out

- **Anachronism trap: *usura* is not "excessive interest."** In medieval law any gain beyond the principal on a *mutuum* was *usura*, however small. The modern sense of usury as a rate above a legal cap is a later shift. Don't read a 5% contract as "not usurious" in thirteenth-century terms.
- **"Loophole" is a conclusion, not a description.** One reading treats these forms as disguises. Another treats their risks as real and the distinction as substantive. The exchange loss in Example 1 is real arithmetic. So is the personal census in Example 2. Report both readings.
- **The bill of exchange was not mainly a credit trick.** It moved real money: wool payments, papal revenues, army pay. The credit rode on a genuine transfer, which is exactly what made it defensible.
- **The commenda traveller is not a borrower.** He owed back nothing if the cargo sank. Calling the investor his "creditor" imports the very contract the form avoided.

## One-liner

> The ban condemned one contract, not profit on money. So merchants priced capital through partnerships, exchange rates and rent charges, and every later fight was over whether the risk those forms claimed was real.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢) *(Formal.)*** A Genoese investor places 400 lire in a unilateral commenda for an 8-month voyage, on the customary three-quarters/one-quarter split. (a) The goods return 500 lire net of expenses. Compute each partner's share of the profit and the investor's return on capital, over the voyage and annualized (simple). (b) The goods return only 320 lire. What does each partner get, and what is the investor's return? (c) In a bilateral commenda with the same 25% gross profit rate, the investor puts in 400 and the traveller 200, with profit split half and half. Compute each partner's return on his own capital. Explain why the investor's matches (a), and what the traveller's extra return pays for.

**P2 (🟡) *(Exegetical.)*** An invented op-ed:

> Medieval merchants proved that interest bans are paperwork. They simply relabelled loans as partnerships and bills of exchange and collected the same interest as before. Today's regulators should learn that any cap on lending will be evaded the same way.

(a) Name the claim about the medieval contracts that the argument depends on. (b) Give two features of the contracts in this lesson that the claim ignores, each tied to a specific form. (c) Name one historical practice from this lesson that gives the op-ed's claim real support. 150 words or fewer in total.

**P3 (🔴, optional) *(Exegetical.)*** Two historical explanations of why moralists tolerated the bill of exchange, even though its usance was a period of waiting for payment. **Reading A:** they accepted it because the exchange rate made the gain uncertain, so it was a real exchange and not a loan. **Reading B:** they accepted it because merchants, princes and the papacy itself could not do without the exchange network, and the analysis was stretched to fit indispensable practice. Name the single premise the readings divide on. Say what historical comparison would move a defender of either. You may use Aquinas's treatment of credit sales at a raised price (ST II-II q.78 a.2 ad 7). 150 words or fewer.

<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Formal.)*

**Must hit, strict:**
- (a) Profit $= 500 - 400 = 100$. Investor $0.75 \times 100 = 75$; traveller $25$. Investor's return $= 75/400 = 18.75\%$ over 8 months; simple annualized $18.75\% \times 12/8 = 28.125\%$, about $28.1\%$. (Compounded: $1.1875^{1.5} - 1 \approx 29.4\%$; either is fine if labelled.)
- (b) Loss $= 80$. The investor receives the 320 lire; the traveller gets nothing and owes nothing. Investor's return $= -80/400 = -20\%$. The traveller has lost his labour.
- (c) Total capital $600$; at $25\%$ gross the goods return $750$, profit $150$, so $75$ each. Investor: $75/400 = 18.75\%$, the same as (a). Traveller: $75/200 = 37.5\%$.
- Why: $\tfrac{1}{2}$ of the profit on $\tfrac{2}{3}$ of the capital gives the investor $\tfrac{1/2}{2/3} = \tfrac{3}{4}$ of the profit his own money generated, as in the unilateral form. The traveller's 75 = 50 (the $25\%$ his own 200 earned) + 25 (one-quarter of the 100 profit on the investor's 400), which is the payment for his labour.

**Wrong turns:** having the traveller repay part of the loss in (b), which turns the commenda into a loan; computing the traveller's return in (c) as if he were paid only for capital.

**Model answer:** (a) 75 and 25; investor 18.75% in 8 months, ≈28.1% a year simple. (b) Investor gets 320, −20%; traveller gets 0 and owes 0. (c) 75 each; investor 18.75%, traveller 37.5%. The investor's return is the same because half the profit on two-thirds of the capital is three-quarters of his money's profit. The traveller earns 25% on his own 200 (50) plus the customary quarter of the profit on the investor's capital (25) for his labour.

---

**P2** *(Exegetical.)*

**Must hit, strict:**
- (a) The claim that the forms were **economically equivalent** to interest-bearing loans: same fixed, certain return, only the label changed.
- (b) Any two, each tied to a form: the commenda investor **lost his capital** if the venture failed, and his return varied with profit; a round trip on bills **could lose** when the rate at the second place moved (Example 1's 16.5 case); the rent-charge buyer **could not call** his principal and bore market-price and property risk.
- (c) One real support: the personal census redeemable at the buyer's demand, which the lesson calls a loan in all but name; or deposits "at discretion," whose return was not contracted but in practice came to be expected.
- The answer stays exegetical: it names what the op-ed's historical claim gets right and wrong. It does not judge whether rate caps today are wise.

**Wrong turns:** answering whether interest bans are good policy (not asked, and ceded to `philosophy-of-debt`); saying the forms were "totally different" with no concession in (c).

**Model answer:** (a) It assumes the medieval forms paid a fixed, riskless return like a loan, so only the name changed. (b) A commenda investor bore the loss of his capital if the voyage failed, and his share rose and fell with profit. An exchange round trip could lose money if the rate abroad moved against him. (c) Some forms did collapse into loans. A personal census redeemable at the buyer's demand carried neither the property risk nor the lack of a call right, which is why later papal rules required real property.

---

**P3** *(Exegetical.)*

**Must hit, strict:**
- The crux: **whether the moralists' judgments tracked the presence of risk (the contract's structure) independently of how useful or powerful the users were.** A affirms it; B denies that it was the operative cause.
- A comparison that separates the two: cases where **usefulness and risk come apart**. The best in the lesson's materials is the credit sale. It was indispensable to trade, yet Aquinas (ad 7) treats a higher price for waiting as usury, because the delay is itself treated as a loan and no risk is borne. Condemning an indispensable, riskless practice supports A. A pattern of moralists approving riskless arrangements whenever powerful users needed them supports B. Also acceptable: the chronology of toleration relative to papal dependence on banking networks.
- Both readings stated historically, without judging whether the risk argument is sound (`theology-of-debt`, `philosophy-of-debt`).

**Wrong turns:** naming "whether interest is wrong" as the crux (both readings report the same moralists); naming two premises; evidence that doesn't separate risk from usefulness (e.g. "bills were common," which both readings predict).

**Model answer:** A and B agree the bill was tolerated and differ on why. The premise in dispute is that the moralists' line followed the contract's risk structure rather than the practical importance of those using it. A affirms this; B holds that necessity came first and the risk argument followed. The decisive comparison is a practice as indispensable as exchange but without its risk. Credit sales were everywhere in medieval trade, yet Aquinas treated a higher price for waiting as usury, since the delay has the character of a loan. If such useful, riskless practices were consistently condemned, A gains. If the moralists' approvals shifted toward whatever great institutions needed, whether risk was present or not, B gains.

## Flashback

**From Lesson 1.4 (Solon's shaking-off of burdens):** *(Exegetical, then applied.)* Athens, a generation after the reform. An invented lender offers a struggling farmer three arrangements: (i) a loan secured on his field, with a stone planted to mark the claim; (ii) a loan at whatever rate the two of them agree, with no ceiling; (iii) an agreement, written as hire rather than as security, by which the farmer works in the lender's household until the debt is worked off. For each, say whether Solon's measures as the sources report them clearly bar it, clearly permit it, or leave it open, and name the feature that decides. One or two sentences per item.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict:** (i) **Permitted.** The ban was on loans secured on the debtor's *person*; Solon refused to redistribute land and did not forbid lending against it, and later Athenians certainly lent on land. The deciding feature is what is pledged, body or property. (ii) **Permitted.** Nothing in the settlement caps a rate — the law Lysias quotes as ancient (10.18) lets the lender charge what he likes — and a ban on a kind of collateral is not a ban on a price. (iii) **Left open**, and this is the interesting one: nothing here is formally pledged, so the arrangement does not name the body as security, yet its effect is a citizen labouring for a creditor until a debt is cleared. The sources give a rule about what may secure a loan, not about what a debtor may contract to do, so the principle stops short of a verdict. The feature that decides all three is the same: whether the contract makes the person the security.

**Wrong turns:** answering from the *seisachtheia* — the cancellation was a one-time act, so it says nothing about loans made a generation later; inferring an interest cap from the ban on personal security; declaring (iii) barred by citing the *Constitution of Athens* on "loans on the security of the debtor's person," which is precisely the form this arrangement avoids; treating the surviving inscribed *horoi*, overwhelmingly fourth-century, as direct evidence of practice this early.

**Model answer:** (i) Permitted: Solon banned pledging the person, not lending on land, and he refused to touch property. (ii) Permitted: no rate ceiling survives, and the law Lysias calls ancient leaves the rate to the parties. (iii) Open. Formally it pledges nothing, so it slips past a rule written about security; in substance a citizen is working off a debt in a creditor's house, which is what the ban was for. The sources regulate the form of the loan, so they do not settle it.

</details>
