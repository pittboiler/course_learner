# A History of Debt · Lesson 1.3: Release laws in ancient Israel

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 1: Ancient debt: bondage and clean slates · Builds on: [1.2 Debt bondage and the royal clean slate](01-02-debt-bondage-and-the-royal-clean-slate.md) · Unlocks: [1.4 Solon's shaking-off of burdens](01-04-solons-shaking-off-of-burdens.md)

## Why this matters

In [1.2](01-02-debt-bondage-and-the-royal-clean-slate.md) a Babylonian king cancelled debts when he chose to, usually when he took the throne. The laws of the Hebrew Bible do something stranger: they put the release on a **calendar**. Every seventh year debts between Israelites lapse and Hebrew servants go free. Every fiftieth year land goes back to the families that sold it. A release that anyone can see coming is a different institution from one a king proclaims. Creditors can plan around it, and the text itself worries that they will. These are the laws Jubilee 2000 took its name from, and that "modern Jubilee" op-eds still invoke. This lesson reads them as *law* in their Near Eastern setting. What they mean theologically is [`theology-of-debt`](../../theology-of-debt/syllabus.md)'s question.

## The idea

Picture a village of smallholders. A bad harvest comes, a family borrows seed grain, and a second bad year means it cannot repay. Across the ancient Near East the slide went the same way. First the family pledged or sold its land. Then it handed over a son or daughter to work off the debt. Finally the debtor himself went into service. Left alone, this process turns free farmers into tenants and bondservants, and turns a few creditors into large landholders.

Mesopotamian kings interrupted the slide from above, from time to time ([1.2](01-02-debt-bondage-and-the-royal-clean-slate.md)). The biblical codes interrupt it with **time limits written into the obligation itself**:

- a Hebrew servant's term ends after six years;
- a loan to a fellow Israelite cannot be collected past the seventh year;
- a sale of family land is really a lease that runs out at the Jubilee.

Every debt, in effect, has a statutory expiry date. The price of that design is also in the text: if everyone knows when the expiry date falls, who will lend just before it? Centuries later, Hillel's *prosbul* answered that question.

## Source

Deuteronomy 15:2–3 (Douay–Rheims, public domain), the rule for the seventh year:

> He to whom any thing is owing from his friend or neighbour or brother, cannot demand it again, because it is the year of remission of the Lord. Of the foreigner or stranger thou mayst exact it

"Remission" follows the Latin Vulgate (*remissio*). The Hebrew is *shemittah*, "a letting drop." Read the verbs: the creditor "cannot demand," and from the foreigner he "mayst exact." The rule is written as a limit on the creditor's claim, and it has a scope clause. Rabbinic law read *shemittah* as extinguishing the debt. Some modern commentators have read it as suspending collection for the year. The words alone do not settle which.

## The argument

The release laws of three codes, taken together. The dates of composition are disputed and ceded to [`old-testament`](../../old-testament/syllabus.md) 2.2 and 2.6. The Covenant Code (Exodus 21–23) is usually held to be the oldest, Deuteronomy's core is often tied to the late seventh century BC, and the dating of the Holiness Code (Leviticus 17–26) is contested.

1. **Lending to a poor brother is expected, and interest on it is forbidden** (Exodus 22:25; Leviticus 25:35–37; Deuteronomy 23:19–20, which permits interest from a foreigner). *In words:* inside the community a loan is meant as relief, not as an investment.
2. **A Hebrew servant bought by another Israelite serves six years and goes out free in the seventh** (Exodus 21:2). Deuteronomy 15:12–14 extends this to women and requires that the freed servant be sent off with provisions. *In words:* debt service has a term limit. Hammurabi's §117 set three years for family members given over for debt; this law sets six.
3. **In the seventh year a creditor may not demand repayment from a fellow Israelite, though he may from a foreigner** (Deuteronomy 15:1–3). *In words:* a national release date cuts off debts inside the community, and debts owed by outsiders are excluded.
4. **The creditor must not refuse to lend because the release is near** (15:7–10):

   > …thou say in thy heart: The seventh year of remission draweth nigh; and thou turn away thy eyes from thy poor brother, denying to lend him that which he asketh

   *In words:* the law foresees its own side effect, a credit freeze before the release, and forbids it. The only sanction it names is that the poor man may "cry against thee to the Lord."
5. **In the fiftieth year *deror* is proclaimed: every man returns to his holding and to his family** (Leviticus 25:10, 13), and an Israelite who sold himself works "as a hireling" until then (25:39–41). *In words:* a second, longer cycle undoes land sales and bondage that the seven-year rules did not reach.
6. **The land cannot be sold in perpetuity, because it belongs to the Lord; a sale is priced by the harvests left before the Jubilee** (25:14–16, 23). *In words:* what a buyer acquires is the use of the land for a fixed number of years, a lease with a known end.

∴ **No debt can permanently strip an Israelite household of its land or its freedom.** Every claim a creditor holds runs out on a date fixed in advance.

*Deror* is widely treated as cognate with Akkadian *andurarum*, the "release" of the Babylonian edicts. The same word appears in Jeremiah 34:8 for Zedekiah's release of slaves and in Ezekiel 46:17 for a "year of release" when land reverts. The biblical laws took a Near Eastern word for a royal act and attached it to a schedule. Michael Hudson, for one, reads this as taking release out of the king's hands, and Weinfeld's comparative study (*Social Justice in Ancient Israel and in the Ancient Near East*, 1995) is the standard statement of the parallel. Both are paraphrased here.

## Timeline

| When | What | What kind of evidence |
|---|---|---|
| 17th c. BC (middle chronology) | Ammi-saduqa's edict cancels non-commercial debts ([1.2](01-02-debt-bondage-and-the-royal-clean-slate.md)) | Royal decree, at the king's discretion |
| Date disputed | Covenant Code: the six-year limit (Exodus 21:2) | Law |
| Often late 7th c. BC | Deuteronomy 15: the seventh-year release of debts | Law |
| 588–587 BC | During the Babylonian siege, Zedekiah proclaims a release of Hebrew slaves; the owners take them back (Jeremiah 34:8–16) | Narrative of a release made *and revoked* |
| Date disputed | Holiness Code: the Jubilee (Leviticus 25) | Law; no narrative anywhere of a Jubilee being held |
| 5th c. BC | Nehemiah's community pledges to forgo the seventh year's crops and every debt claim (Nehemiah 10:31; Douay 2 Esdras 10:31) | A covenant pledge |
| c. 163 BC | Beth-zur surrenders for lack of food "because it was the seventh year" (1 Maccabees 6:49, 53) | The fallow year observed in wartime |
| Late 1st c. BC to early 1st c. AD | Hillel institutes the *prosbul* (Mishnah Shevi'it 10:3–4) | Legal reform, written down c. AD 200 |
| Usually dated AD 55/56 | A loan note from Wadi Murabba'at (Mur 18), which on the usual reading promises repayment even if a release year intervenes | A private contract that drafts around the release |

The pattern matters for the practice dispute. The seventh year leaves real traces: a pledge, a siege and a contract clause. The Jubilee leaves none outside the law itself. Later rabbinic tradition held that the Jubilee lapsed once the tribes east of the Jordan went into exile (Babylonian Talmud, *Arakhin* 32b).

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean: one family under all three rules).** Take a farmer in year 2 of the seven-year cycle and year 20 of the fifty-year cycle. He borrows grain, cannot repay, and does three things.

- *He gives his son into service to the lender.* The son's six years run from the day he entered service, so he goes free in his seventh year of service, which falls in the next cycle. His clock is personal. It does not reset with the national calendar, which is how most interpreters read Exodus 21 and Deuteronomy 15:12.
- *The loan balance is left unpaid.* The lender may not demand it in the seventh year of the national cycle, five years on. That clock is national, so every creditor's claim stops on the same date.
- *He sells a field to a neighbour.* The price is set by the thirty harvests left before the fiftieth year (Leviticus 25:15–16), and a kinsman may buy it back sooner (25:25). In year 50 the field reverts with no payment.

The three clocks differ in length and in whose calendar they follow. Taken together, they bound the worst outcome of a debt crisis at six years of a child's labour and a generation without the field.

**Example 2 (hard: the *prosbul*).** Deuteronomy 15:9 foresaw that lenders would refuse to lend as the seventh year approached. According to the Mishnah, that is what happened. Shevi'it 10:3 reports that Hillel saw people refraining from lending to one another, in breach of the verse. His remedy rested on a rule stated in the same chapter: debts whose documents have been handed over to a court are not released. The *prosbul* (the name is usually derived from Greek, "before the council") is a declaration the creditor makes before judges, delivering his debts to them so that he may collect whenever he wishes (10:4, paraphrased). The release, read as addressed to the individual creditor's demand, no longer reached the loan. With that, lending near the seventh year became safe again.

Watch what this does to the institution. The release still exists, but any creditor who bothers with the formality has opted out. A rule that capped every debt becomes a default that lenders can waive. The Talmud later asked how Hillel could set aside a biblical law. One answer given was that once the Jubilee had lapsed, the seventh-year release of debts held only by rabbinic enactment (*Gittin* 36a–b). Whether the *prosbul* was fidelity to Deuteronomy 15:9 or evasion of 15:2 is [`theology-of-debt`](../../theology-of-debt/syllabus.md) 1.2's question. What historians can use is that it is **evidence**: nobody invents a device to get around a release that nobody observes. And like the Murabba'at clause, it matches what 1.2 describes for Babylonia, where contracts show lenders anticipating release edicts.

## Watch out

- **You might think the Jubilee cancels debts.** Leviticus 25 returns land and persons. It never mentions cancelling loans. The Douay reader meets "remission" together with Challoner's note, which glosses it as release from debts and bondage. That note is an interpretation, and debt cancellation belongs to Deuteronomy's seventh year.
- **You might think "liberty" in Leviticus 25:10 means political freedom.** The King James rendering, "proclaim liberty throughout all the land," is on the Liberty Bell. *Deror* is a legal release of persons and land from obligations, like *andurarum*. It is not a civic right. **Anachronism trap.**
- **You might think the release covered everyone.** The debt release excludes the foreigner (Deuteronomy 15:3), and so does the interest ban (23:20). Leviticus 25:44–46 permits permanent bondmen taken from the surrounding nations. These are rules for members of the community, much as Ammi-saduqa's edict left commercial loans untouched.
- **You might think a law in the Pentateuch proves a practice.** For the seventh year there is outside evidence. For the Jubilee there is none. Treat each separately.

## One-liner

> Israel's laws put the Near Eastern clean slate on a calendar, a six-year term, a seventh-year release and a fiftieth-year return of land, and the calendar invited the credit freeze that Deuteronomy forbade and Hillel's *prosbul* finally engineered away.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢) *(Exegetical.)*** Leviticus 25:10 (Douay–Rheims), with Challoner's note on "remission":

> …shalt proclaim remission to all the inhabitants of thy land: for it is the year of jubilee. Every man shall return to his possession, and every one shall go back to his former family

> *Note:* a general release and discharge from debts and bondage, and a reinstating of every man in his former possessions

A reader concludes: "So the Jubilee was a general cancellation of debts." (a) Which parts of that conclusion does the verse itself support, and which words decide it? (b) Where does the rest come from, and what would you check to test it? 120 words or fewer.

**P2 (🟡) *(Formal (a)–(b) · Exegetical (c).)*** Stylize Deuteronomy 15. Years of the cycle are numbered 1–7. Nothing can be collected in year 7, and whatever is still owed then is cancelled. Loans are interest-free (Leviticus 25:37). A borrower repays at most 8 measures of grain per year, at the end of each year, and borrows at the start of a year.
(a) A lender advances 40 measures. For loans made at the start of year 1, 5 and 6, how much does he recover, and what share of the loan is cancelled?
(b) Give the largest loan that can be made with no loss for each starting year 1–7, and describe the pattern.
(c) Which words of Deuteronomy 15:9 describe the behaviour your table predicts, and what does Hillel's *prosbul* do to the table? 80 words or fewer for (c).

**P3 (🔴, optional) *(Exegetical.)*** Historian A: the Jubilee was utopian legislation that no community ever carried out. Historian B: it codified a real release practice, of the kind the Babylonian edicts attest. Name the single premise one must deny, and say what kind of document would move each of them. 150 words or fewer.

<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical — strict.)*

**Must hit, strict:**
- (a) The verse supports a *release* proclaimed to everyone in the land and two concrete effects: return "to his possession" (land) and going back "to his former family" (persons in service). No word in the verse names debts or their cancellation. "Remission" is a translation of the Vulgate; the Hebrew is *deror*, a release.
- (b) "Discharge from debts" comes from Challoner's **note**, an interpretation. Test it against the chapter (25:13, 25–28 and 39–41 speak of land and persons; 25:35–37 bans interest but cancels nothing) and against Deuteronomy 15:1–3, where the debt release actually sits, on a seven-year cycle.

**Wrong turns:** treating the note as part of the text; treating the loss of land or freedom *as a result of* debt as proof that debts are cancelled. Returning a pledged field is not cancelling the loan.

**Model answer:** (a) The verse supports a general proclamation ("to all the inhabitants") that restores land ("return to his possession") and persons ("go back to his former family"). It says nothing about debts. (b) The debt claim comes from Challoner's note, not the verse. Checking the rest of Leviticus 25 turns up land redemption, reversion and freed servants, with no cancellation of loans. The debt release is in Deuteronomy 15, on a seven-year cycle. The conclusion runs two laws together.

**P2** *(Formal (a)–(b) · Exegetical (c).)*

**(a)** Installments can be collected only at the end of years $k, k+1, \dots, 6$ for a loan made at the start of year $k$, so there are $7-k$ of them, each at most 8.
- Year 1: 6 installments possible; $5 \times 8 = 40$ repays the loan by the end of year 5. Recovered 40, cancelled 0 (0%).
- Year 5: 2 installments; $2 \times 8 = 16$ recovered; $40 - 16 = 24$ cancelled; $24/40 = 60\%$.
- Year 6: 1 installment; 8 recovered; 32 cancelled; $32/40 = 80\%$.

**(b)** Largest loss-free loan $= 8 \times (7-k)$:

| Start year $k$ | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Max loan | 48 | 40 | 32 | 24 | 16 | 8 | 0 |

Credit shrinks by 8 each year toward the release and reaches zero in year 7. The same poor borrower can get one-sixth as much in year 6 as in year 1. (Exact; no rounding.)

**(c) Must hit, strict:** the words "The seventh year of remission draweth nigh; and thou turn away thy eyes … denying to lend." The *prosbul* hands the debt to a court, which puts it outside the release (Mishnah Shevi'it 10). The table flattens: every year's loss-free loan is limited only by the borrower's total capacity to repay, not by the calendar. The release's protection disappears for those loans.

**Wrong turns:** counting a year-7 installment; reading the *prosbul* as cancelling the release for everyone. It works loan by loan, for creditors who use it.

**Model answer (c):** 15:9 describes exactly this falling column: a lender who sees "the seventh year … draweth nigh" and refuses. The *prosbul* removes the calendar from the lender's calculation. A loan delivered to the court survives the year, so the loss-free limit no longer falls with $k$. Credit is restored, and the borrower loses the release.

---

**P3** *(Exegetical — strict on naming one premise; any defensible formulation passes.)*

**Must hit, strict:**
- The crux: *whether evidence of actual releases (Babylonian edicts, the seventh-year traces, Jeremiah 34) is evidence for a **fixed fifty-year return of land**.* B must affirm that this inference transfers; A denies it, holding that the Jubilee's schedule and scope make it a different institution.
- Evidence for B: a dated land sale or redemption priced by years to the Jubilee (25:15–16), or a record of land reverting. Evidence for A: Second Temple deeds that sell family land outright with no Jubilee clause, together with the absence of any such record while seventh-year traces exist.

**Wrong turns:** naming "whether the Bible is historically reliable" (too broad; both can grant the seventh year was kept); citing the *prosbul* or Mur 18 as evidence for the *Jubilee* (they concern the seventh year).

**Model answer:** Both grant that releases happened in the ancient Near East and that Israel's seventh year left traces. They split on one premise: that such evidence bears on a *scheduled, fifty-year* return of land. B needs it, since the Babylonian edicts and Jeremiah 34 show release as a working institution that the Jubilee codified. A denies it: discretionary releases and a fallow year are not a calendar that unwinds every land sale, and nothing records one being held. B would be moved toward A by Second Temple land deeds that sell outright and ignore the Jubilee. A would be moved toward B by a sale or redemption document priced by years to the Jubilee, or a record of land reverting in one.

## Flashback

**From Lesson 1.1 (Credit before coins):** *(Formal.)* The Louvre school tablet dated the doubling of silver at 20% by interpolating in a straight line between the balances at the two whole years that straddle a doubled principal. Run the scribe's method on a **barley** loan at 33⅓% compounded yearly: name the two whole years that straddle doubling, interpolate, and compare with the exact figure $\ln 2/\ln(4/3)$. (b) At 20% the method gave a little under 3.79 against an exact 3.80. Does it err in the same direction at 33⅓%, and why? Show your arithmetic; one sentence for (b).

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict:** the straddling years are **2 and 3**, not the tablet's 3 and 4 — $(4/3)^2 = 16/9 = 1.7778$ is short of 2 and $(4/3)^3 = 64/27 = 2.3704$ is past it. Interpolation: $2 + \dfrac{2 - 16/9}{64/27 - 16/9} = 2 + \dfrac{2/9}{16/27} = 2 + \dfrac{3}{8} = 2.375$ years. Exact: $\ln 2/\ln(4/3) = 0.6931/0.2877 = 2.4094$, i.e. 2.41 years (2 d.p.). The method **understates** by $2.4094 - 2.375 = 0.034$ years, about 1.4%. (b) Same direction as at 20% (3.7870 against 3.8018, 0.4% short), and for the same reason: $(1+r)^t$ is convex, so the straight chord joining two whole-year balances lies **above** the true curve between them and therefore hits 2 sooner than the debt actually does. The error grows with the rate, because a steeper curve bows further away from its chord.

**Wrong turns:** reusing years 3 and 4 because that is what the tablet did, which puts doubling past year 3 when $(4/3)^3$ already exceeds 2; computing with simple interest, which doubles at exactly 3 years; quoting the rule of 72's 2.16 as the interpolated answer — that is a different approximation, and a far worse one here.

**Model answer:** At 33⅓% the balance passes 2 between years 2 and 3: $16/9 = 1.7778$, then $64/27 = 2.3704$. Linear interpolation needs $2/9$ of the $16/27$ gained over that year, which is $3/8$ of it, so the scribal answer is 2.375 years. The exact value is $\ln 2/\ln(4/3) = 2.4094$, so the method is 0.034 years short, about 1.4%. (b) Yes, short again, as at 20%: compound growth is convex, so a straight line drawn between two whole-year balances runs above the real curve and reaches the doubled principal before the debt does.
