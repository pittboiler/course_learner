# A History of Debt · Lesson 6.1: The American mortgage and 2008

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 6: Debt in the twenty-first century · Builds on: [5.5 Jubilee 2000 and HIPC](05-05-jubilee-2000-and-hipc.md), [4.2 Debtors' prisons and the invention of bankruptcy](04-02-debtors-prisons-and-the-invention-of-bankruptcy.md) · Unlocks: [6.2 The Eurozone and Greece](06-02-the-eurozone-and-greece.md)

## Why this matters

Module 5 was about states owing states. This one opens with households, because in 2008 the debts that broke the system were ordinary mortgages on ordinary houses. The American mortgage is not a natural object: its familiar shape, thirty years, fixed rate, paid down to zero, was built by federal agencies in the 1930s out of a foreclosure crisis, and the machinery built to fund it is what carried the losses of 2006–08 into banks around the world. This lesson traces that chain and then maps the live dispute over who actually did the extra borrowing.

## The idea

Before the 1930s, an American mortgage was a short, unamortized loan. Five years was common, half the purchase price was a common maximum, and many loans paid interest only, with the whole principal falling due at the end. A borrower did not expect to repay that principal; he expected to **refinance** it. That works while lenders are willing to roll the loan over. When they are not, an owner who has missed nothing and can still pay the interest loses the house anyway.

That is what happened after 1930. Lenders stopped rolling loans over, and foreclosure became a mass event. The federal response rebuilt the instrument rather than relieving individual debtors. The **Home Owners' Loan Corporation** (1933) bought distressed mortgages from lenders and rewrote them as long, fully **amortizing** loans: fixed monthly payments, each part interest and part principal, so that the debt extinguishes itself. It made roughly a million loans in three years, around a tenth of non-farm owner-occupied homes, standardizing the fifteen-year amortized loan as it went. The **Federal Housing Administration** (1934) then insured private loans on similar terms, with small down payments and twenty- to thirty-year maturities, and **Fannie Mae** (1938) bought FHA-insured loans so that lenders could sell them on rather than hold them.

Three features of that settlement matter for 2008. First, the long fixed-rate amortizing mortgage is a policy artifact, not a market invention. Second, it came with a federal underwriting apparatus that graded neighborhoods, and both HOLC maps and FHA practice treated Black and mixed neighborhoods as bad risks; how far each agency's *own lending* discriminated is now itself disputed (Fishback and co-authors, 2022), but the discriminatory framework is not. Third, a secondary market was built into the design from the start.

That secondary market grew into the machinery of the boom. Fannie was split in 1968 into a shareholder-owned company and Ginnie Mae, Freddie Mac followed in 1970, and pooled mortgages became securities sold to investors. By the 2000s a second, private channel had grown beside the government-sponsored one: **private-label securitization**, in which investment banks pooled loans the agencies would not buy, sliced the cash flows into tranches of different seniority, and sold them worldwide. Subprime originations rose from roughly 160 billion dollars in 2001 to roughly 600 billion in 2006, around a fifth of the market, and most of it was securitized. Homeownership peaked near 69 percent in 2004; national house prices peaked in 2006; mortgage debt roughly doubled between 2001 and 2007.

Then the chain ran backwards, fast: rising delinquencies in 2007 → Bear Stearns rescued in March 2008, with about 30 billion dollars of its assets financed by the Federal Reserve → Fannie and Freddie into federal conservatorship on 7 September → Lehman Brothers' bankruptcy on 15 September → an 85 billion dollar Fed credit line for AIG the next day → TARP, authorizing up to 700 billion dollars, signed on 3 October.

## Source

The Financial Crisis Inquiry Commission, created by Congress in 2009, reported in January 2011. Six of its ten members signed the majority report; four dissented, in two separate statements. Its first conclusion:

> We conclude this financial crisis was avoidable. The crisis was the result of human action and inaction, not of Mother Nature or computer models gone haywire.

Read what is being claimed. Not that the crisis was *caused* by any one actor, but that it was *avoidable*: the counterfactual is the assertion. The rest of the majority's conclusions assign responsibility across regulators, firms and households. The dissents accept much of the narrative and reject the weighting, and the sharpest of them (Peter Wallison's) makes federal housing policy the primary cause. The commission is therefore a source *about* the dispute as well as evidence in it.

## The argument

**The credit-supply account** (Mian and Sufi, 2009; *House of Debt*, 2014):

1. **Compare ZIP codes within the same county.** ZIP codes with an unusually large share of subprime borrowers in 1996 had unusually fast mortgage-credit growth from 2002 to 2005. *In words:* hold the local economy fixed and the extra lending still shows up where the marginal borrowers lived.
2. **That growth came apart from income.** In those ZIP codes relative income growth was falling while credit grew. The authors note that 2002–05 is the only stretch in their eighteen-year window where the two are negatively correlated. *In words:* lending stopped tracking the thing lending is supposed to track.
3. **The wedge is securitization.** The dissociation is closely correlated with the securitization of subprime loans. *In words:* originators who sold the loan on had less reason to care whether it would be repaid.
4. **So the crisis is a supply event, and its aftermath is a debt-overhang event.** Defaults in 2007 spiked hardest in the same ZIP codes; leveraged households then cut spending sharply, which is how a housing loss became a recession. *In words:* credit was pushed, not pulled, and the losses fell on the people least able to absorb them.

**The middle-class account** (Adelino, Schoar and Severino, 2016):

5. **Measure borrowers, not neighborhoods.** At the individual level, mortgage growth and income growth stayed positively related through the boom. *In words:* a ZIP-code average can rise because richer people moved in or bought second homes there.
6. **Originations rose across the whole distribution**, of income and of credit score, not just at the bottom. *In words:* everyone borrowed more.
7. **And defaults moved up-market.** Middle-income, high-income and prime borrowers sharply increased their *share* of delinquencies after 2007. *In words:* if subprime borrowers were the story, the defaults should have stayed where they started.

Related work sharpens point 6: Albanesi, De Giorgi and Nosal (2022) find credit growth concentrated in mid- and high-score borrowers and emphasize real-estate investors holding several mortgages. Mian and Sufi reply (2017) that incomes reported on applications in high-growth areas were systematically overstated, so the individual-level income measure the middle-class account relies on is contaminated.

## Map of positions

| | Extra borrowing came from marginal borrowers | Extra borrowing was spread across the distribution |
|---|---|---|
| **Driver: credit supply loosened** | Mian–Sufi: subprime ZIP codes, securitization, negative income–credit correlation | FCIC majority: a general credit bubble, weak regulation, toxic products at every level |
| **Driver: beliefs about house prices** | Wallison's FCIC dissent: federal affordable-housing mandates pushed high-risk loans; Wallison's loan counts are contested | Adelino–Schoar–Severino; Albanesi et al.: middle-class and prime borrowers and investors levering up on expected price growth |

The two columns disagree about a measurement (who took on the debt, and measured at what unit). The two rows disagree about a mechanism (were lenders pushing, or were borrowers and investors pulling because they expected prices to rise?). A position can be wrong on one axis and right on the other, which is why the dispute has outlived a decade of new data.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean — what amortization buys).** A 200,000-dollar loan at 5 percent nominal annual interest, monthly compounding, so $i = 0.05/12$. The level payment on a loan of principal $P$ over $n$ months is

$$A = P\,\frac{i}{1-(1+i)^{-n}}.$$

*In words:* the payment whose stream, discounted at the loan rate, is worth exactly the amount borrowed.

Over the HOLC's fifteen years ($n = 180$), $A \approx 1{,}581.59$ a month. Over thirty years ($n = 360$), $A \approx 1{,}073.64$: a third less, for the same loan at the same rate. That is the lever the New Deal pulled. Stretching the term cut the monthly burden enough to bring in buyers who could not have carried a fifteen-year loan, without lowering the rate and without forgiving anything. The cost is at the other end: the thirty-year borrower pays about 386,500 dollars in total, against about 284,700, and builds equity slowly at first.

**Example 2 (hard — why 2009 could not repeat 1933).** HOLC worked by *buying the loan*. It dealt with one creditor, paid it in bonds, and then rewrote the contract with the household as the new owner of the debt.

By 2009 a subprime mortgage was typically inside a securitization trust, its cash flows split among tranches. Modifying the loan helps the junior tranches, which absorb the first losses; foreclosure may serve senior holders better. The trust is administered by a **servicer** who owns none of it, is paid a fee, and whose contract constrains what it may agree to. There is no single creditor with authority to settle, and buying the loan back means buying it out of a structure designed not to be unwound. HAMP (2009) therefore worked through incentive payments to servicers rather than by purchase, and produced roughly 1.7 million permanent modifications, against an announced reach of three to four million homeowners.

Note the structure of the failure. It is the creditor-coordination problem of [4.6](04-06-bondholders-gunboats-and-receiverships.md) and [5.4](05-04-from-mexico-1982-to-the-brady-plan.md), moved into household debt: many dispersed creditors with conflicting claims on the same borrower, and no forum that can bind them. [6.3](06-03-holdouts-and-the-law-of-sovereign-restructuring.md) is the same problem for sovereigns.

## Watch out

- **You might think "subprime" means "poor."** It is a classification of the *loan and the borrower's credit record*, not of income: it covers low credit scores, thin documentation and high loan-to-value ratios, and a high earner with a bad payment history qualifies. Several steps in the dispute above turn on refusing to slide between the two.
- **Don't call the pre-1930s balloon mortgage "predatory" or "subprime."** Those categories belong to a later regulatory vocabulary. The short unamortized loan was the ordinary product of its time, and its danger was structural, refinancing risk, not a hidden term.
- **A headline rescue number is not a cost.** TARP's 700 billion dollars was an authorization; less was disbursed, most of the bank money was repaid, and the conservatorship of Fannie and Freddie drew roughly 190 billion dollars of Treasury injections while later returning large sums in dividends. Nominal-versus-net is the same discipline [5.1](05-01-war-debts-and-reparations.md) applied to war debts.
- **The FCIC is a commission, not a verdict.** Its majority conclusions, its two dissents and the academic literature are three separate bodies of argument, and this course reports all three rather than choosing.

## One-liner

> America built the long amortizing mortgage in 1933 to stop a foreclosure crisis, funded it through a securitization machine that by 2006 no one could unwind, and is still arguing about who did the borrowing that broke it.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢) *(Formal.)*** A 200,000-dollar loan, 6 percent nominal annual rate, monthly compounding. (a) Compute the monthly payment on a 30-year fully amortizing fixed-rate loan, and the balance still owed after five years. (b) What share of the first twelve months' payments is interest? (c) Compute the monthly payment on a five-year interest-only balloon loan on the same principal at the same rate, and state what falls due at maturity. (d) In two sentences, name the risk the balloon puts on the borrower that the amortizing loan does not, and say which 1933 institution was built to absorb it. Round money to the nearest cent and shares to 0.1 percentage point.

**P2 (🟡) *(Exegetical.)*** From the Financial Crisis Inquiry Commission's conclusions (2011), on Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac:

> We conclude that these two entities contributed to the crisis, but were not a primary cause.

and, on the affordable-housing goals the Department of Housing and Urban Development set for them, that the commission determined

> these goals only contributed marginally to Fannie's and Freddie's participation in those mortgages.

(a) Which reading of the GSEs does this passage support — engine of the credit expansion, or follower of it — and which words decide it? (b) A dissenting commissioner held that federal housing policy was the crisis's primary cause. Name the one factual question the majority and the dissent must answer differently for both to be arguing about the same evidence. 150 words total.

**P3 (🔴, optional) *(Exegetical.)*** Mian and Sufi (2009) conclude that mortgage credit grew fastest in subprime ZIP codes while relative income there fell. Adelino, Schoar and Severino (2016) conclude that mortgage growth tracked income positively at the individual borrower level and that defaults shifted toward middle-income and prime borrowers. Name the single premise one side must deny, and say what evidence would move it. 150 words or fewer.

**P1** *(Formal.)*

Monthly rate $i = 0.06/12 = 0.005$.

(a) $n = 360$. $(1.005)^{-360} \approx 0.166042$, so

$$A = 200{,}000 \times \frac{0.005}{1-0.166042} \approx 200{,}000 \times 0.0059955 \approx \mathbf{1{,}199.10}$$

per month. Balance after $k = 60$ payments, by the standard forward recursion $B_k = P(1+i)^k - A\frac{(1+i)^k-1}{i}$: $(1.005)^{60} \approx 1.348850$, so $B_{60} \approx 269{,}770 - 1{,}199.10 \times 69.770 \approx \mathbf{186{,}108.71}$. After five years of payments about **6.9 percent** of the principal has been repaid.

(b) Twelve payments total $12 \times 1{,}199.10 = 14{,}389.21$. Summing the monthly interest ($B_{k-1} \times 0.005$) over the first year gives about $11{,}933.19$, so interest is $11{,}933.19 / 14{,}389.21 \approx \mathbf{82.9\ \text{percent}}$ of the first year's payments.

(c) Interest only: $200{,}000 \times 0.005 = \mathbf{1{,}000.00}$ a month, and the **entire 200,000 dollars** of principal falls due at the end of year five.

(d) The balloon borrower must refinance the whole principal on a fixed date, so he bears refinancing risk: if credit tightens or the appraisal falls, he loses the house though he has missed no payment. The HOLC (1933) was built to absorb exactly that, buying such loans and rewriting them as long amortizing ones.

**Wrong turns:** using an annual rate of 6 percent with 30 periods instead of a monthly rate with 360; reading the balloon's lower payment (1,000.00 against 1,199.10) as the cheaper loan, when the missing 199.10 is precisely the principal the amortizing borrower is retiring.

---

**P2** *(Exegetical — strict.)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** follower, not engine. The deciding words are "contributed … but were not a primary cause" and "only contributed marginally" — both concede participation while denying causal primacy, and "marginally" attaches specifically to the HUD goals rather than to the GSEs' conduct generally. A complete answer notes that the passage rejects the *policy* channel (the goals), not GSE risk-taking as such; the majority elsewhere faults their business model and their late move into riskier mortgages.

**Must hit, strict (b):** the classification question — how many outstanding loans counted as high-risk (subprime or Alt-A), and how many of those the government-sponsored enterprises and other federal agencies held or guaranteed. The dissent's count of high-risk government-held loans is far larger than the majority's because it uses looser criteria for "high-risk," so the two sides are assigning shares of different totals.

**Wrong turns:** answering (b) with "whether government policy caused the crisis," which restates the disagreement instead of locating it; treating the passage as a claim that the GSEs lost no money (it says they contributed, and their losses were large).

**Model answer:** (a) Follower. "Contributed to the crisis, but were not a primary cause" concedes participation while denying primacy, and "only contributed marginally" restricts the HUD goals in particular; the majority is rejecting a policy channel, not GSE risk-taking in general. (b) They must answer differently how many outstanding mortgages counted as subprime or otherwise high-risk, and what share of those the GSEs and federal agencies held or guaranteed. The dissent's much larger count rests on broader criteria for classifying a loan as high-risk, so until that measurement is settled the two sides are dividing different totals and cannot be compared.

---

**P3** *(Exegetical — strict on naming one premise; any defensible formulation passes.)*

**Must hit, strict:**
- The crux is a **measurement premise**: that the units and the income data used are informative about who took on the debt — specifically, that a ZIP code's subprime share and its income trend identify the borrowers doing the extra borrowing (Mian–Sufi affirm; Adelino–Schoar–Severino deny, holding that individual-level data reverse the sign), or equivalently that reported applicant income is a valid measure of borrower income (Adelino–Schoar–Severino rely on it; Mian and Sufi's 2017 reply denies it, arguing incomes were overstated where credit grew fastest).
- Both sides can agree on the aggregates: credit grew, prices rose, defaults spiked. Naming "did credit expand?" or "was there a bubble?" misses the crux.
- Evidence that would move it: loan-level data linking borrowers' *verified* incomes (tax records, for example) to their mortgages; whether the extra loans in high-growth ZIP codes went to incumbent residents or to in-movers and investors; whether defaults are measured as a share of all defaults or as a rate within each group, since the two questions can have opposite answers.

**Wrong turns:** naming securitization as the crux — both accounts allow a role for it; declaring one side right rather than locating the premise; treating "share of defaults" and "default rate" as interchangeable.

**Model answer:** The premise in dispute is that the data used identify who actually borrowed. Mian and Sufi affirm that a ZIP code's subprime share and its income path track the marginal borrower; Adelino, Schoar and Severino deny it, holding that individual-level data show mortgage growth rising with income throughout, and that the aggregate pattern reflects composition. Mian and Sufi's reply pushes the same question onto their opponents' measure: incomes stated on applications were systematically overstated where credit grew fastest. What would move it is loan-level evidence on borrowers' verified incomes, matched to their loans, distinguishing incumbent residents from in-movers and investors, and reporting default rates within income groups rather than shares of total defaults.

## Flashback

**From Lesson 5.4 (From Mexico 1982 to the Brady Plan):** A bank holds 100 million dollars of face value in loans to a Brady-plan country and takes the **discount bond**: face cut 35 per cent to 65 million, 30-year bullet, floating coupon that tracks the market and here pays 12 per cent on the reduced face. Principal is collateralized by US Treasury zeros, so discount it at 8 per cent; discount the coupons at the exit yield of 12 per cent. Use $\frac{1-(1.12)^{-30}}{0.12} = 8.0552$ and $(1.08)^{-30} = 0.09938$. A par bond on the same claim is worth 60.28 million. All figures illustrative. (a) Nominal and present-value haircut on the discount bond. (b) Which option does the bank prefer, and what happens to the ranking the nominal haircuts give? (c) One sentence: what unobserved number decides (b)?

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):** nominal haircut **35 per cent** — face falls from 100 to 65. Coupons: $0.12 \times 65 = 7.8$ million a year, so $7.8 \times 8.0552 = 62.830$ million. Principal: $65 \times 0.09938 = 6.460$ million. $PV = 62.830 + 6.460 = 69.29$ million (2 dp), so the present-value haircut is $1 - 69.29/100 = \mathbf{30.7\%}$ (1 dp).

**Must hit, strict (b):** the bank prefers the **discount bond**, 69.29 against 60.28. The ranking inverts. On face, the par bond costs the bank nothing and the discount bond 35 per cent; in present value the discount bond costs 30.7 per cent and the par bond 39.7 per cent. Thirty years of a 6.25 per cent coupon when the market wants 12 is worth less than the 35 per cent of principal the other option surrenders.

**Must hit, strict (c):** the **exit yield** — the rate at which the market discounts the sovereign's coupons, which nobody observes directly at the moment of the deal. It is the whole ranking: at a lower yield the par bond's fixed coupon is worth more, and the two options cross near 10.2 per cent here (at 10 per cent the par bond is worth about 68.9 and the discount bond about 67.7).

**Wrong turns:** discounting the collateralized principal at 12 per cent, which throws away what the Treasury zeros bought; measuring the haircut against the 65 of new face rather than the 100 the bank came in with; assuming a 35 per cent cut in face must be the heavier loss; treating the floating coupon as if it were fixed at 12 per cent forever — here it is priced at the discount rate by construction, which is why the coupon leg is worth almost its own face.

**Model answer:** (a) Nominal 35 per cent. $7.8 \times 8.0552 = 62.83$, plus $65 \times 0.09938 = 6.46$, giving 69.29 million and a present-value haircut of 30.7 per cent. (b) The bank prefers the discount bond, 69.29 against the par bond's 60.28 — so the nominal ranking reverses: zero face loss on the par bond conceals a 39.7 per cent loss in value, while a 35 per cent face cut costs only 30.7 per cent. (c) The exit yield: it decides how much the par bond's below-market coupon is worth, and around 10.2 per cent the two options are worth the same.

</details>
