# A History of Debt · Lesson 3.1: From the Stop of the Exchequer to the Bank of England

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 3: The financial revolutions · Builds on: [2.3 The public debts of city-states](02-03-the-public-debts-of-city-states.md), [2.4 Lending to kings](02-04-lending-to-kings.md) · Unlocks: [3.2 Did 1688 make Britain creditworthy?](03-02-did-1688-make-britain-creditworthy.md)

## Why this matters

On 2 January 1672 Charles II stopped repaying the London goldsmiths who had lent him about 1.2 million pounds, roughly a year of his ordinary revenue. Twenty-two years later, 1,268 people put 1.2 million pounds into a new corporation, the Bank of England, in twelve days, and lent all of it to his successor's government. Within a century that government was carrying a debt Hume and Smith thought would ruin it, and it won most of its wars anyway. This lesson traces what changed between the default and the subscription. Whether the change *caused* the cheaper credit is [3.2](03-02-did-1688-make-britain-creditworthy.md)'s question.

## The idea

A king who borrows is like a man who borrows against his own wages: the lender's security is the king's word and the king's revenue, and both belong to the king. [2.4](02-04-lending-to-kings.md) showed what that meant for the Bardi and the Genoese. [2.3](02-03-the-public-debts-of-city-states.md) showed the alternative: in Venice or Florence the lenders *were* the government, so the debt was funded by taxes the creditors themselves voted.

The Dutch Republic scaled that civic model up to a province. England, after 1688, bolted a version of it onto a monarchy. The recipe had three parts:

1. **Make the debt permanent.** Stop promising to repay principal on a date. Promise an annual payment forever, redeemable when the state chooses. The lender who wants out sells to someone else.
2. **Tie each loan to a named tax voted by the assembly.** Then the security is a stream of revenue the lender can watch, not the king's goodwill.
3. **Give the creditors a collective body.** A corporation of lenders can refuse the next loan; a scattering of goldsmiths cannot.

None of the three was new in 1694. What was new was having all three at once, on a national scale, backed by a heavy and efficiently collected excise.

## Source

Adam Smith, writing in 1776, gave the vocabulary this lesson uses. Governments that mortgaged a revenue for a fixed term, expecting it to pay off principal and interest, raised money one way; those that mortgaged it only for the interest raised it the other:

> "When money was raised in the one way, it was said to be raised by anticipation; when in the other, by perpetual funding, or, more shortly, by funding." (*Wealth of Nations* V.iii)

The Stop of the Exchequer was a crisis of *anticipation*. The Bank of England was an instrument of *funding*.

## The argument

The standard account of the English financial revolution, in the form P. G. M. Dickson (*The Financial Revolution in England*, 1967) and John Brewer (*The Sinews of Power*, 1989) made familiar, runs in steps.

1. **Holland showed that a state could borrow cheaply and permanently.** The province issued *losrenten* (perpetual annuities, redeemable at the issuer's option) and *lijfrenten* (life annuities), mostly to its own burghers, secured on provincial excises. On the usual reconstructions its rate fell to about 4 per cent by the 1650s. Johan de Witt, the Republic's leading statesman, even published a treatise (1671) pricing life annuities against redeemable ones. The Amsterdam *Wisselbank* (1609) gave merchants a stable bank money to settle bills in. *In words:* a republic of creditors borrowed at rates no king could get.
2. **Restoration England borrowed by anticipation, through the goldsmiths.** Sir George Downing, who had served as envoy at The Hague, used the Additional Aid of 1665 to create Treasury Orders: registered, assignable claims on a specific tax, repaid "in course," in strict numbered order. Goldsmith bankers took deposits, bought the Orders, and lent the Crown ever larger sums against future revenue. *In words:* England built a market in short-term claims on taxes, and the goldsmiths became the Crown's bankers.
3. **The Stop showed where the security really lay.** In January 1672, with war against the Dutch coming (declared in March), Charles suspended repayment on Orders and warrants totalling about 1,211,000 pounds. Interest was promised at 6 per cent instead. It began only in 1675, grew erratic after 1680, and then stopped. Edward Backwell and Sir Robert Vyner, two of the largest goldsmiths, were ruined. *In words:* a claim on a tax is only as good as the will of the person who controls the tax.
4. **1688 moved control of revenue toward Parliament.** William III's parliaments granted many revenues for limited terms. They increasingly *appropriated* supply to named purposes, a practice Downing's 1665 clause had begun. And in 1698 they separated the Crown's own household and government costs into a fixed Civil List, about 700,000 pounds a year. War spending became Parliament's business. *In words:* after 1688, the body that voted the taxes also stood behind the loans secured on them.
5. **Parliament then funded loans for perpetuity.** The Million Act of 1693 raised money on a tontine and on life annuities. The Bank of England Act of 1694 raised 1.2 million pounds at 8 per cent, secured on tonnage and liquor duties. The Crown paid the Bank 100,000 pounds a year, the 96,000 in interest plus a 4,000-pound management fee, and the subscribers were chartered as a corporation. *In words:* the lenders became a standing body with a charter, a vote on the next loan, and a statute behind their interest.
6. **The debt rested on the excise.** Brewer's thesis is that Britain became a *fiscal-military state*: a heavily taxed country whose professional excise service, not its land tax, carried the interest on war debts. On the standard figures the debt went from about 1 million pounds in 1688 to roughly 17 million by the peace of 1697. *In words:* credit was cheap because the taxes behind it were real and reliably collected.

Step 4 is where the dispute starts. Whether the constitutional settlement *made* lenders trust the state, or whether other things did, is [3.2](03-02-did-1688-make-britain-creditworthy.md)'s question, and this lesson leaves it open.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean): the goldsmith and the Bank subscriber side by side.**

| | Goldsmith, 1671 | Bank subscriber, 1694 |
|---|---|---|
| Claim | A numbered Order on a named revenue, repaid in course | A share in a corporation owed 100,000 pounds a year |
| Who controls the revenue? | The king's Treasury | Taxes voted for the purpose by Parliament |
| Term | Short; principal expected soon | Perpetual; the state could redeem, the lender could only sell |
| If payment stops | Petition the king; sue in his own courts | The corporation can refuse the next loan; the charter could be ended only by repaying the loan |
| Rate | Above the 6 per cent later promised (accounts of the exact rates vary) | 8 per cent, plus fee: $100{,}000 / 1{,}200{,}000 = 8.33\%$ |

The subscriber's rate was not low. What had changed was the *structure* of the claim: longer, collective, and secured on a parliamentary tax. Smith later called the Bank "a great engine of state" (II.ii), because it handled the payments on the public debt and advanced the land and malt taxes before they came in.

**Example 2 (hard): was the Stop a default?**

The tidy story says "default, then reform, then credibility." The record strains it. Charles suspended *principal*, not interest, and he did pay interest for some years. The bankers then sued. The Bankers' Case ran through the Exchequer courts in the 1690s and reached the House of Lords by 1700. Parliament finally legislated in 1701. From December 1705 the claims, reckoned at 1,328,526 pounds, would carry 3 per cent and be redeemable at half their face value.

Run the arithmetic. Three per cent on the full claim is $0.03 \times 1{,}328{,}526 \approx 39{,}856$ pounds a year. That is exactly 6 per cent on half the claim: $0.06 \times 664{,}263 \approx 39{,}856$. So the settlement kept the promised 6 per cent rate and cut the principal by 50 per cent. That is a nominal haircut of half, before counting the interest missed from the early 1680s to 1705, which was never made up.

So the funded state *did* honour, in part, the unfunded king's debt. That is also a transfer. The post-1688 regime took over its predecessor's obligation, on terms the creditors could not refuse. This is also where the parallel with [2.4](02-04-lending-to-kings.md) strains. Philip II's suspensions converted short-term *asientos* into long-term *juros*, and his bankers went on lending. Charles's goldsmiths mostly did not. Whether that difference lay in the lenders, the institutions or the size of the loss is a comparison the evidence does not settle for you.

## Watch out

- **Anachronism trap: the Bank of England was not a central bank in 1694.** It was a private joint-stock company whose capital was a loan to the government. It had no monopoly of note issue in England in 1694 and no lender-of-last-resort doctrine. Those came in the nineteenth century. Reading the Bank of Walter Bagehot's *Lombard Street* (1873) back into 1694 gets the institution wrong.
- **"Dutch finance" was not simply imported with William.** Downing's Orders (1665) and parliamentary appropriation predate 1688. Holland's debt was provincial and held mainly by its own ruling class. England built a national debt around a monarch and a chartered bank. Say "on the Dutch model" only for the specific features copied: perpetual redeemable annuities and excise-backed funds.
- **Funding is not repayment.** A funded debt was designed never to be paid off, only serviced and occasionally converted to a lower rate. Smith's worry in V.iii is precisely that the mortgaging becomes permanent.
- **The cause of the Stop is disputed.** Do not write "the Stop happened because the Crown was broke" as settled fact; see P2.

## One-liner

> England moved from claims on a king's revenue, which a king could stop, to perpetual annuities on taxes that Parliament voted and a corporation of creditors watched; the Stop of 1672 and the Bank of 1694 are the two ends of that move.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢) *(Formal.)*** (a) A Holland *losrente* pays 4 guilders a year forever on 100 guilders of principal, redeemable by the province at par. Price it at market rates of 4, 5 and 3.5 per cent, ignoring redemption. Then say what the redemption option does to the 3.5 per cent price, and why. (b) A stylized tontine loosely modelled on 1693: 1,000 shares of 100 pounds each, and a fixed pool of 7,000 pounds a year shared equally among the shares whose nominees are still alive. Find each share's annual payment and yield when 800, 400 and 250 nominees survive. How many nominees must survive for a share to pay the same as a 14 per cent life annuity? Round to the nearest penny or 0.1 per cent.

**P2 (🟡) *(Exegetical.)*** Two historians explain the Stop of the Exchequer. **A:** it was fiscal necessity. Ordinary revenue could not cover the Orders falling due, the goldsmiths' credit was exhausted, and a fleet had to be fitted out for a war already agreed on. **B:** it was a political choice. Charles, committed by the Treaty of Dover to fight the Dutch alongside France, took the goldsmiths' money rather than recall a Parliament that would have attached conditions to supply. Name the single premise on which A and B divide, and say what evidence would move it. 150 words or fewer.

**P3 (🔴, optional) *(Exegetical.)*** In *Wealth of Nations* V.iii, Smith says governments kept overloading their funds, and "such improvident anticipations necessarily gave birth to the more ruinous practice of perpetual funding." He adds: "To relieve the present exigency, is always the object which principally interests those immediately concerned in the administration of public affairs." (a) What causal sequence does the first sentence assert, and which word makes it a claim of necessity rather than of history? (b) What explanation of *why* states keep funding does the second sentence give, and is it an explanation from institutions or from the motives of office-holders? (c) Does anything in the two sentences fit the Bank of England story of this lesson, or cut against it? 150 words or fewer in total.

<details>
<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Formal.)*

**(a)** A perpetuity paying $C$ a year at rate $r$ is worth $P = C/r$.
- At 4%: $4/0.04 = 100.00$ guilders (par, 25 years' purchase).
- At 5%: $4/0.05 = 80.00$.
- At 3.5%: $4/0.035 = 114.29$.

The province may redeem at 100. When market rates fall below the coupon, it can borrow new money at 3.5 per cent and pay off the 4 per cent *renten*, or offer holders a lower rate instead of redemption. So no buyer will pay much above 100: the call option caps the price near par. That is the mechanism of the rate conversions Holland used, and Britain later (Smith V.iii: 5 per cent to 4, then to 3.5 and 3).

**(b)** Payment per share $= 7{,}000 / n$; yield $=$ payment $/100$.
- $n = 800$: $8.75$ pounds, $8.8\%$.
- $n = 400$: $17.50$ pounds, $17.5\%$.
- $n = 250$: $28.00$ pounds, $28.0\%$.

Break-even with a 14 per cent life annuity: $7{,}000/n = 14 \Rightarrow n = 500$. Once more than half the nominees have died, the tontine share pays more.

**Must hit, strict:** $P = C/r$ with correct values; the redemption cap at par; payment $7{,}000/n$; break-even at 500.
**Wrong turns:** pricing the 3.5 per cent case at 114.29 and stopping, ignoring the call; dividing the pool by the dead rather than the living.

---

**P2** *(Exegetical.)*

**Must hit, strict:**
- One premise: *whether Charles had a feasible way to finance the coming war without stopping the Orders*, in practice by recalling Parliament for supply on terms he could accept. A says no feasible alternative existed. B says one existed and was declined for political reasons.
- The disagreement is not whether the Crown was short of money (both grant it) or whether a war was coming (both grant it).
- Evidence: Treasury and Privy Council records showing when the Stop was decided relative to war planning and to the choice to keep Parliament prorogued; the goldsmiths' actual lending capacity in late 1671; and signs of how the Commons would have treated a supply request (their conduct when they met in 1673).

**Wrong turns:** naming "whether the Treaty of Dover existed" (both accept it); giving two cruxes; delivering a verdict.

**Model answer:** A and B agree the Crown was short and a Dutch war was planned. They divide on one premise: that Charles had a workable alternative, namely asking Parliament for supply. A denies it; B affirms it and says he avoided it to keep his French policy free of Parliament's conditions. Evidence that would move it: Council and Treasury papers dating the decision relative to the war plans and the prorogation; accounts of whether goldsmith credit was truly exhausted in late 1671; and how the Commons actually handled supply in 1673, which shows whether a grant was obtainable and at what political price.

---

**P3** *(Exegetical.)*

**Must hit, strict:**
- (a) Sequence: short-term mortgaging of a fund; overloading it, or anticipating again before the first term ends; the fund cannot pay principal and interest; it is charged with interest only, a perpetual annuity. "Necessarily" makes it a claim that overloaded anticipation *must* end in funding, not merely that it did.
- (b) The motive of administrators: they care about the present emergency and leave the burden to posterity. It is an explanation from office-holders' incentives, not from constitutional structure.
- (c) Fit: the Bank loan was war finance for a present emergency, funded for perpetuity, and the Stop was an overloaded anticipation. Tension: Smith's sequence is universal and says nothing about Parliament, corporations of creditors or lower rates, which the standard account treats as what was new.

**Wrong turns:** reading "ruinous" as Smith's report of a fact about Britain's collapse (it is his evaluation); treating (b) as a claim about Parliament.

**Model answer:** (a) Overloaded short-term mortgages leave the fund unable to pay principal, so it is charged with interest only. "Necessarily" makes this a general law, not a narrative. (b) Office-holders care about the present crisis and leave the debt to posterity: a motive explanation. (c) It fits the pattern: the goldsmiths' Orders were overloaded anticipations, and 1694 funded a war loan for perpetuity. It cuts against the institutional story, because Smith's mechanism works with or without Parliament and predicts funding everywhere, not credibility in England alone.

</details>

## Flashback

**From Lesson 2.3 (The public debts of city-states):** *(Formal (a)–(b) · Exegetical (c).)* A Venetian household's *estimo* is 8,000 lire. The council levies a forced loan of 3 per cent, credited at face in the Monte at 5 per cent a year, paid in two installments. (a) Give the cash paid, the face credited, the annual interest and each installment. (b) The household collects interest in full for six years and then sells the credit at 72 per 100 of face. Compute the total lire it has received and its gain or loss on the cash paid, undiscounted, as a percentage. (c) Find the selling price per 100 of face at which it would exactly break even undiscounted, and say in one sentence whether an undiscounted reckoning flatters the levy's loan character or its tax character.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):** cash $= 0.03 \times 8{,}000 = 240$ lire, credited at face 240 (Venice credited face equal to cash paid, unlike Florence's multipliers). Interest $= 0.05 \times 240 = 12$ lire a year, so 6 lire twice a year.

**Must hit, strict (b):** six years of interest $= 6 \times 12 = 72$ lire; sale $= 0.72 \times 240 = 172.80$ lire; total received $= 244.80$ lire. Gain $= 244.80 - 240 = 4.80$ lire, or $4.80/240 = 2.0\%$ of the cash paid, over six years.

**Must hit, strict (c):** break-even needs a sale of $240 - 72 = 168$ lire, which is $168/240 = 70$ per 100 of face. The undiscounted reckoning flatters the **loan** character: it treats 12 lire collected six years from now as worth 12 lire paid today, so it hides the cost of waiting. Discounting the same flows at 5 per cent gives about 189.9 lire against 240 paid — on that reckoning roughly a fifth of the levy was a tax even though the nominal count showed a small gain.

**Wrong turns:** crediting face at a multiple of cash (that is Florence's *prestanze*, not Venice's *prestiti*); computing the sale as 72 lire rather than 72 per 100 of face; reading the 2.0 per cent as an annual return rather than a six-year total; concluding from (c) that the levy was "really a loan" because the nominal arithmetic breaks even.

**Model answer:** (a) 240 lire paid, 240 face, 12 lire a year, 6 lire each half-year. (b) 72 in interest plus 172.80 from the sale is 244.80, a gain of 4.80 lire, or 2.0 per cent on the cash over six years. (c) Break-even is a price of 70. Counting undiscounted lire makes the levy look almost entirely like a loan repaid; at a 5 per cent discount rate the same flows are worth about 190 lire against 240 handed over, so the waiting itself was part of what the household paid.

</details>
