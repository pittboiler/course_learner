# A History of Debt · Lesson 6.4: New creditors

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 6: Debt in the twenty-first century · Builds on: [6.3 Holdouts and the law of sovereign restructuring](06-03-holdouts-and-the-law-of-sovereign-restructuring.md) · Unlocks: [6.5 Student and consumer debt](06-05-student-and-consumer-debt.md)

## Why this matters

[6.3](06-03-holdouts-and-the-law-of-sovereign-restructuring.md) left the restructuring problem as a coordination problem among bondholders, half-solved by collective action clauses. That machinery rests on two assumptions inherited from [5.3](05-03-bretton-woods-and-the-lending-boom.md)–[5.5](05-05-jubilee-2000-and-hipc.md): that the official creditors are a single club which meets in one room and grants comparable terms, and that everyone's claims are on the books. By 2020 neither held for the poorest borrowers. The largest bilateral creditor to low-income countries was China, which is not a Paris Club member; much of its lending was done at commercial rates by policy banks under confidential contracts, and a good deal of it was secured on export revenue. This lesson is about what that does to the arithmetic of relief — and about the fight over how to describe it.

## The idea

Put four creditors in a room with a distressed government. Three hold ordinary unsecured claims. The fourth has a pipe running from the debtor's oil terminal into an account he controls offshore, and a contract forbidding the debtor to say so.

Nothing in that picture requires anyone to be sinister. The fourth creditor is doing what secured lenders have always done: taking collateral because the borrower is risky. But the system built after 1956 for sharing losses assumes unsecured claims of known size, ranked equally, whose holders must all say yes. Against that system, three things break at once:

- **You cannot count the debt.** Horn, Reinhart and Trebesch (2021) estimated that around half of China's lending to developing countries was missing from the IMF and World Bank statistics; AidData's *Banking on the Belt and Road* (2021), working from 13,427 projects worth about 843 billion dollars, put the "hidden" exposure at roughly 385 billion. Both are estimates built from project records, not audited totals, and they count different things.
- **You cannot classify the creditor.** Paris Club practice divides claims into official (bilateral aid, softer terms) and commercial. China Exim Bank extends concessional export credits; China Development Bank lends at market-linked rates; both are state-owned. Whether a CDB loan is "official" is not a fact waiting to be looked up — it is the thing being argued about.
- **You cannot make everyone wait.** A creditor paid automatically out of an escrow account is not in the room in the same sense as one who must vote on an exchange offer.

Gelpern, Horn, Morris, Parks and Trebesch, in *How China Lends* (2021), read 100 contracts with 24 governments and found these features recurring: confidentiality obligations, lender-controlled revenue accounts, cross-default clauses, and provisions excluding the debt from collective restructurings — what they call "no Paris Club" clauses. Read that as contract design, not as foreign policy. It is what the argument below turns on.

## The argument

The case for **comparability of treatment**, and where it now binds:

1. **A distressed sovereign can transfer only so much abroad in a given year.** *In words:* the size of the pie is set by the debtor's capacity, not by the sum of what it promised.
2. **Relief granted by one creditor that merely lets another be paid in full is a transfer between creditors, not relief to the debtor.** *In words:* if I cut my claim and you collect yours, my money went to you, not to the country.
3. **So relief must be conditioned on all creditors conceding in comparable measure**, which is the Paris Club's oldest rule. *In words:* nobody moves first for free.
4. **Comparability has to be measured on a common metric — present value, not face value** ([5.4](05-04-from-mexico-1982-to-the-brady-plan.md)'s distinction), which requires knowing every claim's size, rate and maturity. *In words:* you cannot compare concessions you cannot see.
5. **The G20 Common Framework (November 2020) was built to supply the room.** It extends Paris Club practice to G20 creditors outside it, forms an Official Creditor Committee for each debtor, requires an IMF program, and requires the debtor to seek comparable terms from its private creditors. *In words:* a club for the creditors who never joined the club.
6. **A claim secured on a revenue stream is outside the arithmetic of 1–4.** *In words:* a lender already holding the money has no reason to negotiate over it, and his silence is not a veto — it is a fait accompli.
7. **And a creditor who suspects that rival claims are concealed, or senior, will wait rather than concede first.** *In words:* the holdout logic of [6.3](06-03-holdouts-and-the-law-of-sovereign-restructuring.md), now running between classes of creditor rather than within one bond.

∴ Relief arrives late, and each side's delay is evidence for the other's suspicion. The immediate predecessor, the Debt Service Suspension Initiative (April 2020 to December 2021), had deferred roughly 13 billion dollars of payments for about 48 of the 73 eligible countries — a postponement, not a reduction, and it left the harder question to the Framework.

## Map of positions

Two questions run underneath the dispute, and they are independent: is the lending *directed* (a state strategy) or *decentralized* (banks, ministries and firms with their own incentives)? And is the pay-off *capture* (assets, or political leverage over the borrower) or an ordinary *workout* problem (getting repaid, badly)?

| | Pay-off is capture / leverage | Pay-off is a workout problem |
|---|---|---|
| **Directed** | **Debt-trap diplomacy** (Chellaney, 2017): loans are extended where repayment is doubtful, because the default is the point; Hambantota is the exhibit. | **Rescue lending**: Beijing acts deliberately, but to protect claims already made — swap lines and emergency loans to distressed borrowers, a state creditor behaving as a lender of last resort (Horn, Reinhart, Trebesch and Parks, 2023; hedge the details). |
| **Decentralized** | **Contract design** (Gelpern and co-authors, 2021): no master plan is needed — confidentiality, revenue accounts and cross-defaults generate creditor advantage on their own. | **Commercial lending, opacity and coordination failure** (Brautigam, 2020; Brautigam and Rithmire, 2021): policy banks lent commercially, sometimes unwisely; the trouble is that nobody can see the claims or agree on terms. |

The cells are not mutually exclusive, and the evidence that would move you differs by cell: internal decision documents for the top row, contract texts and loan-by-loan terms for the bottom.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean — what the Hambantota record shows).** The port was built in Sri Lanka's south, on a coast its promoters had wanted developed for decades. China Exim Bank lent about 307 million dollars for the first phase under a 2007 buyer's credit; the rate was later fixed at 6.3 percent — high for official lending, and much argued over. The port ran far below capacity. In 2017 the government leased it for 99 years to China Merchants Port Holdings, which paid about 1.12 billion dollars for an 85 percent stake in the operating company. Sri Lanka defaulted in 2022, on an external debt spread across bondholders, multilateral lenders, Japan, India and China.

Now separate three claims the "trap" story runs together: (i) the loan was made knowing it could not be repaid; (ii) default on that loan caused the transfer; (iii) the asset was seized. The record bears on each differently. On (ii) and (iii), Brautigam and Rithmire's point is documentary: the port loan was not in default, and the transaction was a lease sold for cash, with the proceeds going to reserves and other obligations — no creditor foreclosed. Claim (i) is not settled by that, and turns on what the lender expected in 2007. And none of it makes the outcome politically weightless: a strategic deep-water port sits under a 99-year lease to a Chinese state company, which is why the episode is argued about at all. The course reports both readings; which matters more is a question for `international-relations` ([syllabus](../../international-relations/syllabus.md)).

**Example 2 (hard — Zambia, and what comparability costs in time).** Zambia missed a 42.5-million-dollar coupon in November 2020, the first pandemic-era sovereign default in Africa, and applied to the Common Framework in early 2021. Its Official Creditor Committee was co-chaired by China and France — the first time Beijing sat at the head of such a table. An agreement in principle covering about 6.3 billion dollars of official bilateral claims came in June 2023, with a memorandum of understanding that October. Then the sequence that shows the mechanism: a deal struck with bondholders in October 2023 was blocked because the official creditors judged it *not comparable* to their own concession; a revised bondholder agreement followed in March 2024. Roughly three and a half years passed between default and settlement.

Who conceded more is itself contested — the campaign group Debt Justice calculated that bondholders would recover about 73 cents per dollar lent against about 55 for official creditors, an estimate from an advocacy organization, not an agreed figure. The structural point stands whatever the number: comparability was enforced, and enforcing it took years during which Zambia was cut off from markets.

## Watch out

- **"Debt trap" is three claims, not one.** Designed-to-fail lending, default-triggered transfer, and asset seizure are separate, and evidence for one is not evidence for the others. State which claim is being made before asking whether it is true.
- **The nineteenth-century analogy has an edge and a break.** Escrow accounts capturing export receipts do resemble the Caisse de la Dette in Egypt ([4.5](04-05-egypt-the-ottomans-and-debt-administration.md)) or the Dominican customs receivership ([4.6](04-06-bondholders-gunboats-and-receiverships.md)): revenue diverted to creditors before it reaches the treasury. But those were multilateral administrations installed *inside* the debtor state, backed in the end by force and by the loss of self-government. A contractual offshore account is a private arrangement enforced by the threat of cross-default, not by a foreign commissioner in the finance ministry. Importing the nineteenth-century word "receivership" carries the gunboat with it; that is an anachronism, and it does real work in arguments.
- **"China" is not a creditor.** Exim and CDB lend on different terms, and provincial banks, state firms and the central bank's swap lines behave differently again. Comparability of treatment is hard to define across a creditor that is not one entity.
- **Hidden-debt figures are estimates with methods.** Horn and co-authors reconstruct loan-by-loan from many sources; AidData codes project records. Treat their totals as contested and say which one a claim rests on.

## One-liner

> The problem with the new creditors is less trap than arithmetic: losses cannot be shared fairly among claims you cannot see, cannot classify, and cannot make wait.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢) *(Formal.)*** A government borrows 1,500 million dollars for 15 years at a fixed 5 percent, repaid in equal annual instalments. The contract pledges 8,000 barrels of oil a day, sold by the lender's agent into an offshore account it controls; the account pays the year's instalment first and releases any surplus to the government. (Figures illustrative.)

(a) Compute the annual instalment $A$ from $A = P\,\frac{r}{1-(1+r)^{-n}}$. At 75 dollars a barrel, how much does the pledged flow raise, and how much is released? At 40 dollars, what is the shortfall, and how many barrels a day would the lender need to cover the instalment?

(b) Total exports are 30 million barrels a year. At each price, what share of *export revenue* does the instalment take?

(c) Now suppose the country can transfer only 200 million dollars a year in total, and its other creditors are scheduled 155.5 million. The escrow lender is paid in full and first. What share of their scheduled service do the others receive? What would each class receive if all creditors were cut pro rata instead? Round to 0.1. In one sentence, say which premise of the argument above the escrow account defeats.

**P2 (🟡) *(Exegetical.)*** Chellaney's 2017 debt-trap thesis and the Brautigam–Rithmire critique both accept the same documented facts about Hambantota. Name the single premise one side must deny, and say what kind of evidence would move it. Say in one sentence what the dispute is *not* about. 150 words or fewer.

**P3 (🔴, optional) *(Exegetical.)*** An invented op-ed (not the words of any real person):

> Call it what it is. A poor country signs a loan it cannot read, pledges the copper that pays its teachers, and wakes up to find a foreign power collecting its revenue before its own treasury does. We have seen this film. It ends with a commissioner in the finance ministry and a warship in the harbour, and the only question is how long the fiction of sovereignty lasts.

Name the historical analogy doing the work. Name two features of that earlier arrangement ([4.5](04-05-egypt-the-ottomans-and-debt-administration.md), [4.6](04-06-bondholders-gunboats-and-receiverships.md)) the argument depends on, and say for each whether it carries over. 150 words or fewer.

<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Formal.)*

(a) $1.05^{-15} \approx 0.481017$, so $1 - 1.05^{-15} \approx 0.518983$ and

$$A = 1500 \times \frac{0.05}{0.518983} \approx 144.5 \text{ million a year.}$$

Pledged flow: $8{,}000 \times 365 = 2.92$ million barrels a year. At 75 dollars that is $2.92 \times 75 = 219.0$ million, so $219.0 - 144.5 = \mathbf{74.5}$ million is released to the government. At 40 dollars it raises $2.92 \times 40 = 116.8$ million, a shortfall of $144.5 - 116.8 = \mathbf{27.7}$ million. Covering the instalment at 40 dollars needs $144.5/40 \approx 3.613$ million barrels, i.e. $3.613 \times 10^6 / 365 \approx \mathbf{9{,}900}$ barrels a day (about 24 percent more oil).

(b) Export revenue is $30 \times 75 = 2{,}250$ million, so the instalment is $144.5/2250 \approx \mathbf{6.4\%}$. At 40 dollars, revenue is 1,200 million and the instalment is $144.5/1200 \approx \mathbf{12.0\%}$. Halving the price nearly doubles the burden: the claim is fixed in dollars, the resource is not.

(c) Scheduled service totals $144.5 + 155.5 = 300$ million. Paid first, the escrow lender takes 144.5, leaving $200 - 144.5 = 55.5$ million, or $55.5/155.5 \approx \mathbf{35.7\%}$ of the others' scheduled service (they concede about 64 percent). Pro rata, every creditor gets $200/300 \approx 66.7$ percent: the escrow lender 96.3 million, the others 103.7 million. The escrow defeats premise 3, that relief can be conditioned on comparable concessions by all: the collateralized claim is paid whatever the others agree, so their concession partly finances it.

**Wrong turns:** treating the pledged barrels as a fixed *value* rather than a fixed *volume* (the whole point is that the volume is fixed and the value is not); computing the shortfall in (a) and then forgetting that the extra barrels come out of the same 30 million.

---

**P2** *(Exegetical — strict on naming one premise; several formulations pass.)*

**Must hit, strict:**
- The crux: *whether the lender extended the loan expecting repayment*. The trap thesis requires that repayment failure was foreseen and wanted (the asset was the aim); the critique holds the lending was commercial, and the port's troubles a bad bet rather than a plan.
- Evidence that would move it: internal lender and Chinese government decision records, feasibility studies and risk assessments at the time of the 2007 loan; the pattern across the whole portfolio (are strategic assets pledged more often than commercial logic predicts?); whether Chinese creditors elsewhere take assets or reschedule.
- What it is not about: the documented sequence — that the port loan was not in default, that the 2017 transaction was a lease sold for cash, and that the asset is strategically valuable. Both sides accept these.

**Wrong turns:** treating "China gained a strategic asset" as settling the question (an outcome is not an intention); treating "there was no default" as settling it either (a lender can expect distress without foreclosing); answering with a verdict on Chinese lending.

**Model answer:** Both sides agree the phase-one loan was never in default and that the 2017 deal was a 99-year lease sold for about 1.12 billion dollars, cash Colombo needed. They divide on one premise: that Exim lent in 2007 expecting the port to be repaid. Chellaney's thesis needs that premise denied — the unrepayable loan is the instrument — while Brautigam and Rithmire affirm it and treat the outcome as a bad commercial bet plus a government's need for dollars. What would move it is evidence about expectations, not outcomes: the feasibility studies and internal risk assessments behind the loan, and the portfolio-wide record of whether Chinese lenders seize strategic assets or reschedule. The dispute is not about the facts of the lease or the port's strategic value.

---

**P3** *(Exegetical — strict.)*

**Must hit, strict:**
- The analogy is nineteenth-century creditor control of a debtor state's revenue: Egypt's Caisse de la Dette and Dual Control (4.5), or the Dominican customs receivership under the Roosevelt Corollary (4.6). "A commissioner in the finance ministry and a warship in the harbour" names both halves.
- Feature 1 — **revenue diverted to creditors before it reaches the treasury.** Carries over in substance: a lender-controlled escrow account does exactly this, and the borrower's own budget sees the residual.
- Feature 2 — **enforcement by foreign public authority: creditor-state officials installed inside the debtor's institutions, and force or the loss of self-government behind them.** Does not carry over: an escrow is a private contract enforced by cross-default and the loss of future credit, without a foreign official inside the ministry or a threat of occupation.
- Say that the op-ed's conclusion rides on the second feature while its evidence supplies only the first.

**Wrong turns:** dismissing the analogy wholesale because "China has no gunboats" — the revenue-capture half is real and is the reason the comparison keeps being drawn; or accepting it wholesale because both involve foreign creditors.

**Model answer:** The analogy is the nineteenth-century debt administration — the Caisse de la Dette in Egypt, or the Dominican customs receivership — where creditors collected a state's revenue at the source. It depends on two features. First, revenue diverted to creditors before the treasury sees it: this carries over, since a lender-controlled escrow account fed by pledged exports does the same job by contract. Second, enforcement by foreign public power, with creditor-state officials inside the debtor's institutions and force or suspended self-government behind them: this does not carry over. An escrow is enforced by cross-default clauses and the prospect of losing credit, not by a commissioner or a warship. The op-ed's evidence establishes the first feature; its conclusion — the fiction of sovereignty, the warship — rides on the second.

## Flashback

**From Lesson 6.2 (The Eurozone and Greece):** A currency-union member owes 300 billion euros and has nominal GDP of 200 billion (illustrative). To restore competitiveness without a currency of its own, it cuts domestic wages and prices by 8 percent, with real output unchanged. (a) Compute the debt ratio before and after, to three decimals. (b) What one-off write-down of face value, in billions and as a percentage, would put the ratio back where it started? Compare that percentage with the price cut and say what the comparison shows. (c) Now let real output also fall 4 percent alongside the same 8 percent price cut: recompute the ratio. Then, in one sentence, say which term of $d_{t+1} = \frac{1+r}{1+g}d_t - s$ all of this runs through, and why a one-off haircut and a persistently negative $g$ are not the same kind of remedy.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):** $d_0 = 300/200 = \mathbf{1.500}$. After the price cut, nominal GDP is $200 \times 0.92 = 184$, and the debt is denominated in euros, so the numerator does not move: $d_1 = 300/184 = \mathbf{1.630}$ (1.6304 to 4 dp). The successful adjustment raises the ratio by 13 points of GDP.

**Must hit, strict (b):** to get back to 1.500 at the new denominator, face must fall to $1.5 \times 184 = 276$, so the write-down is $300 - 276 = \mathbf{24}$ billion, or $24/300 = \mathbf{8\ \text{percent}}$ — exactly the size of the price cut. That is not a coincidence: since $d = D/(P \cdot Y)$, cutting $P$ by 8 percent does the same to the ratio as raising $D$ by $1/0.92 - 1 \approx 8.7$ percent, so an internal devaluation of $x$ percent costs, in ratio terms, precisely the $x$ percent haircut it was meant to avoid.

**Must hit, strict (c):** nominal GDP is $200 \times 0.92 \times 0.96 = 176.64$, so $d = 300/176.64 = \mathbf{1.698}$. It all runs through $g$, the growth rate of **nominal** GDP in the denominator — and the two remedies differ in kind, because a write-down lowers $d_t$ once while a negative $g$ multiplies it again every year the contraction lasts.

**Wrong turns:** deflating the debt along with prices — the obligation is a fixed euro amount, which is the entire point of giving up the currency; using real growth for $g$ while leaving $r$ nominal; reading (b) as saying a haircut and a wage cut are equivalent policies, when they differ in who bears the loss (creditors in one case, wage and price setters in the other) even where the ratio arithmetic matches.

**Model answer:** (a) $300/200 = 1.500$, then $300/184 = 1.630$. (b) Face must fall to $1.5 \times 184 = 276$, a write-down of 24 billion, or 8 percent — the same percentage as the price cut, because the ratio only ever sees the quotient of the two. (c) $200 \times 0.92 \times 0.96 = 176.64$, so $d = 1.698$. Everything here moves nominal GDP, the $g$ in the identity's denominator; a haircut cuts the stock once, whereas a negative $g$ reapplies itself annually, which is why a record write-down can be swallowed by a few years of contraction.

</details>
