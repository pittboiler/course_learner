# A History of Debt · Lesson 2.3: The public debts of city-states

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 2: Credit under the usury ban · Builds on: [2.1 Commerce around the prohibition](02-01-commerce-around-the-prohibition.md), [2.2 Jewish lenders and the Monti di Pietà](02-02-jewish-lenders-and-the-monti-di-pieta.md) · Unlocks: [2.4 Lending to kings](02-04-lending-to-kings.md)

## Why this matters

Permanent public debt, the kind modern states carry forever and whose shares change hands every day, was not invented by kings or by the Bank of England. It was invented by Italian merchant republics in the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries, out of an institution that looks nothing like a bond: the forced loan. Venice, Florence and Genoa turned compulsory levies on their own citizens into consolidated, interest-bearing, tradable credits, and they borrowed at rates kings could only envy. How they managed it, and what it cost them politically, is the first chapter of every later argument about why some governments can borrow cheaply ([3.2](03-02-did-1688-make-britain-creditworthy.md)).

## The idea

Picture a commune at war. It needs cash this month; taxes arrive over years. So the council tells every household assessed above some level of wealth to hand over a fixed percentage of its assessment. That is a tax. But the council also writes the payment in a register as a *credit* the household holds against the commune, promises interest on it, and lets the household sell the credit to someone else. That is a loan.

The Italian forced loan was both at once, and which one it *mostly* was depended on a single number: the price at which the credit could be sold. If a credit written at 100 sold for 95, the citizen had lent and could get out almost whole. If it sold for 30, seventy percent of his "loan" had quietly become a tax.

Three further moves turned this into public debt in the modern sense:

- **Consolidation.** Instead of a tangle of separate loans with separate terms, the commune rolled them into one fund, a *monte* ("mountain", heap) paying one rate.
- **Transferability.** Credits in the fund could be sold, inherited, pledged and given as dowries. The debt became a kind of property.
- **Perpetuity.** The principal was rarely repaid. The commune serviced the interest, and a holder who wanted his money back sold his credit to another citizen.

Who held these credits matters for everything that follows. They were the city's own merchant families, the same families who sat on its councils.

## Source

Machiavelli, *Florentine Histories* VIII.29, on Genoa's Casa di San Giorgio (anonymous translation, W. Walter Dunne edition, 1901, public domain; in that edition Book VIII, Chapter VI). The creditors of the republic, having been granted its customs revenue, came to administer towns across Genoese territory:

> These the Bank governs and protects, and every year sends its deputies, appointed by vote, without any interference on the part of the republic.

Notice who is doing the governing: not the republic, but its creditors, organized as a body with its own council of one hundred and executive of eight.

## The argument

**The institutions, city by city.**

1. **Venice: forced loans on assessed wealth, consolidated in 1262.** Venetian forced loans (*prestiti*) were levied as a percentage of each household's assessed wealth (*estimo*). In 1262 the Great Council consolidated the outstanding loans into one fund, the Monte (later called the Monte Vecchio, once a new fund was created in the 1480s), paying 5 percent a year in two installments. Credits were registered and, by most accounts, freely transferable. *In words:* Venice made its war debts one standing obligation at one rate, held by its own citizens.
2. **Florence: the Monte Comune, 1345.** Florence consolidated its debts in the 1340s into the Monte Comune at a nominal 5 percent. Its forced loans (*prestanze*) were also assessed on wealth, and in the heavy war years of the later fourteenth century some were credited at two or even three times the cash actually paid. Credits traded among citizens, often well below face value. *In words:* Florence paid "5 percent" on a face value that could be several times what the lender had handed over, and the market price told everyone what the promise was worth.
3. **Genoa: the *compere* and San Giorgio, 1407.** Genoa financed itself through *compere*, loans each secured on a specific tax, with the creditors' claims divided into shares (*luoghi*). In 1407 these were consolidated in the Casa di San Giorgio, a corporation of the state's creditors that collected the pledged revenues itself and, from the mid-fifteenth century, administered territories such as Corsica. *In words:* in Genoa the creditors did not merely lobby the government that owed them; they ran a parallel one.

**Why did they borrow cheaply? Stasavage's thesis** (David Stasavage, *States of Credit*, 2011), paraphrased:

4. **A lender to a government fears that the government will default when defaulting suits those who rule.** *In words:* the risk in public lending is political, not just financial.
5. **Where the lenders themselves control the institutions that decide on repayment, default is harder to choose.** *In words:* you do not vote to stiff yourself.
6. **In compact city-states, merchants could meet in a governing assembly often and cheaply; in large territorial monarchies, representatives were scattered and assemblies met rarely.** *In words:* geography made creditor control feasible in a city and hard in a kingdom.
7. **So city-states with merchant-dominated councils gained access to long-term credit earlier, and at lower rates, than territorial states.** *In words:* nominal rates around 5 percent in Venice and Florence, while kings (the next lesson) commonly paid far more.
8. **But the same merchant oligarchies could close ranks, protect incumbents and block newcomers.** *In words:* the arrangement that made debt cheap could make a polity rigid, so Stasavage presents it as a trade-off, not a triumph.

Stasavage's is not the only explanation on offer. Others weight the cities' dense, easily pledged revenues from customs and excise duties, and their small scale, more heavily than who sat on the council. Weighing those rivals is Module 2's boss problem.

## The case

| | Venice | Florence | Genoa |
|---|---|---|---|
| **How money was raised** | *Prestiti* assessed on *estimo* | *Prestanze* assessed on wealth; the *catasto* of 1427 later reformed the assessment | *Compere*, each secured on a named revenue |
| **Consolidation** | Monte, 1262 | Monte Comune, 1345 | Casa di San Giorgio, 1407 |
| **Nominal interest** | 5% (cut later in bad times) | 5% on face, face sometimes a multiple of cash paid | Varied by *compera*; dividends on *luoghi* depended on revenues |
| **Tradable?** | Yes | Yes, often far below par | Yes, *luoghi* |
| **Creditors' political position** | Same patriciate as the Great Council | Same families as the ruling councils; tax debtors could be barred from office | A separate corporation with its own council, revenues and territories |

In Venice and Florence the creditors controlled the state by *being* its ruling class. In Genoa, where factions (Machiavelli names the Fregosi and Adorni) fought over the government itself, the creditors built an institution that factional victory did not touch.

## Worked examples

All figures below are invented for illustration and use a generic unit, the lira.

**Example 1 (clean: a Venetian-style forced loan).** A household's *estimo* is 5,000 lire. The council levies a forced loan of 2 percent.

- Cash paid: $0.02 \times 5{,}000 = 100$ lire, credited at face 100.
- Interest: $0.05 \times 100 = 5$ lire a year, paid as 2.5 twice a year.
- In peacetime the credit sells at 90: the household can recover 90 of its 100. The "tax" part is 10 lire, and the rest is a real loan paying 5 on 100.
- In a war year the price falls to 60: an immediate sale recovers only 60, so 40 of the 100 has become, for anyone who needs cash now, a tax.

The same legal instrument is mostly loan or mostly tax depending on a price the council does not set.

**Example 2 (hard: a Florentine three-for-one levy).** In a war year a citizen pays 100 lire cash and is credited with 300 in the Monte at 5 percent. The market price is 25.

- Interest on face: $0.05 \times 300 = 15$ lire a year, which is $15/100 = 15\%$ on the cash he paid. Nominally 5 percent, effectively triple.
- Market value of his credit: $0.25 \times 300 = 75$. If he must sell, he loses $100 - 75 = 25$ lire, a quarter of what he paid.
- A buyer who pays 75 for that credit collects 15 a year: a current yield of $15/75 = 20\%$, which is the same as $5/25$.

Look at what the multiplier did. By crediting 300 for 100, the commune paid a return on cash that no one would call 5 percent while keeping the books at 5 percent. Contemporaries noticed. Theologians argued for decades over whether taking Monte interest was usury, a controversy studied by Julius Kirshner. A common defense of the original lender was that he had been compelled and was owed compensation; the buyer who purchased a credit at a deep discount, voluntarily, was harder to defend. The doctrine belongs to `theology-of-debt`; the point here is that the forced character of the loan was doing moral work, not just fiscal work.

Whether the three-for-one citizen was a lender or a taxpayer depends on whether he could wait, and whether the Monte kept paying. That is P1's question.

## Watch out

- **Anachronism trap: calling Monte credits "government bonds" bought by investors.** The original holders did not choose to invest. They were assessed. A secondary market of willing buyers grew on top of a compulsory base, and the two kinds of holder faced very different returns (Example 2).
- **Anachronism trap: reading "creditors in government" as democracy.** Stasavage's mechanism is representation of *merchants*, usually a narrow patriciate. The same feature that made default unattractive also excluded most residents from power.
- **You might think 5 percent nominal means 5 percent cost.** With multipliers on face value and prices far below par, the effective rate on cash paid and the yield to a buyer could each be several times the nominal rate.
- **You might take Machiavelli's origin story of San Giorgio as chronology.** He compresses a long history of *compere* into one founding after a war with Venice. The corporation of 1407 consolidated arrangements that were already old.

## One-liner

> Italian republics made a tax look like a loan, then made the loan into property, and borrowed cheaply because the people who held it were the people who ruled.

## Problems

**P1 (🟡) *(Formal (a) · Exegetical (b).)*** A Florentine citizen pays 60 lire cash in a forced loan and is credited with 120 in the Monte at a nominal 5 percent. The credit trades at 30 per 100 of face value. (a) Compute (i) the current yield to a buyer at that price, (ii) the citizen's annual interest as a percentage of the cash he paid, (iii) his loss, in lire and as a share of his cash, if he sells at once, and (iv) how many years of interest, undiscounted, he must collect to match what he would lose by selling now. Round percentages to one decimal place. (b) Historians disagree about whether forced loans like this were "really" a tax or "really" a loan. Name the single question the two readings divide on, and say what kind of evidence would move it. 150 words or fewer for (b). *(Do not answer why cities borrowed more cheaply than kings.)*

**P2 (🟡) *(Exegetical.)*** Close-read Machiavelli, *Florentine Histories* VIII.29 (anonymous translation, 1901). Having described San Giorgio's government, he says the institution is something no philosopher's republic ever imagined:

> exhibiting within the same community, and among the same citizens, liberty and tyranny, integrity and corruption, justice and injustice

He ends by predicting that if San Giorgio came to possess the whole city, "the republic will become more distinguished than that of Venice."

Two readers disagree. Reader A: Machiavelli presents creditor government as a pathology, a divided city. Reader B: he presents it as the healthy part of Genoa, the model its politics should grow into. Which reading does the passage support, and which words decide it? Say what the phrase "liberty and tyranny" refers to on your reading. 150 words or fewer.

**P3 (🔴, optional) *(Exegetical.)*** An invented op-ed:

> "Our creditors want guarantees; our citizens want services. Genoa solved this five centuries ago. It handed its tax revenues and even its towns to the Casa di San Giorgio, and the Bank ran them so well that Machiavelli praised it above the republic itself. A debtor nation today should do the same: place its ports and customs under an authority run by its bondholders, and watch its borrowing costs fall."

Name the historical analogy doing the work and the two features of San Giorgio the argument depends on, and say for each whether it carries over to the proposal. 150 words or fewer.

<details>
<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Formal (a) · Exegetical (b).)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** arithmetic computed and shown; percentages rounded to one decimal place.
- (i) Coupon per 100 of face is 5; price is 30. Current yield $= 5/30 = 0.1667 \approx 16.7\%$.
- (ii) Interest $= 0.05 \times 120 = 6$ lire a year. On cash: $6/60 = 10.0\%$.
- (iii) Sale value $= 0.30 \times 120 = 36$ lire. Loss $= 60 - 36 = 24$ lire, or $24/60 = 40.0\%$ of his cash.
- (iv) $24 / 6 = 4$ years of interest, ignoring discounting and assuming the Monte keeps paying.

**Must hit, strict (b):**
- The dividing question is the *value of the claim the citizen received relative to what he paid*, which turns on whether the commune actually kept paying interest and what the credit fetched: were forced lenders, on average, made roughly whole in expectation, or did they receive a claim worth much less than their cash?
- Evidence: long series of Monte prices; records of whether and when interest was paid, cut or suspended; who held credits over time (did the original assessed households keep them and collect, or sell early to buyers at discounts?); the treatment of those who could not pay the levy.
- Recognizing that the answer can differ by holder (the household that could wait vs the one forced to sell) earns credit.

**Wrong turns:** computing the yield on cash as 5 percent because the nominal rate is 5 percent; computing the buyer's yield on the citizen's 60 rather than on the market price; in (b), answering with the legal form ("it was registered as a credit, so it was a loan"), which is exactly what the tax reading disputes.

**Model answer (b):** Both sides agree about the legal form. They divide over whether the credit a citizen received was, in practice, worth about what he paid. If interest was paid reliably and credits held their price, the levy was a compulsory loan; if interest was cut or suspended and credits sold at a third of face, much of it was a tax with a receipt attached. What moves it: price series for Monte credits, the record of interest payments and reductions, and evidence on whether assessed households held their credits or sold them early, since the same levy was a loan to a family that could wait and a tax on one that could not.

---

**P2** *(Exegetical — strict.)*

**Must hit, strict:**
- The passage supports **Reader B more than Reader A**, with the qualification that Machiavelli describes a *divided* city in which the good part is San Giorgio.
- Deciding words: the closing prediction that possession of the whole city by San Giorgio would make Genoa "more distinguished than that of Venice", a conditional of praise, not alarm. And, from the preceding sentences of VIII.29, citizens transfer their affection from the commune to San Giorgio because of the commune's tyranny and the Bank's excellent regulations.
- "Liberty and tyranny" are distributed across the two institutions: tyranny, corruption and injustice belong to the commune fought over by factions; liberty, integrity and justice to San Giorgio. The contrast is inside Genoa, not inside San Giorgio.

**Wrong turns:** reading "liberty and tyranny" as a verdict that San Giorgio itself is half-tyrannical; treating the claim of novelty (nothing philosophers imagined) as condemnation, when it marks novelty; relying on the modern gloss "state within a state" as if it were Machiavelli's phrase. A strong answer may add a translation caution: the Italian says the whole city passing to San Giorgio "will certainly happen" in time, where this translation softens it to a mere possibility; that strengthens B.

**Model answer:** Reader B, with a qualification. Machiavelli does describe a divided city, but he assigns the halves: the citizens withdraw their affection from the commune for its tyranny and give it to San Giorgio for its good administration. So "liberty and tyranny" names the contrast between the creditors' institution and the factional government, not a flaw in San Giorgio. The ending decides it: if San Giorgio took the whole city, Genoa would outshine Venice, which was the model republic of his day. That is praise of the creditor order, not alarm at it. Reader A is right that the city is split, wrong about which side he regrets.

---

**P3** *(Exegetical — strict on identifying features; the verdict on the proposal is not graded.)*

**Must hit, strict:**
- The analogy: a creditor-run authority administering pledged revenues and territory (San Giorgio) as a model for a modern bondholder authority, with Machiavelli's praise as endorsement.
- Feature 1, **who the creditors were**: San Giorgio's shareholders were Genoese citizens, many from the governing class, so creditor rule was rule by insiders. Modern bondholders are largely foreign or institutional. Does not carry over as stated; the political meaning of "creditor control" flips from self-government to external control (a bridge to 4.5–4.6).
- Feature 2, **why rates were low**: on Stasavage's reading, cheap credit came from lenders' control of the state's decision to repay, in a compact polity. Handing assets to creditors outside the political community gives them security, not that representation. Whether it lowers rates is a separate empirical question (`economics-of-debt`).
- Also acceptable: Machiavelli's praise was of San Giorgio's *administration* compared with Genoa's factional government, not of the bargain for the debtor.

**Wrong turns:** arguing that the proposal is good or bad on its merits; treating San Giorgio's creditors as foreign lenders.

**Model answer:** The op-ed treats Genoa's creditor corporation as proof that bondholder-run authorities lower borrowing costs. It rests on two features. First, San Giorgio's creditors *were* Genoa's citizens, often its ruling families, so "creditor control" meant the city governing itself through another body; a modern authority run by mostly foreign bondholders is external control, which carries different consent and legitimacy. Second, the cheap credit rested on lenders controlling the decision to repay; pledging ports gives foreign creditors collateral, not a vote, so the mechanism does not transfer automatically. Machiavelli's praise, finally, compared San Giorgio to Genoa's factional government; it was not a claim about borrowing costs.

## Flashback

**From Lesson 2.1 (Commerce around the prohibition):** *(Exegetical.)* Sort three contracts with the lesson's two questions: who owns the money during the venture, and at whose risk it is used. (a) A Venetian investor hands 300 lire of cloth to a traveller bound for Alexandria; the traveller undertakes to restore the 300 whatever happens, and keeps a quarter of any profit. (b) A merchant advances 500 lire to a trader going to the Champagne fairs, repayable at 600 only if the goods arrive; if the convoy is plundered, nothing is owed. (c) The town of Ghent sells a burgher a yearly rent of 60 pounds for 1,000 pounds down, charged on its toll revenues and redeemable only by the town. For each, say whether it is a *mutuum* in substance and which of the two variables decides. Two sentences or fewer per part.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict:**
- (a) A loan in substance, despite the partnership label. The guarantee of the capital moves the risk of loss off the investor, so he is owed a certain 300 and his share of profit is gain beyond the principal by reason of that certainty. **Risk decides**; the commenda's whole defence was that the investor could lose his capital.
- (b) A loan in substance — the sea-loan shape, read as condemned by *Naviganti*. Ownership of the 500 passes to the trader, who owes a fixed 600 out of whatever he has; the risk of plunder is real, but a loan with a risk premium is still a loan. **Ownership decides**, and the risk test alone gives the wrong answer here.
- (c) Not a *mutuum* on the form's own terms: only the town may redeem, so the burgher cannot demand his 1,000 back — he has bought an income, not lent a sum. **Ownership (the missing call right) decides.** Credit for naming the strain: the rent rests on a stream of tolls rather than a specific fruit-bearing thing, which is the feature later rules (Pius V, 1569) insisted on.

**Wrong turns:** treating (a) as a partnership because it is labelled one, or because the traveller still risks his labour; clearing (b) because the lender can lose everything, which mistakes real risk for the absence of a loan; calling (c) a loan because the cash flows match one at 6 percent a year — they match in the vineyard case too, and the clause that separates them is who may call the principal.

**Model answer:** (a) *Mutuum*. The traveller's promise to restore the 300 whatever happens leaves the investor with a certain sum, so the profit share is a gain beyond principal; risk is the variable that has moved. (b) *Mutuum*. Ownership of the 500 passes to the trader and he owes a stated 600, so the 100 is exacted by reason of the loan; the shipwreck-style risk does not change the form, which is why *Naviganti* was read as condemning it. (c) Not a *mutuum*. The buyer has no right to call his 1,000 and holds only a rent, so he bought a thing; the pressure point is that the security is toll revenue rather than fruitful property.

</details>
