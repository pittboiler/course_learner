# A History of Debt · Lesson 5.3: Bretton Woods and the lending boom

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 5: Reparations to Jubilee: the twentieth century · Builds on: [5.2 Could Germany pay?](05-02-could-germany-pay.md) · Unlocks: [5.4 From Mexico 1982 to the Brady Plan](05-04-from-mexico-1982-to-the-brady-plan.md)

## Why this matters

In July 1944, delegates from forty-four nations met at a hotel in New Hampshire to design the machinery the interwar years had lacked. The question on the table was the one this module has been circling since 1919: when a country cannot pay what it owes abroad, who has to change course — the debtor, the creditor, or both? The answer written into the Articles of Agreement shaped every debt crisis that followed. And when the exchange-rate system built on those Articles collapsed in 1971 and oil money flooded the banks, the financing of deficits moved from the official institutions to private syndicates lending at floating rates. That move is why Lesson 5.4 opens in Mexico in August 1982.

## The idea

Two men wrote the competing blueprints, and the difference between them is a difference about where the pain of adjustment should fall.

Britain, John Maynard Keynes's client, expected to come out of the war a chronic debtor. The United States, Harry Dexter White's, expected to be the world's creditor for a generation. Keynes wanted an institution that made a persistent *surplus* as uncomfortable as a persistent deficit — a clearing house with a unit of account of its own (*bancor*), automatic overdrafts, and charges levied on countries whose balances drifted too far from zero in **either** direction. White wanted a fund of subscribed currencies and gold, finite, lent to deficit countries at the Fund's discretion, with the creditor under no obligation at all.

The creditor's plan won, as creditors' plans do. What emerged was pegged but adjustable exchange rates, a Fund of about 8.8 billion dollars in quotas, a sister institution for reconstruction and development lending, and one small symmetric concession — the scarce-currency clause. Adjustment would be the deficit country's job, and access to the Fund's money would come with conditions.

Hold onto that, because the second half of the lesson is what happened when a much larger, much less conditional source of foreign finance appeared.

## Source

Articles of Agreement of the International Monetary Fund, 22 July 1944, Article I, purpose (v):

> To give confidence to members by making the Fund's resources available to them under adequate safeguards, thus providing them with opportunity to correct maladjustments in their balance of payments without resorting to measures destructive of national or international prosperity.

Two phrases carry the whole design. "Under adequate safeguards" is the seed of conditionality: the resources are available, but guarded. And "correct maladjustments in their balance of payments" names whose balance of payments gets corrected — the member drawing the money, which is the deficit country.

## The argument

Keynes's case for symmetric adjustment, as he put it in the British proposals published in April 1943:

1. **A deficit in one country is a surplus in another; the imbalance is a single fact with two sides.** *In words:* nobody runs a deficit alone.
2. **Under the pre-war gold standard the entire burden of correction fell on the debtor,** who had to deflate wages and prices until imports fell, while the surplus country could simply accumulate reserves. *In words:* the rules punished only one of the two parties to the imbalance.
3. **Deflation in debtor countries contracts world demand, which reduces everyone's exports.** *In words:* forcing the debtor to shrink makes the world poorer, creditors included.
4. **So a good system charges both sides for persistent imbalance.** Keynes's mechanism: each member gets a quota fixed by its pre-war trade, and pays a charge to the Union's reserve fund when its average balance — credit *or* debit — exceeds a quarter of that quota, with a further charge above a half. A large persistent creditor also has to discuss corrective measures with the governing board: revaluation, domestic expansion, lower tariffs, foreign lending. *In words:* running a big surplus should cost you something too.
5. **And financing should be large and close to automatic,** through overdrafts denominated in *bancor* rather than a fixed pot of subscribed money. *In words:* a country in trouble should not have to beg.
6. **Otherwise debtors will adjust by restricting trade and payments,** which is what the 1930s showed. *In words:* if you deny a country finance, it does not adjust gracefully; it closes its borders.

The American reply was not that Keynes had misdescribed the 1930s. It was that premise 5 gives debtors an open line of credit backed largely by one country's exports, that the Congress would never appropriate an unlimited liability, and that credit without conditions invites postponement of adjustment rather than adjustment. The 1944 compromise conceded premise 1 in a single clause: if demand for one member's currency exhausts the Fund's supply of it, the Fund "shall formally declare such currency scarce," and other members may then ration it. The clause has never been invoked.

## Map of positions

| | Keynes's Clearing Union (1943) | White's Stabilization Fund (1943) | Articles of Agreement (1944) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Reserve asset** | *Bancor*, a new unit of account | Gold and subscribed national currencies | Gold and the dollar; par values set in gold or in the 1944 dollar |
| **Size** | Overdrafts scaled to pre-war trade, several times larger | Roughly 5 billion dollars | About 8.8 billion dollars of quotas |
| **Who adjusts** | Both sides, by design | The deficit country | The deficit country |
| **Creditor obligation** | Charges on large credit balances; corrective measures discussed with the board | None | None, except the scarce-currency clause |
| **Access to finance** | Close to automatic within quota | At the Fund's discretion | Limited and guarded: purchases capped at 25 percent of quota in twelve months, Fund holdings of a member's currency capped at 200 percent of quota |

The World Bank (IBRD), with authorized capital of 10 billion dollars, was the conference's other creation: long-term project lending, not balance-of-payments finance. The Fund's own machinery filled in later. The stand-by arrangement — a pre-approved line a member can draw on if it meets agreed conditions — dates from 1952, and binding performance criteria came later still, from the late 1950s. In 1956 Argentina's official creditors met in Paris to reschedule what it owed them, and that ad hoc meeting became the **Paris Club**, the standing forum where government creditors restructure together, in practice alongside an IMF program.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean: one imbalance, two designs).** Country D runs a deficit of about 4 billion dollars against country S over three years. D's quota is 6 billion, S's is 8 billion (illustrative figures).

*Under Keynes's plan.* D's debit balance of 4 billion exceeds a quarter of its quota (1.5 billion) by 2.5 billion, and exceeds half (3 billion) by 1 billion. Charge: $0.01 \times 2.5 + 0.01 \times 1.0 = 0.035$ billion, i.e. **35 million dollars a year**. S's credit balance of 4 billion exceeds a quarter of its quota (2 billion) by 2 billion and does not reach half (4 billion). Charge: $0.01 \times 2.0 =$ **20 million dollars**. Both are billed; the creditor also owes the board an explanation. (Using end balances as the average, to keep the arithmetic visible.)

*Under the Articles.* D may buy currency up to 25 percent of quota in any twelve months, or 1.5 billion a year — 4.5 billion over three years, just enough — and only if the Fund is satisfied, with more conditions attached the deeper D goes. S pays nothing and explains nothing, unless its currency is declared scarce. Same imbalance, two different answers to "whose problem is this?"

**Example 2 (hard: the system's afterlife, 1971–1981).** On 15 August 1971 Nixon suspended the dollar's convertibility into gold, and by 1973 the major currencies floated. Then oil prices roughly quadrupled over 1973–74. Oil exporters ran enormous surpluses; oil importers, rich and poor, ran deficits.

Now ask Keynes's question of the new arrangement. The surpluses were deposited in banks, largely in the offshore Eurodollar market. The banks lent them on to oil-importing governments and state enterprises through **syndicated loans**: many banks sharing one large credit, priced not at a fixed coupon but at a floating rate — the London interbank offered rate, later standardized as LIBOR, plus a spread, reset every six months. Deficit countries were financed lavishly and asked for almost nothing in return: no performance criteria, no board discussion. Non-oil developing countries' external debt rose from roughly 130 billion dollars in 1973 to something like 470 billion by 1980 on IMF-derived figures (round numbers; the series are revised). Through much of the decade, inflation left real interest rates on that debt near zero or negative.

Two readings of the same decade, both held by serious people. **Recycling as success:** private banks did in months what no official institution had the resources to do, and the world avoided the trade collapse that followed the shock of 1931. **Recycling as the fuse:** the borrower now carried the interest-rate risk, maturities were short, no conditionality slowed the borrowing, and bankers reasoned — in a remark usually attributed to Citibank's Walter Wriston — that sovereigns do not go bankrupt. When American policy drove interest rates to their 1981 peak, every one of those loans repriced at once. Which reading you take determines what you think 1982 was: a liquidity accident or a design flaw. That is Lesson 5.4's crux, and the models behind it are [`economics-of-debt`](../../economics-of-debt/syllabus.md)'s.

## Watch out

- **Bretton Woods was not a gold standard.** Members pegged to the dollar (or to gold); only foreign official holders could convert dollars into US gold, at 35 dollars an ounce. Par values were adjustable with the Fund's concurrence for a "fundamental disequilibrium" — a phrase the Articles never define.
- **Anachronism trap: the word "conditionality" is not in the 1944 text,** and neither are performance criteria or structural adjustment. The Articles say "adequate safeguards"; the machinery familiar from the 1980s was built up by board decisions from 1952 onwards. Reading the later apparatus back into 1944 makes the founders look more coercive, and White's plan more explicit, than they were.
- **The Fund is not the Bank.** Short-term balance-of-payments credit against a member's own currency is not the same instrument as a long-term development loan. Blurring them makes the 1970s look like a failure of "the Bretton Woods institutions" when the lending that mattered was private.
- **"Petrodollar recycling" does not mean oil exporters lent to oil importers.** Banks stood in the middle, borrowing short from depositors and lending long to sovereigns — and passing the interest-rate risk to the borrower through the floating rate. Who bore which risk is the whole story.

## One-liner

> Bretton Woods decided that the deficit country adjusts; the 1970s decided that it could postpone adjusting, as long as it borrowed at whatever rate the next reset brought.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢) *(Formal.)*** All figures illustrative. A developing country owes 20 billion dollars of syndicated bank debt, priced at LIBOR plus 1.0 percentage point, reset every six months, principal repaid at maturity. Its exports are 25 billion dollars a year.
(a) Compute the annual interest bill when LIBOR is 8 percent and when LIBOR is 16 percent.
(b) Express each as a percentage of exports.
(c) By what percentage would exports have to grow to restore the ratio in (b) to its lower value?
(d) If instead the extra interest in the high-rate year is met by new borrowing on the same terms, what is the following year's interest bill? One sentence: who would have borne this risk had the debt been fixed-rate bonds?

**P2 (🟡) *(Exegetical.)*** The Articles' scarce-currency clause (Article VII, Section 3) provides that when demand for a member's currency threatens the Fund's ability to supply it, the Fund "shall formally declare such currency scarce," and that such a declaration authorizes any member, after consulting the Fund, "temporarily to impose limitations on the freedom of exchange operations in the scarce currency."
Using this clause and the Source passage (Article I(v)): (a) does the treaty place any burden of adjustment on a persistent surplus country, and which words decide it? (b) Name one feature of the clause that limits how far it goes toward Keynes's premise 4. 150 words or fewer in total.

**P3 (🔴, optional) *(Exegetical.)*** Keynes held that persistent surpluses are as much a cause of world imbalance as persistent deficits, and should be charged accordingly; the American negotiators held that financing must be limited and conditional. Name the single premise in the lesson's reconstruction that the American side must deny (or sharply weaken) for its position to stand, state the strongest reason it had for denying it, and say what evidence from 1945–1975 would bear on the dispute. 150 words or fewer.

**P2** *(Exegetical.)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** essentially no affirmative burden. Article I(v) puts correction on the member drawing resources — "correct maladjustments in **their** balance of payments" — i.e. the deficit country; and the scarce-currency clause imposes nothing on the surplus country itself. Its operative words authorize *other members* to impose exchange limitations; the words that decide are "declare such currency scarce" (a finding about the Fund's holdings, not about the surplus country's conduct) and the permission granted to members to restrict, which is a right of others rather than a duty of the creditor.
**Must hit, strict (b):** any one of — the trigger is the Fund's supply of the currency, not the size or persistence of the surplus; the consequence is discriminatory trade or exchange restriction by others, not a charge or corrective obligation on the creditor; the limitations are "temporary" and require consultation; the Fund has discretion whether to declare. Keynes's premise 4 wanted an automatic, priced penalty on the creditor plus a duty to discuss corrective measures; this gives none of that.
**Wrong turns:** reading the clause as a penalty on the surplus country; saying the treaty is silent on creditors altogether (the clause exists, and is the concession); arguing about whether the clause was wise (not asked, and evaluative).
**Model answer:** Almost none. Article I(v) makes correction the job of the member drawing resources — its own balance of payments is what gets corrected — and the scarce-currency clause imposes no obligation on the creditor. Its operative words let other members "temporarily impose limitations" on dealings in the scarce currency; the burden it creates is borne by those who need the currency, and the trigger is the Fund's dwindling holdings, not the surplus itself. Limiting feature: the clause is discretionary and defensive. Keynes wanted an automatic charge on any large credit balance and a duty on the creditor to discuss revaluation or expansion; the treaty offers a permission to ration, exercisable by others, after the Fund decides.

---

**P3** *(Exegetical.)*

**Must hit, strict:** premise 5 — that financing should be large and near-automatic — is the one the Americans deny outright; a good answer may instead target premise 4 (charges on creditors) provided it explains that the US denial is of the *obligation*, not of the arithmetic of premise 1. The strongest reason: an automatic overdraft facility of that size is an open-ended claim on the resources of the one certain creditor, which no legislature would grant, and unconditional credit lets a debtor postpone correcting the underlying imbalance rather than correct it (a moral-hazard argument, not a denial that surpluses have two sides).
**Must hit, strict (evidence):** something that would distinguish the two stories — whether deficit countries with easy access did adjust or postponed; whether the postwar dollar shortage was relieved by Fund drawings or by unilateral American transfer (Marshall aid) and by the 1949 devaluations; whether surplus countries' later persistent surpluses transmitted deflationary pressure; how the scarce-currency clause fared in practice, never once invoked.
**Wrong turns:** naming premise 1 or 2 (the Americans did not dispute the description of the 1930s); answering with a verdict on who was right; citing evidence from after 1975, which the question excludes.
**Model answer:** Premise 5. The Americans accepted that imbalance has two sides but denied that finance should be large and automatic: an unlimited overdraft in a unit like *bancor* is a claim on US goods that Congress cannot commit to, and credit without conditions rewards postponement rather than correction — so access must be capped and guarded. Evidence that bears on it: whether early drawers under stand-by arrangements actually adjusted or merely delayed; whether the postwar dollar shortage was in fact resolved by Fund resources or by Marshall aid and the 1949 devaluations, which suggests the Fund was too small for the task Keynes assigned it; and whether the scarce-currency clause's total disuse reflects creditor discipline or a mechanism designed never to bite.

## Flashback

**From Lesson 5.1 (War debts and reparations):** *(Formal.)* Take Keynes's 1919 table of wartime loans between governments (million dollars).
