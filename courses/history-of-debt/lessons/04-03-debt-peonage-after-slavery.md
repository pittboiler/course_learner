# A History of Debt · Lesson 4.3: Debt peonage after slavery

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 4: Debt, nation and empire in the long nineteenth century · Builds on: [4.2 Debtors' prisons and the invention of bankruptcy](04-02-debtors-prisons-and-the-invention-of-bankruptcy.md), [1.5 *Nexum* and the Roman plebs](01-05-nexum-and-the-roman-plebs.md) · Unlocks: [4.4 Haiti's independence debt](04-04-haitis-independence-debt.md)

## Why this matters

In 1865 the Thirteenth Amendment abolished slavery. Within a generation, many Black farm workers in the cotton South were again tied to a particular employer. Most were not held by title. They were held by a debt, by a lien on their crop, or by a criminal statute that made quitting a job look like fraud. [1.5](01-05-nexum-and-the-roman-plebs.md) asked what Rome's abolition of *nexum* left in place. This lesson asks the same question of American emancipation: once owning a person is illegal, which legal tools can still bind a person through debt, and which did the federal courts strike down?

## The idea

Separate three layers, because the historical debates turn on keeping them apart.

**1. Contract and credit (civil).** After the war, freedpeople wanted land, and planters wanted gang labor under supervision. Neither got its way. What emerged was *sharecropping*: a family worked its own plot, the landowner supplied land, housing, a mule, tools and seed, and the crop was divided at harvest, often half and half. A *share tenant* supplied his own mule and tools and paid a smaller share as rent. Nobody had cash until the crop was sold, so someone had to "furnish" the family with food, clothing and fertilizer from spring to settlement. That was the landlord or a *furnishing merchant*. Southern legislatures passed **crop lien** laws from the late 1860s: the creditor took a legal claim on the growing crop as security. At settlement the crop was sold and the account squared. If the crop fell short, the balance carried into next year, and so did the lien.

Goods on credit carried a *credit price* above the cash price. That markup is interest under another name. Roger Ransom and Richard Sutch (*One Kind of Freedom*, 1977) estimated implicit annual rates on the order of 60 percent (a figure critics dispute). They argued that each merchant held a *territorial monopoly*, since a farmer with an outstanding lien could not take his crop to a rival. They also argued that merchants insisted on cotton as collateral, which pushed farmers away from growing their own food and deeper into credit. That is the **lock-in** thesis.

**2. Criminal law attached to contract.** A civil debt lets the creditor sue, and a worker with nothing loses little. So several states, in forms that were race-neutral on their face, made leaving a labor contract a crime. The **Black Codes** of 1865–66 (Mississippi's and South Carolina's first) had done this openly: vagrancy laws for the unemployed, penalties for breaking a labor contract, penalties for "enticing" another's worker. Federal civil rights legislation and Reconstruction swept the explicit codes away. Their successors were written without racial terms: false-pretenses statutes aimed at labor advances, enticement and vagrancy laws, and **criminal-surety** systems.

**3. The state as supplier of labor.** The Thirteenth Amendment excepts punishment for crime. Southern states leased convicts to mines, railroads and plantations (*convict leasing*; Alabama ended its state system in 1928; see Douglas Blackmon, *Slavery by Another Name*, 2008). This lesson only touches it, but it is the backstop that made a criminal charge so coercive.

*Peonage*, in the sense the courts used, sits where layers 1 and 2 meet: labor compelled because of a debt.

## Source

*Bailey v. Alabama*, 219 U.S. 219 (1911), Justice Hughes for the Court (a US federal opinion, public domain). Alonzo Bailey signed a one-year contract as a farm hand, took an advance of 15 dollars to be deducted from his wages, and quit after about a month. Alabama's statute made that refusal, without repayment, prima facie evidence of intent to defraud. Alabama's evidence rules also barred him from testifying about his own uncommunicated intent. He was fined 30 dollars and, in default, sentenced to hard labor.

> If it cannot punish the servant as a criminal for the mere failure or refusal to serve without paying his debt, it is not permitted to accomplish the same result by creating a statutory presumption

Read the structure. "If it cannot … it is not permitted …" is an argument from the forbidden end to the forbidden means. The Court does not find that Alabama intended to oppress. It judges the statute by what it does in operation.

## The argument

Hughes's reasoning, reconstructed in the Court's terms:

1. **The Thirteenth Amendment aimed to make labor free**, abolishing any control by which one person's service is coerced for another's benefit. *In words:* the amendment targets compulsion to work, not just the legal title "slave."
2. **The Peonage Act of 1867 (passed with New Mexico in view) enforces it** by abolishing service or labor performed as peons to pay off a debt, and voiding any state law that maintains it. *In words:* Congress made working off a debt under compulsion illegal everywhere.
3. **Peonage is compulsory service based on indebtedness, however it began** (adopted from *Clyatt v. United States*, 1905). A debtor may choose to work off a debt, but he must stay free to quit and be sued instead. *In words:* a voluntary start doesn't cure a compelled continuation.
4. **A state cannot jail a worker simply for quitting while he owes an advance.** *In words:* that is peonage with a prison as the enforcer.
5. **Alabama's presumption reaches that result indirectly.** Failure to work plus nonrepayment convicts, and the accused cannot testify to his real intent, so the "fraud" is in practice just the unpaid debt. *In words:* calling it fraud changes the label, not the operation.
6. **∴ The statute, so far as it creates the presumption, violates the Thirteenth Amendment and the Peonage Act.**

**Holmes's dissent** (Lurton joining) denies step 5's inference. A state may make breach of contract a crime, as it may make it a civil wrong. The presumption only lets a jury decide, and working in a jail for no private master is not peonage. *In words:* legal pressure to keep a promise is not bodily compulsion by a master.

Both opinions insist the case be decided as if it arose outside the South. Hughes set aside the fact that Bailey was Black. Historians have documented that Booker T. Washington quietly helped support the litigation.

## Timeline

| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| 1865 | Thirteenth Amendment ratified (with its exception for punishment for crime); first Black Codes |
| 1866–67 | Civil Rights Act and military Reconstruction override the explicit codes |

| 1877 | End of federal Reconstruction; enforcement shifts to state courts |
| 1903, 1907 | Alabama adds, then enlarges, the prima facie presumption in its labor-fraud statute |
| 1905 | *Clyatt*: Peonage Act upheld against private persons; conviction reversed for want of proof |
| 1911 | *Bailey v. Alabama* strikes the presumption |
| 1914 | *United States v. Reynolds* strikes Alabama's criminal-surety system |
| 1928 | Alabama ends state convict leasing |
| 1944 | *Pollock v. Williams* strikes Florida's version of the Bailey statute, still on the books |

The last row is the point. Bailey struck down one statute; similar laws and the practice behind them outlived it by decades (Pete Daniel, *The Shadow of Slavery*, 1972).

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean: the criminal-surety system in *Reynolds*).** Ed Rivers was convicted of petit larceny in Alabama and fined 15 dollars plus 43.75 dollars in costs. J. A. Reynolds acted as his surety and paid, and Rivers signed a contract to work off the debt as a farm hand for nine months and twenty-four days at 6 dollars a month.

Check the arithmetic: the debt is $15 + 43.75 = 58.75$ dollars, and $6 \times 9.8 = 58.80$ dollars. The contract simply converts the debt into months of labor. Alabama law made breaking that contract a crime. Rivers quit, was prosecuted at Reynolds's instance, fined one cent plus 87.05 dollars in costs, and signed with a new surety for over fourteen months. The Court (Justice Day) noted he could have served at most about 68 days at county hard labor for the original offense. The system kept the convict, in the Court's phrase, "chained to an everturning wheel of servitude." Run the Bailey test: the service is owed to a private creditor, it liquidates a debt, and quitting brings rearrest. That is peonage, and the Court held it so. Holmes concurred.

**Example 2 (hard: lock-in without a statute).** A sharecropper ends 1890 owing 40 dollars to the landlord who furnished him. No criminal law makes his leaving a crime. Still, the lien follows the crop, neighboring landlords may refuse to hire a man "in debt" (and enticement laws let his creditor sue them), and the local sheriff may be the landlord's ally.

Is this peonage? Under *Clyatt*'s definition only if the service is *compelled*, and on paper it isn't: he may leave and be sued. Here the legal category strains. Ransom and Sutch read debt, monopoly credit and racial exclusion as a system of lock-in regardless of formal freedom. Their critics, including Robert Higgs (*Competition and Coercion*, 1977) and Gavin Wright (*Old South, New South*, 1986), point to heavy year-end mobility of tenants and landlords competing for labor, and read high credit prices as the cost of lending to poor borrowers against a risky crop. On that reading, where coercion existed it came from violence and the criminal law, not from the credit market. The court cases cannot settle this: they reached statutes and prosecutions, not the everyday settlement of accounts.

## Watch out

- **You might think *Bailey* ended peonage.** It voided a presumption. Florida's similar statute was still being enforced in the 1940s (*Pollock*, 1944), and prosecutions under the federal act continued long after.
- **You might think "peonage" means any heavy debt.** Legally it is *compelled service* tied to a debt. A crushing debt a worker is free to walk away from and be sued for is not peonage in the courts' sense, which is exactly why the lock-in debate is about economics, not law.
- **Anachronism trap: reading *Bailey* as a race-discrimination holding.** Hughes explicitly declined to decide on equal protection grounds. The case is a Thirteenth Amendment and statutory holding about the freedom to quit. Its racial effect was obvious to contemporaries, and the Court still chose not to rest on it.
- **You might treat the 60 percent figure as settled.** It depends on how long goods were actually outstanding and on default risk, which is precisely what critics contest.

## One-liner

> After emancipation, a debt bound a worker only when the law made quitting a crime, and the Court struck down those laws one statute at a time, while historians still dispute how far credit alone did the binding.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢) *(Exegetical.)*** Two sentences from *Bailey v. Alabama* (1911). Hughes, for the Court, on the statutory presumption: "an instrument of compulsion peculiarly effective as against the poor and the ignorant, its most likely victims." Holmes, dissenting: "Peonage is service to a private master at which a man is kept by bodily compulsion against his will." (a) What does each sentence imply about whether a threat of *criminal punishment* can make service compulsory? Name the words that decide it. (b) Hughes's sentence names the statute's likely victims. Does that make the majority's holding one about discrimination? Cite the lesson's account of the opinion. 150 words or fewer in total.

**P2 (🟡) *(Formal.)*** On 1 March a furnishing merchant sells cornmeal whose cash price is 100 cents for a credit price of 130 cents, payable at settlement on 1 November.
(a) Compute the implicit annual rate, both simple and compounded monthly (effective annual).
(b) A family actually draws goods of equal cash value on the first of each month from March through August, all at the 30 percent markup, all payable 1 November. Using the average time outstanding, compute the simple annual rate.
(c) Suppose the merchant's own cost of funds is 10 percent a year compounded, and an account that fails pays nothing. For the single March sale in (a), what share of accounts must fail for the 30 percent markup merely to break even? Say in one sentence which side of the lock-in debate this number is useful to.

**P3 (🔴, optional) *(Exegetical.)*** Ransom and Sutch say high credit prices reflected merchants' territorial monopoly and locked farmers into debt and cotton. Their critics say credit prices reflected risk and cost, and farmers moved freely between landlords. Name the single premise one side must deny, and say what evidence from county records would move it. 150 words or fewer.

<details>
<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical, strict.)*

**Must hit, strict (a):**
- Hughes: yes. The presumption is an "instrument of compulsion," so fear of criminal punishment compels service (the majority adopts a state court's view that fear of a criminal statute compels more powerfully than any guard an employer could post).
- Holmes: no. Peonage requires "bodily compulsion" by a "private master." A criminal penalty imposed by the state, even served in jail, is neither.
- The deciding words are "compulsion" versus "bodily compulsion" and "private master."

**Must hit, strict (b):**
- No. The holding rests on the Thirteenth Amendment and the Peonage Act. Hughes explicitly set aside Bailey's race and declined an equal-protection ground.
- "Poor and ignorant" describes how the law operates on those with no means to repay or rebut the presumption. It is a class description, not a racial one.

**Wrong turns:** saying Holmes approved of peonage (he defined it narrowly and denied this was it); reading "most likely victims" as a finding of discriminatory intent. The Court expressly imputed no motive.

**Model answer:** (a) For Hughes a criminal threat compels: the presumption is "an instrument of compulsion." For Holmes compulsion must be "bodily" and by a "private master," and a state penalty for breach is neither, so no peonage. (b) No. The Court decided under the Thirteenth Amendment and the 1867 Act, set aside Bailey's race, and declined the equal-protection route. "Poor and ignorant" names who cannot repay an advance or rebut the presumption, a point about how the statute works, not a finding of racial discrimination.

---

**P2** *(Formal.)* Rates rounded to one decimal place.

**Must hit, strict:**
- (a) Markup $m = 0.30$ over $t = 8$ months. Simple: $0.30 \times \tfrac{12}{8} = 0.45$, i.e. **45.0%**. Effective: $1.30^{12/8} - 1 = 1.4822 - 1$, i.e. **48.2%**.
- (b) Months outstanding: $8, 7, 6, 5, 4, 3$, average $\tfrac{33}{6} = 5.5$. Simple annual rate $0.30 \times \tfrac{12}{5.5} = 0.6545$, i.e. **65.5%**. (Optional: solving $\sum (1+r)^{d_i} = 6 \times 1.30$ gives $r \approx 4.8\%$ a month, about 76.0% effective annual.)
- (c) Merchant's cost factor over 8 months: $1.10^{8/12} = 1.0656$. Break-even requires $(1-p)\times 1.30 = 1.0656$, so $1-p = 0.8197$ and $p \approx$ **18.0%**. The number serves the *critics* if default and loss rates near that level can be documented, because the markup would then be a cost of lending and not a monopoly rent. It serves Ransom and Sutch if actual losses were far lower.

**Wrong turns:** annualizing (b) over 8 months (understates the rate, since most goods were outstanding for less time); in (c), treating the 10 percent cost as simple over a year instead of scaling it to 8 months (gives $p \approx 15.4\%$; allow it with the method shown, but it is not the question as set).

**Model answer:** as above. One sentence for (c): about 18 percent of accounts failing would make the markup no more than break-even, so documented losses near that level support the risk-and-cost reading.

---

**P3** *(Exegetical, strict on naming one premise.)*

**Must hit, strict:**
- The crux: *whether the rural credit market was competitive, so that a farmer could take his crop and business to another merchant or landlord at the next settlement.* Ransom and Sutch deny it (territorial monopoly plus the lien); the critics affirm it.
- Both sides agree credit prices were high. The dispute is what explains them.
- Evidence: the number of merchants per locality and their spacing; how often tenants changed landlord or merchant between seasons, and whether indebted ones moved as often; realized default and loss rates on merchants' books; whether markups fell where rival stores were close.

**Wrong turns:** naming "were credit prices high?" (agreed); naming racism as the crux (both sides grant discrimination; the dispute is whether the credit market itself bound farmers); listing two premises.

**Model answer:** The premise in dispute is that a Southern farmer could, at settlement, change his merchant or landlord and get credit elsewhere on similar terms. Ransom and Sutch deny it: the lien and the distance between stores gave each merchant a local monopoly, so markups were rents, and debt kept farmers in cotton. Critics affirm it: tenants moved often, and markups covered the risk of lending against a single crop. County evidence that would move either side: store counts and distances, tenant turnover by debt status, merchants' ledgers showing actual losses, and whether markups were lower where stores competed.

## Flashback

**From Lesson 4.1 (Hamilton's assumption plan):** In 1841–42 eight states and the Florida Territory defaulted on debts run up for canals and banks, and Congress refused to assume them. Take Hamilton's six premises for assumption as Lesson 4.1 reconstructs them. *(Exegetical.)* (a) Which of them could an 1842 assumption bill reuse unchanged, and which one do the 1840s debts lack the factual basis for? (b) In 1790 Virginia's objection was that its taxpayers would be taxed to service Massachusetts's debt. Say what that objection becomes in 1842, and name the question Hamilton's premises never answer. Three sentences or fewer per part.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):**
- Premises 1, 2 and 4 carry over unchanged, because each is a general claim about credit rather than about the Revolution: a state will need to borrow again, a doubtful borrower pays a standing premium, and a funded debt that is an object of confidence does economic work an unfunded one does not. (Premise 6, that a divided creditor class works against the national credit, is also still available in form.)
- **Premise 3 is the one without a factual basis in 1842.** Its force came from the Revolutionary debt's extra moral weight — "It was the price of liberty" — and from the states having borrowed for the common cause. Canals and banks were each state's own venture, so the special claim on the Union is gone.

**Must hit, strict (b):**
- The objection becomes: states that stayed out of the boom, or taxed themselves to pay what they borrowed, would now be taxed for states that speculated — and because the borrowing was each state's own choice rather than a shared war, it also raises what assumption would teach states about who stands behind their debts.
- The question never answered: **who pays.** Hamilton's premises compare *creditors* and say nothing about the distribution of the burden among taxpayers.

**Wrong turns:** calling the 1842 refusal a reversal of 1790, when 1790 was a founding bargain and the later refusal simply left states answerable for their own borrowing; delivering a verdict on whether Congress should have assumed, which is not asked; answering (a) with premise 5's dollar figures, which are 1790-specific detail rather than a ground that fails.

**Model answer:** (a) Premises 1, 2 and 4 hold as written: a state will borrow again, doubtful credit is a permanent tax, and a properly funded debt does work no unfunded one does. Premise 3 fails — the Revolutionary debt was "the price of liberty," borrowed for the common cause, while the 1840s debts were each state's own canals and banks. (b) Virginia's complaint becomes the prudent states' complaint: those that abstained or taxed themselves to repay would be taxed for those that speculated, and this time the spending was nobody's common cause. Hamilton's premises weigh what creditors are owed and never reach the question of who should be taxed to pay them.

</details>
