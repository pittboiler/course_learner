# A History of Debt · Lesson 1.5: *Nexum* and the Roman plebs

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 1: Ancient debt: bondage and clean slates · Builds on: [1.4 Solon's shaking-off of burdens](01-04-solons-shaking-off-of-burdens.md) · Unlocks: [2.1 Commerce around the prohibition](02-01-commerce-around-the-prohibition.md)

## Why this matters

Mesopotamian kings wiped slates from above, and Solon rewrote Athenian law in one stroke. Rome did neither. Its debt crisis ran for about two centuries as a fight between two orders of citizens: plebeian soldiers who owed, and patrician creditors who sat in the senate. Along the way it produced the most frightening debt law in antiquity, a strike that created the tribunate, and a statute whose principle still sounds modern: *the debtor's goods, not his body, answer for the loan*. It also left a question every later abolition of debt bondage raises (4.2, 4.3): when the law stops a creditor taking a person, does bondage end, or does it just move into a courtroom?

## The idea

Picture a smallholder in early Rome. He farms a few acres, serves in the legion every summer, and pays the war tax. A bad campaign season (crops burned, cattle driven off) leaves him needing seed and bread. He has one asset a creditor really wants: his labor.

Rome gave him two routes into bondage.

1. **By contract: *nexum*.** A formal act "by bronze and scales" (*per aes et libram*): weighed bronze, a scale-holder, witnesses, set words. By it a free man bound himself (Varro says, his labor) to the lender until he paid. He became *nexus*, "bound." He stayed legally free, a citizen, but he worked for and could be chained by his creditor.
2. **By judgment: *addictio*.** A debtor who lost in court and still did not pay could be seized and assigned (*addictus*) to the creditor by the magistrate.

What exactly *nexum* was is disputed. Our sources are late (Livy and Varro wrote in the first century BC) and the institution had been dead for two and a half centuries. Was it a loan that let the creditor seize the debtor without going to court? A self-sale into service until the debt was worked off? A general name for bronze-and-scales transactions? Romanists still argue about it. What is secure is the social fact: citizens in chains in creditors' houses, and a plebs that treated that as the central injustice of the Republic.

## Source

The Twelve Tables (c. 451–450 BC), conventionally numbered Table III, survive for this procedure only in quotation. Aulus Gellius, writing in the second century AD, has a jurist recite them (*Attic Nights* XX.1.45–49). After a confessed or judged debt the debtor has 30 days. Then the creditor may lay hands on him, bring him before the magistrate, and, if no one pays or stands surety, take him home in chains. Gellius adds that he was held for 60 days and brought to the assembly on three successive market days (*nundinae*), with the sum announced. On the third he could be put to death or sold abroad "across the Tiber." If there were several creditors, the law said:

> *Tertiis nundinis partis secanto. Si plus minusve secuerunt, se fraude esto.*
>
> On the third market day let them cut shares. If they cut more or less, let it be without penalty.
>
> (the lesson's translation)

Read *partis*, "shares": shares of *what*? Gellius's jurist takes it literally, the debtor's body, and defends it as a terror meant never to be used. He adds that he never read or heard of anyone being cut up (XX.1.52). A minority of modern scholars read it as a division of the debtor's value or estate. The Latin does not settle it.

## The argument

Two arguments collide in the sources. State each in its holders' terms.

**The creditor's case, as Gellius's jurist Sextus Caecilius makes it (XX.1).**

1. Credit rests on *fides*, good faith, which Rome held sacred. *In words:* lending happens only if promises are kept.
2. A poor debtor has nothing to seize but himself. *In words:* without the person as security, the poor could not borrow at all.
3. So the law made default fearsome, "to sanction good faith" (*sanciendae fidei gratia*), up to the threat of the cutting. *In words:* the cruelty was a deterrent aimed at keeping the promise, not at the debtor.
4. A terror that works is rarely carried out. *In words:* judge the rule by how often it had to be used.

**The plebs' case, as Livy tells it (II.23–33).**

1. The debtor is a citizen and a soldier who fights for the city's liberty. *In words:* his service is what keeps Rome free.
2. War itself caused his debts: ravaged land, war tax, absence from the farm. *In words:* he did not borrow out of vice.
3. A citizen enslaved by fellow citizens is freer abroad than at home. *In words:* bondage for debt contradicts the citizenship it serves.
4. So the plebs may refuse service (the levy boycotts, the secession) until the bondage stops. *In words:* withdrawing labor is legitimate leverage against a law that takes the laborer.

The **settlement principle** of 326 BC answers both. Credit may still be secured, but on property: *goods, not person*. Whether that fully conceded the plebeian premise, or just changed the route to the same bondage, is the lesson's crux.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean: running the Twelve Tables procedure).** Gaius owes 1,000 asses to two creditors and loses in court. Walk the clock.

- **Days 1–30:** grace. He can pay or find money.
- **Day 31:** *manus iniectio*, laying on of hands. A creditor drags him before the magistrate. If no one pays or vouches for him, the creditor leads him off bound, in fetters of fifteen pounds (the texts disagree whether that is a maximum or a minimum), and must give him a pound of spelt a day if he does not live on his own.
- **Next 60 days:** chained, but free to settle (Gellius says there was a right to come to terms). On three consecutive market days (roughly every eight days) he is paraded in the Comitium and his debt proclaimed, so any kinsman, patron or friend can pay.
- **Third market day:** sale "across the Tiber," outside Roman territory, since a citizen could not be a slave at home, or death, or the division of "shares."

Notice the design. Almost every step is publicity and delay. The law squeezes the debtor's *network* (family, patron, neighbors) far more than it plans to use the knife. That is exactly Caecilius's premise 4. Notice too what is missing: nothing here requires a *nexum* contract. This is execution after judgment, and that matters for P1 and P3.

**Example 2 (hard: was Caesar's settlement a clean slate?).** In 49 BC credit froze across Italy. Debtors expected *tabulae novae*, the cancellation Catiline had promised, and creditors hoarded. Caesar refused cancellation. He tells us (*Civil War* III.1) that he appointed arbitrators to value debtors' land and goods at pre-war prices and hand them to creditors. He presents this as the best way to remove the fear of new account books and to protect debtors' standing. Suetonius (*Julius* 42) adds a second rule: anything already paid as interest, in cash or by written transfer, was deducted from the principal. He estimates creditors lost about a quarter of what they were owed.

Run the tests from 1.2 and 1.4. **Was the debt cancelled?** No: principal survived, reduced. **Was the relief general?** Yes for all debtors, but its size depended on each debtor's history. Someone who had paid years of interest gained a lot; someone who had borrowed last month gained almost nothing. **Who bore the loss?** Creditors, twice over: once through the valuation (property in a war slump counted at boom prices) and once through the interest deduction, which in effect turned an interest-bearing loan into an interest-free one after the fact. The radicals wanted more. Caelius, as praetor in 48, pushed for further relief and was removed from office, and Dolabella agitated for cancellation in 47.

So the case strains the categories. Caesar's measure is not a *misharum* edict or a *seisachtheia*, but it rewrote contracts retroactively, which is what creditors feared from those. Historians who stress his continuity with the Licinian–Sextian deduction of 367 read it as Roman tradition. Those who stress its political timing read it as buying debtors' loyalty while reassuring creditors that the worst was off the table. The lesson's job is to see that both readings fit the same two texts.

## Watch out

- **Anachronism: "debt slavery."** A *nexus* or *addictus* was not a slave in Roman law. He kept his citizenship, and sale into slavery had to happen abroad. Livy's centurion says he was taken "not into slavery only" as rhetoric. The distinction mattered to Romans: it is why abolition could be framed as restoring *liberty* to citizens, not freeing slaves.
- **Livy is not an eyewitness.** He wrote under Augustus, after Catiline and Caesar had made debt the hottest issue in politics. His speeches for plebeians of 495 may carry first-century grievances. Use him for what Romans believed their history was, and hedge on the details.
- **The *Lex Poetelia* did not end debt enforcement.** On Livy's wording it made goods the security and freed the *nexi*. Judgment debtors could still be *addicti*. Gellius, some five centuries later, says he sees many debtors adjudged and chained in his own day (XX.1.51).
- ***Tabulae novae* is not a technical term for total cancellation.** "New books" covered anything from full cancellation to partial write-downs. Check what each proposal actually did.

## One-liner

> Rome moved the security for a debt from the debtor's body to his goods, and argued for the next few centuries about whether a courtroom had simply taken over the chains.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢) *(Exegetical.)*** Livy VIII.28 (Roberts's translation, 1905) gives the senate's proposal after the Publilius scandal:

> "that no man be kept in irons or in the stocks, except such as have been guilty of some crime … and, further, that the goods and not the person of the debtor shall be the security for the debt."

and then the outcome:

> "So the nexi were released, and it was forbidden for any to become nexi in the future."

A reader claims this passage shows the law abolished *all* personal execution for debt, including against debtors judged in court. Which words support that broad reading, which words support a narrow one (abolition of *nexum* only), and what in Example 1 shows why the difference matters? 150 words or fewer.

**P2 (🟡) *(Formal.)*** An invented case. In 53 BC Lucius borrowed 40,000 sesterces at 1 percent a month (12 percent a year, simple interest), paying the interest in full at the end of each year. By 49 BC he has made four interest payments and owes the full principal. Apply Caesar's two rules.
(a) What principal remains after the interest deduction, and what percentage relief is that?
(b) Lucius settles by ceding a vineyard valued at its pre-war price of 16,000 sesterces (its current market price is 12,000) and paying the rest in cash. What does the creditor receive, at market value, against the 40,000 contract? Counting the interest he collected over four years, what did he get back in total, in nominal sesterces, for his 40,000?
(c) Suetonius says creditors lost "about a quarter." For a loan like Lucius's, how many years of paid interest alone produce a 25 percent cut? What does that suggest about the typical loan in 49 BC?

**P3 (🔴, optional) *(Exegetical.)*** (a) An invented op-ed: "Rome ended debt slavery with one law in 326 BC. If a republic could abolish bondage at a stroke 2,300 years ago, we can abolish medical debt at a stroke today." Name the historical analogy doing the work, and one way it breaks. (b) Name the crux between historians who say the *Lex Poetelia Papiria* ended debt bondage in Rome and those who say it relabelled it, and say what evidence would move it. 150 words or fewer in total.

<details>
<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical, strict.)*

**Must hit, strict:**
- **Broad reading:** "the goods and not the person of the debtor shall be the security for the debt" is stated as a general principle with no restriction to *nexum*. "No man be kept in irons or in the stocks" is general too.
- **Narrow reading:** the outcome sentence speaks only of *nexi*: those bound by contract are released and future *nexum* is banned. The exception "except such as have been guilty of some crime" leaves room for bondage in other cases. (The Latin *pecuniae creditae*, "for money lent," restricts the principle to loans. A bonus point, not required.)
- **Why it matters:** Example 1's procedure is execution after *judgment*, which needs no *nexum*. If the law abolished only *nexum*, a judged debtor could still be seized and held (*addictio*). That fits Gellius's report of chained judgment debtors in his own day.

**Wrong turns:** treating Roberts's "crime" as settling that debt could never count (the Latin *noxa* covers wrongful harm, so how it applied to debt is itself an interpretive question); calling the broad reading false instead of showing which words carry it.

**Model answer:** The broad reading rests on the principle "the goods and not the person of the debtor shall be the security," stated without restriction, and on "no man be kept in irons." The narrow reading rests on the outcome clause: it is the *nexi* who are released, and becoming *nexi* that is forbidden. So the one thing the passage certainly records is the end of *nexum*. "Except such as have been guilty of some crime" keeps a category of lawful bondage open, and the Latin limits the principle to money lent. The difference matters because the Twelve Tables' execution runs on a court judgment, not a *nexum* contract. On the narrow reading, a judged debtor could still be led away bound, which is what Gellius says he still saw.

---

**P2** *(Formal.)* Exact arithmetic, no rounding needed except in (c).

**(a)** Annual interest: 40,000 × 0.12 = 4,800. Paid: 4 × 4,800 = 19,200. Principal remaining: 40,000 − 19,200 = **20,800**. Relief: 19,200 / 40,000 = **48 percent**.

**(b)** Vineyard credited at 16,000, so cash owed: 20,800 − 16,000 = 4,800. At market value the creditor receives 12,000 + 4,800 = **16,800** against the 40,000 contract (a 58 percent shortfall, 23,200). Total nominal return over the loan's life: 19,200 interest + 16,800 = **36,000** for 40,000 lent, a nominal loss of 4,000 even before counting time value. Without the valuation rule he would have got exactly 40,000 back: the interest deduction alone turns the loan into an interest-free one. The valuation rule then costs him the further 4,000 (16,000 credited − 12,000 market).

**(c)** Years needed: 0.25 / 0.12 = 2.083, about **2 years and 1 month** (rounded to the month). A four-year borrower got 48 percent. So an average cut of about a quarter suggests many loans were younger, carried lower rates, or had interest unpaid (the deduction counts only interest actually paid). It says nothing about how widely the rule was applied.

**Wrong turns:** deducting interest *owed* rather than *paid*; comparing the vineyard at market value against the pre-war credit (the rule credits pre-war value); treating Suetonius's quarter as a figure for one loan.

---

**P3** *(Exegetical.)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** the analogy is **abolition of personal bondage = cancellation of a class of debts**. It breaks because the *Lex Poetelia* changed the *remedy* (goods, not person), not the *debt*: the *nexi* were released from bondage, but on Livy's wording nothing says their debts were cancelled. (Also acceptable: one law vs a two-century struggle with repeated partial measures; a free citizen's status vs a modern claim with no bondage involved.)

**Must hit, strict (b):** the crux is **whether judgment execution (*addictio*) went on doing the work *nexum* had done**. *Ended* side: contract bondage was gone, and (a view associated with M. I. Finley) Rome turned to chattel slaves from conquest for dependent labor. *Relabelled* side: the court route survived, debt still drove a secession to the Janiculum around 287 (Livy, *Periochae* 11), Gellius saw chained debtors, and Varro mentions *obaerarii*, debt-bound laborers, in Asia, Egypt and Illyricum in his day. Evidence that would move it: how often *addictio* was actually used on Italian smallholders after 300 BC, from legal texts or other documents.

**Wrong turns:** answering "was it good?" (not asked); treating the op-ed's claim that bondage ended "at a stroke" as settled by Livy's single chapter.

**Model answer:** (a) The op-ed equates abolishing bondage with abolishing debts. The *Lex Poetelia* changed what a creditor could seize, not whether the debt was owed. (b) Both sides agree *nexum* ended. They divide on whether execution after judgment kept debtors in bondage by another name. Counts of *addictio* in practice would move it.

</details>

## Flashback

**From Lesson 1.3 (Release laws in ancient Israel):** *(Exegetical.)* Deuteronomy 23:19–20 (Douay–Rheims):

> Thou shalt not lend to thy brother money to usury, nor corn, nor any other thing: But to the stranger.

A reader concludes: "Israel's law banned lending at interest." (a) State the rule the two verses actually give, and name the words that fix its scope. (b) Deuteronomy 15:3 puts a scope clause on the seventh-year release too. Do the two clauses place the outsider on the same side of the line or on opposite sides? In one sentence, say what a loan to a stranger looks like under both rules at once. 100 words or fewer.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):** the ban is on charging increase to "thy brother," a member of the community, and it covers advances of any kind — "money … nor corn, nor any other thing," so barley loans are inside it, not just silver. "But to the stranger" is the exemption that fixes the scope. The verses forbid interest on loans *within* the community; they do not forbid interest as such.

**Must hit, strict (b):** the same side. 15:3 lets the creditor exact repayment from the foreigner while the brother's debt cannot be demanded in the seventh year, so both clauses draw the same membership line, and the outsider falls outside both protections. Under the two together, a loan to a stranger bears interest and survives the release year: an ordinary commercial claim, where the brother's loan is interest-free relief with an expiry date.

**Wrong turns:** reading "usury" here as *excessive* interest, which the pairing with "without usury" for the loan of what the brother "wanteth" does not support; treating the two clauses as opposite because one forbids and the other permits — both exclude the foreigner from the community's protections; importing Challoner's note that scripture "everywhere condemns usury" into the scope of the verse, when the note is a later gloss and the question is what the law reached.

**Model answer:** (a) It bars taking increase from a fellow Israelite on any kind of advance — money, corn or anything else — and expressly allows it with "the stranger." The scope word is "brother." (b) The same side: 15:3 likewise excludes the foreigner, from whom the creditor may exact after the seventh year. So a loan to a stranger both carries interest and outlives the release, which makes it a plain commercial credit, while the brother's loan is relief with a statutory expiry.

</details>
