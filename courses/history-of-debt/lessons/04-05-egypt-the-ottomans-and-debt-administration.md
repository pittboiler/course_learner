# A History of Debt · Lesson 4.5: Egypt, the Ottomans, and debt administration

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 4: Debt, nation and empire in the long nineteenth century · Builds on: [4.4 Haiti's independence debt](04-04-haitis-independence-debt.md), [4.1 Hamilton's assumption plan](04-01-hamiltons-assumption-plan.md) · Unlocks: [4.6 Bondholders, gunboats, and receiverships](04-06-bondholders-gunboats-and-receiverships.md)

## Why this matters

Haiti ([4.4](04-04-haitis-independence-debt.md)) paid a debt forced on it from outside. Egypt borrowed willingly, on the open markets of London and Paris, and still ended up with foreigners collecting its taxes, auditing its budget, sitting in its cabinet and finally, in 1882, occupying its territory. The Ottoman Empire defaulted in the same years and handed its creditors a slice of its revenues too, yet it stayed sovereign. The institutions built for both, the *Caisse de la Dette Publique* in Cairo and the Ottoman Public Debt Administration in Constantinople, were the first international agencies whose job was to run part of a borrowing state for its creditors. Every later argument about conditionality and troikas ([6.2](06-02-the-eurozone-and-greece.md)) sits somewhere on the ladder they built.

## The idea

A lender to a sovereign has a problem no court can fix: there is nothing to seize. The nineteenth-century answer was to get closer and closer to the money before it reached the government. Picture a ladder of creditor control:

1. **Pledge a revenue.** The loan contract names a tax, a railway or an estate as security. It is a promise; the government still collects the money.
2. **Collect the pledged revenue.** Creditors' agents take the pledged tax straight from the local collectors, so the government never touches it.
3. **Audit the budget.** Foreign controllers watch *all* revenue and spending, because a pledged tax is worthless if the rest of the budget collapses.
4. **Sit in the cabinet.** Foreigners run the ministries that spend.
5. **Occupy.** A foreign army guarantees the arrangement.

Egypt climbed all five rungs in about twenty years (1862 to 1882). The Ottoman Empire climbed to rung 2 and stopped. Why the same instrument went so much further in one place is the question this lesson leaves you able to ask precisely.

The engine was a price gap. Egypt's bonds were sold well below face value, but interest was owed on the face value. So the government paid 7 per cent on money it never received.

## Source

Two public-domain witnesses, on opposite sides. Evelyn Baring, later Lord Cromer, was the British Commissioner of the Debt from 1877, then Controller-General, then Britain's ruler of Egypt in all but name (1883–1907). Of the commissioners' position in 1878 he wrote (*Modern Egypt*, 1908, vol. I, ch. III):

> The position of the Commissioners was one of great difficulty. They were the representatives of the bondholders. An obligation, therefore, rested on them to support the just claims of the bondholders by every legitimate means in their power.

He goes on to argue that the interests of bondholders and Egyptian taxpayers, properly understood, largely coincided, and asks whether one could "use the bondholding interest as a lever to improve the Egyptian administration."

Wilfrid Scawen Blunt, poet and anti-imperialist, knew the same men and backed the Egyptian nationalists. On Stephen Cave's 1876 British inquiry into Egyptian finance (*Secret History of the English Occupation of Egypt*, 1907, ch. I), he says Cave should have insisted that

> in justice, and indeed it might have been maintained in law, Ismail's debts were personal not public ones, and should have been so treated.

Cave's failure to insist, Blunt continues, began the political intervention for the bondholders.

## The argument

The case for creditor administration, in the terms its architects used (Goschen's 1876 mission, the commissioners, Cromer):

1. **The debts are obligations of the Egyptian state.** They were contracted by its ruler and secured on its revenues. *In words:* the bondholders lent to Egypt, not to a man.
2. **Egypt cannot currently pay them in full.** Treasury bills were suspended in April 1876, after the Ottoman default had shut the markets. *In words:* the question is no longer whether there will be losses, but how they are shared and policed.
3. **The Khedive's own administration cannot be trusted to keep a settlement.** Earlier arrangements had been broken, accounts hidden, taxes taken years in advance. *In words:* a promise from the same hands that made the mess is worth little.
4. **So the revenues pledged to the debt must be collected by agents answerable to the creditors, and the whole budget watched.** *In words:* move the money out of reach before it can be diverted.
5. **Honest administration serves the taxpayer too.** Stop arbitrary exactions and the peasant gains as much as the bondholder (Cromer's "lever"). *In words:* creditor control is also reform.
6. **Therefore a Caisse, controllers, and if need be a reformed ministry are justified.**

Blunt denies premise 1. If Ismail's debts were really his personal debts, then premises 4–6 make Egyptian peasants pay a ruler's private creditors, and the "reform" of premise 5 is tax collection for foreigners. Nationalists would also press premise 5 directly: whose reform, decided by whom?

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean): nominal versus received.** Ali Coşkun Tunçer's table of Egypt's foreign loans (*Sovereign Debt and International Financial Control*, 2015, drawing on the Council of Foreign Bondholders' reports) lists nine issues from 1862 to 1873. Their face values sum to about 68.8 million pounds, which is where the familiar "68 million" comes from. Sold at their issue prices they would have raised about 55.8 million, or 81 per cent of face. Bankers' commissions and syndicate discounts came off that, so Egypt's net receipts were lower still. Critical accounts put them near 46 million, but that figure is less secure.

Take the 1873 loan: 32 million pounds nominal, 7 per cent coupon, issued at 79.

- Raised at the issue price: $32 \times 0.79 = 25.28$ million pounds.
- Annual interest owed on face value: $32 \times 0.07 = 2.24$ million pounds.
- Interest as a share of money raised: $7/79 = 8.86$ per cent.

*In words:* a "7 per cent loan" cost Egypt nearly 9 per cent on what the issue price delivered, before commissions, and the 6.72 million gap between face and proceeds still had to be repaid. Tunçer's "effective rate" column is exactly this coupon-over-price ratio. Across the whole list, the coupons came to about 4.86 million pounds a year, 8.7 per cent of issue-price proceeds.

**Example 2 (hard): the same instrument, two outcomes.** The Ottoman state stopped paying in 1875–76 on a nominal debt of roughly 200 million pounds. The Decree of Muharrem (December 1881) cut it from about 191 million to about 106 million, a nominal reduction of 44.5 per cent ($1 - 106/191$). In return the empire assigned specified revenues to a council of creditors' representatives: the salt and tobacco monopolies, stamps, spirits, fisheries, the silk tithe, and tributes and surpluses from territories it no longer governed directly. The Administration hired its own tax staff and paid the bondholders directly.

On the ladder, that is rung 2, with nothing above it. Egypt's 1876 settlement also reduced its debt: Goschen and Joubert set the unified debt at 59 million, at 6 per cent plus a 1 per cent sinking fund. But it added controllers over the whole budget (rung 3), then ministers (rung 4). Why the difference?

Several answers are on the table, and a careful reader keeps them apart:

- **Revenue base.** A council can hold a few monopolies without running a state. Egypt's loans had been secured on railways, land tax, the Khedive's estates and nearly all general revenue, so policing the pledge meant policing everything.
- **Great-power rivalry.** Egypt had the Canal, and only two powers really contested it. Constantinople's creditors were spread across Britain, France, Germany, Austria-Hungary and Italy, and none of them wanted another to govern the Sultan.
- **Legal status.** Egypt was an autonomous Ottoman province, and the Sultan could depose its ruler. No one could depose the Sultan in the same way.

Tunçer's own framing treats Egypt as the exception among his cases: there, financial control became a prelude to colonial rule, while elsewhere it coexisted with sovereignty. Which factor weighs most is historians' territory. What the pair shows is that a debt-control agency does not by itself decide how far control goes.

## Watch out

- **Anachronism trap: "odious debt."** Blunt's line that Ismail's debts were "personal not public" sounds like the later doctrine of odious debt, which Alexander Sack systematized in 1927. Blunt was making an argument about justice and about Egyptian law in 1907. Don't credit him with a doctrine that did not yet exist. The doctrine itself belongs to [`philosophy-of-debt`](../../philosophy-of-debt/syllabus.md).
- **You might think the Caisse was an early IMF.** It was set up by decree of a debtor ruler, staffed by creditor governments' nominees, and it served bondholders. It lent nothing and had no mandate for the debtor's economy. The resemblance is conditionality, not design ([5.3](05-03-bretton-woods-and-the-lending-boom.md)).
- **Both witnesses are parties.** Cromer wrote in 1908 after a quarter-century running the system he describes. Blunt wrote as the nationalists' ally, and his account of the Canal-share purchase leans on an antisemitic picture of the Rothschilds as a hidden hand. Label that as his prejudice, not evidence. Read both for what they saw and for what they had reason to say.
- **"Ismail spent too much" is not the whole story.** The cotton bust after 1865, the Panic of 1873 and contagion from the Ottoman default all hit a borrower who was already exposed. Monocausal accounts on either side flatten this.

## One-liner

> Egypt paid 7 per cent on money it never received, and its creditors climbed from pledged revenues to the cabinet; the Ottomans handed over a few monopolies and stopped there.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢) *(Formal.)*** A government issues a bond with face value 100, a 7 per cent annual coupon and repayment at par in 30 years, at an issue price of 70. (a) What interest rate does it pay on the money received each year? (b) How much face value must it issue to raise 1 million pounds at that price, and what is its annual interest bill? (c) Its yield to maturity $y$ solves $70 = \sum_{t=1}^{30} \frac{7}{(1+y)^t} + \frac{100}{(1+y)^{30}}$. Given $y \approx 10.25\%$, explain in one sentence why it is higher than your answer to (a). Then say how bankers' commissions that cut the government's net proceeds to 68 would move it. Round to two decimals.

**P2 (🟡) *(Exegetical.)*** Use the two passages in **Source**. (a) Name the one premise of **The argument** that Cromer's passage takes for granted and Blunt's passage denies, and quote the word or words in each that decide it. (b) Cromer claims the interests of bondholders and taxpayers "properly understood" largely coincided. Say what fact about Egypt in 1876–79 would have to hold for Blunt's denial to break that claim, and what would have to hold for the claim to survive even if Blunt were right. 150 words or fewer in total.

**P3 (🔴, optional) *(Exegetical.)*** Two invented positions on the Caisse (not on the 1882 occupation). **Stabilizer:** creditor administration made Egypt's promises believable again; after 1885 Egypt borrowed at effective rates near 4 per cent, against roughly 7.5–10 per cent before 1876. **Wedge:** it transferred fiscal sovereignty to foreign governments and built the machinery a later occupation took over. Name the single premise the two divide on, and say what comparative evidence would move it. 150 words or fewer.

<details>
<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Formal.)*

**Must hit, strict:**
- (a) Coupon over price: $7/70 = 0.10$, so **10.00 per cent** a year on money received, against 7 per cent nominal.
- (b) Face value needed: $1{,}000{,}000 / 0.70 = 1{,}428{,}571$ pounds, about 1.43 million. Annual interest: $1{,}428{,}571 \times 0.07 = 100{,}000$ pounds, which is 10 per cent of the 1 million actually raised.
- (c) The yield to maturity (10.25 per cent, found numerically) exceeds the current yield because the government must also repay 100 at maturity for every 70 it received. The 30 per cent redemption gain to the holder is an extra cost spread over 30 years. At a net price of 68, solving the same equation gives $y \approx 10.55\%$. Commissions raise the true cost, because the government owes the same coupons and principal on less money.

**Wrong turns:** computing 7 per cent of 70 (4.90) and calling that the rate; saying the yield to maturity is lower because "the bond is repaid"; treating the commission as a one-off fee that leaves the borrowing rate unchanged.

**Model answer:** (a) $7/70 = 10.00\%$. (b) About 1.43 million pounds of face value; 100,000 pounds a year in interest, 10 per cent of the million raised. (c) At maturity the government repays 100 on each bond it sold for 70, so the true cost is above the coupon yield: 10.25 per cent. At a net price of 68 it rises to about 10.55 per cent. (Computed numerically; rounded to two decimals.)

---

**P2** *(Exegetical.)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** Premise 1, that the debts are obligations of the Egyptian state. Cromer's words: "the **just claims** of the bondholders." His commissioners are to press those claims, which presupposes that Egypt owes them. Blunt's words: "**personal not public** ones," reinforced by "in justice" and "in law."

**Must hit, strict (b):** For the denial to break the claim: the debt service was being extracted from taxpayers (taxes collected in advance, harsh collection) for debts Egypt did not rightly owe, so any gain from reform went first to a private ruler's creditors. For the claim to survive: honest collection and an end to arbitrary exactions made taxpayers better off *even while* paying the debt, so both groups gained against the old regime, whoever the debts properly belonged to.

**Wrong turns:** naming premise 5 in (a), which Blunt's quoted passage does not address; reading Cromer as indifferent to the peasants, when his next sentences say the opposite.

**Model answer:** (a) Premise 1. Cromer calls them "just claims," which assumes Egypt owes them; Blunt says the debts were "personal not public." (b) Blunt's denial breaks the identity of interests if the peasants' taxes were serving debts that were not theirs, so the "reform" mainly moved money from them to the Khedive's creditors. The claim survives if reform left taxpayers better off than Ismail's collectors had, even after the debt was paid.

---

**P3** *(Exegetical.)*

**Must hit, strict:**
- One premise: *whether, without creditor administration, Egypt could have restored its credit and its finances while keeping fiscal sovereignty.* Stabilizer denies it: control was the price of credibility. Wedge affirms it, or holds that the price was not worth paying. Equivalent statements pass.
- It is not a disagreement about the facts cited: both can accept the fall in borrowing costs and the transfer of fiscal power.
- Comparative evidence: cases with lighter or no control. The Ottoman Empire kept political sovereignty and still regained market access under a narrower agency. So did defaulters that settled with no agency at all ([4.6](04-06-bondholders-gunboats-and-receiverships.md)). Rates after settlement in each, and how long it took, bear on it. So does whether Egypt's fall in rates came from the Caisse or from British occupation.

**Wrong turns:** turning it into Hopkins versus Robinson–Gallagher on why Britain invaded (that is the boss problem, and a different crux); naming two premises; saying who is right.

**Model answer:** The positions divide on a counterfactual: could Egypt have made its promises credible again without handing its revenues to creditor agents? Stabilizer says no; Wedge says yes, or that the cost in sovereignty outweighed the gain. Both can accept the rate data. Comparisons would move it: how fast lightly controlled defaulters, like the Ottoman Empire under a narrower agency, and uncontrolled ones regained credit, and on what terms. A further test would separate the Caisse's effect on Egyptian yields from the effect of the British occupation after 1882.

</details>

## Flashback

**From Lesson 4.3 (Debt peonage after slavery):** Sort three cases under the courts' test for peonage. *(Exegetical.)* (a) A sawmill hand takes a 20-dollar advance, quits in a week, and is convicted of fraud on independent evidence that he took advances from three employers the same month and worked for none; the statute carries no presumption clause. (b) A man convicted of vagrancy is sentenced to sixty days on the county roads; no private person paid anything for him. (c) A store debtor freely agrees to work off a 30-dollar account on the merchant's farm, and when he leaves after two months the sheriff arrests him under a statute making departure before the debt is worked off a misdemeanor. Which are peonage, and which feature decides each? One sentence per case.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict:**
- (a) **Not peonage.** *Bailey* struck the statutory presumption, not the power to punish fraud proved in the ordinary way. What made Alabama's law peonage was that failure to work plus nonrepayment convicted on their own, so the "fraud" was just the unpaid debt; here intent is proved by independent evidence, and the debt is not doing the convicting.
- (b) **Not peonage.** The Thirteenth Amendment excepts punishment for crime, and no debt to a private creditor is being liquidated — the deciding features in *Clyatt*'s definition are a debt and a private beneficiary, and both are missing.
- (c) **Peonage.** Service compelled to pay off a debt, whatever its beginning: a voluntary start does not cure a compelled continuation, and arrest on departure is the compulsion.

**Wrong turns:** calling (a) peonage because an advance stands in the background — that reads *Bailey* as forbidding every criminal case where a debt appears; calling (b) peonage because the labor is hard and compelled — compulsion alone is not the test, a private debt must be being worked off; calling (c) lawful because the worker agreed — the agreement is exactly what *Clyatt* says cannot cure it.

**Model answer:** (a) Not peonage: *Bailey* voided the presumption that made nonpayment its own proof of fraud, and here intent is shown independently, so the debt is not what convicts. (b) Not peonage: the amendment's exception covers punishment for crime, and no private creditor is paid off — there is no debt being worked out. (c) Peonage: the service liquidates a private debt and quitting brings arrest, and under *Clyatt* a voluntary beginning does not save a compelled continuation.

</details>
