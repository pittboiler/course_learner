# A History of Debt · Lesson 1.4: Solon's shaking-off of burdens

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 1: Ancient debt: bondage and clean slates · Builds on: [1.2 Debt bondage and the royal clean slate](01-02-debt-bondage-and-the-royal-clean-slate.md), [1.3 Release laws in ancient Israel](01-03-release-laws-in-ancient-israel.md) · Unlocks: [1.5 *Nexum* and the Roman plebs](01-05-nexum-and-the-roman-plebs.md)

## Why this matters

A Babylonian king could free debt slaves in his accession year, and leave his successor to do it again a generation later. Early-sixth-century Athens tried something different. Solon paired a one-time release with a **permanent rule**: no Athenian could ever again pledge his own body for a loan. The *Constitution of Athens* ranks that ban first among the democratic features of Solon's laws. It is the first surviving case of a citizen body turning "who may bind whom through debt" into constitutional law. It also comes to us through sources written centuries later, which disagree about what was cancelled and even about who the victims were.

## The idea

Picture Attica around 600 BC. A few families control most of the good land. Below them are two overlapping kinds of dependent. Some farmers, the *hektemoroi* ("sixth-parters"), owe a share of their harvest to the rich. Others have borrowed on the security of their own persons, and when they default they are seized. They work as slaves at home or are sold abroad, and sometimes their children go with them. Civil strife (*stasis*) gets bad enough that both sides agree to hand one man extraordinary authority. Solon is made archon, in 594/3 BC by the traditional date (some modern scholars put the legislation a couple of decades later), and mediator.

He does three things. He **releases**: debts are cancelled, the boundary stones (*horoi*) planted on encumbered land are pulled up, citizens enslaved in Attica are freed, and some sold abroad are brought home. He **forbids**: from now on no loan may be secured on a person. And he **refuses**: no redistribution of land, which the poor had demanded, and no restoration of the old order, which the rich had hoped for (*Constitution of Athens* 11).

The Greek name for the package is *seisachtheia*, "shaking-off of burdens." Compare it with 1.2 and 1.3. The Babylonian *misharum* edict cleared arrears and left the institution of debt bondage standing, so the next king had to do it again. Deuteronomy put release on a fixed seven-year clock. Solon did something else. He abolished the kind of collateral that made bondage possible, and he left property as it was. Lending itself survived. A law the orator Lysias quotes as ancient (10.18), often taken to be Solon's, let lenders set whatever interest rate they chose.

## Source

Plutarch, *Life of Solon* 13.2 (Perrin, 1914, public domain), on the condition before the reform:

> All the common people were in debt to the rich. For they either tilled their lands for them, paying them a sixth of the increase (whence they were called Hectemorioi and Thetes), or else they pledged their persons for debts

Two things to notice. First, Plutarch puts both groups under one heading, "in debt." Second, the "either … or" makes them *different* groups: the sixth-payers are not described as having pledged their persons. Plutarch wrote around AD 100, some seven centuries after Solon. The *Constitution of Athens*, written in Aristotle's school in the 330s–320s BC, is the older of our two accounts. Both lean on the only contemporary evidence we have, Solon's own poems, which each of them quotes.

## The argument

**A. Solon's case for his settlement, as the poems give it** (fr. 36 West and others, quoted in *Constitution of Athens* 12 and Plutarch 15–16):

1. **The city is being destroyed by the rich's love of wealth and arrogance** (*Const. Ath.* 5). *In words:* the crisis is the creditors' fault, not a natural disaster.
2. **Citizens and the land of Attica itself have been enslaved.** Perrin's Plutarch (15.5) renders the boast: "He took away the record-stones that everywhere were planted; / Before, Earth was in bondage, now she is free." *In words:* the stones marked land under someone else's claim, and removing them freed the land.
3. **Some citizens were sold abroad and some held in servitude at home, and he brought back the first and freed the second** (fr. 36). *In words:* the release covered persons as well as land.
4. **He gave "equal laws" to base and noble alike and would not give the poor an equal share of the land** (*Const. Ath.* 12). *In words:* freedom for citizens, not equal property.
5. ∴ **Release plus a ban on personal security, without redistribution, was the settlement that saved the city from tyranny and civil war.** *In words:* he took the middle course between the two factions, and he took it on purpose.

**B. The *Constitution of Athens*' institutional reading** (chs. 2, 6, 9):

1. **Before Solon, loans were secured on the debtor's person and the land was held by a few** (ch. 2, repeated in ch. 4). *In words:* the bondage came from what could be pledged.
2. **Solon "liberated the people once and for all, by prohibiting all loans on the security of the debtor's person: and in addition he made laws by which he cancelled all debts, public and private"** (ch. 6, Kenyon). *In words:* the ban did the permanent work, and the cancellation cleared what was already owed.
3. **That prohibition is the first and chief of Solon's democratic measures** (ch. 9). *In words:* a citizen who cannot be enslaved can take part in the city.
4. ∴ **The *seisachtheia* was a constitutional act, not only a debt relief.**

Read the order in B2. The ban comes first, and the cancellation is "in addition."

## Map of positions

Two separate disputes cross here. One is about *who* the *hektemoroi* were. The other is about *what* the *seisachtheia* did to debts.

| | **Debts cancelled outright** (*Const. Ath.* 6; Plutarch's "most writers") | **Relief short of cancellation** (Androtion, in Plutarch 15.4) |
|---|---|---|
| ***Hektemoroi* were debtors**: the sixth was interest or instalments on a defaulted loan, with *horoi* as mortgage markers | The classic picture: a clean slate for all debts, land unencumbered. This comes closest to a Mesopotamian edict, but it is paired with a ban | Sixth-parters stay debtors on easier terms. Hard to square with the poem's claim that Earth was set free |
| ***Hektemoroi* were dependent cultivators**: the sixth was rent or tribute owed by status, not a loan | "Debts" and "sixth-parting" are two different wrongs, and the reform ended both. The *horoi* marked lordly claims, not mortgages | Debt relief touched only borrowers, so the end of sixth-parting needs another explanation |

The sources settle neither dispute. The debate on the *hektemoroi* belongs to this lesson. The debate between cancellation and Androtion is the boss problem's.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean: run the settlement on four households, c. 590 BC).**

- *Kallias pledged himself, defaulted, and works as a slave on his creditor's estate in Attica.* The release frees him, and the debt is cancelled on the cancellation account. The ban means his son cannot fall the same way.
- *Kallias's brother was sold to a buyer overseas.* The poem says Solon brought such men back. The sources do not say how, or who paid their foreign owners. Treat it as a claim with no mechanism attached.
- *Nikias is a hektemoros who pays a share of his crop, and a horos stands on his field.* The stone comes out. On the debt reading, his loan is gone. On the dependency reading, a status obligation is abolished. Either way, no later Athenian source treats sixth-parters as a living class, which is decent evidence that the reform ended it, whatever it was.
- *In 580, Nikias wants a loan.* He can pledge goods, and later Athenians certainly pledged land (see Watch out). He cannot pledge his body. Nothing caps his interest rate.

The general rule: the *seisachtheia* changed what could be **collateral**, not whether credit existed. That is the feature that separates it from a *misharum* edict.

**Example 2 (hard: diagnose an invented speech).** An agriculture minister in an invented country tells parliament: *"We need our own seisachtheia. Cancel every farmer's arrears, and forbid banks from ever again taking a family's home as collateral. Solon did it and Athens became a democracy."*

The analogy rests on the *pairing*: one-time relief plus a permanent rule on collateral. That is the right feature to borrow, since it is exactly what sets Solon apart from the Babylonian kings. Now test where it breaks.

1. **What was banned.** Solon banned security on the *person*, meaning liberty and citizenship. A home is property. The closer Athenian parallel is the *horoi* on land, and Solon did not ban lending on land.
2. **Who bore the loss.** In Athens citizens owed citizens (and, on the cancellation account, the city), and a mediator with near-tyrannical authority imposed the settlement. The speech's creditors are banks, which lend out other people's deposits. The loss lands on a different set of people, so the political bargain is different.
3. **What happened is contested.** "Solon cancelled everything" is one ancient account. Androtion's is another.
4. **The causal claim.** Athenian democracy came generations later, with Cleisthenes (508/7). The *Constitution of Athens* calls the ban democratic in *tendency*, not the founding of democracy.

Diagnosis: the analogy takes the structure from Solon and quietly changes what counts as collateral. Whether the policy is wise is not this course's question. That argument belongs to [`philosophy-of-debt`](../../philosophy-of-debt/syllabus.md) and [`economics-of-debt`](../../economics-of-debt/syllabus.md).

## Watch out

- **Anachronism trap: coins and mortgages.** Athens probably struck no coins until the mid-sixth century, decades after Solon (on most current estimates). The sources' talk of drachmas and minas projects later money back onto his time, and his "debts" were likely in kind or in weighed silver (1.1). Likewise, the inscribed mortgage stones that survive are overwhelmingly fourth-century and later (Finley, *Studies in Land and Credit in Ancient Athens*, 1952). When Plutarch and Aristotle's school read Solon's *horoi* as mortgage markers, they may be reading their own world into his.
- **You might think the ban ended slavery or bondage in Athens.** It protected *Athenians* from enslavement for debt. Athens remained a slave-owning society.
- **You might think a sixth "paid" is certain.** Plutarch says they *paid* a sixth. Some later ancient lexicographers reportedly took the name the other way, as *keeping* a sixth. The *Constitution of Athens* says only "the rent thus indicated." P2 shows how much turns on this.
- **You might think the *seisachtheia* redistributed land.** Both sources say Solon refused, and that the poor were angry about it (*Const. Ath.* 11–12; Plutarch 16.1).

## One-liner

> Solon did not just wipe the slate. He changed what a debtor could put on it: never again his own body.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢) *(Exegetical.)*** The *Constitution of Athens* 2 (Kenyon):

> They were known as Pelatae and also as Hectemori, because they cultivated the lands of the rich at the rent thus indicated. … if the tenants failed to pay their rent they were liable to be haled into slavery

(a) Which reading of the *hektemoroi* does this passage support, debtors or dependent cultivators? Name the words that decide it. (b) Compare it with Plutarch 13.2 (the Source): what does Plutarch's wording do that this passage does not? (c) Does either passage settle whether the sixth was paid or kept? 150 words or fewer in total.

**P2 (🟡) *(Formal (a)–(c) · Exegetical (d).)*** Illustrative figures only. A sixth-parter's field yields 48 *medimnoi* (a measure of grain) of barley in a normal year. He must set aside 8 for seed, and his household eats 30.
(a) Compute what he keeps, and his surplus or shortfall after seed and food, on reading A (he *pays* a sixth) and on reading B (he *keeps* a sixth).
(b) For each reading, find the smallest harvest that covers seed and food.
(c) On reading A, a bad year cuts the harvest by 25%. He borrows the shortfall in barley at 33⅓% a year, compounding (the Mesopotamian barley convention from [1.1](01-01-credit-before-coins.md), used only for illustration; no Athenian rate is attested for his time). Each later normal harvest he pays his whole surplus. Write the recurrence for the debt after each payment, give the debt after 5 payments, and find the largest loan he could ever pay off.
(d) In one sentence: which reading makes the sources' claim that non-payment led to enslavement easier to understand, and why?

**P3 (🔴, optional) *(Exegetical.)*** One historian holds that the *hektemoroi* were defaulted debtors paying a sixth on loans secured by their land. Another holds that they were dependent cultivators owing a customary share to landowners, with no loan involved. Name the single premise one side must deny, and name two kinds of evidence that would move it. 150 words or fewer.

<details>
<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical, strict.)*

**Must hit, strict:**
- (a) **Dependent cultivators (tenancy).** The deciding words are "cultivated the lands of the rich" (the land belongs to the rich, not to the farmer) and "rent" / "tenants" (a lease, not a loan). "Pelatae" (dependents or clients) points the same way. The penalty of enslavement attaches to unpaid *rent*. The Greek word Kenyon renders "rent" is a leasing term (*misthosis*).
- (b) Plutarch files the sixth-payers under "in debt to the rich" and so frames their obligation as debt. His "either … or" also separates them from those who pledged their persons. The Kenyon passage never calls the sixth-parters debtors.
- (c) **No.** Kenyon's "the rent thus indicated" names no fraction. Plutarch says "paying … a sixth," but he is one late source, and the ambiguity in the name itself stays open.

**Wrong turns:** reading "haled into slavery" as proof that they were debtors (the passage makes slavery the penalty for unpaid *rent*); claiming the two sources agree because both use the name *hektemoroi*.

**Model answer:** (a) Tenancy: they "cultivated the lands of the rich" at a "rent," and they are called "tenants" and "Pelatae." (b) Plutarch calls all the poor "in debt" and sets the sixth-payers beside, not among, those who pledged their persons. That makes the sixth a debt burden without making it a personal pledge. (c) Neither: Kenyon's passage gives no fraction, and Plutarch's "paying a sixth" is one late witness.

---

**P2** *(Formal (a)–(c) · Exegetical (d).)*

**Must hit, strict:**
- (a) Reading A: pays $\tfrac16 \cdot 48 = 8$, keeps $40$; $40 - 8 - 30 = +2$ surplus. Reading B: keeps $\tfrac16 \cdot 48 = 8$ (pays 40); $8 - 8 - 30 = -30$ shortfall.
- (b) A: $\tfrac56 H \ge 38 \Rightarrow H \ge 45.6$ *medimnoi*. B: $\tfrac16 H \ge 38 \Rightarrow H \ge 228$, almost five times a normal harvest.
- (c) Bad harvest $0.75 \times 48 = 36$; keeps $\tfrac56 \cdot 36 = 30$; needs $38$; borrows $8$. With $D_0 = 8$ and payment 2 per year: $D_t = \tfrac43 D_{t-1} - 2$. Fixed point $D^* = 2 \div \tfrac13 = 6$, so $D_t = 6 + 2\left(\tfrac43\right)^t$. After 5 payments: $6 + 2 \cdot \tfrac{1024}{243} = \tfrac{3506}{243} \approx 14.43$ *medimnoi* (rounded to two decimals). The path: 8.67, 9.56, 10.74, 12.32, 14.43. The largest loan he could ever repay is anything **below 6**. At exactly 6 the debt never falls, and above 6 it grows without limit.
- (d) Reading B: a farmer who keeps only a sixth cannot live off his harvest, so default, and enslavement for it, is near-certain. On reading A, enslavement needs a shock plus compounding, as in (c).

**Wrong turns:** applying the 25% cut to the surplus instead of the harvest; using simple interest (the problem says compounding); in (d), claiming that (c) proves reading A (the arithmetic shows both readings *can* produce bondage, just by different paths).

**Model answer (d):** Reading B makes enslavement for non-payment almost automatic, since the sixth kept cannot feed the household. Reading A needs a bad year and a loan whose interest outruns the surplus.

---

**P3** *(Exegetical, strict on naming one premise; any defensible statement of it passes.)*

**Must hit, strict:**
- The crux: **whether the sixth arose from a loan**, an obligation that began with borrowing and that cancelling debts would erase, **or from a status or tenure relationship** on land the payer did not own. The debt side must affirm that the sixth-parters had borrowed. The dependency side denies it.
- Evidence that would move it (any two): an early-sixth-century *horos* or other document naming a creditor and a sum; evidence about whether Attic land could be sold or pledged before Solon (if it could not, mortgage is harder to believe); the dating of coinage and weighed-silver lending in Attica; comparative Near Eastern evidence that sharecropping arose from default (1.2); ancient vocabulary for the sixth, since "rent" in *Const. Ath.* 2 and "in debt" in Plutarch 13 are both late.

**Wrong turns:** naming cancellation vs Androtion's relief (a different question); treating the two late sources as independent confirmation, when both may be drawing on the same poems; saying that late coinage refutes the debt reading, since loans in kind and in weighed silver existed long before coins (1.1).

**Model answer:** The premise in dispute is that the sixth was a payment *on a loan*. The debt reading needs it: the sixth-parters had borrowed and pledged their land, and the *horoi* marked the pledge. The dependency reading denies it: they farmed land the rich controlled and owed a share by custom or status. What would move it: first, any early document on a *horos* naming a creditor and a sum, which would support debt; second, firm evidence that Attic land was not alienable before Solon, which would support dependency. Each side can absorb the late sources' vocabulary, so only earlier evidence decides.

</details>

## Flashback

**From Lesson 1.2 (Debt bondage and the royal clean slate):** *(Exegetical, then applied.)* A *misharum* edict is proclaimed at the end of a year in which a storm flattened the barley. Three items sit on one lender's books: (i) an interest-bearing barley loan to a cultivator whose crop the storm destroyed this year; (ii) that cultivator's daughter, handed over three full years ago and still working in the lender's house; (iii) 30 shekels advanced to a trading agent for a *harrānum*. For each, say what the standing laws (Hammurabi §48, §117) had already done and what the edict adds. Then say for which item the edict adds least, and which item only an edict could reach. Two sentences or fewer per item.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict:** (i) §48 excuses grain and interest **for that year** alone — the ruined tablet is rewritten — and leaves the principal standing into the next harvest; the edict cancels the loan itself, principal and accrued interest, as a consumption debt of the protected population, and it can be clawed back if the lender squeezed payment out of him in breach. (ii) §117 already caps the service: three years' work, freed in the fourth, running automatically without any royal act, so the edict brings her release forward by at most the remainder of that fourth year. (iii) Neither section touches a *harrānum* advance, and the edict deliberately spares commercial ventures, so the agent still owes 30 shekels. **Adds least:** (ii), where the standing rule was about to do the same work. **Only an edict reaches:** (i)'s principal — §48 postpones, only the decree extinguishes.

**Wrong turns:** reading §48 as cancelling the debt rather than suspending that year's grain and interest; freeing the daughter *because* of the edict when her fourth year was due anyway; cancelling the venture silver on the ground that the borrower is poorer than the merchant — the edict sorts by the kind of debt, not by the parties' means; assuming that her release settles the underlying claim, which §117 never says.

**Model answer:** (i) §48 gave the cultivator a year's grace and no more; the edict breaks the tablet outright, principal included, because this is a consumption loan of the kind *andurārum* reaches. (ii) §117 was already going to free her in the fourth year; the edict only shortens the wait, and neither says the debt is thereby paid. (iii) Nothing touched the venture advance before, and the edict exempts commercial credit, so it stands in full. The edict adds least on the daughter, and it is the only instrument that reaches the barley principal.

</details>
