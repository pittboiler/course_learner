# A History of Debt · Lesson 5.4: From Mexico 1982 to the Brady Plan

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 5: Reparations to Jubilee: the twentieth century · Builds on: [5.3 Bretton Woods and the lending boom](05-03-bretton-woods-and-the-lending-boom.md), [4.6 Bondholders, gunboats, and receiverships](04-06-bondholders-gunboats-and-receiverships.md) · Unlocks: [5.5 Jubilee 2000 and HIPC](05-05-jubilee-2000-and-hipc.md)

## Why this matters

For seven years after August 1982, the official answer to the developing-country debt crisis was that nobody had lost any money yet. Debts were rescheduled, banks were pressed to lend more, debtors ran trade surpluses, and the loans stayed on bank books at face value. Then, in 1989, the United States Treasury proposed that the banks write some of the debt off. That reversal — from *more lending* to *less debt* — is the template every later restructuring argues about, and where the modern vocabulary of sovereign crisis (liquidity versus solvency, haircut, conditionality) took shape.

## The idea

The 1970s loans were floating-rate ([5.3](05-03-bretton-woods-and-the-lending-boom.md)): the borrower pays LIBOR plus a spread, reset every six months. That puts the entire interest-rate risk on the debtor. When the Federal Reserve under Paul Volcker drove rates up — the effective federal funds rate averaged 19.1 per cent in June 1981, the highest monthly figure on record — the cost of a debt already contracted roughly doubled, while world recession cut the commodity prices that were supposed to pay it and new lending stopped.

Mexico went first. In mid-August 1982 its finance minister, Jesús Silva Herzog, told the US Treasury, the Federal Reserve and the IMF that Mexico could not meet payments on some 80 billion dollars of debt. (Accounts differ on the exact day.) Within days there was a standstill on principal and bridge financing from the US government and the Bank for International Settlements; within months, the same script was running in Brazil, Argentina, Chile and well beyond Latin America.

What made this different from the defaults of [4.6](04-06-bondholders-gunboats-and-receiverships.md) was the *creditors'* position. The debt was held not by scattered bondholders but by a few hundred commercial banks, and the largest were enormously exposed. By 1982 the nine largest US money-center banks held Latin American debt equal to about 176 per cent of their capital, and total developing-country debt near 290 per cent (FDIC, *History of the Eighties*, 1997). Write those loans down by a third and the write-down eats the banks.

So the crisis had three players, not two: debtor governments, banks that could not afford to recognize a loss, and official creditors (the IMF, the US Treasury, the Fed) who feared a banking collapse more than they feared a slow workout. The strategy that followed — reschedule, lend a bit more, adjust, wait — was built around the third player's problem. What changed by 1989 was not the debtors' arithmetic but the banks' capacity to absorb a loss.

## The argument

**The liquidity case** (the official strategy, 1982–85; its best-known public defense is William Cline's, 1983):

1. The shock is a spike in world interest rates, a collapse in commodity prices and a sudden stop in new lending — all temporary. *In words:* the borrowers are solvent, just cash-starved.
2. If every bank tries to get out at once, a solvent borrower is pushed into default; if each lends new money in proportion to its existing exposure, the borrower keeps paying and every loan performs. *In words:* a coordinated non-run is better for the banks than an individually rational run.
3. Devaluation, trade surpluses and fiscal contraction, supervised through IMF conditionality, restore the capacity to service the debt. *In words:* the debtor earns the foreign exchange to pay.
4. Given time, growth and rebuilt bank capital, the debt is repaid at face value and no loss is ever booked. *In words:* if nobody writes anything down, nobody becomes insolvent.
5. ∴ Roll over, add new money, adjust, and wait.

This is the logic of **concerted** (critics said "involuntary") **lending**. When Mexico's IMF program was assembled in late 1982, the Fund's managing director, Jacques de Larosière, made Fund money conditional on the banks putting in their share: roughly 5 billion dollars of new money from more than 500 banks alongside about 1.3 billion from the Fund and 2 billion from governments, with some 19 billion of existing obligations rescheduled. The IMF, as its own history puts it, chose to bail the banks *in* rather than out.

**The debt-overhang case** (1987–89; associated with Jeffrey Sachs and with Paul Krugman's 1988 formalization):

1. After years of surpluses and slashed imports, debt ratios had not fallen, and by the late 1980s the loans were trading in a secondary market at deep discounts to face. *In words:* the people with money on the line no longer believed in full repayment.
2. When debt exceeds what a country can plausibly pay, the excess acts like a tax on adjustment: investment, reform and any windfall accrue mostly to creditors. *In words:* an overhang discourages the very effort that would repay it.
3. Therefore reducing face value or coupon can *raise* the expected value of what creditors actually collect. *In words:* on the far side of the curve, both sides gain from writing debt down.
4. Since 1987 the banks have provisioned; a write-down no longer threatens their solvency. Official money can guarantee the residual principal and make the exchange attractive. *In words:* the constraint that ruled out debt reduction in 1982 has gone.
5. ∴ Convert loans into smaller, tradable, partly collateralized bonds.

That is the Brady Plan, announced by Treasury Secretary Nicholas Brady on 10 March 1989. Note what the two arguments share: both take repayment as the goal and adjustment as the price. Neither is a forgiveness argument in the sense [5.5](05-05-jubilee-2000-and-hipc.md) will meet.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean: the rate shock, in round numbers).** Take a stylized debtor with 80 billion dollars of floating-rate debt and 20 billion dollars of annual exports — the right order of magnitude for Mexico around 1981, and illustrative only. At an all-in rate of 8 per cent the interest bill is $0.08 \times 80 = 6.4$ billion, or 32 per cent of exports. At 16 per cent it is 12.8 billion, or 64 per cent. Nothing about the country changed. No new borrowing occurred. Two-thirds of export earnings now go to interest before a single import is paid for, and the only ways to close the gap are to export more, import less, or borrow the difference. The first two take years; the third is what the banks stopped doing in 1982. This is why the first phase looked like a liquidity problem: the debtor's *own* conduct explained none of it.

**Example 2 (hard: why cheap debt is not a cheap escape).** By the late 1980s the loans traded far below face, which suggested an obvious move: let the country buy its own debt back at the market price.

Suppose a country owes 100 (face) and creditors collectively expect to be paid 40 in present value — so the debt trades at 40 cents. The country spends 8 of hard-won reserves buying back 20 of face at that price. Its face debt falls to 80. But capacity to pay is a fact about the country, not about how much paper is outstanding: creditors still expect about 40. Over 80 of face, that is 50 cents.

Tally it. The country handed over 8 in cash. The market value of its remaining obligations is 40 — exactly what the whole stock was worth before. The creditors who held on saw their claims rise from 32 (80 face at 40 cents) to 40. The 8 went to them, and the debtor bought nothing. This is the Bulow–Rogoff objection to buybacks (1988), and it is why a Brady exchange is a *simultaneous, collective* swap with collateral attached rather than quiet purchases in the market: what matters is whether the deal changes what the country will be asked to pay, not what it owes on paper. The underlying model belongs to [`economics-of-debt`](../../economics-of-debt/syllabus.md); the discounting mechanics are [`mathematical-finance` 4.1](../../mathematical-finance/lessons/04-01-term-structure-bond-pricing.md).

**The Brady menu, concretely.** Mexico's 1990 agreement covered about 49 billion dollars of bank claims. Each bank chose: a *discount bond* with face cut by 35 per cent carrying a floating rate of LIBOR plus 13/16 of a point; a *par bond* keeping full face but paying a fixed 6.25 per cent, well below market; or new money. Principal on the bonds was collateralized by 30-year US Treasury zero-coupon bonds, bought with loans from the IMF, the World Bank and Japan, with a rolling guarantee covering roughly a year and a half of interest. Take-up was roughly half discount bonds, somewhat over 40 per cent par bonds, about a tenth new money. Across all the Brady deals, private lenders wrote off on the order of 60 billion dollars, about a third of the debt involved (Federal Reserve History).

## Watch out

- **You might think the par bond was a gift to the banks because the face value was untouched.** The nominal haircut is zero; the present-value haircut is not. A 6.25 per cent fixed coupon for 30 years, when the market wants 12, is worth far less than face — that is P1.
- **You might think "bailout" here means what it meant in 2008.** It is an anachronism trap. No government injected capital into a US bank in 1982. The support was regulatory forbearance (loans carried at face), bridge loans to *debtors*, and IMF programs; even the Brady public money bought collateral rather than bank equity. Whether that amounted to a bailout by another route is a real question, which is P3 — but do not answer it with a word borrowed from a later crisis.
- **You might think the crisis was Latin American.** Latin America dominated the exposure numbers and the politics, but the same trap caught Poland, Yugoslavia, the Philippines, Nigeria and much of sub-Saharan Africa — and the poorest debtors, whose creditors were mostly governments and the Fund rather than banks, got nothing from a plan aimed at commercial bank debt. That gap is where [5.5](05-05-jubilee-2000-and-hipc.md) begins.
- **You might think "lost decade" settles the argument about adjustment.** The term (*década perdida*) reports a real outcome: regional income per head at the end of the 1980s was roughly back where it had started, with deep cuts in investment and public spending. What *caused* it is contested. Critics of conditionality — the 1987 UNICEF study *Adjustment with a Human Face* is the best-known — argued the programs' design forced the burden onto the poor; defenders argued the alternative to adjustment was not the status quo but disorderly default, and that the countries which reformed earliest recovered first. This course reports both; the normative question is [`philosophy-of-debt`](../../philosophy-of-debt/syllabus.md)'s.

## One-liner

> The debt did not become unpayable in 1989 — it was unpayable in 1982; what changed in between was that the banks could finally afford to say so.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢) *(Formal.)*** A bank holds 100 million dollars of face value in loans to a Brady-plan country. It takes the **par bond**: same 100 million face, 30-year bullet maturity, fixed coupon 6.25 per cent annually. The principal is fully collateralized by US Treasury zeros, so treat the principal repayment as risk-free and discount it at 8 per cent; the coupons carry the sovereign's risk, so discount them at an exit yield of 12 per cent. Ignore the interest guarantee. All figures illustrative. (a) What is the nominal (face) haircut? (b) What is the present value of the par bond, and the present-value haircut? Show the arithmetic and state your rounding. (c) What fixed coupon would have made the present-value haircut zero? (d) One sentence: why do the debtor's press release and the bank's internal file quote different haircut numbers?

**P2 (🟡) *(Exegetical.)*** In 1982–85 the official strategy treated the crisis as illiquidity; by 1988–89 the Treasury treated it as insolvency, and the two camps are set out in "The argument" above. (a) Name the single premise that one side affirms and the other denies. (b) State two kinds of evidence available *at the time* that bear on it, and say what each would have to show. (c) Explain why the fall in secondary-market prices is not by itself decisive. Do not say which diagnosis was correct. 150 words or fewer.

**P3 (🔴, optional) *(Exegetical.)*** The paragraph below is an invented newspaper column from 1989. It is not a quotation from any real person. Diagnose it: (a) name the concept actually doing the work in the argument, and the factual claim on which the column's conclusion depends; (b) name one piece of evidence that would support that claim and one that would undercut it. Do not say whether the Brady Plan was a good policy. 150 words or fewer.

> "Read the fine print. The Treasury has not forgiven anybody. It has arranged for public institutions to lend Mexico the money to buy American government bonds, which are then pledged to the very banks whose reckless lending caused this mess — so that what was a doubtful loan becomes a guaranteed one. The write-down the banks accept is a write-down of numbers they had already stopped believing. Call it what it is: the taxpayer standing behind Citibank's 1977 loan book, seven years late and dressed as generosity to the poor."

<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Formal.)*

**Must hit, strict:**

- (a) **Zero.** Face is unchanged at 100 million; that is what makes it a *par* bond.
- (b) Two pieces, discounted separately.
  - Coupons: 6.25 million a year for 30 years at 12 per cent. Annuity factor $a = \dfrac{1-(1.12)^{-30}}{0.12} = 8.0552$ (4 dp). Value $= 6.25 \times 8.0552 = 50.345$ million.
  - Principal: collateralized, so risk-free at 8 per cent. $100/(1.08)^{30} = 100/10.0627 = 9.938$ million. (This is also, usefully, what the collateral costs the debtor up front: about 9.9 million per 100 of face.)
  - Total $PV = 50.345 + 9.938 = 60.28$ million (2 dp).
  - Present-value haircut $= 1 - 60.28/100 = \mathbf{39.7\%}$ (1 dp).
- (c) Set $c \times 8.0552 + 9.938 = 100$, so $c = 90.062/8.0552 = 11.18$ per cent (2 dp). A coupon of about 11.2 per cent, not 6.25, would have left the bank whole in present value.
- (d) Because they are quoting different haircuts: the debtor's headline is the nominal one (zero here, or 35 per cent on the discount bond), while the bank's loss is the present-value one, which depends on an exit yield nobody observes directly.

**Wrong turns:** discounting the principal at 12 per cent as well — that ignores the collateral, and gives $PV = 50.345 + 3.338 = 53.68$, a 46.3 per cent haircut, an answer worth stating only to show what the Treasury zeros bought; treating "par" as meaning the bond is worth par; computing (c) by dividing 100 by 8.0552 and forgetting the collateral is already worth 9.9.

**Model answer:** (a) 0 per cent. (b) $6.25 \times 8.0552 + 100/1.08^{30} = 50.35 + 9.94 = 60.28$, so a present-value haircut of about 39.7 per cent (figures to 2 dp, haircut to 1 dp). (c) $c = (100 - 9.94)/8.0552 \approx 11.18$ per cent. (d) One is quoting face value, the other present value.

---

**P2** *(Exegetical — strict on the crux; no verdict asked or graded.)*

**Must hit, strict:**
- (a) The crux is the premise that **the debtors' inability to pay was temporary — that at an achievable primary surplus, and with rates and terms of trade returning to normal, the debt could be serviced in full.** The liquidity camp affirms it (hence new money and rescheduling at face); the overhang camp denies it (hence face and coupon reduction).
- (b) Two of: the *trajectory of debt ratios after years of adjustment* — if surpluses since 1983 had visibly shrunk debt-to-exports, the liquidity premise survives; if ratios were flat or worse despite large surpluses, it is in trouble. *Investment and growth in the debtor* — a liquidity story predicts investment resuming once the rollover holds; an overhang story predicts it staying depressed because returns accrue to creditors. *Whether banks lent voluntarily* — new money had to be conscripted, which tells you what lenders believed. *Counterfactual arithmetic* on the surplus needed to stabilize the ratio at plausible rates, and whether any government had sustained one that large for that long.
- (c) Secondary prices are informative but not decisive: they are thin-market prices, they are depressed by regulatory and tax reasons banks had to sell, and they price what creditors *expect to collect*, which folds in the probability of a future write-down as well as the country's capacity. A low price is consistent with a solvent debtor expected to be let off, and with an insolvent one.

**Wrong turns:** naming "liquidity vs solvency" as the premise (that is the conclusion, restated); making the crux a moral claim about who caused the crisis; saying the crux was settled by 1989 events — the same evidence was read both ways at the time.

**Model answer:** The sides divide on whether the inability to pay was temporary: whether, at a surplus a government could actually sustain, the debt was serviceable in full. Evidence available then: debt-to-export ratios after several years of large surpluses (falling supports liquidity, flat undercuts it); investment in the debtors (recovery supports liquidity, continued stagnation fits overhang); and the fact that new bank money had to be coordinated rather than offered. Secondary prices alone cannot settle it — they were thin, driven partly by regulatory and tax pressure to sell, and they price expected collections, which already embed the chance of a future write-off. A deep discount is compatible with a debtor that can pay but is expected to be relieved.

---

**P3** *(Exegetical — strict; no evaluation of the policy is asked or graded.)*

**Must hit, strict:**
- (a) The concept doing the work is **incidence: who ultimately bears the loss.** The column concedes debt reduction happened and argues it is cosmetic, because the write-down is of value the banks had already provisioned away, while public money converts the *remaining* claim from doubtful to guaranteed. The factual claim the conclusion depends on is that **the banks' position was improved, or at least not worsened, by the deal relative to the alternative** — i.e. that the enhancement was worth at least as much as the face and coupon they gave up. (Credit also for naming the closely related claim that public institutions bore risk that the banks would otherwise have borne.)
- (b) Supporting evidence: bank share prices and bond prices rising on the announcement and on individual deals; the size of the official loans financing collateral compared with the reduction in debt service; the gap between prior loan-loss reserves and the realized write-down; banks choosing the collateralized options over new money. Undercutting evidence: the deals being priced close to prevailing secondary-market values, so banks got roughly what the market already said the claims were worth; official money being lent to the *debtor* and repayable by it, not granted to banks; the debtors' subsequent fall in spreads and return to market access, which is a gain the column's account does not predict; the fact that participation was negotiated, with holdout banks conceding.

**Wrong turns:** treating "reckless lending" as the argument (it is rhetorical framing, not a premise the conclusion needs); answering whether the plan was justified; assuming the collateral was a grant rather than a loan the debtor repays.

**Model answer:** (a) The column is an argument about incidence — who really absorbed the loss. It grants that face value fell and claims that this was value the banks had already written off, while official lending turned the remainder into a guaranteed claim. Its conclusion depends on the factual claim that the enhancement was worth at least as much to the banks as what they surrendered. (b) It would be supported by bank equity prices rising on the deals, by official collateral loans large relative to the debt service actually relieved, and by banks avoiding the new-money option. It would be undercut if the exchanges were priced near existing secondary-market levels, if the collateral loans were serviced by the debtor, and by the debtors' subsequent regained market access.

## Flashback

**From Lesson 5.2 (Could Germany pay?):** It is 1930, and four proposals sit on a German finance minister's desk. For each, say whether it eases the **budgetary** problem, the **transfer** problem, both, or neither, and why — one sentence each. Then one sentence more: which of the four did the years 1924–29 actually run on, and what did leaning on it conceal?

1. A new property tax raising 500 million marks a year from German landowners.
2. The creditor powers cut their tariffs on German manufactures.
3. Germany floats a dollar bond in New York and pays the year's annuity out of the proceeds.
4. The creditors agree to take the annuity as coal and dyestuffs shipped from the Ruhr, valued at market prices.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict:** (1) **Budgetary only.** It raises marks in Berlin, which is precisely the operation the transfer problem is *not* about; nothing in it earns a pound or a dollar. (2) **Transfer only.** It attacks premise 5 — creditors protecting their producers against exactly the goods Germany must sell — and so lowers the price cut premise 3 demands; it puts nothing in the treasury. (3) **Both, and neither.** A foreign loan hands the government the resources and the foreign exchange in one move, so both problems go quiet for a year; but the annuity is discharged by contracting a new debt abroad, so the transfer is postponed rather than made. (4) **Transfer only.** Payment in kind removes the currency conversion altogether, but the treasury must still buy the coal from German producers in marks. And: 1924–29 ran on (3), the American lending circle, which made the schedule look affordable while the export adjustment was never tested — the same Germany was called able to pay in 1929 and unable in 1931 with its productive capacity unchanged.

**Wrong turns:** calling (1) transfer relief because the tax "funds the payment" — collecting marks is the budgetary operation by definition; calling (2) budgetary relief because higher exports raise tax revenue (true, and second-order — the clause bites on foreign exchange); treating (3) as a solution rather than a deferral, or missing that it is what actually happened; ruling (4) out because reparations were "payable in money," when deliveries in kind were in the schedule from the start.

**Model answer:** (1) Budgetary: it raises marks, and marks in Berlin are not yet a payment to a foreign creditor. (2) Transfer: it relaxes the creditor-tariff obstacle, letting Germany earn foreign exchange, but leaves the budget as it was. (3) Both at once and neither in substance — the loan supplies the money and the currency, and replaces the obligation with a new one owed abroad. (4) Transfer: paying in goods skips the conversion, while the treasury still has to buy those goods in marks. Germany ran on (3) from 1924, and the subsidy hid the fact that no one had tested whether the export surplus of premise 3 could ever be earned.
