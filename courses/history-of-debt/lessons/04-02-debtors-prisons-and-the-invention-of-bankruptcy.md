# A History of Debt · Lesson 4.2: Debtors' prisons and the invention of bankruptcy

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 4: Debt, nation and empire in the long nineteenth century · Builds on: [4.1 Hamilton's assumption plan](04-01-hamiltons-assumption-plan.md) · Unlocks: [4.3 Debt peonage after slavery](04-03-debt-peonage-after-slavery.md)

## Why this matters

Hamilton's Report promised that a *state* would honor every contract at face value. In the same decades, a private debtor who could not honor his could be locked up — one of Hamilton's own allies, Robert Morris, spent three years in a Philadelphia debtors' prison. Between about 1700 and 1900 Anglo-American law turned that answer upside down: the insolvent stopped being jailed and started being *discharged*, released from his debts with a fresh start. That shift is the ancestor of every modern argument about letting debtors walk away, from consumer bankruptcy to student loans to sovereigns that have no bankruptcy court at all.

## The idea

A creditor facing a debtor who will not pay has two problems. He does not know whether the debtor *cannot* pay or *will not*. And he is not the only creditor.

The debtors' prison solved the first problem crudely. Lock the debtor up and wait: a man hiding money, or with a rich uncle, pays to get out; a man with nothing stays in. Jail is a screening device run on the debtor's body. It does nothing for the second problem — each creditor sues separately, and the first to arrest gets the leverage.

Bankruptcy solved the second problem. Seize *all* the debtor's assets at once, sell them, and split the proceeds among *all* creditors pro rata (in proportion to their claims). No race to the courthouse.

Then came the move this lesson is about. To make the debtor cooperate with the seizure — hand over the books, disclose the hidden plate — the law offered him a prize: a **discharge** of the unpaid balance. What started as a bribe for disclosure became, over two centuries, something closer to a right. Why it spread is still disputed.

## Source

Mr Pickwick, jailed in the Fleet on principle (he refuses to pay damages he thinks unjust), watches the prisoners drink and play skittles. Dickens, *The Pickwick Papers* (1836–37), ch. XLI:

> 'imprisonment for debt is scarcely any punishment at all.'

Sam Weller corrects him: the idlers do not mind it, and the damage falls on

> 'them as vould pay if they could, and gets low by being boxed up.'

Read Sam's line as an argument about sorting. Jail was supposed to separate *won't* from *can't*. Sam says it sorts backwards: the hardened shirker is comfortable, while the honest debtor, who would work his way out, is ruined.

## The argument

Two institutional logics, each in the terms its defenders used.

**The logic of the prison (common-law civil process).**

1. **Arrest on *mesne process*:** a creditor could swear to a debt and have the debtor arrested *before* any judgment, held unless he found bail. *In words:* the body was security for the lawsuit.
2. **Arrest in execution:** after judgment, the creditor could take the body (the writ *capias ad satisfaciendum*) and keep the debtor jailed until he paid or the creditor released him. *In words:* no fixed term — the key was in the creditor's pocket.
3. **Coercion reveals ability.** Anyone who can pay will, rather than stay in. *In words:* jail forces hidden money and family help into the open.
4. **Therefore imprisonment is not punishment but enforcement.** *In words:* its defenders saw it as a civil remedy, not a sentence — and it was paid for by the prisoner, who owed fees to the keeper of the Fleet, the Marshalsea or the King's Bench.

**The logic of bankruptcy (statute, for traders).**

1. **A failing trader's estate should be administered collectively.** The Tudor statute of 1542 set up commissioners to seize and distribute a fleeing debtor's goods; the Elizabethan act of 1571 confined bankruptcy to merchants and traders, a restriction that lasted until 1861. *In words:* one sale, one pro-rata split, no race.
2. **Only the debtor knows where the assets are.** *In words:* the estate is only as big as his disclosure.
3. **So reward disclosure and punish concealment.** The Act of 1705 (4 Anne c.17) gave a conforming bankrupt an allowance from his estate and a discharge from the remaining debts, and made fraudulent concealment a capital felony; a 1706 amendment required the discharge certificate to be signed by four-fifths of creditors in number and value. *In words:* carrot and noose together.
4. **Therefore discharge serves creditors.** *In words:* in its origin the fresh start was a price creditors paid for a bigger dividend, and they kept a veto over it.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean): the same failure, two legal worlds, London c. 1760.** A linen draper and a country gentleman each owe 12,000 pounds and hold assets worth a third of it. The draper is a *trader*. A creditor petitions, commissioners seize everything, assignees sell it and pay a dividend in the pound. If he conforms and four-fifths of creditors in number and value sign his certificate, he walks away discharged, and anything he earns next year is his. The gentleman is *not* a trader, so bankruptcy is closed to him. Any single creditor with a judgment can take his body, and he stays in the King's Bench until he pays, the creditor relents, or Parliament passes one of its periodic insolvency acts releasing prisoners who surrender their property. Even then his debts survive, and his future property stays liable. Same insolvency, opposite outcomes, drawn along a *status* line: the law treated commercial failure as a hazard of trade and a gentleman's debts as a matter of personal obligation.

**Example 2 (hard): Robert Morris.** The financier of the Revolution, and Hamilton's ally on public credit, borrowed heavily to speculate in western land, failed, and was jailed for debt in 1798. He was released in 1801 under the new federal Act and never recovered his fortune. The case strains every clean category. Was his failure *sin* or *misfortune*? His creditors, many of them ruined in turn, had reason to call it reckless gambling with other people's money. His defenders pointed to his public service and to a credit collapse no one controlled. Bruce Mann (*Republic of Debtors*, 2002) reads the whole era through this contest: in a commercial republic, failure was slowly recast from moral fault toward economic misfortune. Yet the 1800 Act that freed Morris was a *creditors'* remedy limited to merchants, and it was repealed within three years, with critics charging, among other things, that it favored merchants and speculators. The discharge he got was moral progress on one reading, and on another a class privilege. Edward Balleisen (*Navigating Failure*, 2001) shows the same double edge in the short-lived 1841 Act.

## Watch out

- **Anachronism: "bankruptcy" as a debtor's right.** Before 1841 in the US, and for most of this period in England, bankruptcy was mainly something a creditor did *to* a trader, and discharge was a conditional reward that creditors could veto. Reading the modern voluntary petition back into 1705 hides the whole story.
- **Imprisonment was civil, not criminal.** No court sentenced a debtor to a term. That is why jails charged him fees, and why, as Dickens's narrator complains (*Pickwick*, ch. XLII), the law fed a convicted felon but left a destitute debtor on the poor side to charity. It is also why the 1869 Act could "abolish" imprisonment for debt while keeping short committals for debtors who could pay a court-ordered instalment and did not.
- **Don't collapse bankruptcy and insolvency.** In England they were separate tracks for separate classes until 1861. Insolvency relief freed the body; bankruptcy freed the future income.
- **Modern "debtors' prisons" are a different institution.** Jailing people today for unpaid court fines or missed hearings involves criminal-justice debts and contempt, not private creditors' writs. The analogy is contested, and its constitutional side belongs to [`constitutional-law`](../../constitutional-law/syllabus.md).

## One-liner

> The debtors' prison tried to squeeze out what the debtor was hiding; bankruptcy bought it with a discharge — and whether that fresh start was creditors' bargain or moral reform is the question.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢) *(Exegetical.)*** Dickens, *Little Dorrit* (1855–57), Book I, ch. 6. Dr Haggage, a long-term prisoner, on the Marshalsea:

> 'We are quiet here; we don't get badgered here; there's no knocker here, sir, to be hammered at by creditors and bring a man's heart into his mouth.'

And the narrator on Mr Dorrit, a few lines later: "the lock and key that kept him in, kept numbers of his troubles out."

(a) What do these passages imply about imprisonment's working as *coercion*, the third premise of the prison's logic? Name the words that decide it. (b) Is this the same criticism Sam Weller makes in the Source, or a different one? 150 words or fewer in total.

**P2 (🟡) *(Formal.)*** A London trader, c. 1760, owes 12,000 pounds: A 4,000; B 3,000; C 2,500; D 1,500; E 1,000. In bankruptcy his estate sells for 4,200 pounds, and fees and commission costs take 20% of that. Instead he offers a *composition*: he keeps trading and pays 7s 6d in the pound (20 shillings = 1 pound, 12 pence = 1 shilling) to every creditor, provided *all* sign. (a) Compute the bankruptcy dividend in shillings and pence in the pound, and each creditor's recovery under both options. (b) D refuses to sign unless paid in full, and threatens to take the debtor's body in execution, which would end his trading. If the others pay D in full out of the composition fund, what do they receive in the pound? What is the most D could extract before the others would rather see the debtor bankrupt? (c) Suppose a rule, like the 1706 certificate threshold, let the composition bind everyone once four-fifths of creditors in number and value sign. Does it bind D? Round money to the nearest pound or penny, and say which.

**P3 (🔴, optional) *(Exegetical.)*** One historian says discharge spread because it served *creditors*: it bought disclosure and a larger, faster dividend, and creditors kept control of it. Another says it spread because of *moral change*: failure was recast as misfortune in a commercial society (Mann's reading). Name the single premise the two divide on, and say what evidence from the English or American record would move it. 150 words or fewer. No verdict required.

<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical — strict.)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** coercion works only if prison is *worse* than paying. Both passages say that for some debtors it is *better*, because it shields them from creditors' pressure. The deciding words: "no knocker … to be hammered at by creditors" (the creditors' leverage stops at the gate) and "kept numbers of his troubles out" (the jail protects its prisoner). So the threat loses its bite for exactly the long-term prisoners it was meant to squeeze.
**Must hit, strict (b):** it is a *different* criticism. Sam says jail sorts badly: shirkers don't mind it, and it ruins the honest. Haggage and the narrator say jail can become a *refuge* that destroys the incentive to pay at all, even for a man like Dorrit, who is not a shirker.
**Wrong turns:** reading Haggage as simply condemning the prison (he is praising it, and Dickens makes the praise damning); treating (b) as the same point because both involve prisoners not minding.
**Model answer:** (a) The coercion premise assumes jail is worse than paying. Haggage calls it "freedom", and the narrator says the lock "kept numbers of his troubles out": the knocker, the dunning letters and the creditor on the doormat all stop at the wall. For a long-term debtor the prison is a shelter, so the threat no longer moves him. (b) It is a different point. Sam says the prison hits the wrong people: idlers enjoy it, and the honest are crushed. Dickens here says it can neutralize even an honest man, like Dorrit, by making surrender comfortable. Sam's complaint is about sorting. This one is about the incentive vanishing.

**P2** *(Formal.)*

**(a)** Net estate $= 4{,}200 \times (1 - 0.20) = 3{,}360$ pounds. Dividend $= 3{,}360 / 12{,}000 = 0.28$ in the pound $= 0.28 \times 20 = 5.6$ shillings $= 5$s $+ 0.6 \times 12 = 7.2$d, so **5s 7d** (to the nearest penny).
Composition: 7s 6d $= 7.5/20 = 0.375$ in the pound.

| Creditor | Claim | Bankruptcy (×0.28) | Composition (×0.375) |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | 4,000 | 1,120 | 1,500 |
| B | 3,000 | 840 | 1,125 |
| C | 2,500 | 700 | 937.50 |
| D | 1,500 | 420 | 562.50 |
| E | 1,000 | 280 | 375 |

The composition beats bankruptcy for everyone: fund 4,500 against 3,360.

**(b)** Fund $= 0.375 \times 12{,}000 = 4{,}500$. Pay D 1,500 in full, leaving $3{,}000$ for claims of $10{,}500$: $3{,}000/10{,}500 = 0.2857$ in the pound $= 5.714$s $= 5$s $8.57$d, so **5s 9d** (nearest penny). That is barely above bankruptcy's 5s 7d.
The most D can extract is the amount that leaves the others exactly their bankruptcy dividend: $4{,}500 - 0.28 \times 10{,}500 = 4{,}500 - 2{,}940 = 1{,}560$ pounds. That is **104% of his claim**. The holdout can capture the whole surplus the composition creates. His threat is credible only because the others lose more from a jailed, non-trading debtor than from paying him off; if they call it, D gets 420.

**(c)** Signers A, B, C, E: 4 of 5 in number $= 80\%$, which meets four-fifths. By value, $10{,}500/12{,}000 = 87.5\% \ge 80\%$. **Yes, it binds D**, and his holdout value falls to his 562.50 share. (Rounding: shillings and pence to the nearest penny; pounds exact.)

**Must hit, strict:** 3,360 net; 5s 7d; the two recovery columns; 5s 9d with D paid; 1,560; both thresholds checked.
**Wrong turns:** applying the 20% cost to the composition; checking the four-fifths rule by number only; dividing the leftover 3,000 by 12,000 instead of 10,500.

---

**P3** *(Exegetical — strict on the crux; no verdict.)*

**Must hit, strict:** the dividing premise is *who wanted discharge and on whose terms*. On the creditor account, discharge expanded where and when it raised creditors' net recoveries, and it stayed under their control (consent thresholds). On the moral account, it expanded because legislators and the public came to see failure as misfortune, *even against* creditors' interests. Evidence that would move it: who petitioned for and against each act; whether discharge rules tightened creditor control or loosened it (1706 four-fifths veto vs the 1841 voluntary petition for non-merchants); whether passage tracked panics and debtor numbers (1800, 1841, 1867) rather than dividend data; and whether repeal followed creditor complaints about dividends.
**Wrong turns:** stating a verdict; treating the accounts as exclusive when the evidence can show both at different dates; naming "economics vs morality" without a testable premise.
**Model answer:** They divide on whether discharge spread *with* creditors' interest or *against* it. If creditors wanted it, discharge should come with creditor vetoes and should track recoveries. The 1706 four-fifths certificate fits that. If moral change drove it, discharge should arrive as a debtor-initiated right, pushed through over creditors' objections after panics. The American 1841 Act, voluntary and open to non-merchants, passed after 1837 and repealed within two years, fits that better. Decisive evidence: the petitions and votes behind each act, dividend rates before and after, and who sought each repeal.

## Flashback

**From Lesson 3.4 (Hume's prophecy: carrying a great debt):** *(Formal (a)–(b) · Exegetical (c).)* Illustrative figures, in the shape of Britain's return to gold. A state owes 800, fixed in nominal terms, and runs a primary surplus that exactly covers its interest bill, so the stock never moves. Nominal income in year 0 is 500. Over the next six years real output grows 2% a year while prices fall 3% a year. (a) Compute nominal income in year 6 and the debt ratio then. (b) Recompute both with prices flat, and give the difference in the year-6 ratio. (c) In one sentence, which term of $d_{t+1} = \frac{1+r}{1+g}d_t - s$ is the deflation working through, and what does the example show about a government that pays every penny of interest out of taxes? Show your arithmetic and round the ratio to three decimals.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):** nominal income compounds at $(1+0.02)(1-0.03) = 0.9894$ a year, that is $-1.06\%$, **not** $2 - 3 = -1\%$. Over six years the factor is $0.9894^6 = 0.938062$, so income is $500 \times 0.938062 = 469.03$. The ratio is $800/469.03 = 1.706$, against $d_0 = 800/500 = 1.600$: a rise of about 0.106 of income with not a penny added to the debt.

**Must hit, strict (b):** with prices flat, $1.02^6 = 1.126162$, income is $563.08$ and the ratio is $800/563.08 = 1.421$. Six years of mild deflation cost about $1.706 - 1.421 = 0.285$ of income in the ratio — roughly eighteen years' worth of the 1.6%-of-GDP primary surplus Britain averaged after 1822 ($0.285/0.016 \approx 17.8$).

**Must hit, strict (c):** the deflation works through $g$. The identity's $r$ and $g$ must both be nominal or both real; here nominal growth is negative, so the growth term pushes the ratio **up**, which is the same thing as saying deflation raised the real interest rate. A government can pay all its interest out of taxes, so that the debt never grows by a shilling, and still watch the burden climb — which is why the return to gold at the prewar parity in 1821 raised Britain's ratio after the fighting had stopped.

**Wrong turns:** netting the rates to $-1\%$ and dropping the cross term; treating a constant nominal stock as a constant burden; putting a real growth rate and a nominal interest rate into the same identity.

**Model answer:** (a) $500 \times (1.02 \times 0.97)^6 = 500 \times 0.938062 = 469.03$, so $d_6 = 800/469.03 = 1.706$, up from 1.600. (b) With stable prices, $500 \times 1.126162 = 563.08$ and $d_6 = 1.421$; the deflation costs 0.285 of income. (c) It works through $g$, which must be measured on the same footing as $r$: nominal income fell 1.06% a year, so the growth term added to the ratio instead of eating it, and a state servicing its debt in full out of taxes still got heavier every year.
