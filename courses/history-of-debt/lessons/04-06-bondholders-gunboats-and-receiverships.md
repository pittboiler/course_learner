# A History of Debt · Lesson 4.6: Bondholders, gunboats, and receiverships

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 4: Debt, nation and empire in the long nineteenth century · Builds on: [4.5 Egypt, the Ottomans, and debt administration](04-05-egypt-the-ottomans-and-debt-administration.md), [3.2 Did 1688 make Britain creditworthy?](03-02-did-1688-make-britain-creditworthy.md) · Unlocks: [5.1 War debts and reparations](05-01-war-debts-and-reparations.md)

## Why this matters

A sovereign borrower has no bankruptcy court above it and no bailiff who can seize its palace. So why did nineteenth-century governments ever repay foreigners? There are two stock answers. Creditor states sent warships and took over customs houses. Or borrowers repaid to keep their names good in London. Egypt ([4.5](04-05-egypt-the-ottomans-and-debt-administration.md)) looked like the first answer. This lesson gives you the wider record, the Caribbean receiverships that came out of it, and the historians' dispute over what that record shows. That dispute still frames how economists model sovereign default.

## The idea

Start with the creditor's problem. Bondholders are thousands of scattered private people, and a defaulting government is a sovereign. Four levers were available, in rising order of force:

1. **Organize.** Bondholders form committees to negotiate as one. Where a stock exchange refuses to list a defaulter's new loans, the committee has a threat.
2. **Lobby your government.** Ask the Foreign Office or the State Department to take up the claim diplomatically.
3. **Coerce.** Blockade ports, seize customs houses, bombard.
4. **Administer.** Put foreign officials in charge of the revenues that service the debt. That was the Egyptian Caisse, the Ottoman Public Debt Administration, and later the American customs receiverships.

The question is which of these levers actually did the work. One school looks at defaulters that got a gunboat or a receiver and finds that they repaid and borrowed again cheaply. The other school looks at *why* the gunboats came and finds that they were rarely sent for bonds at all.

## Source

Theodore Roosevelt, annual message to Congress, 6 December 1904 (public domain). This is the passage later called the Roosevelt Corollary to the Monroe Doctrine.

> "If a nation shows that it knows how to act with reasonable efficiency and decency in social and political matters, if it keeps order and pays its obligations, it need fear no interference from the United States."

> "Chronic wrongdoing, or an impotence which results in a general loosening of the ties of civilized society, may in America, as elsewhere, ultimately require intervention by some civilized nation"

The United States, bound by the Monroe Doctrine, might therefore be forced

> "however reluctantly, in flagrant cases of such wrongdoing or impotence, to the exercise of an international police power."

Read the grammar before the politics. Paying obligations shows up in the *safe-harbor* sentence, next to keeping order. The *trigger* sentence names "wrongdoing" and "impotence", not default.

## The argument

**The episodes, briefly.**

- **1820s.** The new Latin American republics borrowed heavily in London in 1822–25, on the order of 20 million pounds nominal. After the crash of 1825–26, nearly all of them were in default by about 1828. Brazil was the main exception. The Poyais scheme is the sidelight: Gregor MacGregor sold bonds and land for a Central American "country" that did not exist, and emigrants who sailed there in 1822–23 died in numbers.
- **Organization.** British bondholders formed the Council of Foreign Bondholders in 1868. It was licensed as the Corporation of Foreign Bondholders in 1873, but it was a private body, not an arm of the state.
- **Official reluctance.** Palmerston's circular of 1848 said that whether Britain took up bondholders' claims was a matter for the government's discretion, not a right bondholders could invoke. The reserved power was rarely used. Investors who lent abroad were largely left to bear their own risk.
- **Venezuela, 1902–03.** Britain, Germany and Italy blockaded Venezuela over claims that mixed injuries to their nationals during civil war with unpaid debts. In February 1904 a Hague arbitral tribunal gave the blockading powers priority over the claims of non-blockading states. That rewarded force.
- **Drago and Porter.** Argentina's foreign minister Luis María Drago argued in December 1902 that public debt could not justify armed intervention or occupation of an American state. The Hague Convention of 1907 (the Porter Convention) adopted a narrower rule. States agreed "not to have recourse to armed force for the recovery of contract debts", *unless* the debtor refused arbitration or ignored the award.
- **Receiverships.** Roosevelt's 1904 message came out of the Dominican crisis. Under a *modus vivendi* of 1905, US officials collected Dominican customs and paid 55 per cent toward the debts and 45 per cent to the government. A 1907 convention formalized the receivership. By then claims of over 30 million dollars had been scaled down to about 17 million and refinanced with a 20-million-dollar New York loan. Nicaragua (1911) and Haiti (1915, under occupation; see [4.4](04-04-haitis-independence-debt.md)) followed with variants.
- **Newfoundland, 1934.** A British dominion near default was carried by British and Canadian loans. After a royal commission (1933), its own legislature asked for the constitution to be suspended. An appointed Commission of Government ruled from 1934 to 1949. No gunboat was involved, and self-government was still lost.

**The supersanctions argument** (Mitchener and Weidenmier, "Supersanctions and Sovereign Debt Repayment", 2005):

1. Between 1870 and 1913, creditor powers answered a number of defaults with *supersanctions*: military pressure, or external control over the debtor's finances. *In words:* gunboats and receivers were a real, recurring response to default.
2. After a supersanction, the country's new bonds traded at much lower spreads, its estimated default risk fell, and its time spent in default shrank sharply. *In words:* markets priced these interventions as making repayment far more likely.
3. Countries that gave up fiscal sovereignty for a long period were able to borrow large sums again. *In words:* foreign control was bad for sovereignty and good for credit.
4. ∴ The threat of supersanctions was an important enforcement mechanism in the gold-standard era. *In words:* force, or the credible prospect of it, helped make debts get paid.

**The reputation argument** (Tomz, *Reputation and International Cooperation*, 2007):

1. Lenders sort governments by type, learning from past conduct in good and bad times. *In words:* a record of repaying, or of defaulting only when times were hard, is information.
2. Governments that defaulted without excuse paid in exclusion or dearer credit. Those whose defaults matched visible bad luck were forgiven more readily. *In words:* markets punished *unexcused* default through what they believed about the borrower.
3. The military disputes that coincided with default were mostly about something else: torts against nationals, civil wars, territory. Creditor governments seldom used force for bondholders as such. *In words:* the correlation between default and gunboats is largely spurious.
4. ∴ Reputation, not coercion, sustained lending and repayment. *In words:* borrowers paid to stay creditworthy.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean: why a debtor accepts a receiver).** In the Dominican Republic around 1904, the customs houses were the state's main revenue source and the prize of every revolt. Whoever held the ports could pay an army. European claimants were pressing, and some had been promised specific customs houses as security. Run Roosevelt's corollary on it. If Washington did nothing, Venezuela and the 1904 award suggested a European power might blockade or occupy a port, which the Monroe Doctrine forbade. If Washington intervened, it had to act as the "civilized nation" itself. The receivership was the middle path: no annexation and no European flag, but American collectors. Suppose illustrative collections of 2.0 million dollars. Creditors' depository gets $0.55 \times 2.0 = 1.1$ million, and the government gets $0.45 \times 2.0 = 0.9$ million. The government's gamble, and its defenders' claim, was that honest collection would raise the total enough that 45 per cent beat what had previously reached the treasury. Critics answered that it put a foreign hand on the one lever of Dominican statecraft.

**Example 2 (hard: coding Venezuela).** Is Venezuela 1902 a case of force enforcing debt? The same facts support both readings.

- *Mitchener–Weidenmier coding:* a defaulter, a blockade, a settlement that earmarked customs revenue for claims. It counts as a supersanction, and the later credit record is evidence of its effect.
- *Tomz coding:* the triggering grievances were largely injuries and property claims from the civil war under Cipriano Castro. Bonds were a secondary issue, and bondholders were not the powers' priority. It counts as a tort dispute that happened to coincide with a default.

Notice what the disagreement is about. Both sides agree the blockade happened, and neither need dispute the bond prices. They divide on **what the event was**: an enforcement action, or a coincidence the data count as one. How you code the case fixes whether it counts for force at all. That is why the crux sits in the archives (what ministers said they were doing) and not in the spreads.

Map the two sides onto `economics-of-debt`: reputation resembles Eaton–Gersovitz, and sanctions resemble Bulow–Rogoff (see [`economics-of-debt`](../../economics-of-debt/syllabus.md)). Repeated-game reputation is the folk theorem of [`game-theory-refresher` 2.3](../../game-theory-refresher/lessons/02-03-repeated-games-folk-theorem.md). Which of these did more to get debts paid is Module 4's boss problem, and this lesson does not settle it.

## Watch out

- **You might think the Council of Foreign Bondholders was British policy.** It was a private association. Its leverage came from organization, publicity and access to listings, and it had to *lobby* a Foreign Office that usually declined.
- **You might think the Porter Convention outlawed debt-collecting force.** It restricted it. Force stayed lawful against a debtor that refused arbitration or defied an award, which is narrower than Drago's position.
- **You might think "sanctions" here means modern trade embargoes.** That would be an anachronism. Mitchener and Weidenmier's "supersanctions" are a category the authors built: gunboats plus fiscal control. Contemporaries spoke of intervention, protection and control. Do not read UN-style sanctions regimes back into 1902.
- **You might think Roosevelt announced that the US would collect Europeans' debts.** The text makes disorder and "impotence" the trigger and paying obligations one sign of good conduct. Default matters because it invites *someone else's* intervention.

## One-liner

> Nineteenth-century creditors could organize, lobby, coerce or take over the customs house; whether debts got paid because of the gunboats or because of the borrowers' reputations turns on what you think the gunboats were actually sent for.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢) *(Formal.)*** A Caribbean government's customs receipts actually reaching its treasury were 1.9 million dollars a year before a receivership. Under the receivership, honest collection brings in 3.0 million, split 55 per cent to the creditors' depository and 45 per cent to the government. All figures are illustrative. (a) How much does the government now receive, and is it better or worse off than before? (b) What collections would leave it exactly as well off as before? (c) The adjusted debt is 20 million dollars at 5 per cent, and the creditors' whole share goes to service and repayment each year. How many years until it is paid off? What if collections are only 2.0 million? Show your arithmetic.

**P2 (🟡) *(Exegetical.)*** Use the Roosevelt passages in the Source section. (a) On the text, is non-payment of debts by itself grounds for US intervention? Name the words that decide it. (b) What work do "as elsewhere" and "some civilized nation" do in the argument? Why would the claim to exercise the police power *itself* follow from the Monroe Doctrine? Connect your answer to the 1904 Hague award. 150 words or fewer in total.

**P3 (🔴, optional) *(Exegetical.)*** Mitchener and Weidenmier count Venezuela 1902 and the Dominican receivership among cases where supersanctions improved repayment. Tomz holds that force was rarely used to enforce debts. (a) Name the single premise about these episodes that one side affirms and the other denies. (b) Say what kind of evidence would move it, and why bond spreads alone cannot. Do not judge which enforcement mechanism mattered more. 150 words or fewer.

**P1** *(Formal.)*

**Must hit, strict:**
- (a) Government: $0.45 \times 3.0 = 1.35$ million. Creditors: $0.55 \times 3.0 = 1.65$ million. Compared with 1.9 before, the government is **worse off by 0.55 million** ($1.35 - 1.90 = -0.55$).
- (b) Break-even collections $C$ satisfy $0.45\,C = 1.9$, so $C = 1.9/0.45 \approx 4.22$ million (rounded to two decimals). Collections must rise about 41 per cent above 3.0 million, and to about 2.2 times the old net receipts, before the receivership stops costing the government anything.
- (c) This is an annuity. With payment $P$, debt $D$ and rate $r$, the time to repay is $n = -\ln(1 - rD/P)/\ln(1+r)$.
  - At 3.0 million: $P = 1.65$ and $rD = 0.05 \times 20 = 1.0$. So $1 - 1.0/1.65 = 0.3939$, $\ln 0.3939 = -0.9316$ and $\ln 1.05 = 0.04879$, giving $n \approx 19.1$ years.
  - At 2.0 million: $P = 1.10$, $1 - 1.0/1.10 = 0.0909$ and $\ln 0.0909 = -2.398$, giving $n \approx 49.1$ years. The payment barely exceeds the 1.0 million of annual interest, so almost nothing amortizes.

**Wrong turns:** comparing 1.35 with 3.0 instead of with 1.9 (the relevant comparison is what the treasury actually got before); dividing $D/P$ (about 12 years) and forgetting interest; missing that repayment time explodes as the creditors' share approaches the interest bill.

**Model answer:** (a) 1.35m, down 0.55m from 1.9m. (b) $1.9/0.45 \approx 4.22$m. (c) $n = -\ln(1 - 1/1.65)/\ln 1.05 \approx 19.1$ years; at 2.0m collections, $n = -\ln(1 - 1/1.1)/\ln 1.05 \approx 49.1$ years.

**P2** *(Exegetical.)*

**Must hit, strict:**
- (a) **No, not by itself.** "Pays its obligations" appears in the safe-harbor sentence as one mark of good conduct, alongside "keeps order". The trigger sentence names "chronic wrongdoing" or "an impotence which results in a general loosening of the ties of civilized society", and requires "flagrant cases". Default can be *evidence* of impotence, but the text never states it as the ground.
- (b) "As elsewhere" and "some civilized nation" make intervention a general norm among civilized powers, not a US prerogative. Europeans may claim it too. Combined with the Monroe Doctrine's bar on European intervention in the hemisphere, the United States must do the policing itself or tolerate European action. The 1904 Hague award had just rewarded the blockading powers with priority, so it made European force a live incentive to pre-empt.

**Wrong turns:** reading "pays its obligations" as the trigger; treating "however reluctantly" as settling the motive (it is the message's self-description, not evidence); saying the Corollary cites the Porter Convention (1907, later).

**Model answer:** (a) No. Payment sits in the sentence promising *non*-interference, paired with keeping order. The trigger is "chronic wrongdoing" or "impotence" loosening civilized society, and only in "flagrant cases". Default counts as a symptom, not a ground. (b) "As elsewhere … some civilized nation" states a general norm that any civilized power may intervene. Because the Monroe Doctrine bars Europeans from doing so in the Americas, the United States has to act itself or stand aside. After the 1904 award gave blockaders priority, European force looked like a rewarded strategy, so the Corollary pre-empts it by making Washington the policeman.

---

**P3** *(Exegetical, strict on naming one premise; no verdict on force vs reputation is asked or graded.)*

**Must hit, strict:**
- (a) The crux: *whether the military and fiscal interventions that followed defaults were undertaken to enforce the debts*, so that they belong in the sample as enforcement. Mitchener–Weidenmier affirm it by coding them as supersanctions. Tomz denies it for most cases: the disputes were about torts, civil wars or territory and only coincided with default.
- (b) Evidence: diplomatic archives and cabinet papers showing ministers' stated aims, whether bondholder claims were pressed or dropped in the settlements, and cases of default with no intervention against cases of intervention with no default. Spreads cannot settle it, because a fall in spreads after an intervention fits both readings. Order restored for any reason makes repayment likelier, so price movements do not reveal *why* the gunboats came.

**Wrong turns:** stating the crux as "force vs reputation" (that is the conclusion, not the premise); arguing which side is right (the boss problem's task); denying that the blockade happened.

**Model answer:** The sides divide on whether post-default interventions were *debt enforcement*. Mitchener and Weidenmier treat Venezuela and the Dominican receivership as sanctions applied because of default. Tomz reads most such episodes as tort or political disputes that coincided with default. What would move it is the record of intent and settlement: what ministers wrote, whether bondholder claims drove the terms, and how often defaults went unpunished while interventions happened without default. Spreads cannot decide it, because any intervention that restores order and earmarks revenue should lower spreads, whatever it was sent for.

## Flashback

**From Lesson 4.4 (Haiti's independence debt):** *(Formal.)* A state owes an indemnity of 40 million francs in four equal annual installments, the first due now. Having no revenue to spare, it pays that first installment out of a bond issue: 5 per cent coupon on face value, principal repaid in one lump after 25 years, sold at 88. Ignore commissions. (a) What face value must it issue to deliver exactly 10 million francs, and what do the coupons cost a year? (b) Add up the nominal claims against the state immediately before and immediately after the operation (indemnity still outstanding, bond principal, coupons still to be paid), and say in one sentence what "double debt" names. Round to two decimals.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):**
- Face value: $10 / 0.88 = 11.36$ million francs (11.3636 before rounding). Selling at 88 means each 100 of face brings 88, so delivering 10 million takes more than 10 million of face.
- Coupons: $0.05 \times 11.3636 = 0.57$ million francs a year (0.5682).

**Must hit, strict (b):**
- Before: 40 million of indemnity, and nothing else.
- After: indemnity $40 - 10 = 30$, plus bond principal 11.36, plus coupons $25 \times 0.5682 = 14.20$. Total $30 + 11.36 + 14.20 = 55.57$ million.
- Discharging 10 million created 25.57 million of new nominal claims, so the total rose by 15.57 million.
- The one sentence: "double debt" does not mean the sum doubled; it means the indemnity was refinanced rather than paid down, so the state now faces a second creditor — bondholders, with their own discount and interest — alongside what is left of the indemnity fund.

**Wrong turns:** issuing 10 million of *face* value, which delivers only 8.8 million and leaves the installment short; adding the bond to the full 40 million, when the installment is discharged and the indemnity falls to 30; concluding from the rising nominal total that the operation was a bad deal in present value — at a 6 per cent discount the bond's obligations are worth about 9.91 million against the 10 million raised, and the real burden is that a one-off payment became twenty-five years of charges on revenue.

**Model answer:** (a) 11.36 million francs of face value, since $10/0.88 = 11.3636$; the coupons cost $0.05 \times 11.3636 = 0.57$ million a year. (b) Before: 40 million. After: 30 of indemnity, 11.36 of principal and $25 \times 0.5682 = 14.20$ of coupons, for 55.57 million — a rise of 15.57 million for a 10 million payment. "Double debt" names the structure, not the size: the installment was borrowed rather than paid, so the indemnity fund and the bondholders are two creditors at once, and the discount means more is owed than was ever received.

</details>
