# A History of Debt · Lesson 4.1: Hamilton's assumption plan

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 4: Debt, nation and empire in the long nineteenth century · Builds on: [3.4 Hume's prophecy: carrying a great debt](03-04-humes-prophecy-carrying-a-great-debt.md) · Unlocks: [4.2 Debtors' prisons and the invention of bankruptcy](04-02-debtors-prisons-and-the-invention-of-bankruptcy.md)

## Why this matters

Hume and Smith looked at Britain's debt and saw ruin coming. Fifteen years after Hume's gloomiest essay, a new American Treasury secretary looked at the same British system and saw a model worth copying. In January 1790 Alexander Hamilton proposed that the new federal government pay its Revolutionary debts at face value to whoever held them, and take over the states' war debts too. Both halves were bitterly fought. The fight set a pattern that recurs through the rest of this course: a borrower settles an old debt it cannot easily afford, in order to be able to borrow again. And it keeps raising the same question: when a debt has changed hands at a deep discount, who deserves the gain from paying it?

## The idea

Picture a Continental soldier in 1783. He is paid in paper: a certificate promising the United States will pay, say, 100 dollars with interest. The Confederation Congress cannot tax, so it pays neither interest nor principal. By the late 1780s his certificate trades at a fraction of face. Critics of the Report said a buyer might have paid three or four shillings for a claim to twenty, 15 to 20 cents on the dollar. The soldier needs cash, so he sells, often to a merchant in a coastal city. Then the Constitution gives the new government the power to tax imports. Prices of certificates jump as holders expect to be paid.

Now the government must decide how much to pay, and to whom.

- **Funding at par.** Hamilton said the debt should be converted into new federal "stock" (bonds) backed by earmarked revenue, and honoured at its face value, not at the depressed market price. Whoever holds the certificate gets the full claim.
- **Discrimination.** James Madison, Hamilton's co-author on *The Federalist*, answered that this handed a windfall to people who had, in the words of the scheme's supporters that Hamilton summarized, "speculated on his distresses." On 11 February 1790 he proposed to pay present holders the highest market price the securities had reached, and pay the balance to the original holders. The House committee rejected it, 36 to 13, on 22 February.
- **Assumption.** The states had borrowed for the war as well, and Hamilton estimated their debts at about 25 million dollars. He proposed the Union take them over on the same terms. States that had already paid much of their debt, Virginia above all, objected to being taxed for states that had not, Massachusetts and South Carolina among them.

Hamilton's reply on discrimination rested on contract and on markets. He argued that the certificate was a promise to the holder, and that the transferability made it valuable. A government that re-opened past sales would teach every future lender that its paper was unsafe to buy. He added that the Constitution itself (Article VI) had kept the old debts as valid as they were under the Confederation.

## Source

Hamilton, *First Report on Public Credit* (dated 9 January 1790), from the section on discrimination (public domain):

> The nature of the contract, in its origin, is, that the public will pay the sum expressed in the security, to the first holder or his assignee.

The load-bearing words are "or his assignee." Madison's case needs the original transaction to have been something less than a sale of the whole claim. Hamilton's case needs the assignee to have bought all of it.

## The argument

**A. Hamilton's case for funding and assumption** (the Report, paraphrased; the debt figures are his own).

1. **Nations must borrow in emergencies, above all in war, and a country with little monied capital must borrow more urgently than most.** *In words:* the United States will need credit again, soon and badly.
2. **A nation of doubtful credit pays a premium on every loan and every purchase on time.** *In words:* bad credit is a tax you pay forever.
3. **Credit is kept by good faith, meaning the punctual performance of contracts, and the Revolutionary debt carries extra moral weight.** Hamilton's phrase: "It was the price of liberty." *In words:* this debt, of all debts, must be honoured.
4. **A properly funded debt passes as money, which increases capital, lowers interest rates and raises land values; an unfunded one is a speculative commodity that drains cash from productive use.** *In words:* funding the debt benefits people who hold none of it.
5. **The debt is large: about 54.1 million dollars owed by the Union** (about 11.7 million foreign, 40.4 million domestic, of which 13 million was unpaid interest, plus about 2 million unliquidated), **and about 25 million owed by the states.** Paying full interest would require taxes that would hurt the creditors themselves. *In words:* full nominal terms are unaffordable, so creditors should accept voluntary modified terms. Hamilton expected rates to fall toward 5 and then 4 percent.
6. **State creditors would be worse off than federal ones, because the Union now controls import duties; a divided creditor class would work against the national credit.** *In words:* assumption joins every creditor's interest to the Union's.

∴ **Fund the federal and state debts together, at face value, on modified terms, with revenue pledged to them and a sinking fund.** Hamilton added that creating any debt should always come with the means of paying it off. He was not claiming that debt is good in itself.

**B. Madison's case for discrimination** (from his February 1790 speeches, paraphrased).

1. **The original holders gave real services or goods, and were paid in paper the public then failed to honour.** *In words:* they were the ones who lent to the Revolution.
2. **They sold under necessity, and the low price reflected the public's own default.** *In words:* the discount was the government's breach, not the soldier's bad bargain.
3. **Paying the purchaser the full face value turns the public's breach into the purchaser's gain.** *In words:* justice follows the injury, not the paper.
4. **The purchaser loses nothing he reasonably expected if he receives the highest market price.** *In words:* the buyer is made whole, and only the windfall goes back to the soldier.

∴ **Pay the present holder the highest market price and the original holder the rest.** Madison's plan did not save the Treasury a cent, because the full sum was still paid. It moved the gain from one claimant to another.

The Funding Act simplified Hamilton's menu of options. For each 100 dollars of principal, holders received 66⅔ dollars in stock paying 6 percent from 1791 and 33⅓ dollars in "deferred" 6 percent stock paying nothing until 1801. Unpaid interest was funded in 3 percent stock. Assumption was capped at 21.5 million dollars of state debt.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean: two soldiers).** Two soldiers each hold a 1,000-dollar certificate. Asa sold his in 1788 for 200 dollars. Ben kept his. Under the Act, whoever holds the certificate gets 666.67 dollars of immediate 6 percent stock and 333.33 dollars of deferred stock. Ben gets that package. Asa's buyer gets exactly the same package, and Asa gets nothing more. Under Madison's plan the buyer would have received the highest market price, and Asa the rest of the claim. Ben is treated identically under both plans. The whole dispute is about Asa and his buyer, and it turns on the Source's word "assignee." Hamilton also pointed out a practical problem: many sales went through several hands, so the government would have to trace every one.

Notice what "par" meant. The deferred third paid nothing for ten years, and unpaid interest earned only 3 percent. The holders' claim was honoured at face value in name, but was worth less than face in present value. Hamilton defended the trimmed terms as voluntary: subscribers accepted them in exchange for secure payment, and "par" was never meant as an accounting of present value.

**Example 2 (hard: Virginia's taxpayers).** Hamilton's fairness premise was that all creditors should be repaid on the same terms, since the states had borrowed for the common cause. Run it from Virginia's side. Virginia had retired much of its war debt through its own taxes and land sales. Under assumption, Virginians would now pay federal import duties that serviced Massachusetts's unpaid debt. What is equal for creditors is unequal for taxpayers. Hamilton's premise does not settle this, because it compares creditors and says nothing about who pays.

The political settlement did not decide the principle. It bought votes. The capital went to the Potomac, and the settlement of state accounts with the Union was eased for Virginia (historians differ on how much that mattered). How decisive the dinner was is itself disputed. Jefferson's account was written after he had turned against Hamilton, and some historians give the residence bargain less weight than Jefferson did. Two readings of the whole plan remain in play:

- **The nation-building reading.** Funding and assumption tied wealthy creditors to the federal government, created a liquid national bond market, and gave the new state the credit it would later use. Economic historians such as Richard Sylla stress this financial-revolution side.
- **The redistribution reading.** The plan moved wealth from taxpayers and original holders to northern speculators and to members of Congress who held securities. This line runs from the Jeffersonian critics through Charles Beard (1913) and E. James Ferguson's *The Power of the Purse* (1961), who documented how concentrated the holdings had become.

Both readings can be true of the same statute. The historians' disagreement is over which effect mattered more.

## Watch out

- **Anachronism: "vulture fund."** Calling the 1790 buyers "speculators" is fair, since contemporaries did. Mapping them onto modern distressed-debt funds imports a sovereign-bankruptcy setting and a litigation strategy that did not exist. Hold that comparison for [6.3](06-03-holdouts-and-the-law-of-sovereign-restructuring.md).
- **"Funding at par" is not "paid in full in present value."** The deferred stock and the 3 percent interest stock cut the value of the claim. What was protected was each holder's *nominal* claim, with no distinction by holder.
- **Madison was not proposing a partial default.** His plan paid the whole sum and divided it differently. A plan that pays buyers what they paid and keeps the difference is a haircut, a different thing.
- **The 1840s sequel is not a reversal of 1790.** Assumption in 1790 was a founding bargain. Refusing to assume in the 1840s left states responsible for their own borrowing, and later readers have taken it as a precedent, as [6.2](06-02-the-eurozone-and-greece.md) shows. Whether each choice was *right* is `economics-of-debt`'s moral-hazard question, not this lesson's.

## One-liner

> Hamilton paid the Revolution's paper at face value to whoever held it and took on the states' debts too, buying national credit at the price of a windfall that Madison thought belonged to the soldiers.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢) *(Exegetical.)*** From the *First Report on Public Credit*:

> It is a well known fact, that in countries in which the national debt is properly funded, and an object of established confidence, it answers most of the purposes of money.

(a) Name the two conditions this sentence attaches to its claim, quoting the words that state each. (b) A critic writes: "Hamilton thought public debt was a blessing in itself." Using this sentence and one other claim of the Report reported in the lesson, say whether the text supports that reading. 80 words or fewer for (b).

**P2 (🟡) *(Formal.)*** In January 1788 a merchant buys a 1,000-dollar certificate of principal (no unpaid interest attached) for 200 dollars. He subscribes it under the 1790 Funding Act terms in the lesson. Use annual interest (ignore quarterly payment) and round to the cent.
(a) What is his annual interest income in 1791–1800, and from 1801 on? What is each as a yield on his 200-dollar cost?
(b) Treat both stocks as perpetual 6 percent annuities and value them at 1 January 1791 at a 6 percent discount rate, so each is worth its face value on the date its interest starts. What is his package worth?
(c) Under the scheme Hamilton describes, in which purchasers receive only what they paid plus interest, he would receive his cost plus 6 percent simple interest for three years. How much is that, and what multiple of it is the value in (b)?
(d) In one sentence: why is the value in (b) below 1,000 dollars even though the debt was "funded at par"?

**P3 (🔴, optional) *(Exegetical.)*** An invented op-ed:

> When Port Halden defaulted, hedge funds bought its bonds at 30 cents on the dollar. Now they demand 100. That windfall belongs to the taxpayers, not to Wall Street. In 1790 James Madison showed the way: pay the buyers what they paid and not a cent more.

Name the historical analogy doing the work, state two features of Madison's 1790 proposal the analogy relies on, and say for each whether it carries over. Take no side on Port Halden. 150 words or fewer.

<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical, strict.)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** (1) the debt must be funded: "properly funded"; (2) lenders must trust it: "an object of established confidence." Both are conditions. The claim is about *that* kind of debt, not debt as such.
**Must hit, strict (b):** the text does not support the reading. The benefit is conditional on proper funding and confidence (and the Report says an unfunded debt does the opposite). Hamilton also held that creating debt should always come with the means of paying it off, and he proposed a sinking fund.
**Wrong turns:** listing "well known fact" as a condition; answering (b) with a verdict on whether public debt *is* beneficial, which the problem does not ask.
**Model answer (b):** No. The sentence makes debt money-like only when "properly funded" and trusted, and the Report treats an unfunded debt as a drain on circulating cash. Hamilton also insisted that creating debt should always come with the means of extinguishing it. His claim is about funding an existing debt well, not about borrowing more.

---

**P2** *(Formal.)*

**Must hit:**
- (a) Immediate stock is $\tfrac23 \times 1000 = 666.67$ dollars, paying $0.06 \times 666.67 = 40.00$ dollars a year from 1791. Deferred stock is $333.33$ dollars, adding $0.06 \times 333.33 = 20.00$ dollars from 1801. Income is **40.00 dollars a year for 1791–1800** (yield on cost $40/200 = 20\%$) and **60.00 dollars a year from 1801** ($60/200 = 30\%$).
- (b) Immediate stock at 6 percent is worth par, $666.67$. The deferred stock is worth par in 1801, discounted ten years: $333.33 / 1.06^{10} = 333.33 / 1.790848 = 186.13$. Total **852.80 dollars**.
- (c) $200 \times (1 + 0.06 \times 3) = 236.00$ dollars. Multiple: $852.80 / 236.00 = 3.61$ (to two decimal places). Against his cost alone, $852.80/200 = 4.26$.
- (d) A third of the claim earns nothing for ten years, and a delayed payment is worth less today: the face value was kept, but its present value was cut by about 15 percent at a 6 percent discount rate.

**Wrong turns:** discounting the immediate stock as well; discounting the deferred stock for nine or eleven years; using compound interest in (c) without saying so (which gives 238.20 dollars and a multiple of 3.58, also acceptable if stated); reading "par" in (d) as proof that holders got full present value.

**Model answer:** (a) 40 dollars a year (20 percent on cost) until 1800, then 60 dollars (30 percent). (b) $666.67 + 333.33/1.06^{10} = 666.67 + 186.13 = 852.80$ dollars. (c) 236.00 dollars. The Act's package is worth 3.61 times that. (d) The deferred third pays nothing until 1801, so its present value is below face even though the nominal claim is intact.

---

**P3** *(Exegetical, strict.)*

**Must hit, strict:**
- The analogy: Madison's 1790 discrimination proposal, i.e. treating the buyer of a depreciated public debt differently from its original holder.
- Feature 1: **the full sum was still paid.** Madison gave the buyer the highest market price and gave the *balance to the original holder*. The public saved nothing. The op-ed keeps the difference for taxpayers. That is a haircut or partial default, not discrimination, so this feature does not carry over.
- Feature 2 (any one of): **identifiable wronged original holders** (soldiers and suppliers paid in paper), who have no counterpart in the op-ed. **Depreciation caused by the debtor's own default**, which does carry over if Port Halden's default drove the price down. **The highest market price, not the purchase price,** which is more generous to buyers than the op-ed's rule, so it only partly carries over.
**Wrong turns:** arguing whether the funds deserve 100 cents (a verdict the problem excludes); treating Madison's plan as adopted law; calling it Hamilton's plan.
**Model answer:** The op-ed borrows Madison's 1790 proposal to discriminate between original and present holders. It depends first on the idea that a buyer of depreciated paper deserves less than face. But Madison's plan still paid the whole debt: buyers got the highest market price and original holders got the rest. The op-ed gives the difference to the debtor's taxpayers, which is a haircut, not discrimination. That feature does not carry over. Second, Madison had a class of injured original creditors, soldiers paid in paper they had to sell. The op-ed names no original bondholders at all. That also fails, though the idea that the debtor's own default caused the discount may still apply.

</details>

## Flashback

**From Lesson 3.3 (The South Sea bubble):** About 12 million pounds of formerly irredeemable annuities ended up inside the redeemable South Sea annuity, on which the state paid 5 percent, cut to 4 percent in 1727 and eventually to 3 percent. *(Formal (a)–(b) · Exegetical (c).)* (a) Compute the state's annual interest bill on that 12 million at each of the three rates. (b) Capitalize the saving from 5 percent down to 3 percent as a perpetuity, discounting at 3 percent. (c) Velde puts the conversion's ex post gain to the state at about 1.8 million pounds in present value. Name two reasons your figure in (b) is so much larger. Show the arithmetic; two sentences for (c).

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a)–(b):**
- (a) $0.05 \times 12{,}000{,}000 = 600{,}000$; $0.04 \times 12{,}000{,}000 = 480{,}000$; $0.03 \times 12{,}000{,}000 = 360{,}000$ pounds a year.
- (b) The saving is $600{,}000 - 360{,}000 = 240{,}000$ pounds a year, and $240{,}000 / 0.03 = 8{,}000{,}000$ pounds — about 4.4 times Velde's figure.

**Must hit, strict (c):** any two of —
- **Timing.** Nothing was saved before 1727, and 3 percent came only decades later, so the stream has to be discounted from a long way off. A 240,000 perpetuity starting 30 years out is worth $8{,}000{,}000 / 1.03^{30} \approx 3.3$ million.
- **The counterfactual is not "5 percent forever."** Market rates had already fallen before 1720 — that is why the irredeemables were a problem at all — so only the part of the cut the state could not have obtained otherwise belongs to the conversion.
- **A perpetuity is the wrong instrument.** The annuities replaced were finite, up to 99 years, and the new stock was redeemable, so an infinite stream overstates the saving.
- **The state gave things up.** The premium in stock that bought the annuitants' consent, and the roughly 7 million pounds the Company had promised and Parliament remitted.

**Wrong turns:** crediting the conversion with the whole 5-to-3 percent cut, when what it bought was *redeemability*, the power to cut the rate at all; computing the saving against 4 percent and calling it the total; reading Velde's 1.8 million as cash received rather than a present value.

**Model answer:** (a) 600,000, 480,000 and 360,000 pounds a year. (b) The cut saves 240,000 a year, so $240{,}000 / 0.03 = 8{,}000{,}000$ pounds. (c) The savings were distant — nothing until 1727, 3 percent only much later — and even a perpetuity beginning 30 years out is worth about 3.3 million at that discount rate. And the calculation credits 1720 with the entire rate cut, when rates were falling anyway; what the conversion actually bought was the right to redeem, without which no cut was possible at all.

</details>
