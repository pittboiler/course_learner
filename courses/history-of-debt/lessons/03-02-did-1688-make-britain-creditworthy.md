# A History of Debt · Lesson 3.2: Did 1688 make Britain creditworthy?

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 3: The financial revolutions · Builds on: [3.1 From the Stop of the Exchequer to the Bank of England](03-01-from-the-stop-of-the-exchequer-to-the-bank-of-england.md) · Unlocks: [3.3 The South Sea bubble](03-03-the-south-sea-bubble.md)

## Why this matters

In 1672 Charles II stopped paying his bankers. Twenty-five years later his successors' government owed about seventeen times what the Crown had owed in 1688, and by the 1730s it borrowed at around 3 percent. Something changed. In 1989 Douglass North and Barry Weingast named the change: the Revolution of 1688 tied the sovereign's hands, and a ruler whose hands are tied can be trusted with other people's money. "Constitutions and Commitment" became one of the most-cited articles in economic history. It is the founding text of the claim that good institutions make states rich, and it is still quoted whenever someone says a country can cut its borrowing costs by fixing its constitution. So it matters whether the evidence says what the thesis needs.

## The idea

A lender to a king faces a simple problem: the borrower writes the rules. A king can delay payment, force a "loan" to be renewed, change the tax that services it, or stop the Exchequer, and no court he appoints will stop him. Lenders know this. So they lend little, for short terms, at high rates, or not at all. The king would be better off if he could *promise* not to do these things, but a promise from someone who can break it at no cost is worth nothing. This is a **commitment problem**: the power that makes you dangerous is the same power that makes your promise empty.

North and Weingast's answer: you fix it by giving the power away. After 1688 the Crown could not tax without Parliament, could not rewrite a loan without Parliament, and could not lean on judges to ratify either. Now changing the terms of the debt took the agreement of several parties, including the one whose members held the debt. Promises became credible, and lenders responded.

The critics do not deny that Britain became a great borrower. They ask two sharper questions. **When** did lenders start charging less, and was the timing right for 1688? And **what** made the promise credible: rules on paper, or who held power under those rules?

## Source

The Bill of Rights (1689), article 4, as enacted (public domain):

> That levying money for or to the use of the Crown by pretence of prerogative, without grant of Parliament, for longer time, or in other manner than the same is or shall be granted, is illegal;

Read what the clause constrains and what it leaves out. It limits how the Crown may *raise* money. It says nothing about lenders, repayment, or interest. Any protection for creditors has to be inferred, which is exactly what the thesis does.

## The argument

North and Weingast, "Constitutions and Commitment" (*Journal of Economic History*, 1989), reconstructed:

1. **Before 1688 the Crown could and did alter its obligations unilaterally.** Their evidence runs from the early Stuarts' forced loans to the Stop of the Exchequer. James I's 1617 London loan, for instance, was rolled over against the lenders' wishes. *In words:* the sovereign was both a party to the contract and its only enforcer.
2. **Wealth holders anticipated this.** On their figures the Crown owed only about £1 million on the eve of the Revolution, borrowed short, and paid anywhere from 6 to 30 percent, while Holland borrowed long at 4 percent. *In words:* lenders priced the king's discretion into every loan.
3. **The Revolution settlement moved fiscal power to Parliament.** It gave Parliament control of taxation and appropriation, abolished prerogative courts, and made the Crown's income depend on regular sessions. The Act of Settlement (1701) later gave judges tenure during good behaviour. *In words:* the king lost the tools he had used to rewrite deals.
4. **Changing a debt's terms now needed several independent vetoes:** Crown, Lords and Commons. The Bank of England (1694) added a check of its own. *In words:* any one party wanting to default could be blocked by another.
5. **Parliament represented the wealth holders who lent.** *In words:* the body with the veto was the body with the most to lose from default.
6. **So the government's promise to repay became credible, and it could borrow far more at far lower rates.** *In words:* tie your hands and people will lend to you.

The predicted evidence, from their tables: debt rose from about £1 million (1688) to £16.7 million (1697), roughly 40 percent of national income on their estimate, and to £54 million by 1720. Long-term government loans fell from 14 percent in the early 1690s to 6–8 percent by the decade's end and about 3 percent by the 1730s. They added a caveat that matters later: credibility grew *as society gained experience* with the new institutions.

## Map of positions

| Position | What made Britain creditworthy? | When should rates have fallen? | Key evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| **North and Weingast (1989)** | Constitutional rules: parliamentary supremacy, veto players, independent courts | Soon after 1688, then further with experience | Debt growth plus falling rates on government loans, 1690s–1730s |
| **Clark (1996)** | Secure property long predates 1688; the Revolution changed little for private wealth | No break at 1688 | Returns on land and rent charges show no shift around 1688 |
| **Sussman and Yafeh (2006)** | Not the rules, at least not soon; war, peace and financial development | Not for decades | Government yields stayed high and volatile for about forty years, moved with war and with Dutch rates, and were no lower than those of less constitutional states |
| **Stasavage (2003)** | Who governed under the rules: a Whig coalition that included the creditors | When and while the Whigs held power | Borrowing costs rose when the Tories took office in 1710 and fell under Whig control |
| **Quinn (2001)** | A complication, not a rival cause | — | A goldsmith-banker's private lending rates *rose* in the 1690s as government borrowing crowded out private credit |

The rows are not symmetrical. Clark and Sussman–Yafeh attack the **timing**. Stasavage accepts that politics made the debt credible and disputes the **mechanism**. North and Weingast's learning clause is a possible reply to the first kind of critic. It does nothing against the second, which needs a different reply (Example 2).

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean: the Stop of the Exchequer, rerun after 1688).**

Suppose a ruler in 1700 wants what Charles II wanted in 1672: suspend repayment and redirect the revenue to a war. Run the premises. The revenue that services the loans was voted by statute for that purpose, so redirecting it means a new statute (P3). A new statute needs the Commons and the Lords as well as the Crown, and any of the three can block it (P4). Many members of the Commons, and the Bank's directors, hold the debt or represent those who do (P5). A creditor who is not paid can go to judges the Crown can no longer dismiss at will once the 1701 Act takes effect (P3). Every step Charles took alone now needs a coalition that includes the victims. The thesis gives a clean verdict: the 1672 move is no longer available. Notice what did the work. It was not the Bill of Rights clause in the Source, which only bars taxing without Parliament. It was the combination of that clause with appropriation and with who sat in Parliament.

**Example 2 (hard: a Parliament that wants to default).**

Now let the pressure come from the legislature instead of the Crown. After 1688 Parliament is supreme. If a majority there, say landowners tired of paying land tax to service bondholders, wants to cut interest or delay payment, which veto stops it? The Crown's veto was fading, and after 1708 no monarch refused assent to a bill. The Lords rarely blocked money bills. P4's multiple vetoes are much weaker against the body that holds supremacy.

This is Stasavage's opening. Supremacy moved the power to default from the king to Parliament; it did not abolish it. What protected creditors was that *the majority in Parliament* did not want to default, and that depended on which coalition held the majority. He finds that when Harley's Tory ministry replaced the Whigs in 1710, borrowing costs rose. The Tories were the party of the landed interest and hostile to the new moneyed men. Under the long Whig supremacy after 1715, costs fell. If that holds, the rules were necessary but not sufficient, and the same constitution with a different majority would have produced a less creditworthy state.

A defender of North and Weingast has replies at full strength. Tory governments did not in fact repudiate. The veto structure made outright default slow and visible, which raised its cost even for a hostile majority. And a party system in which creditors can win power at all is itself a product of the 1688 settlement. The dispute is not whether politics mattered but whether "institutions" names the rules alone or the rules plus the coalitions they allow. The formal version of this commitment problem belongs to [`institutions-and-development`](../../institutions-and-development/syllabus.md) 1.3. The sovereign-debt model belongs to [`economics-of-debt`](../../economics-of-debt/syllabus.md) 6.3. Here it stays an argument about evidence.

## Watch out

- **"Rates fell after 1688" is not "rates fell because of 1688."** Rates fell over forty years that included peace after 1713, the growth of the Bank and a secondary market in government debt, and falling rates in Holland too. A decline across decades fits many causes; the thesis needs one tied to the settlement.
- **Watch the 14 percent.** The early-1690s loans in North and Weingast's table were annuity and lottery-style schemes. Critics note that their yields are not directly comparable with later perpetual loans, so part of the "fall" may be a change in instruments.
- **Anachronism trap: "constitution" does not mean a written document.** There was no single founding text in 1689. The "constitution" North and Weingast describe is a settlement built from statutes (the Bill of Rights, annual supply, the 1701 Act) and from practice. Reading it as a modern entrenched constitution with judicial review imports a court that could strike down Parliament's acts. England had no such court.
- **Stasavage is not saying institutions don't matter.** His claim is that the same rules yield different credibility under different coalitions. His Italian city-states argument in [2.3](02-03-the-public-debts-of-city-states.md) has the same shape: creditors in government.

## One-liner

> 1688 gave Parliament the power to default; whether Britain became creditworthy depends on whether you credit the rules that moved that power, or the creditors who ended up holding it.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢) *(Formal.)*** The yields below are **invented for illustration** and are not historical data. British long-term government debt yields 7.0 percent in "year A" and 3.2 percent in "year B". Dutch provincial debt yields 4.0 percent and 2.8 percent in the same years.
(a) Compute the British–Dutch spread in each year, in basis points, and the change.
(b) Split the fall in British yields into the part shared with Holland and the British-specific part, and give the British-specific share as a percentage.
(c) For a British debt of £50 million, how much annual interest does the British-specific part save, and how much does the common part save?

**P2 (🟡) *(Exegetical.)*** North and Weingast say post-1688 credibility came from constitutional rules; Stasavage says it came from a creditor-friendly Whig coalition governing under those rules. (a) Name the single premise in the lesson's reconstruction of North and Weingast that Stasavage must deny or weaken, and say why. (b) Describe the *kind* of historical comparison that would separate the two explanations: what must be held fixed, and what must vary? 150 words or fewer in total.

**P3 (🔴, optional) *(Exegetical.)*** An invented op-ed:

> "Our borrowing costs are a verdict on our institutions. England proved it in 1688: once Parliament bound the king, lenders flocked in and rates collapsed. Pass the constitutional amendment requiring a two-thirds legislative vote for any change to debt terms, and the markets will reward us within the year."

Name the historical claim the argument relies on, identify which two of North and Weingast's premises it carries over, and give two distinct ways the analogy breaks, each drawn from a position in the lesson. 150 words or fewer.

<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Formal.)*

(a) Spread = British yield − Dutch yield. Year A: $7.0 - 4.0 = 3.0$ percentage points = **300 bp**. Year B: $3.2 - 2.8 = 0.4$ points = **40 bp**. Change: $300 - 40 = $ **260 bp** of compression.

(b) British fall: $7.0 - 3.2 = 3.8$ points (380 bp). Common fall, proxied by the Dutch fall: $4.0 - 2.8 = 1.2$ points (120 bp). British-specific: $380 - 120 = 260$ bp, equal to the spread compression, as it must be. Share: $260/380 = 0.684\ldots \approx$ **68.4 percent** (rounded to one decimal).

(c) British-specific saving: $0.026 \times 50{,}000{,}000 = $ **£1.3 million a year**. Common saving: $0.012 \times 50{,}000{,}000 = $ **£0.6 million a year**. Check: total $0.038 \times 50{,}000{,}000 = 1{,}900{,}000$, i.e. £1.9 million, and $1.3 + 0.6 = 1.9$. (Computed in Python; exact.)

**Wrong turns:** reporting the whole 380 bp fall as the effect of British institutions; treating the Dutch fall as zero; mixing percent and basis points (1 point = 100 bp).

---

**P2** *(Exegetical.)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** P4 (multiple veto players protect creditors), or equivalently P5 read as a *constant* (Parliament reliably represents creditors). Stasavage's point is that after 1688 Parliament itself held the power to default, and the Crown's and Lords' vetoes were weak against it. So protection depended on which coalition held the Commons, not on the veto structure.
**Must hit, strict (b):** a comparison that holds the rules fixed while the governing coalition changes (e.g. the same constitution before and after a change of ministry, as in 1710), and/or one that holds the coalition fixed while the rules change. If credibility moves with the coalition and the rules stay the same, that favours Stasavage. If it moves with the rules alone, that favours North and Weingast.
**Wrong turns:** saying Stasavage denies that institutions matter; picking P1, which both sides accept; proposing a comparison in which rules and coalition change together (1688 itself), which cannot separate them.
**Model answer:** (a) P4. Stasavage grants the settlement but denies that vetoes protected creditors, since Parliament was the supreme veto and could itself default; what protected them was a Whig majority that included creditors. (b) Hold the rules constant and let the coalition vary, as in the change of ministry in 1710 and the later Whig supremacy. Or hold the coalition constant across a rule change. Tracking credibility across such pairs separates the two; 1688 alone cannot, because both changed at once.

---

**P3** *(Exegetical.)*

**Must hit, strict:** the claim is that 1688's constitutional constraints *caused* the fall in British borrowing costs, and did so quickly. The op-ed carries over P4 (vetoes block changes to debt terms, here a supermajority) and P6 (credible commitment leads to lower rates). Two breaks from different positions: (i) **timing** (Sussman–Yafeh, Clark): rates stayed high and volatile for decades, so "within the year" is not what the British record shows; (ii) **mechanism** (Stasavage): credibility tracked the governing coalition, so a rule without creditor-friendly majorities may not bind. An acceptable third: the 1689 settlement was not an entrenched written amendment, so the institutional analogy is loose.
**Wrong turns:** arguing that the amendment is bad policy (not asked); naming a single break twice under different labels; treating the 14 percent to 3 percent fall as settled proof.
**Model answer:** It relies on North and Weingast's reading that binding the sovereign in 1688 caused British borrowing costs to fall. It imports P4 (a supermajority as a veto on changing debt terms) and P6 (commitment brings cheaper credit). First break, on timing: Sussman and Yafeh find British yields high and volatile for about forty years after 1688, and Clark finds no break in private rates, so no quick reward is shown. Second break, on mechanism: Stasavage ties credibility to a Whig coalition that included creditors, and costs rose when the Tories came in during 1710. So a rule may not bind if the majority does not want it to.

## Flashback

**From Lesson 2.4 (Lending to kings):** *(Exegetical.)* Take this as given: the Riccardi of Lucca were Edward I's bankers from the 1270s, advancing money against the wool customs, which they were allowed to collect themselves, and handling the king's transfers abroad and papal crusade money. In 1294, at the outbreak of war with France, Edward seized their assets in England and the firm went under. (a) Name which of the three tools a lender has against a sovereign the Riccardi were relying on, and the matching hazard each ran into. (b) Drelichman and Voth conclude that Philip II's bankers profited despite his suspensions. Say what you would have to measure to run that test on the Riccardi, and which single figure decides it. Two sentences or fewer per part.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):** tool 1, **grabbing the cash flow** — they collected the customs before the money reached the king; the hazard is that the right to collect is itself a royal grant, revocable at will, which is precisely what 1294 revoked. And tool 3, **holding something the king needs next year** — the transfer services that moved his money abroad; the hazard is that the lever only works if lenders act together, and a single firm can be replaced (Edward turned to other Italian companies, the Frescobaldi and later the Bardi). Credit for noting that tool 2, pricing the risk, is what part (b) tests.

**Must hit, strict (b):** the whole relationship, not the year of the seizure — the return earned across roughly twenty years of service, including interest and the trade privileges, wool licences and papal business that came with the position, set against what was lost when the assets were seized. The deciding figure is the **annualized return over the full relationship** (the counterpart of the roughly 15 percent benchmark for Philip's bankers), compared with what the same capital would have earned in ordinary merchant business; a large loss in 1294 is consistent with either verdict. An answer that also names the hard part — that the premium in the good years is only visible in contract-level archives — earns credit.

**Wrong turns:** treating the collapse in 1294 as settling the profitability question, which is exactly the inference Drelichman and Voth refuse for Philip II; calling the customs collection "collateral" in the modern sense of an asset the lender could keep against the sovereign's will; naming the Genoese coalition's cohesion as something the Riccardi had.

**Model answer:** (a) They used tool 1, collecting the wool customs at source, and tool 3, running the king's transfers and his papal money. Both failed in the way the lesson predicts: the collection right was a grant Edward could revoke, and one firm's services could be replaced by another Italian company, so there was no coalition to make withdrawal costly. (b) You would need the return over the whole relationship — interest plus the privileges and licences that came with being the king's banker, across the 1270s to 1294 — measured against the loss on seizure. The figure that decides it is the annualized return over those two decades against the merchant alternative, not the size of the 1294 loss.

</details>
