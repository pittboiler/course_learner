# A History of Debt · Lesson 4.4: Haiti's independence debt

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 4: Debt, nation and empire in the long nineteenth century · Builds on: [4.3 Debt peonage after slavery](04-03-debt-peonage-after-slavery.md), [4.1 Hamilton's assumption plan](04-01-hamiltons-assumption-plan.md) · Unlocks: [4.5 Egypt, the Ottomans, and debt administration](04-05-egypt-the-ottomans-and-debt-administration.md)

## Why this matters

In [4.1](04-01-hamiltons-assumption-plan.md) a new nation used debt to build its credit. In [4.3](04-03-debt-peonage-after-slavery.md) debt kept binding people whom abolition had formally freed. Haiti combines the two. The first state founded by a successful slave revolt was made to take on a debt as the price of being recognized as a state, and the debt was owed to the people who had enslaved its citizens. The episode now sits at the center of live arguments about reparations. It is also the cleanest case in the course of a debt that bought nothing at all: no war won, no canal dug. That makes it a natural test for every later question about debt as an instrument of control ([4.5](04-05-egypt-the-ottomans-and-debt-administration.md), [4.6](04-06-bondholders-gunboats-and-receiverships.md)).

## The idea

Think of a hostage negotiation conducted through a bank. France never reconquered Saint-Domingue after its army was destroyed in 1802–03, and Haiti declared independence on 1 January 1804. But no great power would recognize the new state, and French governments kept both the colonists' claims and the idea of reconquest alive. In 1825 Charles X offered recognition in the form of a royal ordinance: independence in return for 150 million francs and a trade privilege.

Haiti could not pay from revenue. The annual figures historians give run from under 3 million francs to claims many times that, but every estimate puts the indemnity far beyond anything the treasury could manage. So Haiti borrowed in Paris to pay the first installment. From then on it owed twice, once to the French state's fund for the colonists and once to French bondholders. The arrangement is now widely called the **double debt**.

The mechanism is the lesson. Recognition was converted into a financial claim, the claim into a bond, and the bond into a lasting lever over Haiti's budget. That lever outlived the original creditors: later loans, a French-controlled national bank, and finally American bankers and Marines inherited it.

## Source

The ordinance of 17 April 1825, verified against the French text reprinted in *Outre-Mers* 90 (2003). The translations are the lesson's own.

Article 2:

> The present inhabitants of the French part of Saint-Domingue shall pay … in five equal installments, year by year, … the sum of one hundred and fifty million francs, intended to indemnify the former colonists who shall claim an indemnity.

Article 3:

> We grant, on these conditions, by the present ordinance, to the present inhabitants of the French part of Saint-Domingue, the full and entire independence of their government.

Three features of the wording matter. The payer is "the present inhabitants", and Haiti is never named. The verb is *concédons*, "we grant", so the king presents independence as his to give rather than as a fact he acknowledges. And the grant is made "on these conditions": the payment in Article 2 and, in Article 1, customs duties halved for French ships. The first installment fell due on 31 December 1825, and the money went to the Caisse des dépôts et consignations, which would distribute it to the former colonists.

## The argument

Two cases were made in 1825, each in its authors' own terms.

**The French case for the settlement,** as the preamble and its defenders framed it:

1. **The colonists lost property to the revolution, and the French state owes them redress.** *In words:* in the Restoration's legal vocabulary, the planters were victims of confiscation.
2. **The king may lawfully settle the colony's status by ordinance.** France had never renounced sovereignty, and the preamble cites articles 14 and 73 of the Charter, on royal ordinances and on the special legal regime of colonies. *In words:* to Paris, Saint-Domingue was still French until the king said otherwise.
3. **Those who now hold the land should compensate the people they dispossessed, and French commerce should keep a privileged place.** *In words:* the price of the grant falls on the grant's beneficiaries.
4. **Therefore independence is granted on condition of indemnity and preferential duties.** *In words:* recognition is a transaction, not an acknowledgment.

**Boyer's case for accepting,** as historians commonly reconstruct it:

1. **Without French recognition, Haiti stays diplomatically isolated and exposed to reconquest.** The expedition of 1802 was within living memory. *In words:* recognition was security.
2. **The ordinance arrived with warships.** Baron de Mackau delivered it in July 1825, backed by a squadron reportedly of about fourteen warships. *In words:* refusing risked blockade or worse.
3. **Recognition would open trade and credit on normal terms.** *In words:* a recognized state could borrow and sell abroad.
4. **Therefore accepting a heavy but finite payment beats an open-ended threat.** *In words:* Boyer bet that the money was the lesser danger.

Critics at the time, including French deputies and the abolitionist Zachary Macaulay, called the sum exorbitant. Some colonists thought it absurdly low. Whether "indemnity" is the right name for a payment by the formerly enslaved to their enslavers is a moral question, and belongs to [`philosophy-of-debt`](../../philosophy-of-debt/syllabus.md).

## Timeline

| Year | Event |
|---|---|
| 1791–1804 | Revolution in Saint-Domingue. The French expedition of 1802–03 fails, and independence is declared in 1804 |
| 1825 | The ordinance of 17 April. Boyer accepts in July. A 30-million-franc loan is floated in Paris, with bids run by Ternaux and underwriting by a Laffitte-led syndicate |
| 1826–28 | Haiti pays the first installment but misses the second. The loan is serviced briefly, reportedly with French Treasury help, and then a moratorium follows |
| 1838 | Treaty of 12 February. The unpaid indemnity is cut from 120 to 60 million francs, making 90 million in all, and Louis-Philippe formally recognizes Haiti as a free, sovereign and independent state |
| 1843–69 | Payments are suspended in 1843, resume in 1849, and stop again in 1867–69 |
| c. 1883 | The indemnity itself is paid off |
| 1875–1910 | New Paris loans with heavy fees. A Paris-controlled Banque Nationale d'Haïti (1880–81) handles the treasury, and in 1910 it is reorganized with French, German and American capital, National City Bank among the owners |
| 1914–15 | In December 1914 US Marines carry about half a million dollars in gold from the bank to New York. The US occupation (1915–34) takes over the customs |
| 1920s–1947 | New York bonds refinance the French debts. Debt service takes more than 30% of revenue in 1925–36. By the *Times*'s count, the last payments on this chain of debt come in 1947 |

Sources for the dates and shares: Oosterlinck, Panizza, Weidemaier and Gulati, "A Debt of Dishonor," *Boston University Law Review* (2022), drawing on Blancpain; the *New York Times* series "The Ransom" (2022).

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean): what was 150 million worth in 1825?** Five installments of 30 million, the first due at once. At a 6% discount rate the value at the first due date is

$$PV = 30\left(1 + \frac{1}{1.06} + \frac{1}{1.06^2} + \frac{1}{1.06^3} + \frac{1}{1.06^4}\right) = 30 \times 4.4651 \approx 134.0 \text{ million francs}.$$

Here $PV$ is the present value, meaning the lump sum today that is worth the same as the stream of payments. *In words:* spreading the payments over five years takes only about 11% off the headline, so the burden really was close to 150 million.

The loan made things worse. The bonds had a face value of 30 million at a 6% coupon, but they sold at 80% of par. Only about 24 million reached the Caisse, 6 million short of the 30 million installment it was meant to cover, and Haiti owed the bondholders the full 30 million plus interest. The coupon alone came to $0.06 \times 30 = 1.8$ million a year. If Haiti's revenue was near the roughly 2.6 million francs a year that one historian (Beauvois) estimates for 1818–24, the coupon would have taken about 70% of it before a franc of indemnity was paid. Other estimates of revenue run much higher, so treat that ratio as an illustration.

**Example 2 (hard): why the modern cost estimates range so widely.** The *Times* counted about 112 million francs actually paid on the indemnity and the 1825 loan together, roughly 560 million of today's dollars. It then asked what that money would have added to Haiti's economy. Its figure was at least 21 billion dollars, and up to 115 billion if Haiti had grown like its Latin American neighbors. Oosterlinck and coauthors ran simpler counterfactuals on the 90 million francs: about 0.45 billion 2020 dollars at a 0% real return, 2.9 billion at 1%, 22 billion at 2.1% (Haiti's own long-run growth), and 125 billion at 3%.

The spread is pure compounding. Over roughly 195 years, a real rate $g$ doubles money every $\ln 2/\ln(1+g)$ years: about 70 years at 1%, 33 at 2.1%, and 23 at 3%. That means about 2.8, 5.9 and 8.3 doublings, or factors of about 7, 57 and 320. Their published figures come in lower than $450\text{m} \times$ factor because the payments were spread across the century. *In words:* the arithmetic is easy, and the dispute is entirely about the counterfactual. Would the money have been invested and left untouched, and would Haiti have grown like its neighbors? Both sets of authors flag the problem, and it cuts both ways. A counterfactual fund would probably have been raided by later rulers, which overstates the cost. But the debt may itself have fed the instability that held growth down, which understates it. Neither side can price that without a theory of Haitian growth, and that theory is exactly what is contested.

## Watch out

- **"Haiti paid for its independence."** Strictly, Haiti had won independence in 1804. The ordinance *says* it grants independence, which is why many historians describe the payment as buying *recognition*. Keep the text's claim and the historians' description apart.
- **"Double debt" does not mean 300 million.** The loan financed the indemnity rather than being added to it. The doubling is in the creditors and the costs: a fund plus bondholders, a discount plus interest.
- **Anachronism trap: reading 1826–28 as a modern sovereign restructuring.** There was no Paris Club and no IMF. The indemnity was owed to a French state fund by a population France did not yet fully recognize as a state, and the loan was a separate private bond with its own creditors. Don't read later [receivership](04-06-bondholders-gunboats-and-receiverships.md) machinery back into 1825.
- **The 21–115 billion range is an estimate built on assumptions, not a measured loss.** Report it as the *Times*'s model-dependent estimate, which is contested. The 90 million francs of indemnity is the hard figure.

## One-liner

> France turned recognition into a debt and the debt into a bond, and the fiscal lever lasted from 1825 into the American occupation. How much of Haiti's poverty it explains is still disputed.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢) *(Formal.)*** (a) Suppose the 1825 loan (30 million face, 6%, 25 years) had been repaid as a level annuity on its face value. Find the annual payment. Then show that discounting those payments at 8.5% gives about the 24 million Haiti actually received, and say what that 8.5% measures. (b) In 1838 the unpaid 120 million, which under the 1825 terms meant four installments of 30 million, was replaced by 60 million. As a stylized version, suppose the new sum was paid in 30 equal annual installments. The real schedule was uneven. Treat both streams as starting at once, and compute the nominal reduction and the present-value reduction at 6%. State your rounding.

**P2 (🟡) *(Exegetical.)*** Alongside Articles 2–3 in the Source, read the preamble, where the king acts "wishing to provide for what is demanded by the interest of French commerce, the misfortunes of the former colonists of Saint-Domingue, and the precarious state of the present inhabitants of that island". Then read Article 1, which says duties in Haitian ports are to be "equal and uniform for all flags, except the French flag, in favor of which these duties shall be reduced by half." A reader says the ordinance is "a treaty between two states settling a property dispute." Which words in the ordinance count against each part of that description? 150 words or fewer.

**P3 (🔴, optional) *(Exegetical.)*** One position, associated with the *Times*'s "The Ransom" and the economists it consulted, holds that the indemnity and its loans were a major cause of Haiti's long underdevelopment. Critics, including some economists of Haiti, stress causes that operated before 1825 or after the debt ended: coups and political instability, heavy military spending, the breakup of plantations into smallholdings, and predatory rulers. Name the single premise the two sides divide on, and say what evidence would move it. 150 words or fewer.

**Must hit (a):** $A = \dfrac{30 \times 0.06}{1 - 1.06^{-25}} = \dfrac{1.8}{0.76700} \approx 2.347$ million a year. The annuity factor at 6% over 25 years is $(1-1.06^{-25})/0.06 = 12.783$, and $30/12.783 = 2.347$. At 8.5% the factor is $(1-1.085^{-25})/0.085 = 10.234$, and $2.347 \times 10.234 \approx 24.02$ million, which is about the 24 million received. So 8.5% is the loan's effective cost to Haiti: the rate that equates what it got with what it owed. The discount at issue pushed the 6% coupon up to about 8.5%.

**Must hit (b):** Old stream: $30(1 + a_{\overline{3}|}) = 30(1 + 2.6730) = 110.19$ million, where $a_{\overline{n}|} = (1-1.06^{-n})/0.06$. New stream: 30 payments of 2 million each, so $2(1 + a_{\overline{29}|}) = 2(1 + 13.5907) = 29.18$ million. Nominal reduction: $1 - 60/120 = 50\%$. PV reduction: $1 - 29.18/110.19 \approx 73.5\%$. Everything is rounded to two decimals in millions, and percentages to one decimal.

**Wrong turns:** comparing the new PV with 120 million rather than with the PV of the old stream (that gives 75.7%); reading 8.5% as the coupon; computing the coupon-only yield $1.8/24 = 7.5\%$, which ignores repayment of the extra 6 million of face value.

**Model answer:** (a) 2.35 million a year; the 8.5% is the effective borrowing rate. (b) The cut is 50% nominal and about 73% in present value, which is why a debtor and a creditor can describe the same 1838 deal very differently ([5.4](05-04-from-mexico-1982-to-the-brady-plan.md)).

**P2** *(Exegetical, strict.)*

**Must hit, strict:**
- *Not a treaty between two states:* it is a unilateral royal ordinance ("We grant … by the present ordinance"). The other party is "the present inhabitants of the French part of Saint-Domingue", not a named state or government. Its authority comes from the king's own Charter.
- *Not only a property settlement:* independence itself is the thing granted "on these conditions". Article 1 attaches a lasting trade privilege ("except the French flag … reduced by half"). The preamble puts "the interest of French commerce" first among the purposes.
- *What does fit:* Article 2 does frame the money as indemnity to "former colonists", so the property-claim element is really in the text.

**Wrong turns:** citing the 1838 treaty as evidence about the 1825 text; arguing about whether the payment was just (not asked, and ceded to `philosophy-of-debt`).

**Model answer:** The ordinance is not a treaty. It speaks in the king's voice ("We grant … by the present ordinance") and binds "the present inhabitants of the French part of Saint-Domingue", never naming Haiti or its government. That wording treats Haiti as a population receiving a concession, not a state entering an agreement. Nor is it only a property settlement. Independence is the thing granted "on these conditions", Article 1 adds a permanent commercial privilege for "the French flag", and the preamble lists "the interest of French commerce" first. The description does get one thing right: Article 2 presents the 150 million as an indemnity for the "former colonists", so a property claim is part of what the text asserts.

**P3** *(Exegetical, strict on naming one premise; any defensible formulation passes.)*

**Must hit, strict:**
- One premise: whether, absent the indemnity, Haiti's institutions and growth would have been substantially different. Equivalently, whether instability and predation were largely *independent* of the debt or partly *produced* by it.
- Evidence that bears on it: fiscal records of what debt service displaced (schools, public works) and when; whether coups and tax revolts followed payment crises; growth in periods of heavy vs light debt service; comparison with similar post-colonial states that carried no such indemnity.

**Wrong turns:** naming the size of the payments as the crux (both sides accept roughly 112 million francs); treating the question as moral (whether France owes reparations is a different question); declaring a winner.

**Model answer, one of several:** Both sides accept the payments. They divide on a counterfactual premise: that without the indemnity, Haiti's political instability and extractive rule would have been much less severe, so its growth could have tracked its neighbors'. The *Times* position needs the debt to be a cause of those conditions, or at least independent of them. Critics hold that those conditions had their own roots, some of which came before 1825. Several kinds of evidence would move this. One is showing that fiscal squeezes from payments came before coups or cuts to public investment. Another is comparing growth in decades of heavy and light debt service. A third is comparing Haiti with post-colonial states that had similar starting points but no indemnity.

## Flashback

**From Lesson 4.2 (Debtors' prisons and the invention of bankruptcy):** *(Exegetical.)* Take the Debtors Act of 1869 as given: it abolished imprisonment for debt, but kept a committal of up to six weeks for a debtor whom the court finds to have had the means to pay an instalment it ordered and who did not pay. (a) Which premise of the prison's logic does that exception keep, which two does it drop, and what feature of the old process goes with them? (b) Sam Weller's complaint in the Source was about sorting. Does it bite against the exception in the same way? Two sentences or fewer per part.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):** it keeps **premise 3** — coercion extracts what a debtor can pay — but only after ability has been established, not as a way of finding it out. It drops **premise 1** (arrest on mesne process, the body as security before any judgment) and **premise 2** (arrest in execution for the debt itself). What goes with them is the creditor's key: detention is now for a fixed short term on a court's finding of means, not open-ended and releasable at the creditor's pleasure.

**Must hit, strict (b):** not in the same way. Sam's charge was that the jail sorted backwards, comforting the shirker and crushing the honest debtor, precisely because it made no finding about ability. The exception is built to sort first and commit second, so the objection now bites only where the finding is unreliable — the same can-versus-won't problem, moved from the jail to the judge.

**Wrong turns:** concluding that imprisonment for debt survived unchanged because an exception remained; naming premise 4 (that imprisonment is enforcement, not punishment) as the casualty, when the 1869 committal is still civil process; saying Sam's objection simply disappears.

**Model answer:** (a) It keeps premise 3, coercion against a debtor who can pay, and drops premises 1 and 2, arrest before judgment and arrest for the debt as such. The key leaves the creditor's pocket: the term is short and fixed, and a court must first find that the money was there. (b) Only in a weakened form. Sam's point was that the old jail sorted the wrong way because it never asked who could pay; the exception asks first, so what is left of his complaint is whether a court can really tell "can't" from "won't" — the same question the prison had tried to answer with time.
