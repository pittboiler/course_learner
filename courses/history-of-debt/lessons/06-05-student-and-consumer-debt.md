# A History of Debt · Lesson 6.5: Student and consumer debt

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 6: Debt in the twenty-first century · Builds on: [6.4 New creditors](06-04-new-creditors.md) · [4.2 Debtors' prisons and the invention of bankruptcy](04-02-debtors-prisons-and-the-invention-of-bankruptcy.md) · Unlocks: Module 6's boss problem, and the rest of the debt thread

## Why this matters

This course opened with debts that could take a man's children, and with kings who wiped them out. It ends with debt that ordinary households carry as a normal condition of life. Americans owe roughly 1.26 trillion dollars on credit cards (Federal Reserve Bank of New York, household debt report, mid-2026) and about 1.7 trillion in federal student loans, spread over some 43 million borrowers (Federal Student Aid's portfolio data; the Supreme Court used 1.6 trillion and 43 million in 2023). Neither institution fell out of the sky. Each was shaped by the two legal choices this course has watched people fight over since Module 1: whether the law caps what a lender may charge, and whether a debtor can ever walk away.

## The idea

Think of two knobs on any credit system.

**The ceiling.** Is there a maximum lawful rate? Medieval Europe set it at zero for a true loan and built elaborate institutions around that (2.1, 2.2). American states set theirs in statute — 12 percent, 18 percent, whatever the legislature chose.

**The exit.** Can the debtor be released from what he cannot pay? That is the discharge invented for traders and slowly extended to everyone (4.2).

The two mass consumer institutions of modern America sit at opposite corners. The credit card has **no effective ceiling and a working exit**: card debt is wiped out in an ordinary bankruptcy, and since 1978 the rate has been set by whichever state the issuing bank chose to live in. The federal student loan has **a ceiling set by Congress and almost no exit**: rates are statutory, but the debt survives bankruptcy unless the borrower wins a separate lawsuit on a demanding standard.

And the politics follow the missing knob. Where the ceiling is gone, the fight is over price, disclosure and fees. Where the exit is closed, every question about relief becomes a question for Congress and the President — because the judge who hands out fresh starts cannot. That is why student-debt relief arrives as a *political program* rather than a courtroom proceeding, and why the Supreme Court's part in 2023 was to police the executive's authority rather than any borrower's hardship.

## Source

*Marquette National Bank of Minneapolis v. First of Omaha Service Corp.*, 439 U.S. 299 (1978). A Nebraska bank mailed BankAmericards to Minnesota residents and charged them Nebraska's rate, which exceeded Minnesota's usury limit. Justice Brennan, for a unanimous Court, held for the Nebraska bank, and closed like this:

> "But the protection of state usury laws is an issue of legislative policy, and any plea to alter § 85 to further that end is better addressed to the wisdom of Congress than to the judgment of this Court."

Earlier in the same passage, on the objection that exporting rates would gut state usury law: the impairment, the Court said, "has always been implicit in the structure of the National Bank Act."

## The argument

**Chain A — how a ceiling stops binding.** The Court's reasoning, in its own terms.

1. Section 85 of the National Bank Act lets a national bank charge interest at the rate allowed by the laws of the state where the bank is *located*. *In words:* one federal statute picks which state's usury law governs.
2. A bank is located in the state named in its organization certificate — where it approves the credit, issues the cards and receives the payments — not wherever a cardholder happens to live. *In words:* mailing a card into Minnesota does not move the bank to Minnesota.
3. Therefore a Nebraska bank may charge Minnesota customers the Nebraska rate, whatever Minnesota's ceiling says.
4. Any resulting damage to state usury laws is for Congress to repair, not the Court. *In words:* the Justices saw the consequence and declined to fix it.

The step the opinion does not take is the political one. If the lender's home state sets the price, a state can bid for the lending business. South Dakota removed its interest ceiling in 1980 and Citibank moved its card operation there the next year; Delaware's Financial Center Development Act followed in 1981. Within a few years most large card portfolios were domiciled in a handful of states with no cap. State usury statutes were never repealed. They were routed around.

**Chain B — how an exit gets closed.** The student loan, built in four moves.

1. Federal student lending — the National Defense Education Act (1958), then the guaranteed loans of the Higher Education Act (1965), with the Student Loan Marketing Association ("Sallie Mae") created in 1972 to buy and finance them — lends without collateral and without underwriting. *In words:* nothing is pledged and no income is checked, because the point is to lend to people who have neither.
2. The thing bought cannot be repossessed. *In words:* you can foreclose on a house (6.1); you cannot foreclose on an education.
3. So enforcement has to be written into statute, and Congress wrote it. The Education Amendments of 1976 made government-backed student loans nondischargeable absent "undue hardship"; the provision entered the Bankruptcy Code as 11 U.S.C. § 523(a)(8). A waiting period after which the loans became dischargeable — five years, extended to seven in 1990 — was abolished in 1998. In 2005 the bar was extended to private education loans. *In words:* over thirty years the exit narrowed, then shut.
4. "Undue hardship" was then given a hard reading. Under the test from *Brunner v. New York State Higher Education Services Corp.* (2d Cir. 1987), the debtor must show he cannot maintain a minimal standard of living while repaying, that this will persist for much of the repayment period, and that he has made good-faith efforts to repay — proved in a separate adversary proceeding inside the bankruptcy. *In words:* not impossible, but a lawsuit most debtors never file.

Conclusion: relief, if it comes, comes from politics — income-driven repayment plans, targeted cancellations, and in 2022 a general one.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean): what exportation is worth.** A cardholder in a state with an 18 percent ceiling carries 5,000 dollars and pays 150 dollars a month. At 18 percent (1.5 percent a month) she is clear in about 47 months. Her issuer is domiciled in a state with no ceiling and charges 24 percent (2 percent a month): the same payment now takes 56 months and costs about 3,322 dollars in interest rather than about 1,984. Nothing about her, the loan or the payment changed. The only thing that changed was which state's law the contract points at — which is what *Marquette* decides, and why the states began competing to be that state.

**Example 2 (hard): the 2022 plan and the Court.** The Secretary of Education invoked the HEROES Act of 2003, which lets him "waive or modify" provisions of the student-aid statutes as necessary in connection with a national emergency, and cancelled up to 10,000 dollars of federal loans for borrowers with incomes below 125,000 dollars, and up to 20,000 for former Pell Grant recipients. The Court's opinion put the scale at roughly 430 billion dollars of principal, erasing about 20 million borrowers' balances entirely and lowering the median balance of the other 23 million from about 29,400 to 13,600 dollars.

In *Biden v. Nebraska*, 600 U.S. 477 (2023), the Court held 6–3 that the Act did not authorize it. Chief Justice Roberts read "modify" as carrying a connotation of increment or limitation — modest adjustments, not transformation — and held that a program of this size and significance required clear congressional authorization, the "major questions" approach. Missouri had standing because the plan would cost MOHELA, a state-created loan servicer, revenue. Justice Kagan's dissent argued that the states had no standing at all and that "waive or modify" in an emergency statute means what it says; on that reading the majority substituted its own judgment for the political branches'.

Two things to notice, neither of them a verdict. First, the dispute is entirely about *authority* — who may cancel, under which words — and not about whether cancellation is deserved or wise. Second, the case only arises because of Chain B: if student loans were dischargeable like card debt, the relief would be happening one debtor at a time in bankruptcy court, invisibly, and no one would need to read the HEROES Act. Subsequent plans have had a similar history; income-driven repayment has been repeatedly revised and litigated, and the SAVE plan adopted in 2023 was being wound down by 2026 in favour of a successor plan enacted by Congress.

## Watch out

- **Anachronism: "*Marquette* repealed usury laws."** It repealed nothing. State ceilings remain on the books and still bind lenders who cannot export — and it was *states*, not the Court, that then dropped their own caps to attract issuers. The holding was about which state's law applies to a national bank.
- **Anachronism in the other direction: modern "usury" is not the medieval prohibition.** A rate cap concedes the thing the scholastic doctrine denied — that a loan may yield a return at all (2.1). Calling a 36 percent cap a "restoration" of the old ban misdescribes both.
- **Student loans are not literally nondischargeable.** They are dischargeable on a showing of undue hardship. What the statute and *Brunner* did was make the showing hard and put it behind a separate lawsuit; guidance issued in 2022 tried to make that process more usable. "Impossible" overstates it, and the overstatement hides where the discretion actually sits.
- **Don't read *Biden v. Nebraska* as a ruling about forgiveness.** It is a holding about statutory authority and standing. A reader who takes it as the Court's view on whether debts should be cancelled has mistaken the question presented.

## One-liner

> Consumer credit became a mass institution when the law stopped capping its price, and student debt became a political question when the law closed its exit.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢) *(Exegetical.)*** From the majority opinion in *Biden v. Nebraska*, 600 U.S. 477 (2023):

> "We hold today that the Act allows the Secretary to 'waive or modify' existing statutory or regulatory provisions applicable to financial assistance programs under the Education Act, not to rewrite that statute from the ground up."

(a) Which words carry the holding, and what distinction is the sentence drawing? (b) On the Court's approach, what would the Secretary have needed in order to win? 150 words or fewer in total.

**P2 (🟡) *(Formal.)*** A card balance of 5,000 dollars at 24 percent APR, compounded monthly (so 2 percent a month), with no new purchases. Round money to the nearest cent and months up to whole months.

(a) The minimum payment is 2 percent of the balance. Write the balance next month in terms of the balance this month, and say what happens to the debt.
(b) The minimum is instead the month's interest plus 1 percent of the balance. Show that the balance falls by a fixed proportion each month, give the formula for the balance after $m$ months, and compute how many months until it first falls below 1,000 dollars. Real minimums carry a floor — say 25 dollars, whichever is greater. What ends the debt, and roughly when?
(c) The borrower instead pays a fixed 150 dollars a month. Using $n = -\ln\!\left(1 - \frac{rB}{P}\right)/\ln(1+r)$, with $B$ the balance, $r$ the monthly rate and $P$ the payment, find the number of payments and the total interest. Repeat at a 12 percent APR ceiling, and say in one line what the ceiling was worth to this borrower.

**P3 (🔴, optional) *(Exegetical.)*** (a) An invented op-ed argues:

> "For eight hundred years Europe had a word for a loan at 300 percent, and the word was sin. A national ceiling of 36 percent would not be an innovation. It would be a restoration — putting back the limit that every Christian society kept, until one Supreme Court case in 1978 swept it away."

Name the historical analogy doing the work, two features of the medieval prohibition (2.1–2.2) the analogy depends on, and whether each carries over. (b) In the 1970s, supporters of the new bankruptcy bar said graduates were shedding loans they could have repaid on the eve of well-paid careers; opponents said such filings were rare and that one class of debtor should not lose the fresh start. Name the single premise they divide on and what evidence would move it. 150 words or fewer in total; no verdict required.

<details>
<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical — strict.)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** the decisive words are "waive or modify" against "rewrite … from the ground up", and "existing … provisions". The distinction is between *adjusting provisions Congress wrote* and *replacing the scheme with a different one*: the verbs presuppose a statute that stays in place and is altered at the margin. "Existing" does real work too — the Secretary must act on provisions already there, not create a new discharge category. (Credit for noting that the Court reads "modify" as incremental, following its earlier reading of that word.)
**Must hit, strict (b):** either (i) a plan small and incremental enough to count as a modification of existing provisions rather than a new program, or (ii) clear congressional authorization for a program of this economic and political significance — the major-questions requirement. Mentioning that standing had to be established first (Missouri through MOHELA) is a bonus, not required.
**Wrong turns:** answering that the Court found cancellation unfair or bad policy — it says nothing about that; treating "national emergency" as the disputed term, when the Court's holding turns on the verbs.
**Model answer:** (a) The holding rides on "waive or modify" versus "rewrite that statute from the ground up", plus "existing". The Act lets the Secretary adjust provisions Congress already enacted; it does not let him build a new forgiveness program on their site. "Modify" is read as incremental change, so scale is what converts a modification into a rewrite. (b) He would have had to show either that the plan was a genuine, limited adjustment of existing provisions, or that Congress had clearly authorized a cancellation of this magnitude — which the majority found nowhere in the HEROES Act.

---

**P2** *(Formal.)*

**(a)** $B_{m+1} = B_m(1.02) - 0.02B_m = B_m$. The balance never moves: the payment is exactly the interest. At 5,000 dollars the minimum is 100 a month, the interest is 100 a month, and the debt is perpetual — an interest-only loan with no maturity date. (This is why the CARD Act's minimum-payment disclosure box exists.)

**(b)** Payment $= 0.02B_m + 0.01B_m = 0.03B_m$, so
$$B_{m+1} = 1.02B_m - 0.03B_m = 0.99B_m, \qquad B_m = 5000(0.99)^m.$$
The balance falls 1 percent a month and never reaches zero in finite time. Below 1,000 dollars: $(0.99)^m < 0.2$, so $m > \ln(0.2)/\ln(0.99) = 160.14$, i.e. **month 161** (13 years 5 months); the balance is 1,001.39 at month 160 and 991.37 at month 161. What ends the debt is the *floor* — the "or 25 dollars, whichever is greater" clause. With a 25-dollar floor the percentage minimum binds until the balance reaches 833.33 (3 percent of it equals 25), which happens after 179 payments totalling 12,518.06; the flat 25 then clears the remaining 827.31 in 55 more months. Total: **234 months (19.5 years) and 13,886.95 dollars, of which 8,886.95 is interest** — on a 5,000-dollar balance.

**(c)** $r = 0.02$, $B = 5000$, $P = 150$: $rB/P = 100/150 = 0.6667$, so
$$n = -\ln(1 - 0.6667)/\ln(1.02) = \ln 3/\ln 1.02 = 1.0986/0.019803 = 55.48 \Rightarrow 56 \text{ payments}$$
(55 of 150 dollars and a final 72.09). Total paid 8,322.09, **interest 3,322.09**.
At 12 percent, $r = 0.01$: $rB/P = 50/150 = 0.3333$, $n = \ln 1.5/\ln 1.01 = 0.405465/0.00995 = 40.75 \Rightarrow$ **41 payments**, total 6,112.48, **interest 1,112.48**.
The ceiling is worth about 2,210 dollars and 15 months to this borrower — which is the stake states were playing for after *Marquette*.

**Must hit, strict:** (a) the balance is constant and why; (b) the 0.99 factor, $5000(0.99)^m$, month 161, and the recognition that a geometric minimum alone never retires the debt; (c) 56 and 41 payments with interest of about 3,322 and about 1,112.
**Wrong turns:** using 24 percent as a monthly rate; treating the 2-percent minimum as 2 percent of the *original* balance (that would amortize); rounding $n$ down instead of up.

---

**P3** *(Exegetical — strict; no verdict.)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** the analogy is the medieval usury prohibition, invoked as precedent for a modern rate cap. Two features it needs, neither of which carries cleanly: (i) the prohibition was on *any* gain from a loan (*mutuum*), not on a rate above a threshold — a 36 percent ceiling grants precisely what the doctrine denied, so a cap is a different institution, not the old one restored; (ii) it was never a universal, unbroken practice — Christian Europe ran on the workarounds of 2.1 (partnerships, bills of exchange, *census*) and on licensed lenders and the Monti di Pietà of 2.2, which charged fees; "every Christian society kept it" describes the doctrine, not the practice. A third available point: *Marquette* did not sweep away state ceilings; it decided which state's ceiling applies, and the states then repealed their own.
**Must hit, strict (b):** the dividing premise is whether student debtors who filed *could* have repaid — strategic use of bankruptcy by borrowers with good prospects — or *could not*. Evidence that would move it: filing rates among recent graduates compared with other debtors, how soon after graduation filings came, and the post-filing earnings and repayment records of those who filed. (Contemporary studies found such filings rare, which is what opponents pointed to.)
**Wrong turns:** arguing whether a 36 percent cap is justified, or whether student loans *should* be dischargeable — those belong to [`philosophy-of-debt`](../../philosophy-of-debt/syllabus.md); naming "morality versus economics" instead of a testable premise.
**Model answer:** (a) The analogy is the medieval ban on usury. It needs the ban to have been a limit on *rates* and to have been generally observed. Neither holds: the doctrine forbade any return on a loan, so a 36 percent cap concedes its central point, and medieval credit ran on partnerships, exchange contracts and licensed pawn lending. Nor did a court abolish American caps; states did, competing for issuers after 1978. (b) They divide on whether filers could have paid. Filing rates by recent graduates, the timing of filings relative to graduation, and post-filing earnings would settle it.

</details>

## Flashback

**From Lesson 6.3 (Holdouts and the law of sovereign restructuring):** A sovereign must cut every claim below by 40 percent. For each, name what — if anything — can bind a creditor who refuses, and who exercises it. One or two sentences each.

1. Bonds issued under the sovereign's own law, held mostly at home.
2. New York-law bonds issued in 2019 with single-limb aggregated collective action clauses.
3. New York-law bonds issued in 1998 with no such clause, immunity from suit waived.
4. A loan from another state's policy bank, with no bondholder vote of any kind.

Then one sentence: what does a corporate bankruptcy court supply that none of your four answers does?

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (1):** its own **legislature**. A sovereign that is also the lawmaker of the governing law can amend the terms — Greece's 2012 statute retrofitting collective action clauses into Greek-law bonds and then voting them through. The power stops at the border of that law.

**Must hit, strict (2):** the **clause**, exercised by a creditor supermajority, not by any court: one vote across all covered series binds every holder in every series, including a series that voted no — conditional on the uniformly applicable requirement that all holders be offered the same menu. Nobody needs to sue.

**Must hit, strict (3):** **nothing binds.** The restructuring is an offer, and a refuser keeps his original claim. He can sue in New York, win a judgment, and then discover that a judgment is not collection — the assets are at home or shielded — so his leverage has to be built out of something else, as the holdouts built theirs out of the *pari passu* clause and an injunction reaching the payment chain.

**Must hit, strict (4):** **nothing contractual.** No clause, no vote, no forum: only a negotiation with that government, in which comparability of treatment is an expectation creditors press on each other, not a rule anything enforces.

**Must hit, strict (closing):** a bankruptcy court gathers *every* claim into a single proceeding, whatever instrument it sits in, counts the classes and binds the dissenters — and can enforce the outcome. Nothing in the sovereign world does that; the proposal to build it (the SDRM) was shelved in 2003, which is why the answers above are four different mechanisms instead of one.

**Wrong turns:** saying (3) is covered because collective action clauses are now standard — a clause binds only the instrument that contains it, and outstanding bonds without one are untouched; saying (1) cannot be bound because there is no court, when the debtor's own parliament is the relevant power; treating a New York judgment as itself a way of getting paid; calling comparability of treatment in (4) a legal obligation.

**Model answer:** (1) The sovereign's own legislature, which can rewrite the terms of bonds governed by its law — Greece, 2012. (2) The aggregated clause itself: a single supermajority vote across series binds dissenters, exercised by creditors, with the same menu offered to all. (3) Nothing. A holdout keeps his claim and can sue; the judgment is easy and collecting it is not, so his leverage comes from blocking the sovereign's payments to everyone else. (4) Nothing formal — a bilateral negotiation, with comparability of treatment as a norm creditors invoke, not a rule. A bankruptcy court would supply what none of these can: one forum with jurisdiction over all claims, able to bind a dissenting minority and enforce the result.

</details>

## Where this leaves you

Twenty-nine lessons, one recurring question: when a debt cannot be paid, who decides who takes the loss? A Babylonian king by edict, Solon by statute, a Roman assembly, a bankruptcy commission, a bondholders' committee, a gunboat, the IMF, a New York judge, a legislature. The instruments change completely; the question never does. What the history gives you is not an answer to it but a stocked memory — so that the next time someone says a debt is sacred, or that cancelling it would be unprecedented, you know exactly which precedents are in play and where each one stops fitting.
