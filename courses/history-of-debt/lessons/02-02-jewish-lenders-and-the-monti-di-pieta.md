# A History of Debt · Lesson 2.2: Jewish lenders and the Monti di Pietà

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 2: Credit under the usury ban · Builds on: [2.1 Commerce around the prohibition](02-01-commerce-around-the-prohibition.md) · Unlocks: [2.3 The public debts of city-states](02-03-the-public-debts-of-city-states.md)

## Why this matters

[2.1](02-01-commerce-around-the-prohibition.md) showed merchants financing trade without a formal loan at interest. That trick does nothing for a weaver who needs a few lire until the cloth sells, or a peasant short of seed in April. Small consumption loans against a pledge were a plain loan at interest, and medieval Europe had to decide who could make them. Its answers were a licensed and heavily taxed Jewish minority, Christian lenders whom the law officially did not see, and from 1462 a charitable pawnshop run by Franciscan friars. Each answer was a political settlement about credit, and some of them carried a campaign against a religious minority inside them.

## The idea

Think of the small pawn loan as a service everyone needed and almost no one was allowed to provide openly. Canon law forbade Christians to lend to Christians at interest (the doctrine and its development belong to [`theology-of-debt`](../../theology-of-debt/syllabus.md)). The prohibition applied among Christians, and Jews were not members of the Church. Christian authorities also read Deuteronomy 23:19–20, where interest is forbidden toward "thy brother" and allowed in the words "But to the stranger" (Douay–Rheims), as leaving room for Jews to lend to Christians. Jewish legal authorities had their own long debate over the same verse; none of the debt-thread courses covers it.

So a ruler who wanted credit available, and wanted revenue, could license Jewish lenders, enforce their contracts, and tax them. The same arrangement made a small, legally vulnerable community depend on the ruler's protection. The ruler could also squeeze it, cancel its debts or expel it. Keep that structure in mind: **the license, the tax and the threat were one institution.**

Two cautions before the details. First, most medieval Jews were not moneylenders, and most medieval credit was Christian: Italian merchant-bankers, the Lombards and the Cahorsins, and ordinary neighbors lending to each other. Second, "usury" (*usura*) in canon law meant *any* charge for a loan, not only an excessive one. That is why a council could condemn Jewish *graves et immoderatas usuras*, "heavy and immoderate" usury (the lesson's translation), without implying that a moderate rate was acceptable for Christians.

## The argument

Two cases, each stated as its own actors argued it.

**A. The licensing settlement, as the English Crown operated it (reconstructed by historians).**

1. **Credit against pledges and land is in demand, and Christians may not openly supply it at interest.** *In words:* there is a gap in the market that the law will not let the majority fill.
2. **Jewish lenders may lend to Christians, but only under royal protection, since they cannot hold most land or join most guilds, and their bonds are only as good as the king's courts.** *In words:* the lenders' security depends on the ruler, not on local custom.
3. **So the Crown registers the bonds and taxes the lenders at will.** After the massacre at York in 1190, where rioters burned the records of debts owed to Jews, the Crown set up official chests (*archae*, from 1194) holding copies of every bond, and an Exchequer of the Jews to administer them. *In words:* the king protects the paperwork because the paperwork is his tax base.
4. **Arbitrary levies (tallages) push lenders to call in debts hard, and debts sold on to Christian magnates leave debtors losing land.** *In words:* the ruler's extraction shows up as the lender's severity, and resentment lands on the visible lender.

**B. The case for the Monte di Pietà, as the Franciscan Observant preachers made it.**

1. **The poor need small loans against pledges.** *In words:* the demand is real and cannot be abolished by preaching.
2. **The licensed Jewish bankers charge rates the preachers called ruinous, and lending at interest is in any case sinful for the lender.** *In words:* the preachers' objection was both economic and religious, and it was often voiced as open hostility to Jews.
3. **A civic fund, raised by gifts, can lend against pledges for charity instead of for profit.** *In words:* take the lending out of private hands and put it in a pious public institution.
4. **The fund must charge the borrower a small fee, or staff wages, storage and losses will consume it; the fee pays for costs and is not a price for the loan.** *In words:* a charity that gives its capital away stops existing, so the borrower pays for the service, not for the money.
5. **So the town should found a monte and, in the view of many preachers, stop licensing Jewish lenders.** *In words:* cheap Christian credit replaces Jewish credit.

Dominican critics attacked premise 4. On their view any required charge beyond the principal made the loan usurious, whatever the fee was called. The young Tommaso de Vio (later Cardinal Cajetan) wrote against the monti's fees in the 1490s. The Fifth Lateran Council, in the bull *Inter multiplices* (1515), approved moderate charges for expenses. What the bull defined is taught in [`theology-of-debt`](../../theology-of-debt/syllabus.md) 5.1. This lesson records only that the fight happened and how it was settled.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean): England, 1275–1290.** Run the settlement in part A forward and change one premise. By the 1270s, historians broadly agree, a generation of heavy tallages had left the English Jewish community much poorer and less useful to the Crown as a tax base. Edward I had meanwhile found a different lender, the Riccardi of Lucca, Italian merchant-bankers who advanced money against the customs revenue. The Statute of the Jewry (1275) then removed premise 2: it banned their lending at interest while still largely excluding them from the land and guilds that the alternative trades required. The statute's promise of farming and trade was narrow and temporary. With lending banned, the community had lost the function the Crown had licensed it for. After the coin-clipping trials of 1278–79 and more years of decline, Edward expelled the Jews in 1290. Parliament granted him a large tax in the same year, and historians read the expulsion as part of that bargain.

The lesson of the clean case: once the ruler no longer needs the licensed lender as revenue, the protection that made the lending possible can be withdrawn. Christian credit went on. Only its supplier and its legal form changed.

**Example 2 (hard): why a charity needs a fee.** Take an illustrative monte. (Perugia's council reportedly voted an initial 3,000 florins; the costs below are invented.) It has a fund of 3,000 florins and running costs of 150 florins a year for appraisers, a notary, storage, and losses when unredeemed pledges sell below the loan.

- With no fee, the fund shrinks by 150 florins a year, and after 10 years only 1,500 remain.
- To break even when the whole fund is out on loan, the fee must be $150/3000 = 5\%$ a year.
- Pledges come and go, so suppose only 80% of the fund is lent at any time. The fee must then be $150/2400 = 6.25\%$.

That is roughly the range monti actually charged: a few percent up to about 10, varying by town and period. The hard part is the category, not the arithmetic. Seen from the borrower's side, a 6% "fee for expenses" is a price that rises with the size and length of the loan, just as interest does. The Franciscans said the fee paid for the service; the Dominican critics said a loan-sized, time-based charge is usury under another name. Lateran V settled the dispute for the Church in 1515. The historian's job is to see that the dispute was real, and that the monti's own arithmetic forced it on them. Bernardino da Feltre, by the standard accounts, insisted that a fee was necessary if the monti were to last.

Meanwhile the Jewish banks did not simply vanish. A monte's capital was limited: it lent only a share of a pledge's value, and it often had waiting lists. So many towns kept renewing the *condotte* alongside the monte. Some early monti even borrowed their starting capital from Jewish lenders. Venice never founded a monte in the city itself and required its Jewish pawnbanks to lend at low capped rates (Pullan, *Rich and Poor in Renaissance Venice*, 1971).

## Watch out

- **Anachronism: reading "usury" as "loan-sharking."** In medieval canon law *usura* was any charge on a loan (a *mutuum*) beyond the principal. Lateran IV's *graves et immoderatas* was aimed at Jewish rates specifically; it did not legalize moderate Christian interest.
- **"Jews were forced into moneylending" is one explanation, not the settled one.** Exclusion from land and guilds explains a lot in England and parts of northern Europe. Botticini and Eckstein (*The Chosen Few*, 2012) argue that widespread literacy after the early Middle Ages pulled Jews toward urban trade and credit wherever they lived. Botticini (2000) shows Italian towns actively recruiting Jewish lenders for credit and for fiscal payments. Report the explanations side by side.
- **Don't let the preachers' voice become the lesson's.** Their charge that Jewish rates were ruinous is evidence about the preachers, not a measurement. Capped *condotta* rates, commonly somewhere around 15–30% a year and higher in some places (figures vary by town and decade), were high by modern standards and ordinary for small, risky, short, collateral-heavy loans; Christian pawnbrokers, where tolerated, sometimes charged more.
- **The Lombards and Cahorsins were Christians.** Dante puts usury in the seventh circle beside blasphemy and sodomy, and in Longfellow's translation the round is sealed "with its signet Sodom and Cahors" (*Inferno* XI.49–50). Cahors, a town in southern France, stands for usury itself because its merchants were famous for lending.

## One-liner

> Medieval rulers licensed Jewish lenders to meet a demand the Church forbade Christians to supply, taxed them as a revenue source and discarded them when they ceased to be useful; the Franciscan monti met the same demand as charity, needed a fee to survive, and were founded amid both real concern about poor borrowers and open anti-Jewish preaching.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢) *(Formal.)*** An Italian town has a Jewish bank chartered at 4 *denari* per *lira* per month (simple interest; 1 lira = 20 soldi = 240 denari) and a monte charging 6% a year, simple, which lends at most two-thirds of a pledge's appraised value. A weaver needs 12 lire for 5 months and owns a cloak appraised at 15 lire. He can pledge other goods at the bank for any remainder. (a) Convert the bank's rate to an annual percentage. (b) Compare his total charge if he borrows all 12 lire from the bank with his charge if he borrows the most the monte allows and the rest from the bank. Give both in soldi and denari. (c) Suppose the monte also charges a one-time entry fee of 1 soldo per lira lent (invented). For the monte's part of the loan alone, how many months must a loan run before the monte is cheaper than the bank?

**P2 (🟡) *(Exegetical.)*** Historian X: "The monti were a response to a real demand for cheaper credit among the urban poor; anti-Jewish rhetoric was the idiom of the preachers, not the purpose of the institutions." Historian Y: "The monti were instruments of a campaign to drive out Jewish lenders; cheap credit was the justification." Both can point to documented cases. Name the single premise on which they divide, and say what kind of evidence from town records would move it. 150 words or fewer.

**P3 (🔴, optional) *(Exegetical.)*** In *Inferno* XVII Dante meets the usurers. In Longfellow's translation: "That from the neck of each there hung a pouch, / Which certain colour had, and certain blazon". One of them says, "A Paduan am I with these Florentines". Commentators identify the blazons as the arms of prominent Florentine and Paduan families. What does this passage, together with "Sodom and Cahors" in XI.50, let a historian infer about who lent at interest in Dante's world, and what can it *not* show? 150 words or fewer.

<details>
<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Formal.)*

(a) 4 denari per lira per month is $4/240 = 1/60$ of the principal per month. Over 12 months: $12/60 = 1/5 = 20\%$ a year.

(b) **All from the bank:** $12 \times 5 \times \tfrac{1}{60} = 1$ lira $= 20$ soldi $= 240$ denari.
**Mixed:** the monte lends at most $\tfrac{2}{3} \times 15 = 10$ lire. Monte charge: $10 \times 0.06 \times \tfrac{5}{12} = 0.25$ lira $= 5$ soldi. Bank charge on the other 2 lire: $2 \times 5 \times \tfrac{1}{60} = \tfrac{1}{6}$ lira $= 40$ denari $= 3$ soldi 4 denari. Total: 8 soldi 4 denari (100 denari). Saving: 140 denari, or 11 soldi 8 denari.

(c) Entry fee on 10 lire: 10 soldi $= 0.5$ lira. Over $t$ months, the monte costs $10 \times 0.06 \times t/12 + 0.5 = 0.05t + 0.5$ lire and the bank costs $10t/60 = t/6$ lire. Setting them equal: $t(\tfrac{1}{6} - \tfrac{1}{20}) = 0.5$, so $t = 0.5 \times \tfrac{60}{7} = \tfrac{30}{7} \approx 4.3$ months. The monte is cheaper for loans longer than about 4.3 months. Check at 5 months: monte $0.75$ lira (15 soldi) vs bank $\tfrac{5}{6}$ lira (16 soldi 8 denari).

**Must hit, strict:** the per-month fraction 1/60; the two-thirds cap applied before comparing; simple interest; the break-even set up with the fixed fee.
**Wrong turns:** treating 4 denari per lira as 4% a month (240 denari to the lira, not 100); letting the monte lend the full 12 lire.

---

**P2** *(Exegetical — strict on naming one premise; any defensible wording passes.)*

**Must hit, strict:**
- The crux: *whether founding a monte was designed and carried out to end Jewish lending, or whether ending it was an optional by-product that towns often chose not to pursue.* X denies that expulsion was the aim; Y affirms it.
- Evidence that splits them: whether monte foundations coincided with votes cancelling or refusing to renew the *condotte*, or whether Jewish banks kept being renewed alongside the monte; whether monte capital came near to meeting pawn demand; timing relative to preaching campaigns and to local pressure from borrowers.
- A recognition that the answer can differ by town (Perugia, Trent, Venice).

**Wrong turns:** naming "whether the preachers were anti-Jewish" (both historians grant the rhetoric); treating the Dominican fee dispute as the crux (it concerns the monti's legitimacy, not their purpose).

**Model answer:** They divide on whether removing Jewish lenders was the institution's goal or only a possible consequence of it. Both accept that demand for small loans was real and that the preaching was hostile to Jews. The decisive records are municipal. If a monte's founding regularly came with a vote to cancel the Jewish bankers' charter, and the monte then ran with capital far too small to replace them, that supports Y: the credit argument was cover. If charters were renewed for decades beside the monte, and councils defended the monte in terms of borrowers' costs and access, that supports X. The likeliest result is variation by town, and naming the premise tells you which records to pull in each.

---

**P3** *(Exegetical.)*

**Must hit, strict:**
- The pouches carry family *blazons*: the usurers are recognizable Christian patrician families of Florence and Padua ("A Paduan am I with these Florentines").
- "Cahors" makes a Christian town's merchants a byword for usury.
- So Dante's world associated usury with prominent Christian lenders, not with Jews alone.
- Limits: a moral poem, not a record of lending. It cannot show the scale of anyone's lending, rates, or the share of credit supplied by any group, and its identifications rest on commentators.

**Wrong turns:** reading the purses as generic greed rather than as heraldry; treating the passage as quantitative evidence.

**Model answer:** The usurers wear purses marked with coats of arms, so Dante identifies them by lineage: they are well-known families, and one says he is a Paduan among Florentines. Together with "Sodom and Cahors," where a French town's merchants stand for usury itself, the text shows that around 1300 an educated Florentine associated lending at interest with prominent Christians, including his own city's elite. The passage cannot measure anything. It gives no rates or volumes and no share of credit by group, and it is satire with a moral purpose. The family identifications come from commentators rather than from the text. It is evidence about reputation, not about market structure.

## Flashback

**From Lesson 1.5 (*Nexum* and the Roman plebs):** *(Formal (a)–(b) · Exegetical (c).)* Illustrative figures, not attested ones. A judged debt of 500 asses. The debtor's whole estate is a plot worth 300 in a normal year but 180 at a forced sale in a bad one, a yoke of oxen worth 60 and tools worth 20. Under the 326 principle the creditor takes goods, not the man. (a) What does the creditor recover, and what share of the debt is that, at forced-sale prices and at normal prices? (b) Bound labour would have been worth 30 asses a year to the creditor, net of the debtor's keep. How many years of it would each shortfall take to cover? (c) In one sentence: which premise of Caecilius's case for the old law does this arithmetic support, and name one thing it does not show. Show your arithmetic.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict:** (a) Forced sale: $180 + 60 + 20 = 260$ asses, and $260/500 = 0.52$, so **52%**, leaving a shortfall of $500 - 260 = 240$. Normal prices: $300 + 60 + 20 = 380$, and $380/500 = 0.76$, so **76%**, shortfall $120$. (b) $240/30 = 8$ years; $120/30 = 4$ years. (c) The premise supported is Caecilius's second: a poor debtor has little to seize but himself, so goods-only execution leaves a large part of the claim unrecoverable — worse in exactly the bad years that produced the default, which is why the price at which property is valued does half the work here (compare Caesar's pre-war valuation rule).

**Not shown, any one:** that credit to the poor actually contracted after 326 — the arithmetic prices one loan, it does not count loans made; that the labour was worth 30 a year, or that a creditor could in fact keep a man at work that long; that the terror of the old procedure deterred default at all, which is Caecilius's separate premise 4; and nothing at all about the plebeian premise that a citizen-soldier may not be bound by fellow citizens, which is a claim about status, not recovery.

**Wrong turns:** valuing the plot at 300 in the bad year, which is the very slump that caused the default; adding the labour years to the goods recovery as though a creditor after 326 could have both; reading the 52% as a verdict that the *Lex Poetelia* was bad policy, which the figures cannot deliver.

**Model answer:** (a) Goods fetch $180 + 60 + 20 = 260$ at forced sale, 52% of 500, leaving 240 owing; at normal prices they fetch 380, 76%, leaving 120. (b) At 30 a year, 8 years of bound labour would close the first gap and 4 the second. (c) It supports Caecilius's claim that the poor debtor's only substantial asset is his own labour, since his goods cover barely half a bad-year default; it does not show that lending to such men dried up once the body was off the table, nor does it touch the plebeian argument from citizenship.

</details>
