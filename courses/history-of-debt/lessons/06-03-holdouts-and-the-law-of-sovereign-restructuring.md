# A History of Debt · Lesson 6.3: Holdouts and the law of sovereign restructuring

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 6: Debt in the twenty-first century · Builds on: [6.2 The Eurozone and Greece](06-02-the-eurozone-and-greece.md), [5.4 From Mexico 1982 to the Brady Plan](05-04-from-mexico-1982-to-the-brady-plan.md) · Unlocks: [6.4 New creditors](06-04-new-creditors.md)

## Why this matters

A bankrupt company goes to a court, which counts the votes of each class of creditor and binds the losers. A bankrupt country has no such court. There is no tribunal that can gather every claim on a sovereign, impose a writedown and hand back a clean balance sheet. Everything the last two centuries have built instead — exchange offers, collective action clauses, the Paris Club, an IMF program — is an attempt to reproduce that result without the power to compel. This lesson is about the creditor who refuses to play along, and about the fifteen years in which a New York courtroom, not a treaty, decided what a holdout could extract.

## The idea

Three facts about sovereigns generate everything else.

**1. You cannot liquidate a country.** A defaulting state's assets are mostly at home, beyond a foreign court's reach; what it holds abroad is largely protected by sovereign immunity (in the US, the Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act of 1976 shields foreign-state property in the United States from attachment, with narrow exceptions). A creditor can usually get a *judgment*. Collecting it is another matter: Argentina lost case after case in New York and simply did not pay.

**2. So a restructuring is an offer, not a ruling.** The sovereign publishes new bonds with a lower face value, a lower coupon, a longer maturity, and invites holders to swap. Nobody is obliged to accept. The Brady deals ([5.4](05-04-from-mexico-1982-to-the-brady-plan.md)) worked this way, and so did every sovereign exchange since.

**3. An offer that most creditors accept makes refusing more attractive.** If 90 percent take 30 cents on the dollar, the sovereign is solvent again — which means the remaining 10 percent have a much better chance of being paid in full. That is the **holdout problem**, and it is the same collective action problem as a bank run in reverse: each creditor's best move depends on how many others cooperate, and the move that is individually rational makes the group's deal harder to reach. The relief is a public good; the holdout free-rides on it.

Two families of solutions were proposed. The **statutory** route would create something like bankruptcy by treaty: in 2001 the IMF's Anne Krueger proposed a Sovereign Debt Restructuring Mechanism (SDRM) that would let a supermajority of creditors bind the rest, with a stay on litigation. It needed an amendment of the IMF's Articles, the United States would not support it, and it was shelved in 2003. The **contractual** route writes the majority rule into each bond: a **collective action clause (CAC)** says that if holders of, say, 75 percent of a series agree to amend its payment terms, the amendment binds everyone in that series. English-law sovereign bonds had carried such clauses for a century; New York-law bonds generally had not, until Mexico issued one with CACs in February 2003 and the practice spread.

Argentina's defaulted bonds predated all of this — and their holders found a third route, through an old piece of boilerplate.

## Source

Argentina's 1994 Fiscal Agency Agreement, the contract governing the bonds at issue, contained a *pari passu* ("with equal step") clause in two sentences. The court called the second one the Equal Treatment Provision:

> The payment obligations of the Republic under the Securities shall at all times rank at least equally with all its other present and future unsecured and unsubordinated External Indebtedness

*(quoted in* NML Capital, Ltd. v. Republic of Argentina*, 699 F.3d 246 (2d Cir. 2012); US federal opinions are public domain)*

Everything turns on **rank**. On the traditional reading, rank is a matter of legal priority: the borrower promises not to *issue* new debt that legally outranks yours, and not to legislate you into an inferior class. On the reading the holdouts pressed, rank is also about payment: if you pay them, you pay me, proportionally. The clause does not say which. As Argentina's own reply brief conceded, quoted by the court, no one is quite sure what the clause means in a sovereign loan.

## The argument

The Second Circuit's reasoning in October 2012, in its terms:

1. **Interpreting the clause is an ordinary question of New York contract law.** *In words:* no special sovereign-debt doctrine applies; read the words the parties wrote.
2. **Argentina's claim that the clause has a settled, subordination-only meaning fails on the record.** The court cited practitioners and scholars disagreeing about the clause's meaning, including Argentina's own authorities. *In words:* you cannot win on custom when the custom is admittedly unsettled.
3. **The two sentences must do different work.** A contract should not be read so that one provision has no effect. The first sentence binds Argentina as *issuer* (no formally superior debt); the second must bind it as *payor*. *In words:* if both sentences meant "don't legally subordinate," the second would be surplus.
4. **So the second sentence "prohibits Argentina, as bond payor, from paying on other bonds without paying on the FAA Bonds."** *In words:* pay the new bonds, pay the old ones too.
5. **Argentina did exactly that, and more.** It paid the exchange bonds in full while its "Lock Law" (Law 26,017, 2005) forbade the executive to reopen the swap or settle with the untendered bonds at all. *In words:* Argentina did not merely fail to pay; it legislated the holdouts into permanent non-payment.
6. **∴ Argentina breached the Equal Treatment Provision, and an injunction requiring "ratable payment" is a permissible remedy.** Because the injunction runs against Argentina's agents too (Federal Rule 65(d)), it reaches the payment chain — the trustee, the clearing systems — through which exchange bondholders are actually paid.

**Argentina's answer**, joined by the United States government and by the Clearing House banks as *amici*: the clause protects against legal subordination only; the remedy converts a promise of equal ranking into a promise of equal payment; and an injunction that blocks payments to consenting creditors punishes third parties and will drive sovereign issuance away from New York. **The court's limit**, stated in 2013: it was not holding that any sovereign breaches its clause whenever it pays one creditor and not another, and it expected such cases to be rare, since newer bonds carry CACs.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean: what aggregation buys).** A sovereign has 10 billion dollars of bonds in ten equal series of 1 billion. The CACs require 75 percent of a series to amend that series.

*Two-limb (series by series):* a fund that buys just over 25 percent of one series — about 251 million dollars, 2.5 percent of the stock — blocks that series and must be paid in full or litigated with. Cheap.

*Single-limb (one vote across all series):* the amendment needs 75 percent of the aggregate 10 billion, so blocking requires just over 25 percent of 10 billion, about 2.5 billion. Ten times the capital for the same veto.

That arithmetic is the whole case for aggregation — and the whole worry about it, since a single vote also lets a majority impose terms on a series that voted no. Single-limb clauses therefore come with a *uniformly applicable* condition: every holder must be offered the same menu.

**Example 2 (hard: an injunction as leverage).** By November 2012 the plaintiffs held judgments Argentina would not satisfy, and its attachable US assets were effectively nil. The injunction did not seize anything. It said: if you pay the exchange bondholders 100 percent of what is then due, you must simultaneously pay the plaintiffs 100 percent of the roughly 1.33 billion dollars of principal and accrued interest they are owed.

Follow the money. Argentina wires a coupon to the indenture trustee (Bank of New York Mellon) in New York; the trustee forwards it to registered owners. Rule 65(d) binds those "in active concert" with Argentina, so the trustee cannot forward. In June 2014 Argentina deposited the coupon anyway; the court ordered it not be passed on; the grace period ran out on 30 July 2014. Argentina was in default with the cash sitting in a New York bank.

Why not settle at once? Its exchange bonds carried a Rights Upon Future Offers clause: better terms given to holdouts before 31 December 2014 could be claimed by everyone who had accepted the swap, which would have unravelled the restructuring. So the standoff ran until the clause expired and a new government settled in 2016. The lever was never the assets. It was access to the payment system.

## Watch out

- **You might think "no bankruptcy court" means "no law."** The binding constraint is *which* law. Greece could rewrite its own Greek-law bonds by statute in 2012; it could not touch its foreign-law bonds, and holders of those were paid in full. Argentina's bonds were New York-law and irrevocably waived immunity from suit, so its legislature's Lock Law bought nothing.
- **Anachronism trap: reading "ratable payment" back into the clause's history.** *Pari passu* is old boilerplate, and research on its nineteenth-century origins (Flandreau) argues it addressed involuntary subordination, not payment order. The court did not claim the payment reading was traditional; it held that on this record no settled meaning had been shown and these two sentences had to differ.
- **You might think Argentina defaulted in 2014 because it could not pay.** It had the money in the trustee's hands. This was a default produced by an injunction, which is exactly why the case is about remedies rather than capacity.
- **You might think CACs closed the problem.** They bind bondholders of the issues that contain them. They do not reach the stock of older bonds, judgments already won, or non-bond claims such as bilateral and policy-bank loans — which is where [6.4](06-04-new-creditors.md) picks up.

## One-liner

> With no court to bind them, sovereigns restructure by invitation — so the whole architecture of modern debt relief is a search for something that can bind the creditor who says no.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢) *(Exegetical.)*** In August 2013 the Second Circuit returned to the case and wrote: "Our decision here does not control the interpretation of all *pari passu* clauses or the obligations of other sovereign debtors under *pari passu* clauses in other debt instruments." It added that such cases were unlikely to recur because Argentina had been "a uniquely recalcitrant debtor" and because newer bonds almost universally include collective action clauses. (a) Which words in the first sentence do the limiting work, and what exactly is left undecided? (b) The court gives two reasons the case should not recur. Are they the same kind of reason? Say what each one would have to be true of for the reassurance to hold. 150 words or fewer in total.

**P2 (🟡) *(Formal.)*** A holdout holds bonds with 100 of face value. The exchange offer is worth 30 today, take it or leave it. Litigating instead pays 150 (principal plus accrued interest) at year 10 with probability $p$, and nothing otherwise. Discount at 10 percent a year, compounded annually. Round rates to one decimal place.
(a) Compute the break-even $p$ at which litigating matches the exchange.
(b) Litigation costs 5, payable today, win or lose. Recompute the break-even $p$.
(c) Now drop the costs again and suppose that if the suit fails the holdout can still take an exchange worth 30 *at year 10*. Recompute the break-even $p$, and say in one sentence what the change implies about a sovereign that promises never to reopen its offer.

**P3 (🔴, optional) *(Exegetical.)*** The statutory camp (the SDRM) and the contractual camp (CACs) agreed that holdouts are a real problem. Name the single premise one side must deny for the disagreement to be live, and say what evidence from restructurings since 2014 would move it. 150 words or fewer.

<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical, strict.)*

**Must hit, strict (a):**
- The limiting words are "here," "all," "other sovereign debtors" and "in other debt instruments": the holding is tied to this clause, in this contract, on this record.
- Left undecided: what *pari passu* clauses mean generally, and whether any other sovereign's conduct breaches its own clause. (Consistent with the panel's earlier statement that it was not holding that a sovereign breaches whenever it pays one creditor and not another.)

**Must hit, strict (b):**
- No, they are different in kind. "Uniquely recalcitrant debtor" is a claim about *this debtor's conduct* — default plus legislation forbidding payment plus refusal to settle. CACs are a claim about *future contracts*.
- For the first to reassure, the combination of conduct must in fact be rare. For the second, CACs must be present in the bonds that actually matter, and must bind the relevant claims — which says nothing about outstanding bonds without them.

**Wrong turns:** treating "does not control" as saying the reasoning has no precedential force at all (lower courts may still follow it; it constrains nobody's *contract* automatically); reading "uniquely recalcitrant" as a finding about Argentina's capacity to pay rather than its conduct.

**Model answer:** (a) "Here," "all," "other sovereign debtors," "in other debt instruments." The court decides this clause on this record and leaves open what *pari passu* means elsewhere and whether other sovereigns breach. (b) Different kinds. The first is about Argentina's conduct — default, a law forbidding payment, refusal to settle — and reassures only if that combination is genuinely rare. The second is about drafting, and reassures only where CACs are actually present and reach the claims at issue; bonds already outstanding without them, and judgments already obtained, are untouched.

**P2** *(Formal.)* Discount factor: $1.1^{10} = 2.5937$ (4 d.p.).

**Must hit, strict:**
- (a) PV of a win: $150 / 2.5937 = 57.83$. Break-even: $p \times 57.83 = 30$, so $p = 30/57.83 = 0.5187$, i.e. **51.9%**.
- (b) The cost is paid today, so compare $p \times 57.83 - 5$ with $30$: $p = 35/57.83 = 0.6052$, i.e. **60.5%**. (Equivalently, the suit must cover 35.)
- (c) PV of 30 received at year 10: $30/2.5937 = 11.57$. Set $p \times 57.83 + (1-p) \times 11.57 = 30$: $p = (30 - 11.57)/(57.83 - 11.57) = 18.43/46.26 = 0.3984$, i.e. **39.8%**.
- One sentence for (c): a credible "this offer will never be improved or reopened" raises the odds a holdout needs before suing (from about 40 to about 52 percent here), which is precisely why sovereigns legislate such promises — Argentina's Lock Law being the extreme case.

**Wrong turns:** discounting the litigation cost in (b) although it is paid today; in (c), forgetting that the fallback is also delayed to year 10 (using 30 undiscounted gives no solution, since the fallback would then equal the offer).

**Model answer:** as computed: 51.9%, 60.5%, 39.8%. The pattern: delay and costs push toward accepting; any chance of a later, equally good deal pushes toward holding out.

---

**P3** *(Exegetical, strict on naming one premise.)*

**Must hit, strict:**
- The crux: *whether clauses adopted instrument by instrument can cover enough of a sovereign's claims, soon enough, to stop holdouts.* The contractual camp affirms it; the statutory camp denies it, holding that only an instrument binding all creditors regardless of the contract they hold can do the job.
- Both sides agree holdouts impose costs and that supermajority voting is the mechanism; they divide on coverage and speed, not on the diagnosis.
- Evidence since 2014: how fast bonds lacking aggregated CACs ran off; whether single-limb votes were used and carried (Argentina and Ecuador in 2020 are the usual test cases, with participation disputes worth noting); whether holdouts appeared anyway; and above all whether *non-bond* claims — bilateral and policy-bank loans, arbitral awards — were restructured on comparable terms, since no bond clause reaches them.

**Wrong turns:** naming "are holdouts bad?" as the crux (both sides grant it); treating the SDRM's political failure as an argument about its design; listing two or three premises instead of one.

**Model answer:** The premise in dispute is that contract terms, adopted one bond at a time, can reach a large enough share of a sovereign's obligations fast enough to make restructuring work. CAC advocates affirm it; SDRM advocates deny it, arguing that only a statutory or treaty mechanism binds every claim, including those the sovereign never put in a bond. Evidence that would move it: the pace at which pre-2014 bonds matured out of the stock; whether aggregated votes were actually invoked and carried after 2014; whether litigious holdouts still appeared; and whether official bilateral and policy-bank loans were brought to comparable terms, since no bond clause can compel them.

## Flashback

**From Lesson 6.1 (The American mortgage and 2008):** *(Formal.)* A private-label trust holds 100 million dollars of mortgage loans, funded by three tranches: senior 80, mezzanine 15, equity 5. Losses are absorbed from the bottom up — equity first, then mezzanine, then senior. All figures illustrative. (a) Allocate a pool loss of 4 million across the three tranches, then a pool loss of 18 million. (b) The servicer must choose between foreclosing, which produces pool losses of 18 million, and modifying, which produces pool losses of 6 million. Give each tranche's loss under each policy, and say which tranche prefers modification, by how much, and which tranches are indifferent. (c) One sentence: what does (b) say about who gets to decide?

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):**
- Loss of 4: equity absorbs $\min(4, 5) = \mathbf{4}$ (1 of its 5 survives); mezzanine **0**, still 15; senior **0**, still 80.
- Loss of 18: equity absorbs its whole 5 and is wiped out; mezzanine absorbs $18 - 5 = \mathbf{13}$, leaving 2 of 15; senior **0**, still 80. Check: $5 + 13 + 0 = 18$. The senior tranche has 20 of subordination beneath it, so no loss reaches it until the pool has lost more than 20 per cent.

**Must hit, strict (b):**

| | Foreclose (pool loss 18) | Modify (pool loss 6) | Difference |
|---|---|---|---|
| Equity (5) | 5 | 5 | 0 |
| Mezzanine (15) | 13 | $6 - 5 = 1$ | **12** |
| Senior (80) | 0 | 0 | 0 |

Only the **mezzanine** prefers modification, and it prefers it by **12 million**. The senior tranche is indifferent because both losses fall short of its 20 of subordination. The equity tranche is indifferent because both losses exceed its 5, so it is wiped out either way — its loss is capped at its own principal.

**Must hit, strict (c):** the policy that destroys 12 million less of value is preferred by exactly one tranche out of three, and the decision belongs to a servicer who holds none of them and is paid a fee; the two tranches holding 85 of the 100 have no stake in the choice at all. That is why "modify or foreclose" could not be settled by adding up the creditors' interests, and why the HOLC — which bought the whole loan and so bore the whole loss — faced no such problem.

**Wrong turns:** splitting losses pro rata across the three tranches, when the waterfall is strictly sequential; charging the senior tranche at an 18 per cent pool loss, when 20 of subordination absorbs it entirely; calling the equity tranche a winner under modification because its loss stops at 5 — it is wiped out under both, so it is indifferent; assuming that a policy with lower total losses makes every holder better off.

**Model answer:** (a) At 4: equity 4, mezzanine 0, senior 0. At 18: equity 5 (wiped), mezzanine 13, senior 0. (b) Foreclose: 5 / 13 / 0. Modify: 5 / 1 / 0. Only the mezzanine gains, by 12 million; the senior is untouched either way behind 20 of subordination, and the equity is wiped out either way. (c) The one policy that preserves 12 million of value has a single interested holder, while the decision sits with a fee-paid servicer who owns none of the trust and the holders of 85 per cent of it are indifferent — no creditor here both cares about the outcome and has the power to choose it.

</details>
