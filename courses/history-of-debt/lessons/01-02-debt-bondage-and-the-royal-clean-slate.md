# A History of Debt · Lesson 1.2: Debt bondage and the royal clean slate

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 1: Ancient debt: bondage and clean slates · Builds on: [1.1 Credit before coins](01-01-credit-before-coins.md) · Unlocks: [1.3 Release laws in ancient Israel](01-03-release-laws-in-ancient-israel.md)

## Why this matters

[1.1](01-01-credit-before-coins.md) showed that barley loans at 33⅓% and silver loans at 20% compound faster than a harvest grows. This lesson covers what followed when a farmer could not pay: his wife, son or daughter went to work in the creditor's house. Babylonian kings answered with two different tools. One was a standing time limit on that servitude. The other was an occasional royal edict that broke the debt tablets. Every later argument about debt relief, from Solon to Jubilee 2000, has this pair somewhere behind it, so it pays to know exactly what the edicts cancelled and what they left alone. It also pays to know why specialists still disagree about how often edicts came and how much they did.

## The idea

Old Babylonian credit came in two kinds, and the whole lesson turns on keeping them apart.

**Consumption debt.** A cultivator borrows barley to eat before the harvest, or falls behind on rent and dues to the palace. If the harvest is poor, the debt grows and his land yields nothing extra to pay it with. His creditor can seize a household member as a *distress*, a person held until the debt is paid (the Laws of Hammurabi §§114–116 regulate this, and punish a creditor whose distrainee dies from beatings). Or the debtor can sell or hand over his dependents to work the debt off.

**Commercial debt.** A merchant (*tamkārum*) advances silver to an agent for a trading venture (*harrānum*, literally "road" or "journey"). Both parties expect profit, and both are traders who chose the risk.

A spiral of the first kind strips a household of land and people. From the palace's point of view that is a disaster, because those people farm the crown's fields, pay its dues and fill its levies. So the king steps in, periodically, with a *misharum* ("justice, equity") act that restores the old order. Consumption debts and arrears are cancelled, and bondservants go home, which is *andurārum*, "release." Commercial loans stand. Think of it less as forgiveness than as a factory reset for the farming household, with the trading economy deliberately left running.

Earlier Sumerian rulers of Lagash, Enmetena and Uruinimgina (both around the 24th century BC; dates hedged), proclaimed *amargi*, literally "return to the mother," the release of pledged persons back to their families. The Babylonian edicts are the most detailed descendants of that tradition. The fullest surviving text is the edict of **Ammi-saduqa**, issued at his accession, c. 1646 BC on the conventional middle chronology. J. J. Finkelstein's translation (in *ANET*, 1969) is the standard English version. It is paraphrased here, never quoted.

## Source

Laws of Hammurabi §117, in L. W. King's translation (1910, public domain):

> "If any one fail to meet a claim for debt, and sell himself, his wife, his son, and daughter for money or give them away to forced labor: they shall work for three years in the house of the man who bought them, or the proprietor, and in the fourth year they shall be set free."

Three things to notice. First, the section names **two routes**: an outright sale ("the man who bought them") and handing someone over to work off the debt ("the proprietor"). The limit covers both. Second, it is a **standing rule** that runs automatically, case by case, with no royal act needed. Third, it caps how long a person can be held; it says nothing about whether the debt itself is thereby cleared. Note too that "himself" is King's rendering. Johns's 1903 translation, and most later ones, list only wife, son and daughter, so don't build an argument on that word. Compare §48: in a year when storm or drought destroys the crop, the debtor pays nothing that year, and King renders it vividly: "he washes his debt-tablet in water."

## The argument

**The rationale of a *misharum* edict, reconstructed.** The edict itself states only that the king has established *misharum* for the land. The premises below are the logic historians read out of its provisions and of royal ideology. They are given in the actors' terms.

1. **The king is charged by the gods with justice for the land.** Hammurabi's prologue and epilogue present him as appointed to make justice appear; year-names record rulers establishing *misharum*. *In words:* restoring equity is part of the royal job description, not an act of charity.
2. **Justice means restoring a prior balance, not creating a new equality.** Freed persons return to the household they came from, and chattel slaves bought from outside stay slaves. *In words:* the target is the status quo before the debt spiral (Charpin's emphasis, as Hudson reports it).
3. **Consumption debts and arrears arise from misfortune and dues, not from chosen ventures.** *In words:* the farmer who borrowed to eat did not take a gamble the way a trading agent did.
4. **Left alone, such debts transfer land and people from the king's subjects to creditors.** *In words:* compounding plus bad harvests turns free cultivators into dependents of the rich.
5. **Those cultivators are the palace's farmers, dues-payers and soldiers.** *In words:* a debt-bonded population is a weaker kingdom.
6. **Commercial advances finance the trade the palace itself depends on.** *In words:* cancelling them would wreck the system rather than restore it.
7. **Creditors will try to evade a release.** Hence the edict voids loans disguised as sales or deposits, orders creditors to refund debts collected by pressure in breach of it, and threatens offenders with death. *In words:* the drafters expected lenders to game the rule, and wrote against it.

**∴** Cancel arrears to the palace and interest-bearing barley and silver loans owed by the edict's protected population (Akkadians and Amorites); free persons held in debt service; leave commercial ventures standing; void the disguises.

## Map of positions

The crux over how general and effective such edicts were is Module 1's boss problem. Here is the terrain only.

| | **Routine clean slates** (Michael Hudson, in essays from the 1990s and *...and forgive them their debts*, 2018) | **Limited, discretionary measures** (a more cautious reading found among many Assyriologists; Van De Mieroop has urged care in reading royal proclamations as descriptions of practice; hedged) |
|---|---|---|
| **How often** | Regular, especially at accession; Hudson's list, built on F. R. Kraus's collection (1984), gives sixteen acts by Babylon's kings from Sumulael (c. 1880 BC) to Ammi-saduqa's second (c. 1636) | Many attestations are year-names whose content is unknown; frequency and scope varied by king |
| **What they did** | Real, enforced restorations; Finkelstein (1965), as Hudson reports, found commissions set up to review sales after an edict | Effect is hard to measure; relief could be partial, local, or mostly a remission of the palace's own arrears |
| **The anti-evasion clauses show…** | That edicts were enforced and expected | That evasion was common enough to need repeated prohibition |
| **Common ground** | The edicts existed; the Ammi-saduqa text cancels consumption, not commercial, debts; bondservants of free status were released | Same |

The same evidence (the anti-evasion clauses) sits on both sides of the table. That is the sign of a live crux, not a settled one.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean): one household on the day of the edict.** Awīl-Adad (an invented cultivator) owes (i) 20 *gur* of barley borrowed before last harvest from a local lender, (ii) arrears of dues to the palace, and (iii) nothing on commerce. His brother-in-law, a trading agent, holds 30 shekels a merchant advanced him for a *harrānum*. Two years ago he handed his daughter over to the barley lender to work off an earlier debt.

- (i) Barley loan, interest included: **cancelled.** The tablet is broken; anything the lender collected from him by pressure in breach of the edict must be refunded.
- (ii) Palace arrears: **remitted.** Here the king is cancelling debts owed to himself.
- The daughter: **released** now. Without the edict §117 would free her only after another year of work, in the fourth year. The edict brings the release forward.
- Brother-in-law's venture silver: **stands.** He still owes the merchant.

Lesson: what gets cancelled depends on the **kind** of debt, not on the debtor's poverty or the amount owed.

**Example 2 (hard): the disguised loan.** A lender foresees that the new king may proclaim *misharum*. So instead of writing a loan tablet, he records that he "bought" the cultivator's field for 10 shekels, while the cultivator keeps farming it and pays a share of the crop, which is interest in all but name. The edict comes.

On paper this is a sale, and sales are not debts. The Ammi-saduqa edict anticipates exactly this. A document drawn up as a sale or a deposit that actually conceals an interest-bearing loan is void, the transaction is judged by its substance, and the creditor faces penalties. So the field returns.

What does the example teach a historian? Two things, pulling in different directions. Lenders adapted their paperwork to royal policy, so the edicts shaped the form of credit even between proclamations. And the drafters knew the paperwork could lie, so the historian must ask of every surviving "sale" tablet whether it was really a sale. That makes it harder to count how much land edicts actually restored. The substance-over-form rule also reappears whenever later authorities police disguised interest, from the medieval "dry exchange" ([2.1](02-01-commerce-around-the-prohibition.md)) to the *prosbul* debate ([1.3](01-03-release-laws-in-ancient-israel.md)).

## Watch out

- **Anachronism trap: "clean slate" is not modern debt forgiveness.** You might think a *misharum* act was a universal amnesty or an early bankruptcy discharge. It restored a *prior order*. It freed the free-born bonded for debt, not slaves bought from outside, and it spared commercial creditors. It was royal discretion, not a right a debtor could file for.
- **You might think the Laws of Hammurabi are a statute book that courts applied.** Many Assyriologists read them as model rulings and royal self-presentation; the extent to which judges cited them is debated. §117 tells you the norm the king proclaimed, not how often it was honored.
- **You might think debt service was chattel slavery.** Under §117 the family member was time-limited and belonged to a free household. §118 treats slaves handed over for debt differently.
- **You might think the dates are firm.** "1646 BC" depends on the middle chronology. Other chronologies shift the whole dynasty by decades, so say "c." and name the chronology.

## One-liner

> Babylonian kings capped debt servitude by law and periodically broke the consumption-debt tablets by decree, sparing merchants' ventures; historians agree on what the edicts said and disagree on how often and how far they bit.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢) *(Formal.)*** A lender's book on the day an edict is proclaimed (all loans at simple interest, amounts in shekels of silver or silver-equivalent):

- 30 shekels lent to farmers for subsistence 9 months ago at 20% a year;
- barley worth 12 shekels lent 6 months ago at 33⅓% a year;
- 40 shekels advanced to a trading agent for a *harrānum* venture (count it at principal).

(a) What share of his outstanding claims (principal plus accrued interest) does an edict of the Ammi-saduqa type wipe out? Compare with the share of principal alone. (b) Stylize the edict as striking any one-year consumption loan with probability $p$, wiping out principal and interest. What $p$ makes a one-year loan at 20% yield the same expected return as a safe alternative paying 10%? (c) At $p = 0.10$, what rate would the loan need? Round to one decimal. (Say in one sentence what this stylization leaves out.)

**P2 (🟡) *(Exegetical.)*** Read §117 (in the Source) with §118, in King's translation: "If he give a male or female slave away for forced labor, and the merchant sublease them, or sell them for money, no objection can be raised." (a) What may a creditor do with a person handed over under §118 that he may not do under §117, and which words in each section decide it? (b) Does §117 say the debt is extinguished when the family member goes free in the fourth year? Point to the text. 150 words or fewer in total.

**P3 (🔴, optional) *(Exegetical.)*** Two historians disagree about whether Old Babylonian release edicts **hurt the supply of credit** to cultivators. One argues that the risk of cancellation made lenders refuse consumption loans, charge more, or disguise them, so the poor were left worse off between edicts. The other argues that the edicts did little damage to credit, because what they cancelled was largely arrears to the palace and its agents and because commercial lending was exempt. Name the single premise they divide on, and say what documentary evidence would move it. Don't argue for either side. 150 words or fewer.

**P1** *(Formal.)*

(a) Claims with simple interest, $A = P(1 + rt)$, where $P$ is principal, $r$ the annual rate and $t$ the time in years:
- Subsistence silver: $30(1 + 0.20 \times 0.75) = 30 \times 1.15 = 34.5$ shekels.
- Barley: $12\left(1 + \tfrac{1}{3} \times 0.5\right) = 12 \times \tfrac{7}{6} = 14$ shekels.
- Venture: $40$ shekels, not cancelled.

Total $34.5 + 14 + 40 = 88.5$. Wiped: $34.5 + 14 = 48.5$. Share $= 48.5 / 88.5 = 0.548$, so **about 54.8%**. Principal only: $42/82 = 0.512$, so **about 51.2%**. The share is higher counting interest because the cancelled loans carry the higher rates and have been accruing.

(b) Expected gross return $(1-p)(1.20)$ must equal $1.10$: $p = 1 - \tfrac{1.10}{1.20} = \tfrac{1}{12} \approx$ **8.3% a year**.

(c) $(1 - 0.10)(1 + r) = 1.10$ gives $1 + r = 1.10/0.90 = 1.2222$, so $r \approx$ **22.2%**, above the conventional 20%.

**What the stylization leaves out:** edicts were not random draws. Accessions were partly foreseeable, and lenders could exit to commercial lending or disguise loans. For scale, Hudson's list has 15 acts from Sin-muballit's first (c. 1812 BC) to Ammi-saduqa's second (c. 1636), roughly one every 12–13 years, but that list and its reading of year-names are contested. The general model of pricing credit under a release is [`economics-of-debt`](../../economics-of-debt/syllabus.md) 8.2.

**Wrong turns:** compounding the interest (the problem says simple); cancelling the venture advance; answering (b) as $p = 10\%$ by subtracting rates instead of solving the expected-return equation.

---

**P2** *(Exegetical — strict.)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** Under §118 the creditor ("the merchant") may sublease the slave or sell them on, and "no objection can be raised." Under §117 the persons handed over "shall work for three years" in that house and "in the fourth year they shall be set free." The deciding contrast is *slave* in §118 against *wife, son, daughter* in §117: the time limit protects free household members, not chattel slaves.

**Must hit, strict (b):** No. §117 limits how long the persons work and orders their release. It is silent on whether the underlying claim is satisfied. Reading the service as paying the debt is an inference, however natural, not the text.

**Wrong turns:** saying §118 slaves are freed after three years too; saying §117 cancels the debt; building on King's "himself" without noting other translations lack it.

**Model answer:** (a) A slave handed over under §118 may be sublet or resold by the merchant, "no objection can be raised"; free family members under §117 may only be kept working "three years" and must be "set free" in the fourth. The word *slave* against *wife, son, daughter* marks the line. (b) The text caps the service and frees the person but never says the claim is discharged. That the three years settle the debt is a reasonable inference the section does not state.

---

**P3** *(Exegetical — strict on isolating one premise; no verdict required or credited.)*

**Must hit, strict:**
- The crux: *whether the claims the edicts cancelled came from a voluntary private lending market whose lenders adjusted supply and terms to expected cancellation.* The first historian affirms it; the second denies that this describes most of what was cancelled.
- Evidence that would move it: (i) the **composition** of claims in the edicts and related lawsuits (palace arrears versus private loans); (ii) **dated loan contracts** in the years just before and after edicts or expected accessions, showing whether volume fell or terms changed; (iii) the **frequency of disguised sales and deposits** and suits under the anti-evasion clauses.

**Wrong turns:** naming "whether edicts were good for the poor," which is a verdict, not a premise; naming the commercial exemption itself as the crux (both accept it); citing the anti-evasion clause as decisive for one side when it bears on both.

**Model answer:** The disputed premise is whether the cancelled debts were mainly loans a private lender chose to make and would stop making, or on worse terms, when cancellation loomed, rather than arrears and dues owed to the palace, which no market supplied. If the former, cancellation risk would plausibly shrink or distort credit; if the latter, the edict mostly wrote off the crown's own claims. Evidence: tally the kinds of claims named in the edicts and in post-edict lawsuits; compare the number and terms of dated private loan tablets in the years around accessions; count sale or deposit documents later voided as disguised loans.
