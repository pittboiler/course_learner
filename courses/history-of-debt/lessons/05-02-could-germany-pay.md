# A History of Debt · Lesson 5.2: Could Germany pay?

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 5: Reparations to Jubilee: the twentieth century · Builds on: [5.1 War debts and reparations](05-01-war-debts-and-reparations.md) · Unlocks: [5.3 Bretton Woods and the lending boom](05-03-bretton-woods-and-the-lending-boom.md)

## Why this matters

Keynes in 1919 said the Allies were asking Germany for more than Germany could pay. Étienne Mantoux in *The Carthaginian Peace* (1946) and Sally Marks in "The Myths of Reparations" (1978) replied, in different registers, that Germany could have paid and preferred not to. That is the oldest live argument in sovereign debt, and it is never only about arithmetic: "cannot pay" and "will not pay" call for different remedies, and each side accuses the other of confusing them. Sitting underneath the dispute is a fact that unsettles both: through the 1920s, American money flowed into Germany at roughly the rate reparations flowed out. Greece in 2012, Argentina in 2001 and Zambia in 2020 all reran this lesson's two questions — capacity or will, and who actually bears the transfer.

## The idea

Paying a debt at home and paying one abroad are different operations.

A government paying domestic bondholders has one problem: raise the money in taxes. Call that the **budgetary problem**. A government paying foreign creditors has that problem *and* a second one: the taxes arrive in its own currency, and the creditor wants his. Somebody must convert marks into dollars, pounds or francs, and the only ways a country gets foreign currency are to sell more goods abroad than it buys, to sell assets, or to borrow. Keynes named this second operation the **transfer problem**, and his claim was that it, not the budget, was where the German schedule would break.

Notice what follows. A country can win the budgetary fight and still fail: the treasury collects the marks, and there is no way to hand them over that the exchange rate will survive. And a country can appear to succeed for years without solving either problem, if someone lends it the foreign currency.

That is what happened. From 1924 the flow ran in a circle. American investors lent to German cities, firms and states; Germany paid reparations to Britain, France, Belgium and Italy; those governments paid war debts to the United States Treasury. The money made a loop, and each leg made the others look affordable. When the lending stopped, the loop stopped with it — and the question of what Germany could "really" pay was asked for the first time without a subsidy in the answer.

## Source

The settlement that finally cleaned this up was the Agreement on German External Debts, signed in London on 27 February 1953 (English is one of its authentic texts). Article 5 is titled "Claims excluded from the Agreement":

> Consideration of governmental claims against Germany arising out of the first World War shall be deferred until a final general settlement of this matter.

And, for the second war:

> Consideration of claims arising out of the second World War … shall be deferred until the final settlement of the problem of reparation.

Read what the clause does. It does not decide whether the claims are good. It parks them — indefinitely, since the "final settlement of the problem of reparation" was understood to wait on a peace treaty with a reunified Germany. The Agreement settled the ordinary *debts*, prewar and postwar, including the Dawes and Young loans that had financed reparations in the first place, and it cut them by roughly half (about 29.7 billion Deutschmarks of claims, reduced to something near 14.5 billion; historians quote slightly different totals depending on what is counted). Reparations, the thing everyone had argued about since 1919, were handled by being set aside.

## The argument

**Keynes's transfer argument** ("The German Transfer Problem", *Economic Journal*, 1929, developing the 1919 book):

1. **Reparations are payable in foreign currency and in kind, not in marks.** *In words:* the creditor needs purchasing power abroad, so marks collected in Berlin are not yet a payment.
2. **So Germany must generate an equal surplus of exports over imports, sell assets, or borrow.** *In words:* the money has to cross the border as goods, property or credit; there is no fourth route.
3. **To sell substantially more abroad, German goods must be made cheaper relative to foreign goods.** *In words:* you cannot force the world to buy more from you at unchanged prices.
4. **Under the gold standard, that price cut cannot come from devaluation, so it must come from lower German wages and prices.** *In words:* with the parity fixed, the adjustment falls on domestic incomes.
5. **Foreign demand is not very responsive to price, and creditor countries protect their own producers against exactly the goods Germany must sell.** *In words:* the creditors want the payment but not the imports that constitute it, so the required price cut is larger than it looks and partly self-defeating.
6. **Therefore the burden of transfer exceeds the burden in the budget, and a schedule affordable in marks may be unpayable in foreign exchange.** *In words:* the sum on the paper is not the real question.

**Bertil Ohlin's reply** (same journal, 1929): paying transfers purchasing power to the recipients, who then spend more — including on German goods — while German demand falls. Trade shifts toward Germany's exports without much price change, so the transfer is close to self-financing. Keynes rejoindered that the shift is neither automatic nor quick, and that the interval is where the damage happens. The model behind the exchange belongs to [`economics-of-debt`](../../economics-of-debt/syllabus.md) 6.1; here it stays an argument about a schedule.

## Map of positions

| Position | Could Germany pay? | Who ultimately bore it? | Leans on |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Keynes (1919, 1929)** | Not the sums demanded; the transfer, not the budget, is binding | Whoever forced the deflation — German wage earners, then everyone, via the slump | Export elasticities, creditor tariffs, the fixed gold parity |
| **Ohlin (1929)** | Yes, with modest adjustment | Germans, through a transfer of purchasing power, not a collapse | The income effects of paying and receiving |
| **Mantoux (1946)** | Yes; Keynes's capacity estimates were falsified by what Germany later mobilized for rearmament | Nobody, in the end — the schedule was evaded | Comparison of 1920s payments with 1930s German military output |
| **Marks (1978)** | Yes; the real obligation was far below the headline, and German governments treated evasion as policy | The Allies, who collected far less than the public figures suggested | The A and B bonds (50 billion gold marks) as the genuine claim, the C bonds largely a device for public opinion; German fiscal and diplomatic conduct |
| **Schuker (1988)** | The question is mis-set: on net, capital moved *toward* Germany | American lenders, through loans never repaid and mark assets wiped out by the inflation | Net capital flows 1919–31: on his accounting the inflow exceeded reparations by roughly 2 percent of German national income a year |

Albrecht Ritschl has argued, in a sovereign-debt reading of the period (summarized here, not quoted), that the seniority given to the Dawes loan made reparations effectively junior to commercial debt, so Germany's eventual default fell on private lenders rather than on the Allied governments — a mechanism for Schuker's ledger rather than a rival to it.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean: the Dawes Plan as an answer to the transfer problem).** The 1924 plan is the transfer argument turned into machinery. Germany paid graduated annuities — beginning around 1 billion gold marks and rising to a "standard" 2.5 billion by 1928–29 — but it paid them *in marks*, into an account in Berlin. An Agent-General for Reparation Payments sat in Berlin, and transfer abroad happened only so far as it could be done without wrecking the exchange rate. A loan of 800 million marks was floated to start the system, much of it placed in New York.

Run the premises. P1 and P2 are conceded: the annuity is only a payment once it crosses the border. P4's gold-standard constraint is respected by making the transfer, not the budget, the variable that gives way. The creditors have accepted Keynes's distinction and kept the sum: Germany owes the annuity, and the risk that it cannot be transferred is carried by the people waiting to be paid. The plan worked, in the narrow sense, for five years — and it worked because American lending supplied the foreign exchange that P3's export adjustment was supposed to earn.

**Example 2 (hard: what happens when the lending stops).** The Young Plan (1929, in force 1930) reduced the schedule and stretched it — 59 annuities running to 1988, on the order of 112 billion marks including interest — created the Bank for International Settlements to handle the payments, and split the annuity into a part Germany could postpone and an unconditional part it could not. Commercializing the debt this way removed most of the transfer protection. Within months the American lending boom ended.

Now every leg of the circle broke at once. Germany's foreign exchange had to come from a trade surplus, which meant the deflation of P4: Brüning's emergency decrees, falling wages, mass unemployment. In June 1931 Hoover announced a one-year moratorium on intergovernmental war debts and reparations alike; a "standstill" froze Germany's short-term bank credits. At Lausanne in July 1932 the Allies effectively ended reparations, asking only that Germany deposit 3 billion marks of bonds to be sold later, conditional on relief on their own war debts from Washington. The relief never came, the bonds were never marketed, and in 1933–34 the European debtors defaulted on the United States; the Johnson Act (April 1934) barred further American lending to governments in default.

So the same country was called able to pay in 1929 and unable in 1931 with no change in its productive capacity. That is the deepest point in the episode: "capacity to pay" was not a fixed property of Germany. It was a property of Germany *plus* the capital market, and the schedule quietly assumed the second would keep behaving.

## Watch out

- **You might think the headline figure was the obligation.** The 132 billion gold marks of 1921 was split into A, B and C bonds, and the C tranche was not scheduled for service in any foreseeable state of the world. Arguing about capacity against the headline number, on either side, argues about the wrong number.
- **You might think "who paid" and "who suffered" are the same question.** Schuker's ledger is about net capital flows; it does not say German deflation was painless, and Keynes's account of that pain does not answer the ledger. The two claims can both be true.
- **You might think the 1923 hyperinflation was reparations being paid.** It was, at most, reparations *not* being paid: the Ruhr occupation, passive resistance and its financing by the printing press. Causation here is contested, and the inflation destroyed domestic mark claims rather than transferring resources to the Allies.
- **Anachronism trap: no floating exchange rate, no IMF.** Devaluation as an adjustment tool and an official lender of last resort are post-1944 furniture ([5.3](05-03-bretton-woods-and-the-lending-boom.md)). In 1929 the alternatives to deflation were default, exchange control or a change in the schedule — which is why the argument took the political form it did.

## One-liner

> A debt owed abroad is paid in goods or in someone else's money; Germany paid mostly in someone else's money, and the question of what it could really pay was never asked with the subsidy switched off.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢) *(Formal.)*** The figures below are **illustrative** — right order of magnitude for the Dawes years, not historical data. In one year, in billions of gold marks: Germany pays reparations $R = 2.0$; Germany receives new long-term foreign lending $L = 1.6$ (overwhelmingly American); the Allied governments pay $W = 1.0$ to the United States on war debts. German exports are 9.0 and German national income is 60.0.

(a) Compute the net flow for each of the three parties (Germany, the Allies, the United States), and check that the three sum to zero.
(b) Express Germany's gross reparations and its *net* outflow as percentages of national income, and gross reparations as a percentage of exports. Round to one decimal place.
(c) Now set $L = 0$, holding $R$ and $W$ fixed. By what factor does Germany's net outflow rise? What extra trade surplus, as a percentage of exports, must Germany generate to transfer the same $R$? One sentence on what this implies about "capacity to pay."

**P2 (🟡) *(Exegetical.)*** Article 34 of the 1953 London Agreement provides for consultation if Germany has difficulty meeting its obligations, and says that in that case:

> attention shall be given to all relevant economic, financial and monetary considerations which relate to the ability to transfer of the Federal Republic of Germany, as influenced by both internal and external factors

(a) Which distinction from this lesson does the phrase "ability to transfer" encode, and which three or four words in the passage decide it? (b) The article opens "without derogating from the obligations which the Federal Republic of Germany has assumed." What does the clause therefore *not* give the debtor? 150 words or fewer in total.

**P3 (🔴, optional) *(Exegetical.)*** Schuker (1988) concludes that on net, capital moved toward Germany in 1919–31, so Germany paid no net reparations; his critics hold that Germany's reparations burden was real. Name the single premise Schuker's critics must deny, state it in one sentence, and say what evidence or accounting decision would move it. Do not adjudicate. 150 words or fewer.

<details>
<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Formal.)* All figures in billions of gold marks; computed in Python, exact at this precision.

(a) Germany: $L - R = 1.6 - 2.0 = -0.4$, i.e. a **net outflow of 0.4**. Allies: $R - W = 2.0 - 1.0 = +1.0$, a **net inflow of 1.0**. United States: $W - L = 1.0 - 1.6 = -0.6$, a **net outflow of 0.6**. Sum: $-0.4 + 1.0 - 0.6 = 0$. It must be zero — every payment is someone's receipt.

(b) Gross reparations over national income: $2.0/60.0 = 0.0333\ldots \approx$ **3.3 percent**. Net outflow over national income: $0.4/60.0 = 0.0066\ldots \approx$ **0.7 percent**. Gross reparations over exports: $2.0/9.0 = 0.2222\ldots \approx$ **22.2 percent**.

(c) With $L=0$, Germany's net outflow is the full $2.0$, up from $0.4$: a factor of $2.0/0.4 =$ **5**. It is now 3.3 percent of national income rather than 0.7. To transfer $R$ without borrowing, Germany must find the missing $1.6$ in trade: $1.6/9.0 = 0.1777\ldots \approx$ **17.8 percent of exports**, which is the export expansion (or import compression) premise 3 of the argument demands. Implication: with the lending on, the burden looks small; the same schedule, same economy and no lending is a five-fold larger transfer. Capacity to pay was partly a property of the capital market, not of Germany.

**Wrong turns:** netting $W$ against $R$ inside Germany's position (Germany is not a party to the war debts); reporting Germany's net outflow as a share of exports and calling it the burden of reparations; treating the zero sum as an accident rather than an identity.

---

**P2** *(Exegetical — strict.)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** the budgetary/transfer distinction — Keynes's point in the argument above, that raising marks at home and converting them into foreign currency are separate operations. Deciding words: **"ability to transfer"** (not ability to pay, and not the state of the budget), and **"external factors"**, which concedes that conditions outside Germany — world demand, creditors' trade policy, the capital market — bear on whether payment is possible. Credit also for "economic, financial and monetary considerations," which opens the inquiry beyond fiscal capacity.
**Must hit, strict (b):** it gives no right to suspend or reduce payment. The obligations stand ("without derogating from"); what the clause creates is a procedure — consultation, with expert advice if the principal parties so decide. Transfer difficulty is a trigger for talks, not a defence.
**Wrong turns:** reading "ability to transfer" as ability to afford, which erases the distinction the clause was written to capture; treating the article as an automatic hardship or escape clause; saying the clause proves Germany could not pay (it is a procedure, not a finding).
**Model answer:** (a) It encodes the budgetary/transfer distinction: the question is not whether Germany can raise the money but whether it can turn it into foreign exchange. "Ability to transfer" is the decisive phrase, and "external factors" concedes that creditors' markets and world conditions govern it, as premise 5 of Keynes's argument claims. (b) No relief. The obligations are expressly undisturbed; a transfer difficulty buys consultation and possibly expert advice, not suspension, reduction or a defence to a claim.

---

**P3** *(Exegetical — strict; no verdict required or rewarded.)*

**Must hit, strict:** the premise is the *ledger rule* — that private capital flowing into Germany and never repaid (loans repudiated after 1933, plus mark-denominated claims destroyed in the inflation) belongs in the same account as reparations flowing out, so that the two can be netted and the net figure answers "did Germany pay?" Critics must deny that they are commensurable: the loans were voluntary investments made at a price for risk, by private American lenders, not transfers by the Allied governments who were owed reparations; and the resources Germany actually gave up in coal, ships, patents and cash are not undone by a later default on a different set of creditors.
**What would move it:** a full flow accounting that separates parties (who lent, who received, who lost) and asks whether the borrowing would have occurred at all absent reparations — the counterfactual on which the netting stands or falls; evidence on the seniority of the Dawes and Young loans, which determines whether commercial lenders were first or last in line; and a decision about how to value the mark assets wiped out by the inflation, which is a large and contestable share of Schuker's figure.
**Wrong turns:** treating the crux as a dispute about the size of the flows (both sides can accept the numbers and still divide on whether they may be netted); answering "who suffered more," which is a different question; ruling on whether Germany was treated harshly, which this problem does not ask.
**Model answer:** The premise is that unrepaid private American loans and inflation-destroyed mark claims may be netted against reparations, so that net capital flow settles who paid. Critics deny commensurability: those lenders were private investors taking a priced risk, not the governments owed reparations, and a later default on them does not restore the coal, ships and cash Germany transferred. Moving it takes a party-by-party flow accounting, a counterfactual on whether Germany would have borrowed absent reparations, evidence on the seniority of the Dawes and Young loans, and a defensible valuation of the wiped-out mark assets.

## Flashback

**From Lesson 4.6 (Bondholders, gunboats, and receiverships):** *(Exegetical.)* Two episodes that lesson names but does not analyze. (a) Palmerston's circular of 1848: place it on the four-lever ladder, and say what it settled about how far that lever reached for bondholders. (b) Newfoundland's Commission of Government (1934): which lever, and what does the episode show about whether that lever needed the one below it? (c) In 1910 a creditor power threatens to land marines over an unpaid contract debt, and the debtor has offered to arbitrate. May it, under the Porter Convention? Under Drago's position as Drago stated it? Two sentences or fewer per part.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):** lever 2, **lobby**. The circular held that whether Britain took up bondholders' claims was a matter of the government's discretion, not a right the bondholders could invoke — and the reserved power was rarely used. So the lever existed but did not oblige: investors who lent abroad were largely left to carry their own risk.

**Must hit, strict (b):** lever 4, **administer** — reached without lever 3. A royal commission (1933), British and Canadian loans, and Newfoundland's own legislature asking for the constitution to be suspended; no blockade, no bombardment, no threat of either. Fiscal sovereignty could be surrendered without a gunboat, which is exactly why "lost financial control" cannot be coded as coercion on its own.

**Must hit, strict (c):** **No under Porter, no under Drago**, on different grounds. Porter bars recourse to armed force for the recovery of contract debts *unless* the debtor refuses arbitration or ignores the award — arbitration has been offered, so the exception never opens. Drago's position is broader and unconditional: public debt cannot justify armed intervention or occupation at all — but as he framed it in 1902, the claim was about American states, so it reaches this case only if the debtor is one.

**Wrong turns:** filing Palmerston under "coerce" because a power was reserved — the circular is about whether the state takes the claim up at all, and its effect was to decline a duty; reading Porter as an outright ban on debt-collecting force (it restricts it) or as licensing force whenever a debt goes unpaid; inferring coercion at Newfoundland from the loss of self-government.

**Model answer:** (a) Lobby (lever 2). The circular made taking up bondholders' claims discretionary rather than owed, and the discretion was seldom exercised, so foreign lenders bore their own risk. (b) Administer (lever 4), arrived at with no coercion: a royal commission, rescue loans, and Newfoundland's own legislature asking that its constitution be suspended. Lever 4 did not require lever 3, so foreign fiscal control is not by itself evidence of force. (c) Not under Porter: force for contract debts is barred unless the debtor refuses arbitration or defies the award, and arbitration was offered. Not under Drago either, on the stronger ground that public debt never justifies armed intervention or occupation — though Drago stated that of American states, so it bites here only if the debtor is one.

</details>
