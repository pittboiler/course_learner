# A History of Debt · Lesson 2.4: Lending to kings

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 2: Credit under the usury ban · Builds on: [2.3 The public debts of city-states](02-03-the-public-debts-of-city-states.md) · Unlocks: [3.1 From the Stop of the Exchequer to the Bank of England](03-01-from-the-stop-of-the-exchequer-to-the-bank-of-england.md)

## Why this matters

A Venetian lending to the Monte ([2.3](02-03-the-public-debts-of-city-states.md)) was lending to a government he and his peers sat in. A banker lending to a king was lending to the one person in the realm no court could summon. Yet kings borrowed enormous sums, and families like the Bardi, the Fuggers and the Spinola kept lending to them. This lesson asks the question every later chapter of sovereign debt repeats: when the debtor is above the law, what makes lending rational, and how often was it?

## The idea

Think of lending to a king as lending to a customer who also owns the courthouse. You cannot sue him or seize his palace. You have only three things, and every lender in this lesson used some mix of them:

1. **Grab the cash flow before it reaches him.** Take a tax, a customs duty or a mine as security, and collect it yourself.
2. **Charge for the risk.** Price the chance of a payment stop into the rate, so that the good years pay for the bad ones.
3. **Hold something he needs next year.** A king at war needs cash wired to an army abroad every month. A creditor who can stop that service has a lever no court could give him.

The hazards mirror the tools. Security is itself a royal grant, which the king can revoke. The risk price can be wrong. And the lever only works if lenders act together, because a king cut off by one banker simply borrows from another.

The medieval story is usually told as a cautionary tale: Edward III ruined the great Florentine companies. The early modern story has been rewritten. Philip II of Spain suspended payments four times, and on the best reconstruction his bankers still made money.

## The argument

**The traditional account of royal lending (Sapori's classic reading of the Bardi and Peruzzi, Braudel on Philip II's bankers).**

1. **Kings borrowed for war on revenues they could not forecast.** *In words:* the loan was sized to the campaign, not to the tax base.
2. **When war went badly, kings stopped paying, and no authority could compel them.** *In words:* the only remedy was to ask nicely.
3. **Lenders nonetheless lent again, even after defaults.** *In words:* they kept walking back into the same trap.
4. **So lending to kings was ruinous, and lenders who did it were reckless or trapped by earlier exposure.** *In words:* great banking houses died of royal debt.

**Drelichman and Voth's reassessment of Philip II (*Lending to the Borrower from Hell*, 2014, building on their 2010–11 articles).**

1. **Most of Castile's debt was never defaulted.** Long-term *juros*, secured on taxes the Cortes designated and paid directly by tax collectors, were serviced throughout the sixteenth century. Only short-term *asientos* were suspended. *In words:* the "bankruptcies" hit one slice of the debt.
2. **Asientos were contingent contracts.** Hundreds of them specified what happened if the silver fleet arrived late or a tax fell short. *In words:* many "missed payments" were outcomes the contract had already priced.
3. **Suspensions were liquidity crises, settled fast.** In their reconstruction, Castile ran large primary surpluses and debt service averaged about half of revenue in 1566–96 (peaking near three quarters in 1574). *In words:* the king was short of cash, not broke.
4. **Lenders earned a premium in normal times that more than covered the haircuts.** Across 1566–1600 their benchmark estimate of bankers' annualized return, after the settlements, is about 15 percent, against 7.14 percent on juros; even assuming total repudiation, returns stay positive. *In words:* the defaults were the insurance the premium paid for.
5. **The Genoese lenders acted as a coalition.** Loans were shared among families, and a banker who broke a lending moratorium could be cheated in turn by the others. The king's fear of losing transfer services to his armies made him settle. *In words:* the enforcement was the bankers' cohesion, not a court or a gunboat.
6. **So Philip was a serial defaulter whose debts were sustainable, and lending to him was a rational, profitable business.**

Their estimates are reconstructions from contract archives, built on assumptions about discount rates, revenue and output, and not every historian of Castilian finance accepts them. Whether Philip's borrowing was *reckless or sustainable* is Module 2's boss question; here you need the argument's shape.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean): reading a settlement.** In 1575 Philip suspended payments; the *medio general* of 1577 repaid bankers, on average, about 62 percent of outstanding asientos, mostly in juros backed by new taxes, and the bankers lent a fresh 5 million ducats. Terms tracked collateral. Drelichman and Voth report recoveries of 70 percent for loans secured on ordinary juros, 55 percent for juros on the Casa de la Contratación, and 42 percent unsecured.

Take three bankers each owed 100,000 ducats. They receive 70,000, 55,000 and 42,000 ducats of face value. The first rule of lending to kings is visible at once: whoever had a claim on a *specific* revenue did best.

Now the second rule: face value is not value. Settlement juros carried 5 percent, while ordinary juros yielded 7.14 percent. Suppose, for illustration, the settlement juros were perpetuities and the market valued them at a 7.14 percent yield. Each ducat of face is then worth

$$\frac{0.05}{0.0714} \approx 0.700 \text{ ducats,}$$

where $0.05$ is the coupon rate and $0.0714$ the yield. The secured banker's 70,000 of face is worth about $0.70 \times 0.700 \approx 0.49$ of his claim. *In words:* the headline haircut was 30 percent, but the present-value haircut on this illustrative valuation was about 51 percent. The same gap between nominal and present-value haircuts returns in 1989 and 2012 ([5.4](05-04-from-mexico-1982-to-the-brady-plan.md), [6.3](06-03-holdouts-and-the-law-of-sovereign-restructuring.md)).

**Example 2 (hard): when the collateral is a royal grant.** The Fuggers did not lend Charles V his election money on trust. Over the following years they took repayment largely in Spanish assets they administered themselves, including the lease of the military orders' estates (*maestrazgos*) and the Almadén mercury mines. That is tool 1 in pure form, and it looks sturdier than Edward III's customs assignments.

It strains in two places. First, a lease exists because the king grants it. In 1557 the suspension reached the German bankers, the Fuggers among them, and by most accounts the settlement of the inherited debts dragged on for years and was partly paid by leasing them further Crown revenues. The collateral bought priority in a renegotiation, not immunity from one. Second, whether the 1519 money *bought* anything is itself disputed. H. J. Cohn (2001) argued that payments to electors were customary and did not decide the vote. If so, the loan financed the ritual cost of an election already leaning Habsburg, and the lender's leverage over Charles was smaller than the famous figures suggest.

The general point: every tool against a sovereign is ultimately something the sovereign allows. Lenders never escaped that; they priced it, spread it, and organized around it.

## Watch out

- **Anachronism trap: a king's "bankruptcy" was no bankruptcy.** No court, trustee or discharge existed. Philip issued a decree suspending payments, and then negotiated. Calling 1575 a bankruptcy imports a legal process ([4.2](04-02-debtors-prisons-and-the-invention-of-bankruptcy.md)) that sovereigns still lack today ([6.3](06-03-holdouts-and-the-law-of-sovereign-restructuring.md)).
- **You might think a default means lenders lost money.** Profit is measured over the whole relationship. A 20 percent cut in 1597 on loans that had paid well above the juro rate for decades can leave the lender ahead; Drelichman and Voth's claim is exactly that.
- **You might think Villani's figures settle the Bardi and Peruzzi story.** Giovanni Villani reported that Edward owed the Bardi 900,000 florins and the Peruzzi 600,000. Villani had been a Peruzzi partner, and Edwin Hunt (1990; *The Medieval Super-Companies*, 1994) argued the companies lacked the capital to lend on that scale, that the sums likely include promised interest and rewards, and that some recovery came through channels outside the English ledgers. The causal role of Edward's default is contested, not settled.
- **You might think the 60 percent debt-to-GDP figure is a sixteenth-century fact.** It is a modern reconstruction; no one in 1575 measured GDP.

## One-liner

> A king cannot be sued, so his lenders collected his revenues directly, priced his suspensions into the rate and stuck together, and whether that made lending to kings ruinous or profitable is exactly what historians dispute.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢) *(Exegetical.)*** From an invented op-ed:

> "The republic has now defaulted four times in a single generation. It is the borrower from hell, a modern Philip II, and history's verdict is clear: serial defaulters ruin the fools who lend to them. Any investor who buys its new bonds is repeating the Genoese bankers' folly and deserves what follows."

(a) Name the historical analogy doing the work and the premise about Philip II's lenders that the argument needs. (b) Name two features of the sixteenth-century case that bear on whether that premise holds, and one feature of modern bond markets that the analogy does not carry over. No verdict on the republic's bonds or on Philip II's creditworthiness. 150 words or fewer.

**P2 (🟡) *(Formal (a)–(c) · Exegetical (d).)*** A Genoese banker advances 100,000 ducats on an asiento. The contract repays 115,000 ducats in 12 months out of the silver fleet. **Contingency clause:** if the fleet is late, payment is postponed 6 months and 1 percent per month simple interest accrues on the 115,000. Three mutually exclusive outcomes: fleet on time (probability 0.65); fleet late, no suspension (0.25); a general suspension, settled after 24 months at 70 percent of the 115,000 (0.10).

(a) Compute the banker's annualized return in each outcome. (b) Discounting at the juro yield of 7.14 percent a year, compute the expected present value of the contract and its expected profit per ducat lent. (c) Holding the probabilities fixed, what recovery rate in the suspension outcome would make the expected present value exactly 100,000? (d) In one sentence, why would Drelichman and Voth not count the late-fleet outcome as a default? State rounding.

**P3 (🔴, optional) *(Exegetical.)*** The traditional account says Edward III's default destroyed the Bardi and Peruzzi; Hunt says the companies' failures had other principal causes. Name the single premise the traditional account needs and Hunt denies, and say what two kinds of archival evidence would move the dispute. 150 words or fewer.

<details>
<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical — strict.)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** the analogy is Philip II as serial defaulter; the needed premise is that his lenders lost money (were ruined or irrational) because of his defaults.

**Must hit, strict (b):** any two of: Drelichman and Voth estimate lenders earned a return (about 15 percent benchmark) well above the juro rate after settlements; the defaults hit only short-term asientos while juros were serviced; many contracts were contingent, so stops were partly priced; settlements came within one to two years. One modern disanalogy: bondholders are dispersed and anonymous, with no family coalition able to cut off a sovereign's transfer services, so the enforcement mechanism does not carry over (acceptable alternatives: modern restructuring law and collective action clauses; different default costs).

**Wrong turns:** declaring the bonds a good or bad buy; treating "four defaults" as proof of insolvency without separating liquidity from solvency; offering the 2(d) verdict on Philip.

**Model answer:** The op-ed leans on Philip II, and needs the premise that his serial defaults ruined his lenders. The best reconstruction cuts against that premise as stated: Drelichman and Voth estimate that asiento lenders earned about 15 percent a year even after the 1577 and 1597 settlements, roughly double the juro rate, and the suspensions touched only short-term debt while juros kept paying. Default risk was priced in, not ignored. What does not carry over is the enforcement: Philip's lenders were a small Genoese coalition who could jointly cut off the transfers his armies needed; the republic's bondholders are dispersed and have no such lever.

---

**P2** *(Formal (a)–(c) · Exegetical (d).)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** on time: $115{,}000/100{,}000 - 1 = 15\%$ over 1 year. Late: payment $115{,}000 \times (1 + 6 \times 0.01) = 121{,}900$ at 1.5 years, annualized $1.219^{1/1.5} - 1 \approx 14.11\%$. Suspension: $0.70 \times 115{,}000 = 80{,}500$ at 2 years, annualized $0.805^{1/2} - 1 \approx -10.28\%$.

**Must hit, strict (b):** present values at $1.0714^t$: $115{,}000/1.0714 \approx 107{,}336$; $121{,}900/1.0714^{1.5} \approx 109{,}920$; $80{,}500/1.0714^{2} \approx 70{,}128$. Expected PV $= 0.65(107{,}336) + 0.25(109{,}920) + 0.10(70{,}128) \approx 104{,}261$ ducats. Expected profit $\approx 4{,}261$, about 0.043 ducats per ducat lent (4.3 percent) above the juro alternative.

**Must hit, strict (c):** the first two outcomes contribute $69{,}769 + 27{,}480 = 97{,}249$. Need $0.10 \times x \times 115{,}000 / 1.0714^2 = 2{,}751$, so $x \approx 0.275$: a recovery of about 27.5 percent (a haircut of about 72.5 percent) breaks even.

**Must hit, strict (d):** the postponement and its interest were written into the contract as a verifiable contingency, so paying late on those terms is performance, not breach.

**Wrong turns:** compounding the 1 percent monthly (the clause is simple interest); forgetting to discount the late and suspension payments for their longer timing; averaging the three annualized returns instead of the cash flows.

**Model answer:** as above. Rounding: returns to 0.01 percentage point, ducats to the nearest ducat, recovery to 0.1 percent.

---

**P3** *(Exegetical — strict on naming one premise.)*

**Must hit, strict:**
- The premise: that the companies' unrecovered losses on loans to Edward III were large relative to their capital and deposits, enough by themselves to make them insolvent. The traditional account needs it; Hunt denies it (the firms could not have lent on Villani's scale, and some advances were recovered off the royal ledgers).
- Evidence: (1) the companies' own surviving account books and bankruptcy liquidation records, showing capital, deposits and the claims actually listed against the English crown; (2) English Exchequer and customs records of assignments, wool licences and repayments, including partial payments after 1345. Timing of depositor withdrawals relative to the Florentine and Neapolitan crises is an acceptable substitute for either.

**Wrong turns:** naming "whether Edward defaulted" (both sides agree he stopped paying); naming two premises; citing Villani's figures as decisive, since his reliability is part of what is disputed.

**Model answer:** The dispute turns on size: were the losses on English crown loans, net of what was recovered, big enough relative to the Bardi's and Peruzzi's capital to sink them on their own? The traditional story, following Villani, says yes. Hunt says no: the firms lacked the resources to lend Villani's sums, and they fell amid Florence's crisis of 1342–43 and depositor flight. The companies' account books and liquidation records, set against English Exchequer and customs records of what was actually paid, would move it.

## Flashback

**From Lesson 2.2 (Jewish lenders and the Monti di Pietà):** *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)* Take three actions of Philip IV of France as given: in 1291, and again in 1311, the crown arrested Italian ("Lombard") bankers resident in France and freed them on payment of large sums, while continuing to employ Italian financiers; in 1306 it expelled the Jews of France, seized their goods, and had its own officials collect the debts that had been owed to them. (a) 2.2 described the licensed lender's position as one institution made of a license, a tax and a threat. Say which element each action instantiates. (b) Does the treatment of the Lombards show that the structure had nothing to do with religious status? Name one feature the two groups shared and one that did not carry across. Two sentences or fewer per part.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):** the arrests-for-release-payments are the **tax collected through the threat** — an extraction on lenders whose security rests on the ruler, not on any court they could use against him, with the crown's continued employment of Italian financiers as the **license** that keeps the arrangement worth re-entering. The 1306 expulsion is the **threat carried out**, and the crown's taking over collection of the debts shows what the licensed lender really was fiscally: an intermediary standing between the ruler and the debtors, so that seizing the lender hands the ruler the claims themselves (2.2's step 4).

**Must hit, any verdict (b):** one genuine shared feature — neither group's position rested on local custom, guild membership or corporate standing, so both were useful *because* they were extractable, and in both cases the ruler's demands were passed on as severity toward borrowers. And one feature that does not carry: the Lombards' home cities and networks survived the squeeze, so they could leave, be re-invited, and price the risk, whereas Jewish lenders faced legal disabilities that closed the alternatives and an expulsion that was permanent and total; the canon-law ban is also why the function existed for them in the first place, and hostile preaching gave the threat a religious idiom the Lombards never faced. Either verdict passes if both moves are made.

**Wrong turns:** concluding that the arrangement was not discriminatory because Christian bankers were squeezed too (the shared fiscal logic and the unequal exit options are separate claims); reading the expulsion as purely fiscal with no reference to the legal position 2.2 sets out; or the reverse, denying the fiscal motive that the seizure of the debt claims makes plain.

**Model answer:** (a) The arrests and ransoms are the tax, levied through the threat, on lenders whose protection is the king's to withdraw; the crown's continued use of Italian financiers is the license. The expulsion is the threat executed, and taking over the debts reveals the lender as a collector standing between crown and debtors. (b) Shared: neither group had standing independent of the ruler, so both were fiscally attractive precisely because they could be squeezed, and both passed that pressure on to borrowers. Not shared: the Lombards could withdraw to their own cities and return on new terms, while Jewish lenders were excluded from the land and crafts that were the alternative, and their removal was final. The fiscal structure generalizes; the trap does not.

</details>
