# A History of Debt · Lesson 3.3: The South Sea bubble

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 3: The financial revolutions · Builds on: [3.2 Did 1688 make Britain creditworthy?](03-02-did-1688-make-britain-creditworthy.md) · Unlocks: [3.4 Hume's prophecy: carrying a great debt](03-04-humes-prophecy-carrying-a-great-debt.md)

## Why this matters

The most famous financial mania in English history was, underneath, a plan to restructure the national debt. In 1720 the South Sea Company offered to swap government annuities for its own shares, and London and Paris ran the same experiment at the same moment: turn the state's creditors into shareholders of a company that holds the state's debt. Both schemes blew up. The episode gave us the word "bubble" and the Victorian image of a crowd gone mad, and it still divides historians over whether the buyers were fools. For this course it matters for a plainer reason. It is a debt conversion, and you can ask of it what you ask of any restructuring: who took the loss, and what did the state keep?

## The idea

By 1719 Britain owed roughly 50 million pounds, a legacy of the wars after 1688 ([3.1](03-01-from-the-stop-of-the-exchequer-to-the-bank-of-england.md)). About 15 million of it was **irredeemable**: long annuities sold in the 1690s and 1700s that paid a fixed sum every year for up to 99 years. The government could not pay them off early or cut their rate. Market rates had fallen, and the Treasury was stuck with contracts it could not touch without the holders' consent.

How do you get consent? Offer the holders something they want more. The South Sea Company, founded by Harley in 1711 to absorb about 9 to 10 million pounds of short-term war debt in return for 6 percent interest and a trading monopoly on Spanish America, proposed a swap. Annuitants would hand their annuities to the Company and receive Company stock. The government would pay the Company a 5 percent annuity on the debt it took in, falling to 4 percent after 1727 and redeemable thereafter. The debt would become one flexible, reducible block.

The trick lay in the exchange rate. Parliament let the Company add stock worth, at par, 20 years' purchase for each long annuity it took in: £2,000 of stock per £100 a year. But the Company, not Parliament, set the terms offered to annuitants, and it offered stock *valued at the market price*. If a £100 share traded at 350, an annuitant could be given a generous-looking package using far fewer than 20 shares, and the Company kept the rest to sell for cash. **The higher the share price, the more of the new stock the Company kept.** Every insider had a reason to want the price up, and the Company lent money on its own shares and sold new shares for small deposits to help it get there.

Across the Channel, John Law had built the model. His bank (1716, made royal at the end of 1718) issued paper money, and his Mississippi Company, merged into the Compagnie des Indes in 1719, took over the French royal debt in exchange for shares. Those shares rose from 500 livres to about 10,000.

## Source

Charles Mackay fixed the popular reading in 1841. From the preface to his 1852 edition of *Memoirs of Extraordinary Popular Delusions*:

> Men, it has been well said, think in herds; it will be seen that they go mad in herds, while they only recover their senses slowly, and one by one.

Mackay was a journalist collecting anecdotes, and historians now treat many of his South Sea stories as unverified. The sentence states the thesis, not the evidence for it. Notice "go mad": the claim is about the buyers' psychology, not about prices, company terms or the debt.

## The argument

**The scheme as its designers argued it** (Blunt, the Company director who drafted it, and the ministers who carried it through Parliament):

1. **The irredeemables are a burden the state cannot lift by law.** *In words:* the government is paying yesterday's high rates on contracts it has no right to redeem.
2. **Holders will give them up voluntarily if offered more than their market value.** *In words:* conversion needs a carrot, because a stick would be a default.
3. **A rising share price lets the Company offer that premium at no cost to the state.** *In words:* stock valued at 350 pays for generosity that no Treasury could afford in cash.
4. **The Company can share the gain with the state.** It bid against the Bank of England and promised a cash payment of about 4.2 million pounds plus sums per annuity converted, some 7 million in all if everything was converted. *In words:* the taxpayer gets paid for letting the Company do the conversion.
5. **So the debt becomes cheaper, redeemable and consolidated, and everyone gains.** *In words:* creditors get a premium, the state gets flexibility and cash, and the Company gets the spread.

Premise 3 is the load-bearing one. It makes everyone's gain depend on a price that the scheme itself was pushing up.

**Two readings of the price.**

- **Mania** (Mackay 1841, and in modern form Kindleberger's *Manias, Panics and Crashes*, 1978): buyers bid shares far above any value the Company's trade or its government annuity could support, carried along by the crowd. Dale, Johnson and Tang (2005) compare the prices of South Sea shares with those of linked instruments such as subscription receipts and argue that the pricing was hard to reconcile with rational behavior. Shea (2007) disputes their method.
- **Rational, or rationally ridden** (Garber, *Famous First Bubbles*, 2000; Temin and Voth, 2004): Garber argues that buyers were betting on a scheme that looked plausible at the time, and that Law's system seemed to be working when they bought. He is sceptical of Mackay-style anecdotes as evidence. Temin and Voth use the trading records of Hoare's Bank. On their reading it knew a bubble was under way, bought anyway, and profited, so informed investors rationally *rode* the bubble instead of attacking it. The formal idea of a rational bubble is in [grad-macro 3.3](../../grad-macro/lessons/03-03-money-rational-bubbles.md).

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean): what the first debt subscription offered.** Take a holder of a 99-year annuity paying £100 a year. On 19 May 1720 the terms came out, from Velde's Table 6: 32 years' purchase, so a nominal £3,200. It was paid as £700 of stock valued at 375, worth $7 \times 375 = 2{,}625$ pounds, plus £575 in bonds and cash. Check: $2{,}625 + 575 = 3{,}200$.

What was that worth in the market that day? Stock traded near 352, so the package was worth $7 \times 352 + 575 = 3{,}039$ pounds. The annuity itself traded at about 29 years' purchase, £2,900. The swap paid a premium of about 4.8 percent. The break-even share price $P$ solves $7P + 575 = 2{,}900$, giving $P \approx 332$.

Now look from the Company's side. Parliament let it add £2,000 of stock for this annuity. It gave the holder £700, so it kept **£1,300 of nominal stock**, worth about $13 \times 352 = 4{,}576$ pounds at that price. The state paid the Company 5 percent on £2,000, which is £100 a year, the same as before until 1727 and £80 after. **The premium to creditors and the Company's profit both came from the share price, not from the Treasury.**

**Example 2 (hard): where the two readings actually divide.** Both camps accept the price path. Both accept that the price far exceeded what the Company's trade could earn. The mania reading infers that buyers misjudged value. The riding reading infers that informed buyers judged correctly that *others* would keep buying for a while. So the crux is not whether prices were too high. It is **whether the marginal buyer expected to sell to someone else before the fall, and could reasonably expect to**.

That premise decides what counts as evidence. Anecdotes about foolish buyers (Mackay) fit both readings, since a rider needs fools to sell to. Trading records of identifiable investors (Temin and Voth's Hoare's Bank) show that *some* buyers were riders, not that the market as a whole was. Price comparisons across linked securities (Dale, Johnson and Tang; Shea's reply) are the most direct test, because a rational market should not price the same claim two ways. That is why their dispute is over method.

The episode also muddles "irrational." The debt subscribers of April 1720 handed over their annuities without knowing the terms, and their stock was not issued until October, when the price was far below its summer peak. Their losses came from the *contract*, whatever their psychology.

**What the state kept.** Velde (2025) finds that the cash payment was remitted, partly in 1721 and the rest soon after, so the government made little or nothing in cash. The immediate interest saving was trivial. What stuck was structural. About 12 million pounds of formerly irredeemable annuities became part of a redeemable South Sea annuity, cut to 4 percent in 1727 and later to 3.5 and 3 percent. Velde puts the ex post gain at about 1.8 million pounds in present value. He also follows Dickson (1967) in calling the 1720–21 crisis the closest Britain came in that century to repudiating its obligations. That is a qualification of [3.2](03-02-did-1688-make-britain-creditworthy.md)'s credible-commitment story, not a verdict on it.

## Watch out

- **"£1,000" is a price per £100 of stock, not a share count.** A rise from 130 to 1,000 is roughly a 670 percent gain. Converted annuitants were paid in *nominal* stock at a stated valuation, so always ask "valued at what price?"
- **You might think the annuitants got rich at the peak.** The first debt subscribers took stock valued at 375, and it was issued in October 1720. The "value" of their package at 1,000 was never something they could collect.
- **Anachronism trap:** "Ponzi scheme," "bailout" and "irrational exuberance" are modern labels. Velde calls the cash flows Ponzi-like *ex post*, which is a finding about who paid whom. It is not a claim that the directors planned a fraud from the start, and that question is still argued.
- **You might repeat Newton's line about calculating the heavens but not the madness of people.** It is not found in anything Newton wrote and comes from later anecdote. That he lost money in 1720 is better attested.

## One-liner

> The South Sea bubble was a debt swap priced in shares: the higher the stock, the sweeter the terms for creditors and the bigger the Company's spread, until the price that paid for everything fell and Parliament had to decide who ate the loss.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢) *(Formal.)*** A holder of £100 a year in a 1710 lottery annuity (a short annuity) joined the first debt subscription. His terms (Velde 2025, Table 6) were £400 of stock valued at 375 plus £200 in bonds and cash. On 19 May 1720 short annuities traded at about 15.69 years' purchase and stock at about 352. (a) Show that the terms equal 17 years' purchase. (b) What was the package worth at 352, and at what share price would it just match the annuity's market value? (c) What was it worth at 150, the stock value Parliament used in 1721? (d) Parliament let the Company add stock equal to 14 years' purchase for short annuities. How much nominal stock did the Company keep, and what was it worth at 352? Show arithmetic and round to the nearest pound.

**P2 (🟡) *(Exegetical (a) · Exegetical (b).)*** Mackay, describing the companies floated in 1720 (*Memoirs of Extraordinary Popular Delusions*, text of the 1852 edition), writes that one company

> which shewed, more completely than any other, the utter madness of the people, was one started by an unknown adventurer, entitled "A company for carrying on an undertaking of great advantage, but nobody to know what it is."

He adds that the fact is attested by "scores of credible witnesses," and names none. (a) What conclusion does Mackay draw, and what evidence does the passage give for it? (b) Suppose the anecdote is true. Which reading of 1720, mania or riding, does it bear on, and why does it bear only weakly on the price of *South Sea* stock? 150 words or fewer in total.

**P3 (🔴, optional) *(Exegetical.)*** From an invented op-ed: "The finance minister wants to swap the country's bonds for shares in a new national investment fund, promising holders a premium. We have seen this before. It is the South Sea Company again, and it will end the same way." Name the historical analogy doing the work. Identify two features of the 1720 scheme the analogy depends on, and say for each whether the proposal as described shares it or whether you would need more information. 150 words or fewer.

<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Formal.)*

(a) Stock: $4 \times 375 = 1{,}500$ pounds. Plus bonds and cash: $1{,}500 + 200 = 1{,}700 = 17 \times 100$ pounds, so 17 years' purchase.

(b) Market value of the annuity: $15.69 \times 100 = 1{,}569$ pounds. Package at 352: $4 \times 352 + 200 = 1{,}608$ pounds, a premium of 39 pounds (about 2.5 percent). Break-even: $4P + 200 = 1{,}569 \Rightarrow P = 1{,}369/4 = 342.25 \approx 342$.

(c) At 150: $4 \times 150 + 200 = 800$ pounds, about 51 percent of the annuity's May value.

(d) The Company was allowed $14 \times 100 = 1{,}400$ pounds of stock and gave £400, so it kept **£1,000** of nominal stock. At 352 that is $10 \times 352 = 3{,}520$ pounds.

**Wrong turns:** multiplying nominal stock by 352 without dividing by 100 (the price is per £100); valuing the package at 375 instead of the market price; forgetting the £200 in bonds and cash.

---

**P2** *(Exegetical, strict.)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** the conclusion is about the buyers' psychology, "the utter madness of the people." The evidence is a single unnamed promoter and a company with no stated purpose. Its authority rests on unnamed "credible witnesses," with no document or source cited. It is an anecdote offered as proof of a general state of mind.

**Must hit, strict (b):** it bears on the **mania** reading, as evidence that some buyers paid for nothing identifiable. It is weak evidence about South Sea stock for two reasons. First, the fringe "bubble companies" were a different market from the Company's shares, which rested on a real government annuity and a real conversion. Second, even real fools fit the riding reading, because riders need buyers to sell to.

**Wrong turns:** treating the anecdote as settled fact, or as refuted, when the task is to weigh what it could show if true; answering "mania, obviously" without separating fringe companies from the Company's stock.

**Model answer:** (a) Mackay infers a crowd-wide madness from one case: a promoter who sold shares in an undisclosed venture. His support is unnamed witnesses, so the passage asserts a psychology and does not document it. (b) If true, it supports the mania reading for the fringe flotations. But the South Sea Company held a genuine government annuity and ran a real conversion, so gullibility in the bubble companies does not show that its stock was mispriced. And a market full of gullible buyers is exactly what Temin and Voth's riders would exploit, so the anecdote cannot separate the two readings.

---

**P3** *(Exegetical, strict.)*

**Must hit, strict:** the analogy is 1720 as a **debt-for-equity conversion that relies on a rising share price** and collapses. Two load-bearing features, each checked against the proposal:
- **The premium is paid in shares valued at a price the scheme itself pushes up.** The op-ed does not say how the fund's shares are priced or whether they trade. You need more information.
- **The terms are set after holders commit** (the April 1720 blank cheque). The op-ed does not say.
- Also acceptable: **the vehicle lends against its own shares or sells shares on small deposits**; **the issuer keeps the unissued stock and profits from the price.** Neither is stated.

A good answer notes that "a premium for swapping bonds into equity" is shared, and that this feature alone is not what failed in 1720.

**Wrong turns:** judging whether the policy is wise, which the task does not ask; listing features of 1720 (the Bubble Act, Walpole) that the analogy does not depend on.

**Model answer:** The analogy is the South Sea conversion, where creditors swapped annuities for shares and the premium depended on a share price the scheme inflated. The proposal shares the premium-for-equity structure. Whether it shares the dangerous parts is not stated: (1) whether the fund's shares are valued at a market price that the operation itself can drive, and (2) whether holders commit before the terms are fixed, as the 1720 annuitants did. Without those, the analogy establishes resemblance, not the same outcome.

## Flashback

**From Lesson 3.1 (From the Stop of the Exchequer to the Bank of England):** *(Formal (a)–(b) · Exegetical (c).)* Illustrative figures. A creditor holds 1,000 pounds of Exchequer Orders on which 6 percent had been promised and on which nothing has been paid for years. Parliament offers two settlements, each a perpetual annuity charged on a duty it has voted for the purpose: **A** writes the principal down to 500 pounds and pays 6 percent on it; **B** keeps the principal at 1,000 pounds and pays 2 percent on it. Comparable funded annuities then yield 4 percent. (a) Give each offer's annual payment and its capital value at 4 percent. (b) Which offer cuts the principal by more, which leaves the creditor further below his 1,000 pounds of face, and which costs the Exchequer more a year? (c) In one sentence, name one thing that makes either settlement a better claim than the Orders it replaces, which none of this arithmetic shows. Show your arithmetic.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):** value a perpetuity at $P = C/r$ using the **market** yield, not the stated rate.
- A: $0.06 \times 500 = 30$ pounds a year; $30/0.04 = 750$ pounds.
- B: $0.02 \times 1{,}000 = 20$ pounds a year; $20/0.04 = 500$ pounds.

**Must hit, strict (b):** A cuts the principal by half and B not at all, so A has the larger nominal write-down. But A is worth 750 against 1,000 of face, a loss of 25 percent, while B is worth 500, a loss of 50 percent, so B leaves the creditor further down. And A costs the Exchequer 30 pounds a year against B's 20. The offer with the loud headline write-down is the dearer one for the state and the better one for the creditor: a cut in principal and a cut in value can rank in opposite directions.

**Must hit, strict (c):** any one of 3.1's three structural features — the annuity is charged on a tax Parliament voted and appropriated to it, so altering it takes a statute rather than a Treasury order; it is perpetual and assignable, so the holder leaves by selling instead of waiting on the Crown's will; a chartered body of creditors can refuse the next loan. The rate says nothing about who controls the revenue behind it.

**Wrong turns:** pricing each annuity at its own stated rate (which gives 500 and 1,000 and inverts the answer); reading A as the better offer because 6 beats 2; valuing the suspended promise as a live claim worth $60/0.04 = 1{,}500$.

**Model answer:** (a) A pays 30 pounds a year and is worth $30/0.04 = 750$; B pays 20 and is worth $20/0.04 = 500$. (b) A halves the principal and B touches it not at all, yet A is 25 percent below face in value and B is 50 percent below, and A costs the Exchequer 30 a year against 20. The big headline cut is the generous offer. (c) Both are charged on a tax voted and appropriated by Parliament and payable forever, so the security is a revenue stream the holder can watch and sell rather than a claim the Treasury can simply stop — which is the whole difference between the Orders of 1672 and the funded debt, and no yield shows it.
