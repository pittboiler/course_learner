# A History of Debt — Syllabus

> Politics & Society · Tier 1 · ~29 lessons · Prereqs: none · Roadmap id: `history-of-debt`

## Goal

This course follows debt as a political institution, from Mesopotamian loan tablets and royal debt cancellations to Greek bailouts and holdout lawsuits. The questions come back again and again: who may bind whom through debt, what happens when debtors cannot pay, and who decides who takes the loss. You will finish able to explain how credit, bondage and relief worked in the ancient world; how merchants, cities and kings borrowed under usury prohibitions; how the Dutch and English financial revolutions made public debt a normal tool of states; how debt built nations, bound freedpeople and served empires; and how the twentieth and twenty-first centuries tried to settle debts too large to pay: reparations, the 1980s crisis, Jubilee 2000, 2008, the Eurozone, and sovereign restructuring without a court. It is the entry point of the **debt thread**. `philosophy-of-debt` asks what is owed and whether forgiveness is just. `theology-of-debt` reads the Jubilee, "forgive us our debts" and the usury doctrine from inside the Catholic tradition. `economics-of-debt` builds the models of default, overhang and debt-deflation. This course supplies the episodes all three argue about. It deliberately skips the moral arguments, the theology and the formal models (see the table), plus monetary history for its own sake (the gold standard appears only where a debt turns on it), corporate finance, and credit traditions outside the Western and imperial line. Islamic commercial law, and Chinese and Indian credit history, are named as gaps, not covered.

## How practice works in this course

Problems follow [HUMANITIES-BUILD-BRIEF.md](../../HUMANITIES-BUILD-BRIEF.md). Every problem part declares its kind, and every solution is a rubric (must-hit moves, wrong turns) plus a model answer. The course leans on four archetypes:

- **Close reading (archetype 7), exegetical.** This is the workhorse. Every passage used is public domain: Plutarch's *Life of Solon* (Perrin, 1914, or Dryden–Clough), the Aristotelian *Constitution of Athens* (Kenyon, 1891), Livy (Roberts, 1905), the Laws of Hammurabi (King, 1910), Deuteronomy and Leviticus (Douay–Rheims), Hume's *Of Public Credit* (1752), Smith's *Wealth of Nations* (1776), Hamilton's *First Report on Public Credit* (1790), Dickens, Cromer's *Modern Egypt* (1908) against Blunt's *Secret History of the English Occupation of Egypt* (1907), Keynes's *Economic Consequences of the Peace* (1919; public domain in the US), and US federal court opinions (*Bailey v. Alabama*, the *NML v. Argentina* opinions). Lessons may translate short Latin, Greek or French passages (the Twelve Tables, the 1825 Haiti ordinance) themselves. Modern historians and modern translations of cuneiform texts are paraphrased, never quoted at length.
- **Find the crux (archetype 5), exegetical.** Nearly every episode here has a live historiographical dispute, and the course treats each one as territory to map, not a verdict to deliver. How general were ancient debt cancellations? Did 1688 make Britain creditworthy? Was debt the cause or the pretext of the 1882 occupation of Egypt? Did gunboats or reputation make countries repay? Was 2008 a subprime crisis or a middle-class one?
- **Diagnose (archetype 4), exegetical.** Invented op-eds and speeches about debt forgiveness, austerity or Chinese lending, where the task is to name the historical analogy doing the work and say where it breaks. Invented quotations are never attributed to real people.
- **Formal model (archetype 8), formal.** Light arithmetic that recurs through the course: compounding and doubling times (1.1), the implicit interest in a bill of exchange (2.1), the debt-to-GDP identity (3.4), annuities and present values for indemnities and reparations (4.4, 5.1), and nominal vs present-value haircuts (5.4, 6.2, 6.3). Where a model stands behind the arithmetic, the problem cites `economics-of-debt`.

Evaluative parts (was Solon's reform a clean slate? was the Brady Plan a success?) are verdict-neutral. Rubrics grade the moves (use the evidence, state the strongest rival reading) and never the conclusion. The same holds for the live political questions (forgiveness, austerity, bailouts): the course reports the positions at full strength and takes none.

## Dangerous Checklist

When you finish, you can:

- [ ] Explain how interest-bearing credit and debt bondage worked in Mesopotamia, Athens and Rome, and say exactly which debts a royal edict, Solon's reform or the Roman abolition of *nexum* did and did not cancel
- [ ] Read a public-domain source on a debt episode (Plutarch, Livy, Hamilton, Keynes, a court opinion) and say which reading the text supports and which words decide it
- [ ] Explain how medieval and early modern merchants, cities and kings borrowed under usury prohibitions (partnerships, bills of exchange, funded civic debt, pawnshops) and why lending to kings was so hazardous
- [ ] State precisely what the credible-commitment thesis about 1688 claims, and name the evidence its critics bring against it
- [ ] Compute a debt-to-GDP path from the accounting identity, and say whether a historical debt burden shrank through surpluses, growth or inflation
- [ ] Trace how debt kept binding persons after bondage was formally abolished (*nexum*, the crop lien, peonage), and name the legal instruments that ended it or failed to
- [ ] Explain how imprisonment for debt gave way to bankruptcy with a discharge, and why that shift was political as well as legal
- [ ] Analyze a case of debt as an instrument of foreign control (Haiti, Egypt, the Caribbean receiverships), and weigh debt-as-cause against debt-as-pretext explanations
- [ ] Explain why German reparations failed, lay out the Keynes–Mantoux dispute, and compare how the 1953 London Agreement, the Brady Plan and HIPC each settled unpayable debts
- [ ] Explain why no sovereign bankruptcy court exists, and how collective action clauses, holdout litigation and official-creditor frameworks try to fill the gap
- [ ] Take two historians' explanations of a debt episode, name the single premise they divide on, and say what evidence would move it
- [ ] Take a contemporary argument about debt (forgiveness, austerity, Chinese lending), name the historical analogy it relies on, and say where the analogy breaks

## Modules

### Module 1: Ancient debt: bondage and clean slates

Credit is older than coinage. So is the political problem of debtors losing their land and their children, and so are the rulers who periodically wiped the slate.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1.1 | Credit before coins | Explain how Mesopotamian palaces, temples and merchants lent at interest, and test the claim that credit came before barter | silver and barley as units of account, interest-bearing loans, compounding and doubling time (**formal**), the barter myth and Graeber's historical claims |
| 1.2 | Debt bondage and the royal clean slate | Say what an Old Babylonian debt-release edict cancelled, and why historians disagree about how general such edicts were | pledging family members, the three-year limit in Hammurabi's laws, *misharum* and *andurarum* edicts, consumption vs commercial debts, **find-the-crux** problems |
| 1.3 | Release laws in ancient Israel | Read the biblical release laws as law in their Near Eastern setting, and see how later practice worked around them | the Hebrew slave's seventh year (Exodus 21), the seventh-year release (Deuteronomy 15), the Jubilee (Leviticus 25), *deror* and *andurarum*, Hillel's *prosbul*; the theology is ceded to `theology-of-debt` |
| 1.4 | Solon's shaking-off of burdens | Reconstruct what the *seisachtheia* did in early sixth-century Athens and weigh the ancient sources' rival accounts | *hektemoroi*, mortgage stones (*horoi*), ban on loans secured on the person, Androtion's minimalist reading, **close reading** |
| 1.5 | *Nexum* and the Roman plebs | Explain Roman debt bondage, the plebeian struggle over it, and what its abolition left in place | the Twelve Tables' execution procedure, Livy on the debt crises of the early Republic, the *Lex Poetelia Papiria* (Livy's and Varro's dates differ), *tabulae novae* from Catiline to Caesar |

**Boss problem 1:** Use Plutarch's *Life of Solon* (chapters 13–16, Perrin's public-domain translation) and the *Constitution of Athens* (chapters 6 and 12, Kenyon). (a) Plutarch reports two accounts of the *seisachtheia*: a general cancellation of debts, and Androtion's view that it was relief by lowering interest and revaluing the currency. State each account and say which words in each source support which account. *(Exegetical.)* (b) On Androtion's account, a mina that had counted as 73 drachmas was made to count as 100. For a debt fixed in drachmas, compute the real relief. Then compare it with a full cancellation, and with a Mesopotamian-style edict that cancels a debtor's consumption loans but not his commercial ones, for a stylized debtor with both kinds. *(Formal.)* (c) Name the crux between historians who read ancient debt cancellations as routine royal policy and those who read them as rare, limited concessions, and say what kind of documentary evidence would move it. *(Exegetical.)* (d) In 300–500 words: was Solon's reform a clean slate in the Mesopotamian sense, or a different kind of measure? Any verdict passes if it uses both sources and answers the strongest rival reading. *(Evaluative.)*

### Module 2: Credit under the usury ban

Christian Europe condemned lending at interest, and it also built banking, public debt and the pawnshop. This module covers the institutions that made that possible; the doctrine itself belongs to `theology-of-debt`.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2.1 | Commerce around the prohibition | Explain how medieval merchants financed trade without making a formal loan at interest | *commenda* and *societas* partnerships, the four-party bill of exchange, exchange risk as the lenders' justification, rent contracts (*census*), implicit interest in an exchange round trip (**formal**) |
| 2.2 | Jewish lenders and the Monti di Pietà | Explain the constraints on Jewish moneylending and the Franciscan pawnshops founded partly to replace it | royal licensing and taxation of Jewish lenders, the Statute of the Jewry (1275) and the expulsion from England (1290), Italian lending charters (*condotte*), Christian lenders (Lombards, Cahorsins), the Monte di Pietà of Perugia (1462) and the fight over its fees |
| 2.3 | The public debts of city-states | Explain how Italian republics turned forced loans into tradable, funded public debt, and why they borrowed more cheaply than kings | forced loans (*prestiti*), Venice's Monte (1262), Florence's Monte Comune, Genoa's Casa di San Giorgio (1407), creditors in government, Stasavage's thesis |
| 2.4 | Lending to kings | Explain why sovereign lending was so risky and what lenders did about it | Edward III and the failures of the Bardi and Peruzzi, the Fuggers and Charles V, Philip II's four suspensions and the Genoese bankers, Drelichman and Voth's "borrower from hell" reassessment, **diagnose** problems |

**Boss problem 2:** (a) A Florentine merchant buys a bill payable in Bruges in one currency, and his agent in Bruges uses the proceeds to buy a return bill payable in Florence. Given the two exchange rates and the usance period for each leg (supplied in the problem), compute the merchant's return and annualize it. Then show how the result changes if the Bruges rate moves against him. *(Formal.)* (b) Using (a), explain the feature of the contract that let merchants and contemporary moralists distinguish it from a loan at interest, and say how a "dry exchange" with no real transfer removed that feature. Report the moralists' reasoning; do not judge it (that is `theology-of-debt`). *(Exegetical.)* (c) Florence and Venice borrowed at far lower rates than the kings of England, France and Spain. Name the crux between an explanation from creditors' control of government and an explanation from revenue base and scale, and say what comparison would separate them. *(Exegetical.)* (d) In 300–500 words: was Philip II a reckless borrower or a sustainable one? Any verdict passes if it says what "sustainable" would require and uses evidence about both his revenues and his lenders' returns. *(Evaluative.)*

### Module 3: The financial revolutions

Between the late sixteenth and the mid-eighteenth century, first Holland and then England learned to borrow cheaply, permanently and in public. Contemporaries were sure this would ruin them.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3.1 | From the Stop of the Exchequer to the Bank of England | Trace how England moved from defaulting on its bankers to a funded national debt on the Dutch model | Holland's provincial annuities (*renten*), the Amsterdam Exchange Bank, Charles II's Stop of the Exchequer (1672), the Revolution of 1688, parliamentary appropriation, the Bank of England (1694), the fiscal-military state |
| 3.2 | Did 1688 make Britain creditworthy? | State the credible-commitment thesis precisely and weigh the evidence against it | North and Weingast (1989), interest-rate evidence (Clark; Sussman and Yafeh), partisan-coalition explanations (Stasavage), a crux from the evidence rather than the model (the formal version is in `institutions-and-development`) |
| 3.3 | The South Sea bubble | Explain the debt-for-equity scheme behind 1720, its collapse, and what the state kept | converting government annuities into company stock, John Law's Mississippi System in parallel, the Bubble Act, Walpole's settlement, rational-bubble vs mania readings (the theory is in [`grad-macro`](../grad-macro/syllabus.md) 3.3) |
| 3.4 | Hume's prophecy: carrying a great debt | Compute how a debt ratio evolves, and explain why Britain did not suffer the ruin that Hume and Smith feared | Hume's *Of Public Credit*, Smith on public debts (*Wealth of Nations* V.3), the debt-to-GDP identity (**formal**), primary surplus, growth vs inflation vs repayment, the debt left by the Napoleonic Wars; sustainability theory ceded to `economics-of-debt` |

**Boss problem 3:** (a) Close-read Hume's forecast in *Of Public Credit* of how a nation that keeps mortgaging its revenues must end. State the mechanism he predicts and the alternatives he considers. *(Exegetical.)* (b) Take the identity $d_{t+1} = \frac{1+r}{1+g}\,d_t - s$, where $d$ is debt over GDP, $r$ the interest rate on the debt, $g$ the growth rate and $s$ the primary surplus as a share of GDP. With illustrative values for a debt of twice GDP (given in the problem and labelled illustrative), compute the surplus that holds the ratio constant and the number of years needed to halve it. Then say which parameter Hume implicitly assumed would stay unfavorable. *(Formal; cites `economics-of-debt`.)* (c) Name the crux between North–Weingast and their interest-rate critics, and say what series of prices or rates would move it. *(Exegetical.)* (d) In 300–500 words: was Hume wrong about public debt, or right about a world that changed after he wrote? Any verdict passes if it engages both (a) and (b). *(Evaluative.)*

### Module 4: Debt, nation and empire in the long nineteenth century

Public credit built new nations. Private debt bound people whom slavery's abolition had nominally freed. Sovereign debt gave creditor powers a lever over weaker states.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4.1 | Hamilton's assumption plan | Explain what the 1790 Report proposed, why assumption and funding at par were fought over, and what the settlement bought | the *First Report on Public Credit*, funding at par, the fight over paying original holders rather than speculators (Madison vs Hamilton), assumption of state debts and the Compromise of 1790, the state defaults of the early 1840s as sequel; Jefferson–Madison on intergenerational debt is ceded to `philosophy-of-debt` |
| 4.2 | Debtors' prisons and the invention of bankruptcy | Trace how Anglo-American law moved from jailing debtors to discharging them | the Fleet and the Marshalsea, the Tudor bankruptcy statute for traders, discharge under Queen Anne, Dickens, the Debtors Act (1869), US federal bankruptcy acts from 1800 to the permanent 1898 Act, failure as sin vs failure as misfortune |
| 4.3 | Debt peonage after slavery | Explain how the crop lien and criminal-surety laws bound Black workers after emancipation, and how the courts treated them | sharecropping and the crop lien, the Peonage Act (1867), *Bailey v. Alabama* (1911) and *United States v. Reynolds* (1914), the Ransom–Sutch "lock-in" thesis and its critics, **close reading** of a court opinion |
| 4.4 | Haiti's independence debt | Explain the 1825 indemnity, the loans raised to pay it, and its long fiscal shadow | Charles X's 1825 ordinance (150 million francs, reduced to 90 million in 1838), the "double debt" of indemnity plus loan, recognition bought with debt, annuity arithmetic (**formal**), the US occupation (1915) and its financial background |
| 4.5 | Egypt, the Ottomans, and debt administration | Trace how borrowing led to foreign financial control and then to occupation | Ismail's borrowing and the Suez Canal, the sale of Egypt's canal shares (1875), the Caisse de la Dette (1876), Dual Control, the Ottoman Public Debt Administration (1881), the occupation of 1882, Cromer against Blunt as sources |
| 4.6 | Bondholders, gunboats, and receiverships | Explain how creditors enforced sovereign debts before international institutions existed | the Latin American defaults of the 1820s, bondholder committees and the Council of Foreign Bondholders, the Venezuelan blockade (1902–03), the Drago Doctrine, the Roosevelt Corollary and the Dominican customs receivership, Newfoundland's loss of self-government (1934), gunboats vs reputation (Mitchener–Weidenmier vs Tomz) |

**Boss problem 4:** (a) Reconstruct, in numbered premises, the argument in Hamilton's *First Report on Public Credit* against paying original holders of the Revolutionary debt differently from those who bought it later. *(Exegetical.)* (b) Apply Hamilton's principle to a hard case from this module: the French bankers who held the loan Haiti raised in 1825, or the European holders of Egyptian bonds in 1876. Does "honor the transferable contract at face value" settle what those holders were owed? Say where the principle stops giving a verdict. *(Exegetical, then evaluative.)* (c) Name the crux between the explanation of the 1882 occupation of Egypt that puts bondholders at the center (Hopkins) and the one that puts strategy and a local political crisis at the center (Robinson and Gallagher), and say what archival evidence would move it. *(Exegetical.)* (d) In 300–500 words: in the nineteenth century, did creditor states' force or debtor states' concern for their reputations do more to make debts get paid? Any verdict passes if it uses at least two episodes and states what the other side's best case would predict about them. Note in one sentence which `economics-of-debt` model each side resembles. *(Evaluative.)*

### Module 5: Reparations to Jubilee: the twentieth century

Two world wars, a new international financial order, and three attempts to settle debts that everyone knew would never be paid in full.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5.1 | War debts and reparations | Explain the web of inter-Allied debts and German reparations the peace of 1919 created | inter-Allied war debts owed to the US, Article 231, the London Schedule of Payments (1921) and its A, B and C bonds, the occupation of the Ruhr (1923), Keynes's *Economic Consequences of the Peace*, present value of an annuity (**formal**) |
| 5.2 | Could Germany pay? | Weigh the Keynes–Mantoux dispute over capacity to pay, and trace how reparations and war debts were wound down | the Dawes (1924) and Young (1929) plans, American loans and the circle of payments, the Hoover Moratorium (1931), Lausanne (1932), the default wave of the 1930s, the London Agreement on German External Debts (1953), Mantoux and Marks against Keynes; the transfer problem is ceded to `economics-of-debt` |
| 5.3 | Bretton Woods and the lending boom | Explain what the IMF and World Bank were designed to do, and how the system that replaced Bretton Woods recycled oil money into sovereign loans | Keynes's clearing-union plan vs White's plan, symmetric vs debtor-side adjustment, conditionality and stand-by arrangements, the Paris Club, the end of dollar–gold convertibility (1971), petrodollar recycling and syndicated bank loans |
| 5.4 | From Mexico 1982 to the Brady Plan | Trace the developing-country debt crisis and the shift from new lending to debt reduction | the Volcker rate shock, Mexico's August 1982 announcement, bank exposure and concerted lending, the Baker Plan (1985), liquidity vs solvency, structural adjustment and its critics, the Brady Plan (1989) and Brady bonds, nominal vs present-value haircuts (**formal**) |
| 5.5 | Jubilee 2000 and HIPC | Explain how a religious-rooted campaign and the official creditors produced debt relief for the poorest countries, and the dispute over what it achieved | the HIPC Initiative (1996, enhanced 1999), the Jubilee 2000 campaign and the Birmingham G8 protest (1998), the Multilateral Debt Relief Initiative (2005), odious-debt episodes (Cuba 1898, Sack's doctrine, Iraq after 2003), relief vs moral hazard as the actors argued it; the doctrine is ceded to `philosophy-of-debt`, the Jubilee theology to `theology-of-debt` |

**Boss problem 5:** (a) Close-read the chapter on reparation in Keynes's *Economic Consequences of the Peace*. Reconstruct his argument that the Allied demands exceeded Germany's capacity to pay, and mark which premises are estimates and which are claims about political limits. *(Exegetical.)* (b) Given an annual payment, a horizon and a discount rate (supplied), compute the present value of a reparations schedule. Then show how much of a headline total like the 1921 figure depends on bonds whose service was never scheduled to begin. *(Formal.)* (c) Name the crux between Keynes and his critics (Mantoux, Marks): is it about Germany's economic capacity or its political will? Say what evidence from 1924–31 bears on it. *(Exegetical.)* (d) In 300–500 words: compare the 1953 London Agreement, the Brady Plan and the HIPC Initiative as ways of settling an unpayable debt. What did each concede, to whom, and on what conditions? Any verdict on which design was best passes if the comparison is made on the same criteria for all three. *(Evaluative.)*

### Module 6: Debt in the twenty-first century

Household leverage, a currency union without a fiscal union, sovereign restructuring with no court, new creditors, and the debts of ordinary borrowers.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 6.1 | The American mortgage and 2008 | Trace the institutions of American housing credit to their breaking point, and weigh the rival accounts of who borrowed and why | the Home Owners' Loan Corporation (1933) and the long fixed-rate mortgage, Fannie Mae, securitization, subprime lending, Lehman and the rescues, the subprime thesis (Mian and Sufi) vs the middle-class thesis (Adelino, Schoar and Severino); leverage-cycle models ceded to `economics-of-debt` |
| 6.2 | The Eurozone and Greece | Explain how a currency union without a fiscal union turned a Greek fiscal crisis into a crisis of the euro | the no-bailout clause (Article 125 TFEU), the 2009 deficit revision, the troika programs, the 2012 private-sector restructuring, "whatever it takes" (2012), the 2015 referendum, the US state defaults of the 1840s as a precedent people invoked, austerity as the actors argued it |
| 6.3 | Holdouts and the law of sovereign restructuring | Explain why sovereigns cannot go bankrupt, and what holdout creditors, courts and contract clauses did about it | the proposed Sovereign Debt Restructuring Mechanism, collective action clauses and aggregation, Argentina's 2001 default and exchange offers, *NML Capital v. Argentina* and the pari passu clause, retrofitting clauses by statute (Greece 2012), creditor coordination as the underlying problem |
| 6.4 | New creditors | Explain how lending by China and other new official creditors changed the restructuring problem | policy-bank lending and collateralized loans, hidden debts, the Hambantota port lease and the "debt-trap" dispute (Brautigam's critique), the G20 Common Framework (2020), Zambia's default (2020), comparability of treatment |
| 6.5 | Student and consumer debt | Trace how consumer credit and student lending became mass institutions, and what legal choices shaped them | the general-purpose credit card, *Marquette v. First of Omaha* (1978) and the export of interest-rate caps, the Higher Education Act (1965) and federal student loans, limits on discharging student debt in bankruptcy, forgiveness and *Biden v. Nebraska* (2023), **diagnose** problems |

**Boss problem 6:** (a) Close-read the Second Circuit's 2012 opinion in *NML Capital v. Argentina* (a US federal court opinion, in the public domain). State the court's reading of the pari passu clause and the alternative reading Argentina offered, and name the words in the clause each reading relies on. *(Exegetical.)* (b) A sovereign swaps a bond for a new one with a lower face value, a lower coupon and a longer maturity. Given the terms and an exit yield (supplied), compute the nominal haircut and the present-value haircut, and explain why creditors and debtors quote different numbers. *(Formal; cites `economics-of-debt`.)* (c) An invented op-ed argues that forgiving student debt is "a modern Jubilee." Diagnose the historical analogy doing the work, name two features of the ancient release laws (1.2–1.3) or of HIPC (5.5) that the analogy depends on, and say whether each carries over. *(Exegetical.)* (d) In 300–500 words: which past settlement is the best precedent for today's sovereign debt problems — 1953, Brady, HIPC or Greece 2012? Any verdict passes if it names the feature that makes the precedent fit, states the strongest disanalogy, and treats the political question (who should bear the loss) as contested. *(Evaluative.)*

## Sources of truth

- Carmen Reinhart and Kenneth Rogoff, *This Time Is Different* (2009): the chronology of sovereign defaults and crises. Their datasets are contested in places; lessons flag this where it matters.
- Sidney Homer and Richard Sylla, *A History of Interest Rates*: rates from Mesopotamia to the present.
- Michael Hudson and Marc Van De Mieroop (eds.), *Debt and Economic Renewal in the Ancient Near East* (2002), read alongside Assyriologists who take the more limited view of the edicts; Mauricio Drelichman and Hans-Joachim Voth, *Lending to the Borrower from Hell* (2014), for early modern sovereign lending.
- Michael Tomz, *Reputation and International Cooperation* (2007), for how sovereign debts were enforced from the 1820s on; David Graeber, *Debt: The First 5,000 Years* (2011), as the provocation whose historical claims the course tests (paraphrased, never quoted at length).
- Public-domain primary texts, listed under "How practice works" above; lessons confirm each translation's date before quoting it.

## Notes

- **Entry point of the debt thread.** The other three courses (`philosophy-of-debt`, `theology-of-debt`, `economics-of-debt`) list this course as a prerequisite or read alongside it; see [HUMANITIES-BUILD-BRIEF.md](../../HUMANITIES-BUILD-BRIEF.md). The handoffs: 1.2–1.3 are the Jubilee as law and practice. `theology-of-debt` reads it as theology, and `economics-of-debt` reads a scheduled release as a credible debt-relief mechanism. 1.1 tests Graeber's history, and `philosophy-of-debt` examines his thesis. 3.2, 4.6 and 5.2 are historians' cruxes that map onto `economics-of-debt`'s default models: commitment, reputation (Eaton–Gersovitz) and sanctions (Bulow–Rogoff). 4.1 hands Jefferson's letter to Madison on whether one generation can bind the next to `philosophy-of-debt`. 5.5 hands odious debt and the moral-hazard argument to `philosophy-of-debt`, and the papal call for debt relief in the Jubilee year 2000 to `theology-of-debt`.
- **Other bridges.** 3.2 ↔ `institutions-and-development` (credible commitment and democratization, moving there from political-economy 5.5). 3.3 ↔ [`grad-macro`](../grad-macro/syllabus.md) 3.3 (rational bubbles). 3.4 ↔ [`grad-macro`](../grad-macro/syllabus.md) 3.4 (Ricardian equivalence). 4.2 and 6.5 ↔ `constitutional-law` (the bankruptcy power). 5.3 ↔ `international-relations` (international organizations).
- **Lesson count.** The roadmap estimated ~24. The syllabus runs 29 (+21%, within the ±25% band) because each of the six eras contains at least one episode that needs its own lesson to carry a close reading or a crux. - **Contested episodes are taught as contested.** The course presents each of these as live disputes, not settled facts: how general and effective ancient cancellations were (1.2, 1.4), what *nexum* was and how much bondage outlived its abolition (1.5), whether Edward III's default broke the Bardi and Peruzzi (2.4), Philip II's creditworthiness (2.4), credible commitment after 1688 (3.2), rational vs irrational 1720 (3.3), crop-lien lock-in (4.3), the causes of 1882 (4.5), gunboats vs reputation (4.6), Keynes vs Mantoux (5.2), whether HIPC relief raised social spending (5.5), subprime vs middle-class 2008 (6.1), and the debt-trap thesis (6.4). The lessons stay verdict-neutral on forgiveness, austerity, bailouts and Chinese lending.
