# Philosophy of Mind — Syllabus

> Philosophy · Tier 2 · ~25 lessons · Prereqs: [`metaphysics`](../metaphysics/syllabus.md) · Roadmap id: `philosophy-of-mind`

## Goal

This course owns the mind-body problem for the whole library: what minds are, how they fit into a physical world, and whether anything other than a living human could have one. It runs from the arguments for dualism (the knowledge argument, zombies and conceivability) through the physicalist programmes (identity theory, functionalism, anomalous monism) and the problem they share, mental causation and exclusion. It then takes up consciousness as its own problem (the hard problem; higher-order, workspace, illusionist and panpsychist answers), intentionality and mental content, and perception. Two further modules complete it. One treats the hylomorphic, Thomist view of the soul as the form of the body as a live option, at the strength its contemporary defenders give it. The other asks whether a machine can think: the Turing test, the Chinese room, and whether large language models understand anything. You will finish able to put any position on the map of views, run it on the standard cases (Mary, the zombie, the China brain, Twin Earth, the hallucination, the room), and name the premise two rival views actually disagree about. The course takes no side. Dualists, physicalists of every stripe, illusionists, panpsychists and hylomorphists each get their best defenders. It deliberately skips personal identity and what a person is ([`metaphysics`](../metaphysics/syllabus.md) 6.5-6.6), free will (metaphysics Module 6), Descartes and the seventeenth-century answers read historically ([`modern-philosophy`](../modern-philosophy/syllabus.md) 1.4, 2.4), the immortality of the soul as doctrine (`creation-grace-last-things`), cognitive psychology as an empirical science, the neuroscience itself ([`neuroscience`](../neuroscience/syllabus.md), cited), and emotion, action theory and the philosophy of psychiatry.

## How practice works in this course

Problems follow [HUMANITIES-BUILD-BRIEF.md](../../HUMANITIES-BUILD-BRIEF.md): every part declares its kind, and every solution is a rubric (must-hit moves, wrong turns) plus a model answer. Here that means:

- **Exegetical parts are strict.** What the knowledge argument concludes (a claim about facts, not about ability), what "supervenience" requires, what Davidson's three principles are and why they look inconsistent, what the systems reply says Searle has overlooked, what a disjunctivist denies about the good case and the bad case. These have right answers, and the most common error graded here is a **conflation**: phenomenal with access consciousness, type with token identity, epiphenomenalism with property dualism, content externalism with the extended mind, "not computable" with "not physical".
- **Evaluative parts are verdict-neutral.** No rubric rewards concluding that physicalism is true, that zombies are possible, that the Chinese room refutes strong AI, that the intellect is immaterial, or that an LLM understands. Must-hit moves are ones every side has to make: state the view precisely, run it on the case, name the premise the objection attacks, and say what the reply costs. Lessons may report where the profession sits (the PhilPapers surveys show a narrow majority of respondents accepting or leaning towards physicalism) but never argue for it in the lesson's voice. Religious and secular views get the same treatment: the Thomist and the illusionist are equally positions to be stated at full strength.
- **Counterexample and find-the-crux are the workhorses.** The field runs on imagined cases, and most disputes bottom out in one premise: whether conceivability entails possibility, whether the physical is causally closed, whether syntax suffices for semantics. **Apply to a hard case** uses real and near-real cases: blindsight, split-brain patients, the octopus's pain, a language model's answer. **Diagnose** uses invented passages (a tech press release saying its chatbot "truly understands", a neuroscientist's op-ed announcing that free will or consciousness has been "shown to be an illusion") that smuggle in a philosophical commitment. Every module uses at least three archetypes.
- **Close reading** uses public-domain texts only: Descartes, *Discourse on Method* Part V (Veitch); Leibniz, *Monadology* (Latta, 1898); Hume, *An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding* §12; T. H. Huxley, "On the Hypothesis that Animals are Automata, and its History" (1874; *Collected Essays* I); William James, *The Principles of Psychology* (1890); Aquinas, *Summa Theologiae* (English Dominican Province, second and revised edition, 1920–22). Modern authors (Nagel, Jackson, Chalmers, Kripke, Putnam, Davidson, Kim, Block, Dennett, Fodor, Millikan, Burge, Searle, Turing, Ross) are paraphrased and cited, never quoted at length.
- **Formal parts are rare:** writing supervenience theses out with quantifiers over worlds and checking which entails which (2.3, Boss 2), and one light computability step (6.1).

## Dangerous Checklist

When you finish, you can:

- [ ] State the inconsistent triad of mind-body causation (mental causes physical; the physical is causally closed; the mental is not physical) and place every major position by the premise it denies
- [ ] Reconstruct the knowledge argument and the zombie argument in numbered premises, and match each physicalist reply (ability hypothesis, acquaintance, phenomenal concepts, type-B identity, denying conceivability) to the premise it attacks
- [ ] Give the case for the identity theory, the multiple-realizability objection, and the functionalist position it produced, then run the China brain and the inverted spectrum against functionalism
- [ ] Write out strong, weak and global supervenience, say which one physicalism needs, and explain why Davidson's anomalous monism is consistent and why Kim thinks it makes the mental idle
- [ ] Reconstruct Kim's exclusion argument and assess the four standard exits (overdetermination, proportionality, the generalization objection, rejecting closure), including what a dualist and a hylomorphist each say
- [ ] Separate phenomenal from access consciousness, state the hard problem and the explanatory gap, and say what higher-order, first-order representationalist and global workspace theories each claim to explain and what they leave out
- [ ] State illusionism and panpsychism at full strength, and give the strongest objection each faces (the illusion-of-what objection; the combination problem)
- [ ] State Brentano's thesis, lay out realism, eliminativism and the intentional stance about folk psychology, and run informational and teleological theories of content on the disjunction problem and Swampman
- [ ] Use Twin Earth and Burge's arthritis case to separate internalist from externalist content, and tell content externalism from the extended mind
- [ ] Reconstruct the argument from hallucination and say what sense-datum theory, adverbialism, intentionalism and naive realism with disjunctivism each deny
- [ ] Explain the hylomorphic view of the soul as the form of the body, reconstruct Aquinas's and Ross's arguments that intellect is not a bodily power, and say how a hylomorphist answers the exclusion problem and the evidence that thought depends on the brain
- [ ] Apply the Turing test, the Chinese room and its replies, and the symbol-grounding problem to a present-day language model, and say what evidence would bear on whether it understands or is conscious

## Modules

### Module 1: The problem, and the case for dualism

What makes the mental look different in kind from the physical, the map of possible answers, and the three arguments that keep dualism a live position.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1.1 | The mind-body problem | State the problem precisely and place every major view on one map | marks of the mental (intentionality, phenomenal consciousness, first-person access), the inconsistent triad (distinctness, mental causation, causal closure), substance dualism, property dualism, physicalism (reductive, nonreductive, eliminative), idealism and neutral monism, hylomorphism as a view the map's categories fit badly (Module 5); the historical answers cited from [`modern-philosophy`](../modern-philosophy/syllabus.md) 2.4 |
| 1.2 | Substance dualism today | Reconstruct the contemporary arguments for a non-physical subject and the interaction objection at its modern strength | the unity or simplicity-of-the-subject argument, Descartes's conceivability and divisibility arguments cited from [`modern-philosophy`](../modern-philosophy/syllabus.md) 1.4, non-Cartesian and emergent dualism (Lowe, Hasker, Swinburne), the causal-closure argument (Papineau), the conservation-of-energy objection and its replies, Kim's pairing problem |
| 1.3 | The knowledge argument | Reconstruct Mary's room and assess the main physicalist replies | Jackson's Mary, knowing a fact vs knowing how, the ability hypothesis (Lewis, Nemirow), the acquaintance hypothesis (Conee), old fact under a new mode of presentation, property dualism as the conclusion, Jackson's later rejection of his own argument |
| 1.4 | Zombies, conceivability, and Kripke | Run the conceivability argument against physicalism and say which premise each physicalist denies | the zombie argument (Chalmers), ideal positive conceivability and the two-dimensional step (semantics cited from [`philosophy-of-language-and-logic`](../philosophy-of-language-and-logic/syllabus.md) 3.3), Kripke's argument that pain is not C-fibre firing and the illusion of contingency (necessity from [`metaphysics`](../metaphysics/syllabus.md) 3.1), type-A vs type-B physicalism, the phenomenal concept strategy (Loar, Papineau) |

**Boss problem 1:** (a) Close-read *Monadology* §17 (Latta, 1898, pp. 227-228), where Leibniz supposes a machine that thinks, feels and perceives, enlarged so that one might go into it as into a mill. The passage makes three claims: that perception is inexplicable on mechanical grounds, that inspecting the machine's interior would find nothing by which to explain a perception, and that perception must therefore be sought in a simple substance. Say which claim is the thesis, which the evidence and which the conclusion, pointing to the words that mark each, and say whether the conclusion is about how perception is explained or about where it is found. Then use §14 to say whether Leibniz's "perception" is consciousness. 150 words or fewer. (b) Reconstruct the zombie argument in numbered premises, stating the step from conceivability to possibility as a premise of its own. Say which premise has a counterpart in the mill passage's finding, and name the principle that carries Leibniz from "never anything by which to explain a perception" to "must be sought for" in a simple substance: is it the zombie argument's step from conceivability to possibility, or something else? Then say what §14 implies for reading the mill as an argument about phenomenal consciousness. (c) Mary leaves her black-and-white room and sees a red tomato. Take the knowledge argument in this form: (P1) before release Mary has complete physical knowledge about colour vision; (C1) so she knows all the physical facts about it; (P2) there is some knowledge about colour experience that she lacks before release; (C2) so there are facts about it she does not know; (C3) so there are non-physical facts. State the ability-hypothesis reply and the phenomenal-concept reply, and for each name the premise it denies or the inference it blocks. 150 words or fewer. (d) In 300-500 words, assess whether the knowledge argument and the zombie argument stand or fall together: can a physicalist consistently answer one with a denial of conceivability and the other with a claim about concepts? Any verdict, if both arguments are stated at full strength, the answer says which premise or step each reply targets in each argument, and it names the cost of the position it ends with: the mixed reply, a single reply to both, or accepting both conclusions. Parts (a)-(c) are exegetical; (d) is evaluative.

### Module 2: Physicalism and mental causation

The physicalist programmes in the order they were built, each an answer to the failure of the last, and the problem that all non-reductive ones inherit: if the physical does all the causing, what is left for the mind to do?

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2.1 | Behaviourism and the identity theory | Say why the identity theory replaced behaviourism, and give its best case | Ryle's category mistake, logical behaviourism and the circularity of belief-desire analyses, Putnam's super-Spartans, type identity (Place, Smart), the topic-neutral analysis, the argument from simplicity, type vs token identity |
| 2.2 | Functionalism | State functionalism, the multiple-realizability argument behind it, and the qualia objections it has to survive | multiple realizability (Putnam; the octopus), machine functionalism, analytic functionalism (Lewis, Armstrong) vs psychofunctionalism, role vs realizer, liberalism and chauvinism, Block's China brain and absent qualia; the inverted spectrum is saved for 3.2 |
| 2.3 | Anomalous monism and supervenience | Define physicalism with supervenience, and explain how Davidson reconciles mental causation with the absence of psychophysical laws | Davidson's three principles (causal interaction, the nomological character of causality, the anomalism of the mental), token identity without type identity, reasons as causes (Davidson 1963), weak, strong and global supervenience (weak, intermediate and strong global) written over worlds, "a minimal physicalism", emergence (weak vs strong), Hempel's dilemma about what counts as "physical" |
| 2.4 | Mental causation and the exclusion problem | Reconstruct Kim's exclusion argument and assess the exits from it | the supervenience or exclusion argument, causal closure, systematic overdetermination (overdetermination from [`metaphysics`](../metaphysics/syllabus.md) 4.2), the "quausation" charge against anomalous monism, proportionality (Yablo; List and Menzies), the generalization objection (Block), epiphenomenalism as the fallback, reductive physicalism as Kim's own exit |

**Boss problem 2:** (a) Close-read two passages of Huxley's "On the Hypothesis that Animals are Automata, and its History" (1874; *Collected Essays* I, pp. 240 and 243-244): the sentence comparing the consciousness of brutes to the steam-whistle that accompanies a locomotive's working without influencing its machinery, and the later passage beginning "It is quite true that, to the best of my judgment". What thesis about mental causation does the steam-whistle express? Of whom is it first stated, which words extend it to human beings, and how strongly do those words assert the extension? 150 words or fewer. (b) Formal: take families A (mental) and B (physical) of monadic properties. Write out strong individual supervenience of A on B with quantifiers over worlds and individuals, and weak, intermediate and strong global supervenience in terms of B-preserving isomorphisms between worlds. Show that strong individual supervenience entails strong global, which entails intermediate, which entails weak. Then give a one-world model in which weak and intermediate global supervenience hold but strong individual supervenience fails, say in one sentence why it is no counterexample to the claim that strong global supervenience entails strong individual supervenience, and name a condition on A and B under which that converse is known to hold. (c) Reconstruct Kim's exclusion argument in numbered premises (mental causation, irreducibility, causal closure, exclusion, no systematic overdetermination). Name the premise that (i) Yablo's proportionality view and (ii) a substance dualist each deny. Then (iii) say what the generalization objection (Block; Baker) does instead of denying a premise, and how Kim answers it. (d) An octopus and a human both withdraw from a noxious stimulus, with very different neural realizers; grant for this part that both are in pain. In 300-500 words, assess whether multiple realizability makes the exclusion problem worse or offers a way out: is "pain caused the withdrawal" a better causal explanation than the realizer-level one? Any verdict, if both the proportionality reading and Kim's reply (species-specific reduction, with a realized property's causal powers inherited from its realizer) are given, and the answer says what each implies for the generalization that pain causes withdrawal in both animals. Part (a) is exegetical; (b) is formal; (c) is exegetical; (d) is evaluative.

Then say which premise of the argument from hallucination disjunctivism characteristically denies, and why that denial is not needed to answer the table argument. 150 words or fewer. (c) A frog snaps at a small dark moving thing, whether it is a fly or a flicked pellet. Say what the frog's state represents on Dretske's informational theory in its 1981 form (content as what the state indicates) and on Millikan's teleosemantics as she applies it to the frog. Show how the pellet poses the disjunction problem for one of them, and say what the pellet strike is on the other. Then name the different indeterminacy charge Fodor brings against teleosemantics with the same frog. 150 words or fewer. (d) Otto, who has early Alzheimer's, consults his notebook for the museum's address, as in Clark and Chalmers. On Twin Earth his intrinsic duplicate consults an intrinsically identical notebook, but there the street names pick out different streets. Assume both notebooks meet Clark and Chalmers's conditions. In 150 words, say (i) whether the two share narrow content, saying where "narrow" draws its boundary and whether the answer changes if the notebooks are inside it, and (ii) whether each has an extended belief about the museum's address and whether the two beliefs have the same wide content, keeping content externalism and the extended-mind thesis apart. (e) In 300-500 words, assess whether perceptual experience is best understood as representation or as relation. State the screening-off argument and the naive realist's reply, and the strongest naive realist objection to intentionalism and the intentionalist's reply. Any verdict, including a hybrid, if each side's argument is stated at the strength its defenders would recognize and the answer says what the preferred view makes of the hallucinating subject. Parts (a)-(d) are exegetical; (e) is evaluative.

### Module 5: Soul as form: the hylomorphic view

A third option that the dualism-physicalism map was not drawn for: the soul as the form of a living body, with one power, intellect, that its defenders argue is not the act of any bodily organ. Taught as a live position against its contemporary critics, not as history.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5.1 | The soul as the form of the body | State hylomorphism as a theory of mind and say how it differs from both substance dualism and functionalism | soul as first actuality of a living body (text cited from [`ancient-medieval-philosophy`](../ancient-medieval-philosophy/syllabus.md) 3.5; the general theory from [`metaphysics`](../metaphysics/syllabus.md) 2.3), vegetative, sensitive and rational powers, one substantial form (ST I q.76 a.1, and a.3 against a plurality of souls), why there is no interaction problem between a form and what it informs, contemporary hylomorphists (Jaworski, Simpson, Oderberg, Feser), the charge that hylomorphism is functionalism renamed |
| 5.2 | Is the intellect a bodily power? | Reconstruct the Thomist and the determinacy arguments that thought is not wholly physical, and the replies | Aquinas on the intellect as subsistent (ST I q.75 a.2), the argument from the scope of what intellect can know, universals and abstraction, Ross's argument from determinate thought (plus vs quus, the paradox cited from [`philosophy-of-language-and-logic`](../philosophy-of-language-and-logic/syllabus.md) 4.3), the reply that the argument proves too much, the corruptionist vs survivalist dispute cited to [`metaphysics`](../metaphysics/syllabus.md) 6.5, immortality as doctrine ceded to `creation-grace-last-things` |

**Boss problem 5:** (a) Close-read the *respondeo* of Aquinas, *Summa Theologiae* I q.75 a.2 (English Dominican Province, second and revised edition, 1922), including the example of the sick man's tongue. Reconstruct the argument that the intellectual principle has an operation *per se* apart from the body in numbered premises, and say which premise the tongue example is meant to support and which the example of the coloured glass vase supports. (b) A functionalist says intellect is a set of roles realized in neural tissue, and grants that we can come to know the nature of any kind of body. Find the crux between that view and the argument in (a): which single premise must the functionalist deny, and what does that premise assume about what knowing a nature requires that the functionalist rejects? 150 words or fewer. (c) A thermometer registers every temperature in its range while having a temperature of its own. Is it a counterexample to the premise the tongue supports? Say what "have it in its own nature" and "know" must mean for it to be one, and for it not to be, and give your verdict, in 150 words. Any verdict. (d) Ross argues that some thinking, such as adding, is determinate; that no physical process is determinate among incompossible functions, since whatever a machine has done fits quaddition as well as addition; and so that such thinking is not wholly physical. He accepts that machines do not really add and that the brain cannot be the organ of thought, so those are consequences of his argument, not objections to it, and the charge that Kripke's sceptic undoes immaterial thought too is set in [`apologetics-foundations`](../apologetics-foundations/syllabus.md) 3.2. Then reply as Ross (section III of his 1992 paper) or Feser (2013) would, in 150 more. (e) In 300-500 words, assess how much the evidence that brain damage impairs thought counts against the Thomist view, using Aquinas's own claims in ST I q.84 a.7 that in this life the intellect understands nothing actually without turning to phantasms, and that a lesion of the organ of imagination or memory hinders understanding. Any verdict, if the Thomist position is stated as its defenders state it, the objection is stated at its strongest (deficits that seem to remove concepts themselves rather than the images they are used with), and the answer says what evidence would tell the two readings apart. Parts (a)-(b) are exegetical; (c)-(e) are evaluative, and (d)'s reply is also graded for fidelity to Ross or Feser.

### Module 6: Machine minds

Whether thinking is computation, whether a machine that behaves as if it understands does understand, and what to say about the language models that make the question practical. Bridges to [`deep-learning`](../deep-learning/syllabus.md) and [`theory-of-computation`](../theory-of-computation/syllabus.md).

## Notes

- **Verdict-neutral across four families.** Every module sets dualist, physicalist, and radical or hylomorphic answers against each other at equal strength: property dualism vs type-B physicalism (1.3-1.4), reductive vs nonreductive physicalism on exclusion (2.4), illusionism vs panpsychism (3.4-3.5), intentionalism vs naive realism (4.5), the Thomist vs the functionalist on intellect (5.2), Searle vs the systems reply (6.2).
