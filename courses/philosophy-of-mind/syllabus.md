# Philosophy of Mind — Syllabus

> Philosophy · Tier 2 · ~25 lessons · Prereqs: [`metaphysics`](../metaphysics/syllabus.md) · Roadmap id: `philosophy-of-mind`

## Goal

This course owns the mind-body problem for the whole library: what minds are, how they fit into a physical world, and whether anything other than a living human could have one. It runs from the arguments for dualism (the knowledge argument, zombies and conceivability) through the physicalist programmes (identity theory, functionalism, anomalous monism) and the problem they share, mental causation and exclusion. It then takes up consciousness as its own problem (the hard problem; higher-order, workspace, illusionist and panpsychist answers), intentionality and mental content, and perception. Two further modules complete it. One treats the hylomorphic, Thomist view of the soul as the form of the body as a live option, at the strength its contemporary defenders give it. The other asks whether a machine can think: the Turing test, the Chinese room, and whether large language models understand anything. You will finish able to put any position on the map of views, run it on the standard cases (Mary, the zombie, the China brain, Twin Earth, the hallucination, the room), and name the premise two rival views actually disagree about. The course takes no side. Dualists, physicalists of every stripe, illusionists, panpsychists and hylomorphists each get their best defenders. It deliberately skips personal identity and what a person is ([`metaphysics`](../metaphysics/syllabus.md) 6.5-6.6), free will (metaphysics Module 6), Descartes and the seventeenth-century answers read historically ([`modern-philosophy`](../modern-philosophy/syllabus.md) 1.4, 2.4), the immortality of the soul as doctrine (`creation-grace-last-things`), cognitive psychology as an empirical science, the neuroscience itself ([`neuroscience`](../neuroscience/syllabus.md), cited), and emotion, action theory and the philosophy of psychiatry.

## How practice works in this course

Problems follow [HUMANITIES-BUILD-BRIEF.md](../../HUMANITIES-BUILD-BRIEF.md): every part declares its kind, and every solution is a rubric (must-hit moves, wrong turns) plus a model answer. Here that means:

- **Exegetical parts are strict.** What the knowledge argument concludes (a claim about facts, not about ability), what "supervenience" requires, what Davidson's three principles are and why they look inconsistent, what the systems reply says Searle has overlooked, what a disjunctivist denies about the good case and the bad case. These have right answers, and the most common error graded here is a **conflation**: phenomenal with access consciousness, type with token identity, epiphenomenalism with property dualism, content externalism with the extended mind, "not computable" with "not physical".
- **Evaluative parts are verdict-neutral.** No rubric rewards concluding that physicalism is true, that zombies are possible, that the Chinese room refutes strong AI, that the intellect is immaterial, or that an LLM understands. Must-hit moves are ones every side has to make: state the view precisely, run it on the case, name the premise the objection attacks, and say what the reply costs. Lessons may report where the profession sits (the PhilPapers surveys show a narrow majority of respondents accepting or leaning towards physicalism) but never argue for it in the lesson's voice. Religious and secular views get the same treatment: the Thomist and the illusionist are equally positions to be stated at full strength.
- **Counterexample and find-the-crux are the workhorses.** The field runs on imagined cases, and most disputes bottom out in one premise: whether conceivability entails possibility, whether the physical is causally closed, whether syntax suffices for semantics. **Apply to a hard case** uses real and near-real cases: blindsight, split-brain patients, the octopus's pain, a language model's answer. **Diagnose** uses invented passages (a tech press release saying its chatbot "truly understands", a neuroscientist's op-ed announcing that free will or consciousness has been "shown to be an illusion") that smuggle in a philosophical commitment. Every module uses at least three archetypes.
- **Close reading** uses public-domain texts only: Descartes, *Discourse on Method* Part V (Veitch); Leibniz, *Monadology* (Latta); Hume, *An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding* §12; T. H. Huxley, "On the Hypothesis that Animals are Automata, and its History" (1874); William James, *The Principles of Psychology* (1890); Aquinas, *Summa Theologiae* (English Dominican Province). Modern authors (Nagel, Jackson, Chalmers, Kripke, Putnam, Davidson, Kim, Block, Dennett, Fodor, Millikan, Burge, Searle, Turing, Ross) are paraphrased and cited, never quoted at length.
- **Formal parts are rare:** writing supervenience theses out with quantifiers over worlds and checking which entails which (2.3, Boss 2), and one light computability step (6.1).

## Dangerous Checklist

When you finish, you can:

- [ ] State the inconsistent triad of mind-body causation (mental causes physical; the physical is causally closed; the mental is not physical) and place every major position by the premise it denies
- [ ] Reconstruct the knowledge argument and the zombie argument in numbered premises, and match each physicalist reply (ability hypothesis, acquaintance, phenomenal concepts, type-B identity, denying conceivability) to the premise it attacks
- [ ] Give the case for the identity theory, the multiple-realizability objection, and the functionalist position it produced, then run the China brain and the inverted spectrum against functionalism
- [ ] Write out strong, weak and global supervenience, say which one physicalism needs, and explain why Davidson's anomalous monism is consistent and why Kim thinks it makes the mental idle
- [ ] Reconstruct Kim's exclusion argument and assess the four standard exits (overdetermination, proportionality, the generalization objection, rejecting closure), including what a dualist and a hylomorphist each say
- [ ] Separate phenomenal from access consciousness, state the hard problem and the explanatory gap, and say what higher-order, first-order representationalist and global workspace theories each claim to explain and what they leave out
- [ ] State illusionism and panpsychism at full strength, and give the strongest objection each faces (the illusion-of-what objection; the combination problem)
- [ ] State Brentano's thesis, lay out realism, eliminativism and the intentional stance about folk psychology, and run informational and teleological theories of content on the disjunction problem and Swampman
- [ ] Use Twin Earth and Burge's arthritis case to separate internalist from externalist content, and tell content externalism from the extended mind
- [ ] Reconstruct the argument from hallucination and say what sense-datum theory, adverbialism, intentionalism and naive realism with disjunctivism each deny
- [ ] Explain the hylomorphic view of the soul as the form of the body, reconstruct Aquinas's and Ross's arguments that intellect is not a bodily power, and say how a hylomorphist answers the exclusion problem and the evidence that thought depends on the brain
- [ ] Apply the Turing test, the Chinese room and its replies, and the symbol-grounding problem to a present-day language model, and say what evidence would bear on whether it understands or is conscious

## Modules

### Module 1: The problem, and the case for dualism

What makes the mental look different in kind from the physical, the map of possible answers, and the three arguments that keep dualism a live position.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1.1 | The mind-body problem | State the problem precisely and place every major view on one map | marks of the mental (intentionality, phenomenal consciousness, first-person access), the inconsistent triad (distinctness, mental causation, causal closure), substance dualism, property dualism, physicalism (reductive, nonreductive, eliminative), idealism and neutral monism, hylomorphism as a view the map's categories fit badly (Module 5); the historical answers cited from [`modern-philosophy`](../modern-philosophy/syllabus.md) 2.4 |
| 1.2 | Substance dualism today | Reconstruct the contemporary arguments for a non-physical subject and the interaction objection at its modern strength | the unity or simplicity-of-the-subject argument, Descartes's conceivability and divisibility arguments cited from [`modern-philosophy`](../modern-philosophy/syllabus.md) 1.4, non-Cartesian and emergent dualism (Lowe, Hasker, Swinburne), the causal-closure argument (Papineau), the conservation-of-energy objection and its replies, Kim's pairing problem |
| 1.3 | The knowledge argument | Reconstruct Mary's room and assess the main physicalist replies | Jackson's Mary, knowing a fact vs knowing how, the ability hypothesis (Lewis, Nemirow), the acquaintance hypothesis (Conee), old fact under a new mode of presentation, property dualism as the conclusion, Jackson's later rejection of his own argument |
| 1.4 | Zombies, conceivability, and Kripke | Run the conceivability argument against physicalism and say which premise each physicalist denies | the zombie argument (Chalmers), ideal positive conceivability and the two-dimensional step (semantics cited from [`philosophy-of-language-and-logic`](../philosophy-of-language-and-logic/syllabus.md) 3.3), Kripke's argument that pain is not C-fibre firing and the illusion of contingency (necessity from [`metaphysics`](../metaphysics/syllabus.md) 3.1), type-A vs type-B physicalism, the phenomenal concept strategy (Loar, Papineau) |

**Boss problem 1:** (a) Close-read *Monadology* §17 (Latta), where Leibniz imagines a machine enlarged so that one could walk inside it as into a mill. What does the passage conclude, and do its words argue that perception cannot be *mechanical*, or that it cannot be *found by inspecting parts*? Point to the words that decide it. (b) Reconstruct the zombie argument in numbered premises, and mark the premise that the mill passage anticipates and the premise it does not. (c) Mary leaves her black-and-white room and sees a red tomato. State the ability-hypothesis reply and the phenomenal-concept reply, and for each name the premise of the knowledge argument it denies. (d) In 300-500 words, assess whether the knowledge argument and the zombie argument stand or fall together: can a physicalist consistently answer one with a denial of conceivability and the other with a claim about concepts? Any verdict, if both arguments are stated at full strength and the reply's cost is named. Parts (a)-(c) are exegetical; (d) is evaluative.

### Module 2: Physicalism and mental causation

The physicalist programmes in the order they were built, each an answer to the failure of the last, and the problem that all non-reductive ones inherit: if the physical does all the causing, what is left for the mind to do?

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2.1 | Behaviourism and the identity theory | Say why the identity theory replaced behaviourism, and give its best case | Ryle's category mistake, logical behaviourism and the circularity of belief-desire analyses, Putnam's super-Spartans, type identity (Place, Smart), the topic-neutral analysis, the argument from simplicity, type vs token identity |
| 2.2 | Functionalism | State functionalism, the multiple-realizability argument behind it, and the qualia objections it has to survive | multiple realizability (Putnam; the octopus), machine functionalism, analytic functionalism (Lewis, Armstrong) vs psychofunctionalism, role vs realizer, liberalism and chauvinism, Block's China brain and absent qualia; the inverted spectrum is saved for 3.2 |
| 2.3 | Anomalous monism and supervenience | Define physicalism with supervenience, and explain how Davidson reconciles mental causation with the absence of psychophysical laws | Davidson's three principles (causal interaction, the nomological character of causality, the anomalism of the mental), token identity without type identity, weak, strong and global supervenience written over worlds, "a minimal physicalism", emergence (weak vs strong), Hempel's dilemma about what counts as "physical" |
| 2.4 | Mental causation and the exclusion problem | Reconstruct Kim's exclusion argument and assess the exits from it | the supervenience or exclusion argument, causal closure, systematic overdetermination (overdetermination from [`metaphysics`](../metaphysics/syllabus.md) 4.2), the "quausation" charge against anomalous monism, proportionality (Yablo; List and Menzies), the generalization objection (Block), epiphenomenalism as the fallback, reductive physicalism as Kim's own exit |

**Boss problem 2:** (a) Close-read the passage of Huxley's "On the Hypothesis that Animals are Automata" (1874) comparing consciousness to the steam-whistle that accompanies a locomotive's working without influencing its machinery. What thesis about mental causation does the analogy express, and do Huxley's words restrict it to animals or extend it to human beings? (b) Formal: write out strong supervenience of the mental on the physical and global supervenience, each with quantifiers over worlds, and show that strong supervenience entails global supervenience. Then say in one sentence why the converse is doubted. (c) Reconstruct Kim's exclusion argument in numbered premises, and name the premise that (i) Yablo's proportionality view, (ii) the generalization objection and (iii) a substance dualist each deny. (d) An octopus and a human both withdraw from a noxious stimulus, with very different neural realizers. In 300-500 words, assess whether multiple realizability makes the exclusion problem worse or offers a way out: is "pain caused the withdrawal" a better causal explanation than the realizer-level one? Any verdict, if both the proportionality reading and Kim's reply are given. Part (a) is exegetical; (b) is formal; (c) is exegetical; (d) is evaluative.

### Module 3: Consciousness

Consciousness taken on its own terms: what exactly resists explanation, the leading theories of what it is (read as philosophical theories, with the science that tests them), and the two radical answers that the problem has made respectable, denial and ubiquity.

|---|---|---|---|
| 3.1 | The hard problem | Separate the questions about consciousness and say which one is thought to be hard, and why | "what it is like" (Nagel's bat, paraphrased), phenomenal vs access consciousness (Block), creature vs state consciousness, the easy and hard problems (Chalmers), the explanatory gap (Levine), explaining vs identifying |
| 3.2 | Representationalism and higher-order theories | Say what first-order and higher-order theories take consciousness to be, and the case that breaks each | transparency of experience (Harman), first-order representationalism (Dretske, Tye), the inverted spectrum and Inverted Earth, higher-order thought (Rosenthal) and higher-order perception (Lycan), the empty higher-order state objection, the animal and infant objection |
| 3.3 | Global workspace and the science of consciousness | Say what the global workspace theory claims, how theories of consciousness are tested, and what the tests can and cannot settle | contrastive method and neural correlates, masking and blindsight (cited from [`neuroscience`](../neuroscience/syllabus.md) 3.2), global workspace and ignition (Baars, Dehaene), integrated information theory named, report vs no-report paradigms, the overflow debate (Block vs Cohen and Dennett), split-brain patients and the unity of consciousness (Nagel's brain bisection), Libet named, with its free-will reading cited to [`metaphysics`](../metaphysics/syllabus.md) Module 6 |
| 3.4 | Illusionism | State illusionism at full strength and the objection it must answer | eliminativism about qualia (Dennett's "Quining Qualia", paraphrased), heterophenomenology, illusionism (Frankish), quasi-phenomenal properties, the meta-problem of consciousness (Chalmers), "an illusion of experience is itself an experience" and the illusionist's reply |
| 3.5 | Panpsychism and Russellian monism | State the case for consciousness at the base of nature and the combination problem it faces | the structuralist argument that physics describes only dispositions (Russell, *The Analysis of Matter*), Russellian monism, the anti-emergence argument (Galen Strawson), constitutive panpsychism vs panprotopsychism, cosmopsychism, the combination problem (James; Goff; Chalmers), idealism named |

**Boss problem 3:** (a) Close-read the passage in James's *Principles of Psychology* (1890), ch. VI, where he imagines a sentence of twelve words distributed one word each to twelve men. What does James take the case to show about how feelings combine, and which words make it an objection to "mind-stuff" rather than to materialism? (b) A patient with a lesion in primary visual cortex points accurately to a light he insists he cannot see (cited from [`neuroscience`](../neuroscience/syllabus.md) 3.2). Say what a higher-order theorist, a global workspace theorist and an illusionist each say is and is not present, using the phenomenal/access distinction. (c) Diagnose an invented neuroscientist's op-ed announcing that brain imaging has "solved consciousness" by finding its neural signature: which of the questions from 3.1 has the finding addressed, and which has it assumed? (d) In 300-500 words, assess whether illusionism or panpsychism pays the lower price for taking the hard problem seriously, stating the combination problem and the illusion-of-what objection at full strength. Any verdict. Parts (a)-(c) are exegetical; (d) is evaluative.

### Module 5: Soul as form: the hylomorphic view

A third option that the dualism-physicalism map was not drawn for: the soul as the form of a living body, with one power, intellect, that its defenders argue is not the act of any bodily organ. Taught as a live position against its contemporary critics, not as history.

|---|---|---|---|

| 5.2 | Is the intellect a bodily power? | Reconstruct the Thomist and the determinacy arguments that thought is not wholly physical, and the replies | Aquinas on the intellect as subsistent (ST I q.75 a.2), the argument from the scope of what intellect can know, universals and abstraction, Ross's argument from determinate thought (plus vs quus, the paradox cited from [`philosophy-of-language-and-logic`](../philosophy-of-language-and-logic/syllabus.md) 4.3), the reply that the argument proves too much, the corruptionist vs survivalist dispute cited to [`metaphysics`](../metaphysics/syllabus.md) 6.5, immortality as doctrine ceded to `creation-grace-last-things` |
| 5.3 | Hylomorphism tested | Say what a hylomorphist makes of causal closure, exclusion, and the dependence of thought on the brain | formal vs efficient causation and why the hylomorphist denies Kim's framing (2.4), structure as a difference-maker (Jaworski), the dependence of intellect on imagination and the brain (ST I q.84 a.7 on injury to the organ), brain lesions and dementia as evidence against the immaterial intellect and the Thomist reply, whether "emergent" dualism and hylomorphism really differ |

**Boss problem 5:** (a) Close-read the *respondeo* of Aquinas, *Summa Theologiae* I q.75 a.2 (English Dominican Province), including the example of the sick man's tongue. ### Module 6: Machine minds

Bridges to [`deep-learning`](../deep-learning/syllabus.md) and [`theory-of-computation`](../theory-of-computation/syllabus.md).

**Boss problem 6:** (a) Close-read the passage in Descartes's *Discourse on Method* Part V (Veitch) giving two means by which a machine resembling us could be known not to be a true man. State both tests, and say whether Descartes presents them as in principle unpassable or as practically unpassable, pointing to the words that decide it. (b) Reconstruct Searle's Chinese room argument in numbered premises, and name the premise the systems reply denies and the premise the robot reply denies. (c) Diagnose an invented press release announcing that a company's chatbot "genuinely understands your needs": which sense of "understand" is doing the work (Turing-test behaviour, grounded content, or conscious grasp), and what would have to be shown for each? (d) In 300-500 words, apply Descartes's tests, the Chinese room and the symbol-grounding problem to a present-day LLM that answers novel questions fluently and whose internal states can be probed for a model of a board game it was never shown. Assess whether it understands anything, and say what further evidence would change your verdict. Any verdict, if Searle's position and the strongest reply are both stated at full strength. Parts (a)-(c) are exegetical; (d) is evaluative.

## Notes

- **Verdict-neutral across four families.** Every module sets dualist, physicalist, and radical or hylomorphic answers against each other at equal strength: property dualism vs type-B physicalism (1.3-1.4), reductive vs nonreductive physicalism on exclusion (2.4), illusionism vs panpsychism (3.4-3.5), intentionalism vs naive realism (4.5), the Thomist vs the functionalist on intellect (5.2), Searle vs the systems reply (6.2). Lessons may report the survey data, never argue for it.
- **Ownership collisions found and resolved.** (1) The roadmap blurb lists personal identity; [`metaphysics`](../metaphysics/syllabus.md) owns it (6.5-6.6) and its Notes ask this course to add only what is specific to consciousness. Done: split brains appear in 3.3 as a question about unity of consciousness, uploading in 6.4 as a question about machine consciousness, and both cite 6.6 for identity. (2) [`modern-philosophy`](../modern-philosophy/syllabus.md) reads Descartes's dualism, Elisabeth's objection and the four seventeenth-century answers historically (1.4, 2.4, Boss 1 and Boss 2). This course does not reconstruct Meditation VI again; 1.2 cites it and moves to the contemporary arguments, and the boss problems use texts that course does not (*Monadology* §17, *Discourse* V). (3) [`ancient-medieval-philosophy`](../ancient-medieval-philosophy/syllabus.md) reads *De Anima* in its own terms (3.5) and Aquinas against Averroes (7.1), and hands the live theory here; Module 5 cites 3.5 for the text and does not re-teach the unity-of-intellect dispute. (4) [`epistemology`](../epistemology/syllabus.md) explicitly skips "theories of perception as philosophy of mind"; 4.4-4.5 take them, and perceptual justification stays there. (5) In the built library, [`neuroscience`](../neuroscience/syllabus.md) 4.3 explicitly declines to address consciousness and 3.2 describes blindsight in one paragraph; no built lesson teaches the Turing test, the Chinese room or theories of consciousness. [`deep-learning`](../deep-learning/syllabus.md) 4.5 treats scaling and emergence claims technically, and 6.3 builds on it without repeating it. (6) [`philosophy-of-language-and-logic`](../philosophy-of-language-and-logic/syllabus.md) uses Twin Earth for kind-term meaning (3.3) and owns the rule-following paradox (4.3), and cedes mental content here; 4.3 and 5.2 cite those lessons rather than re-running the cases.
- **For `thomistic-synthesis` and `creation-grace-last-things`.** Module 5 reads ST I q.75 a.2, q.76 aa.1 and 3, and q.84 a.7 as philosophy of mind and weighs them against modern rivals. `thomistic-synthesis` should cite Module 5 for the live debate and keep the soul's place in Aquinas's system; `creation-grace-last-things` owns the separated soul, the resurrection and immortality as doctrine, with their levels of authority. No doctrinal weight is assigned in this course.
- **Bridges.** The exclusion problem (2.4) is the modern form of the interaction problem Elisabeth raised ([`modern-philosophy`](../modern-philosophy/syllabus.md) 1.4). Multiple realizability (2.2) meets universal approximation in [`deep-learning`](../deep-learning/syllabus.md) 1.2: many weight settings, one function. Connectionism vs the language of thought (4.1) is the old form of the question 6.3 asks about transformers. Ross's determinacy argument (5.2) and the triviality problem for implementation (6.2) are the same worry about physical computation from two directions. The normalization and evidence-accumulation models in [`neuroscience`](../neuroscience/syllabus.md) 4.3 are an example of explaining selection "without a chooser" that the illusionist (3.4) generalizes.
- **Lesson count.** The roadmap estimated ~20; this runs 25 (+25 percent, at the edge of the band). Personal identity leaves (it belongs to metaphysics), but the steering adds three topics beyond the roadmap blurb that each need real space: the hylomorphic view as a live option (three lessons, since it must be stated, argued for, and tested against exclusion and neuroscience), perception (two lessons, since the argument from hallucination and the four answers cannot share one), and the LLM question (split from machine consciousness so each fits in ten minutes). Merges already made to hold the line: behaviourism shares 2.1 with the identity theory; the China brain goes into 2.2 and the inverted spectrum into 3.2; epiphenomenalism is the fallback in 2.4, not its own lesson; Brentano's thesis shares 4.1 with the three stances on folk psychology; phenomenal intentionality and the extended mind share 4.3 with externalism; the Lucas-Penrose argument and Blockhead are folded into 6.1. If a lesson overflows when built, split it and trim elsewhere rather than grow past ~26.
