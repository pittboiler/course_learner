# Engineering Thermodynamics · Reference Card

> Open book. This card is meant to be open while you work — including during
> quizzes. Nothing here is something you're expected to recall cold.

This course is energy bookkeeping for machines. Draw a boundary, fix the state of
the fluid inside it (table, or $pv=RT$), tally every joule crossing as heat or
work (first law), then ask the second law how much of it could ever have become
work. Everything in Module 4 is that ledger run around a loop. **Start every
problem at the sign-convention block and the "which table" flowchart below** —
those two decisions cause more wrong answers than all the algebra combined.

## Definitions

### Closed system

A bag around a fixed chunk of matter: energy may cross, mass may not. Also called
a **control mass**; the piston–cylinder is the archetype. An **isolated** system
blocks energy too.

*Introduced:* [1.1](lessons/01-01-system-vs-control-volume-state.md)

### Control volume

A bag nailed to a region of *space*, with fluid streaming through it. Also called
an **open system**; its boundary is the **control surface**. Turbines, nozzles,
pumps, heat exchangers. The test is never the hardware — it is whether mass
crosses the boundary *during this process*.

*Introduced:* [1.1](lessons/01-01-system-vs-control-volume-state.md)

### Extensive vs. intensive

Cut the system in half: whatever halves is **extensive** ($m, V, U, S$), whatever
stays put is **intensive** ($T, p, \rho$). Any extensive property divided by mass
becomes an intensive **specific property**, written lower case.

$$v = \frac{V}{m}, \qquad u = \frac{U}{m}$$

*Introduced:* [1.1](lessons/01-01-system-vs-control-volume-state.md)

### State postulate

Two independent intensive properties fix the intensive state of a simple
compressible substance — everything else then follows. "Independent" is the whole
catch: inside the vapor dome $T$ and $p$ move together and count as one fact.

*Introduced:* [1.1](lessons/01-01-system-vs-control-volume-state.md)

### Quasi-equilibrium process

A process run slowly enough that the system is never more than a whisker from
equilibrium, so every intermediate state is well defined and plottable. It is the
licence to write $W = \int p\,dV$ with the system's own pressure.

*Introduced:* [1.1](lessons/01-01-system-vs-control-volume-state.md)

### Saturation

At a given pressure a pure substance boils at exactly one temperature, and vice
versa — two views of one curve.

$$T_{sat} = T_{sat}(p), \qquad p_{sat} = p_{sat}(T)$$

*Introduced:* [1.2](lessons/01-02-phase-behavior-pure-substance.md)

### Vapor dome

The fence around "part liquid, part vapor" on $T$–$v$ or $p$–$v$ axes: the
saturated-liquid line is the left edge, the saturated-vapor line the right edge,
compressed liquid lies left of it and superheated vapor right of it. Its peak is
the **critical point**, above which there is no boiling plateau at all — no
meniscus, no latent heat.

*Introduced:* [1.2](lessons/01-02-phase-behavior-pure-substance.md)

### Quality

The progress bar across the boiling plateau: what fraction of the mixture, by
mass, is vapor. Defined **only** inside the dome.

$$x = \frac{m_{vapor}}{m_{total}}, \qquad 0 \le x \le 1$$

*Introduced:* [1.3](lessons/01-03-property-tables-quality.md)

### Ideal gas

Molecules modelled as far-apart points with no volume and no mutual attraction —
true when the fluid is dilute compared to its own critical point.

$$pv = RT, \qquad pV = mRT = nR_uT$$

*Introduced:* [1.4](lessons/01-04-ideal-gas-model-limits.md)

### Compressibility factor

The fudge factor the real gas needs, so $pv = ZRT$. Ideal means $Z = 1$; $Z < 1$
when attraction pulls the molecules in tighter than ideal, $Z > 1$ when their
finite size pushes them apart.

$$Z = \frac{pv}{RT}$$

*Introduced:* [1.4](lessons/01-04-ideal-gas-model-limits.md)

### Boundary work

Energy carried out by a moving wall: pressure times the volume it sweeps, added
along the path. Geometrically, **the area under the process curve on a $p$–$V$
diagram** — which is why it depends on the path, not just the endpoints.

$$W = \int_1^2 p\,dV \quad (\mathrm{kJ})$$

*Introduced:* [1.5](lessons/01-05-work-and-heat.md)

### Path function

Heat and work are not things a system *has*; they are energy caught crossing the
boundary. So there is no "$\Delta Q$" or "$\Delta W$" — only $Q$ and $W$ for a
particular path, and their differentials are inexact ($\delta$, not $d$).

*Introduced:* [1.5](lessons/01-05-work-and-heat.md)

### Enthalpy

Internal energy plus the pressure–volume bundle. For a closed system at constant
pressure it is exactly the heat; for a flowing stream it is the energy each
kilogram actually carries, because the $pv$ piece is the flow work.

$$h = u + pv \quad (\mathrm{kJ/kg})$$

*Introduced:* [2.1](lessons/02-01-first-law-closed-systems.md)

### Specific heats

Kilojoules per kilogram needed to raise the temperature one kelvin — at fixed
volume, and at fixed pressure. They differ because a constant-pressure heating
also spends energy pushing the boundary out, so $c_p > c_v$ always.

$$c_v = \left(\frac{\partial u}{\partial T}\right)_v, \qquad c_p = \left(\frac{\partial h}{\partial T}\right)_p$$

*Introduced:* [2.1](lessons/02-01-first-law-closed-systems.md)

### Polytropic process

The one-parameter family of paths that contains almost every process you care
about; the exponent $n$ picks which.

$$pV^n = \mathrm{const}$$

*Introduced:* [2.2](lessons/02-02-closed-system-processes.md)

### Flow work

The toll each kilogram pays at the door: the work spent shoving it across the
control surface against the local pressure. It is *already inside* $h$, never a
separate term in the energy balance.

$$w_{flow} = pv \quad (\mathrm{kJ/kg})$$

*Introduced:* [2.3](lessons/02-03-mass-energy-balance-control-volumes.md)

### Steady state

Nothing inside the control volume changes with time — so mass in equals mass out
and no energy accumulates. It is what kills the $d/dt$ terms in every Module 2–4
balance.

*Introduced:* [2.3](lessons/02-03-mass-energy-balance-control-volumes.md)

### Throttling

Squeezing a fluid through a valve or capillary: no work, no heat, no meaningful
speed change, so enthalpy comes out unchanged. **Isenthalpic**, strongly
irreversible, and for a real fluid it *cools* (Joule–Thomson) — the trick your
fridge runs on.

$$h_2 = h_1$$

*Introduced:* [2.5](lessons/02-05-steady-flow-devices-no-work.md)

### Reservoir

A body so large its temperature does not move when you add or remove heat. The
furnace, the river, the atmosphere.

*Introduced:* [3.1](lessons/03-01-second-law-carnot-limit.md)

### Reversible

A process that could be run backwards through the same states leaving no mark on
the surroundings — no friction, no heat across a finite temperature gap, no
turbulence. It does not exist; it is the yardstick you compute against.

*Introduced:* [3.1](lessons/03-01-second-law-carnot-limit.md)

### Carnot engine

A reversible engine trading heat with just two reservoirs. Two facts pin it down:
nothing beats it between the same two temperatures, and *all* reversible engines
between those temperatures score identically — regardless of working fluid or
hardware.

*Introduced:* [3.1](lessons/03-01-second-law-carnot-limit.md)

### Entropy

The property whose increment is reversible heat divided by absolute temperature.
Because it is a property, you evaluate it from the endpoints along any convenient
reversible path — even when the real process was violent.

$$dS \equiv \left(\frac{\delta Q}{T}\right)_{rev}, \qquad S_2 - S_1 = \int_1^2\left(\frac{\delta Q}{T}\right)_{rev}$$

*Introduced:* [3.2](lessons/03-02-entropy-clausius-inequality.md)

### Clausius inequality

Sum "heat over boundary temperature" around any loop and you never get a positive
number. Equality is the reversible case; a strictly negative value flags an
irreversible cycle.

$$\oint \frac{\delta Q}{T} \le 0$$

*Introduced:* [3.2](lessons/03-02-entropy-clausius-inequality.md)

### Entropy generation

Entropy manufactured inside the system by irreversibility — friction, mixing,
unrestrained heat flow. It is the unforgeable measure of how sloppy a process
was: zero only in the reversible limit, **never negative**, and a negative answer
means you made an arithmetic error or proposed an impossible device.

$$S_{gen} \ge 0$$

*Introduced:* [3.2](lessons/03-02-entropy-clausius-inequality.md)

### Isentropic process

Adiabatic **and** reversible — the two together force $s_2 = s_1$ through the
entropy balance. A real adiabatic turbine is not isentropic: no heat crosses, yet
friction still raises $s$.

*Introduced:* [3.4](lessons/03-04-isentropic-processes-efficiency.md)

### Isentropic efficiency

How a real device scores against the isentropic ideal, compared at the **same
inlet state and the same exit pressure**. Always arranged as (what you got) over
(what perfection gives) so the answer lands under 1.

*Introduced:* [3.4](lessons/03-04-isentropic-processes-efficiency.md)

### Back-work ratio

The fraction of the turbine's gross output that must be handed straight back to
drive the compressor or pump. Under 1 percent for Rankine (you compress a liquid),
40 to 60 percent for Brayton (you compress a gas) — that single contrast explains
why steam runs base-load power.

$$\mathrm{bwr} = \frac{w_{comp\ or\ pump}}{w_{turb}}$$

*Introduced:* [4.1](lessons/04-01-rankine-vapor-power-cycle.md)

### Air-standard assumptions

The modelling licence that makes gas cycles solvable: the working fluid is air as
an ideal gas, combustion is replaced by external heat addition, exhaust is
replaced by heat rejection to close the loop. **Cold-air-standard** adds constant
specific heats ($c_p = 1.005$, $k = 1.4$).

*Introduced:* [4.2](lessons/04-02-gas-power-cycles-otto-diesel.md)

### Coefficient of performance

Heat moved per unit of work bought. Above 1 routinely, because you are
*relocating* heat, not creating it — no first-law violation (energy still
balances: $q_H = q_L + w_{in}$).

$$\mathrm{COP}_R = \frac{q_L}{w_{in}}, \qquad \mathrm{COP}_{HP} = \frac{q_H}{w_{in}}$$

*Introduced:* [3.1](lessons/03-01-second-law-carnot-limit.md), built out in [4.4](lessons/04-04-refrigeration-heat-pump-cycles.md)

### Dead state

Ambient conditions $(T_0, p_0)$ — equilibrium with the surroundings, where no
further work can be extracted. An ocean of energy sitting at $T_0$ has **zero**
exergy.

*Introduced:* [4.5](lessons/04-05-psychrometrics-exergy.md)

### Flow exergy

The most work a kilogram of a stream could still deliver before it settles to the
dead state: its enthalpy edge, docked by the part the second law forces you to
throw away.

$$\psi = (h - h_0) - T_0(s - s_0) + \frac{V^2}{2} + gz \quad (\mathrm{kJ/kg})$$

*Introduced:* [4.5](lessons/04-05-psychrometrics-exergy.md)

## Formulas and rules

### Sign convention — read this before every problem

This course follows Çengel & Boles: **heat in is positive, work out is positive.**

$$\boxed{\,Q - W = \Delta U\,}, \qquad Q = \text{heat added \textbf{to} the system}, \quad W = \text{work done \textbf{by} the system}$$

| The problem says | You write |
|---|---|
| the gas expands / a turbine delivers power | $W > 0$ |
| the gas is compressed / a pump is driven | $W < 0$ (or quote $W_{in} = -W > 0$) |
| heat is added, a boiler fires | $Q > 0$ |
| heat is rejected, a condenser cools | $Q < 0$ |

**Reservoir problems are the exception:** $Q_H$ and $Q_L$ are written as positive
*magnitudes* with the direction carried by the words, so $W = Q_H - Q_L$.

**Versus [thermodynamics-physics](../thermodynamics-physics/reference.md):** the
sign rule is the *same* ($dU = \delta Q - \delta W$, work by the gas positive), so
nothing flips between the two courses. What differs is dress:

| | this course | thermodynamics-physics |
|---|---|---|
| pressure, volume | $p$, and specific $v$ in m³/**kg** | $P$, $V$ total, molar $v$ |
| lower case means | per **kilogram** | per **mole** |
| gas constant | specific $R = R_u/M$, kJ/(kg·K) | universal $R$, J/(mol·K) |
| heat capacities | $c_v, c_p$ in kJ/(kg·K) | $C_V, C_P$ in J/(mol·K) |
| adiabatic exponent | $k$ | $\gamma$ |
| reservoirs | $T_H, T_L$ and $Q_H, Q_L$ | $T_h, T_c$ and $Q_h, Q_c$ |
| energy unit | kJ (so $1\ \mathrm{kPa\cdot m^3} = 1\ \mathrm{kJ}$) | J |

*From* [1.5](lessons/01-05-work-and-heat.md) *and* [2.1](lessons/02-01-first-law-closed-systems.md)

### Fixing a state — which route to take

1. **Is it water, steam, or a refrigerant near its dome?** Use **tables**.
   Compare the given $T$ against $T_{sat}$ at the given $p$: below it, compressed
   liquid; equal, a saturated mixture needing $x$; above it, superheated vapor.
2. **Is it air or combustion products, far from the critical point?** Use
   $pv = RT$. Safe when $p_R \ll 1$, or $T_R \gtrsim 2$ at moderate pressure;
   unsafe near $T_R \approx 1$, $p_R \approx 1$, where $Z$ can sag to $0.3$.
3. **Rule of thumb for the whole course:** tables for water in vapor power cycles
   (Rankine), ideal gas for air in Otto, Diesel, and Brayton.

*From* [1.2](lessons/01-02-phase-behavior-pure-substance.md), [1.3](lessons/01-03-property-tables-quality.md), [1.4](lessons/01-04-ideal-gas-model-limits.md)

### Reading tables — quality, interpolation, compressed liquid

Inside the dome, every property is the same quality-weighted trip from $f$ to $g$
(the **lever rule**), for $y = v, u, h, s$ alike:

$$\boxed{\,y = y_f + x\,y_{fg}\,}, \qquad y_{fg} = y_g - y_f, \qquad x = \frac{y - y_f}{y_{fg}}$$

| Region | How you look it up |
|---|---|
| saturated mixture | saturated table by $T$ *or* $p$ (they're locked), then $y = y_f + x\,y_{fg}$ |
| superheated vapor | superheated table by $p$ **and** $T$ together — no quality exists |
| compressed liquid | $y \approx y_f(T)$ for $v$ and $u$, keyed on **temperature**, not pressure; refine $h$ with $h \approx h_f(T) + v_f\,[p - p_{sat}(T)]$ |

Between two table rows, interpolate linearly — the fraction of the way across the
inputs is the fraction of the way across the outputs:

$$y = y_1 + \frac{t - t_1}{t_2 - t_1}\,(y_2 - y_1)$$

*From* [1.3](lessons/01-03-property-tables-quality.md)

*From* [1.3](lessons/01-03-property-tables-quality.md), [1.5](lessons/01-05-work-and-heat.md), [4.1](lessons/04-01-rankine-vapor-power-cycle.md), [4.4](lessons/04-04-refrigeration-heat-pump-cycles.md)

### Ideal-gas relations

$$pv = RT, \qquad pV = mRT = nR_uT, \qquad Z = \frac{pv}{RT}, \qquad T_R = \frac{T}{T_{cr}}, \quad p_R = \frac{p}{p_{cr}}$$

For an ideal gas $u$ and $h$ depend on **temperature alone**, so in *any* process:

$$\Delta u = c_v\,\Delta T, \qquad \Delta h = c_p\,\Delta T, \qquad \boxed{\,c_p = c_v + R\,}, \qquad k = \frac{c_p}{c_v}$$

*From* [1.4](lessons/01-04-ideal-gas-model-limits.md), [2.1](lessons/02-01-first-law-closed-systems.md)

### Closed-system first law and the standard processes

$$Q - W = \Delta U = m(u_2 - u_1), \qquad q - w = \Delta u, \qquad \oint \delta Q = \oint \delta W \ \ (\text{a cycle, since } \Delta U = 0)$$

Keep $\Delta\mathrm{KE}$ and $\Delta\mathrm{PE}$ only when they actually change.

| Process | Constraint | Work $W = \int p\,dV$ | Heat |
|---|---|---|---|
| isochoric | $V$ fixed, $n = \infty$ | $0$ | $Q = \Delta U = mc_v\Delta T$ |
| isobaric | $p$ fixed, $n = 0$ | $p\,(V_2 - V_1)$ | $Q = \Delta H = mc_p\Delta T$ |
| isothermal (ideal gas) | $T$ fixed, $n = 1$ | $mRT\ln\dfrac{V_2}{V_1}$ | $Q = W$, $\Delta U = 0$ |
| polytropic | $pV^n = \mathrm{const}$ | $\dfrac{p_2V_2 - p_1V_1}{1-n}$ ($n \ne 1$) | from $Q = \Delta U + W$ |
| adiabatic reversible (ideal gas) | $n = k$ | $\dfrac{p_2V_2 - p_1V_1}{1-k}$ | $Q = 0$, so $W = -\Delta U$ |

*From* [1.5](lessons/01-05-work-and-heat.md), [2.1](lessons/02-01-first-law-closed-systems.md), [2.2](lessons/02-02-closed-system-processes.md)

### Control-volume balances

$$\dot m = \rho A V = \frac{A V}{v}, \qquad \sum_{in}\dot m - \sum_{out}\dot m = \frac{dm_{CV}}{dt} \;\xrightarrow{\ \text{steady}\ }\; \sum_{in}\dot m = \sum_{out}\dot m$$

**Steady-flow energy equation (SFEE)** — heat in positive, shaft work out positive:

$$\boxed{\;\dot Q - \dot W = \sum_{out}\dot m\left(h + \frac{V^2}{2} + gz\right) - \sum_{in}\dot m\left(h + \frac{V^2}{2} + gz\right)\;}$$

Single inlet and outlet, per kilogram:

$$q - w = (h_2 - h_1) + \frac{V_2^2 - V_1^2}{2} + g(z_2 - z_1)$$

Flow work never appears separately — it is already inside every $h$. Typical
$\Delta h$ runs hundreds of kJ/kg, while 50 m/s is only 1.25 kJ/kg and 10 m of
height is 0.1 kJ/kg: **drop KE except in nozzles and diffusers, drop PE except in
tall liquid columns.**

*From* [2.3](lessons/02-03-mass-energy-balance-control-volumes.md)

### Steady-flow devices — the SFEE with terms crossed out

| Device | What's zero | Balance |
|---|---|---|
| turbine | $\dot Q \approx 0$, $\Delta\mathrm{KE}\approx 0$ | $\dot W_{out} = \dot m\,(h_1 - h_2)$ |
| compressor / fan | $\dot Q \approx 0$, $\Delta\mathrm{KE}\approx 0$ | $\dot W_{in} = \dot m\,(h_2 - h_1)$ |
| pump (liquid) | as above, plus $v \approx \mathrm{const}$ | $w_{in} \approx v\,(p_2 - p_1)$ |
| nozzle / diffuser | $\dot W = 0$, $\dot Q \approx 0$ | $h_1 + \dfrac{V_1^2}{2} = h_2 + \dfrac{V_2^2}{2}$, so $V_2 = \sqrt{2(h_1-h_2) + V_1^2}$ |
| throttle | $\dot W = \dot Q = 0$, $\Delta\mathrm{KE}\approx 0$ | $h_2 = h_1$ |
| heat exchanger (both streams boxed) | $\dot W = 0$, adiabatic outside | $\dot m_h(h_{h,in} - h_{h,out}) = \dot m_c(h_{c,out} - h_{c,in})$ |
| heat exchanger (one stream boxed) | $\dot W = 0$ | $\dot Q = \dot m\,(h_{out} - h_{in})$ |
| mixing chamber | $\dot W = \dot Q = 0$ | $\dot m_1 + \dot m_2 = \dot m_3$ and $\dot m_1h_1 + \dot m_2h_2 = \dot m_3h_3$ |

Exchanger **effectiveness**: $\varepsilon = \dot Q_{actual}/\dot Q_{max}$ with
$\dot Q_{max} = (\dot m c_p)_{min}\,(T_{h,in} - T_{c,in})$.

**In the nozzle equation, convert $\Delta h$ from kJ/kg to J/kg (multiply by 1000)
before it meets $V^2$** — the single most common arithmetic error in the course.

*From* [2.4](lessons/02-04-steady-flow-devices-work.md), [2.5](lessons/02-05-steady-flow-devices-no-work.md)

### The second law and the Carnot ceiling

**Kelvin–Planck:** no cyclic device makes net work while touching only one
reservoir. **Clausius:** no cyclic device moves heat cold-to-hot as its sole
effect. They are one law — violate either and you can build a violation of the
other.

$$W = Q_H - Q_L, \qquad \eta = \frac{W}{Q_H} = 1 - \frac{Q_L}{Q_H} \;\le\; \boxed{\,\eta_{Carnot} = 1 - \frac{T_L}{T_H}\,}$$

$$\mathrm{COP}_{R,Carnot} = \frac{T_L}{T_H - T_L}, \qquad \mathrm{COP}_{HP,Carnot} = \frac{T_H}{T_H - T_L}, \qquad \mathrm{COP}_{HP} = \mathrm{COP}_R + 1$$

All temperatures in kelvin. On $T$–$s$ axes a Carnot cycle is a **rectangle**: two
horizontal reversible isotherms, two vertical isentropes, area enclosed = net heat
= net work.

*From* [3.1](lessons/03-01-second-law-carnot-limit.md), [3.2](lessons/03-02-entropy-clausius-inequality.md), [4.4](lessons/04-04-refrigeration-heat-pump-cycles.md)

### Entropy — computing it and auditing with it

$$\oint\frac{\delta Q}{T} \le 0, \qquad dS = \left(\frac{\delta Q}{T}\right)_{rev}, \qquad Q_{rev} = \int_1^2 T\,dS \ \ (\text{area under a reversible } T\text{–}s \text{ path})$$

**The two $Tds$ relations** — property relations, so valid between any two
equilibrium states, reversible path or not:

$$\boxed{\,T\,ds = du + p\,dv\,} \qquad\qquad \boxed{\,T\,ds = dh - v\,dp\,}$$

**Ideal gas, constant specific heats:**

$$\Delta s = c_v\ln\frac{T_2}{T_1} + R\ln\frac{v_2}{v_1} \;=\; c_p\ln\frac{T_2}{T_1} - R\ln\frac{p_2}{p_1}$$

**Inside the dome:** $s = s_f + x\,s_{fg}$, same lever rule as every other property.

**Entropy balance** — the audit. Closed system, and steady-flow control volume:

$$\Delta S = \int_1^2\frac{\delta Q}{T_b} + S_{gen}, \qquad \dot S_{gen} = \sum_{out}\dot m\,s - \sum_{in}\dot m\,s - \sum_k \frac{\dot Q_k}{T_k} \;\ge\; 0$$

| $S_{gen}$ | verdict |
|---|---|
| $=0$ | reversible — the idealized limit |
| $>0$ | irreversible — every real process |
| $<0$ | impossible — the device cannot exist |

Adiabatic single-stream device: $\dot S_{gen} = \dot m(s_{out} - s_{in}) \ge 0$, so
it can only *raise* the fluid's entropy. Heat $Q$ leaking across a finite gap from
$T_H$ to $T_L$ generates $S_{gen} = Q\left(\dfrac{1}{T_L} - \dfrac{1}{T_H}\right)$.

*From* [3.2](lessons/03-02-entropy-clausius-inequality.md), [3.3](lessons/03-03-entropy-balance-tds-relations.md)

### Isentropic relations and isentropic efficiencies

Ideal gas with constant $k$ — the workhorse of every gas cycle:

$$\frac{T_2}{T_1} = \left(\frac{p_2}{p_1}\right)^{(k-1)/k} = \left(\frac{v_1}{v_2}\right)^{k-1}, \qquad pv^k = \mathrm{const}, \qquad Tv^{k-1} = \mathrm{const}$$

Steam has no such shortcut: set $s_{2s} = s_1$ at the exit pressure and go to the
tables. If that entropy lands under the dome,

$$x_{2s} = \frac{s_1 - s_f}{s_{fg}}, \qquad h_{2s} = h_f + x_{2s}\,h_{fg}$$

Efficiencies, all compared at the **same inlet state and same exit pressure**:

$$\eta_T = \frac{h_1 - h_{2a}}{h_1 - h_{2s}}, \qquad \eta_C = \frac{h_{2s} - h_1}{h_{2a} - h_1}, \qquad \eta_N = \frac{V_{2a}^2}{V_{2s}^2}$$

The compressor/pump ratio is **inverted** relative to the turbine, because there
both terms are work *inputs* and the ideal one is smaller. The rule that never
lies: arrange the ratio so the answer sits below 1.

*From* [3.4](lessons/03-04-isentropic-processes-efficiency.md)

### Rankine — the vapor power cycle

States: **1** condenser exit (saturated liquid) → **2** pump exit → **3** boiler
exit (the hot steam) → **4** turbine exit. Ideal Rankine means the pump and
turbine are isentropic ($s_2 = s_1$, $s_4 = s_3$) and the boiler and condenser are
constant-pressure — that is what lets you *find* states 2 and 4.

| Leg | Device | Per kilogram |
|---|---|---|
| 1→2 | pump | $w_p = v_1(p_2 - p_1)$, $\;h_2 = h_1 + w_p$ |
| 2→3 | boiler | $q_{in} = h_3 - h_2$ |
| 3→4 | turbine | $w_t = h_3 - h_4$, with $x_4 = \dfrac{s_3 - s_f}{s_{fg}}$ at $p_4$, then $h_4 = h_f + x_4h_{fg}$ |
| 4→1 | condenser | $q_{out} = h_4 - h_1$ |

$$w_{net} = (h_3-h_4) - (h_2-h_1) = q_{in} - q_{out}, \qquad \boxed{\;\eta_{th} = \frac{w_{net}}{q_{in}} = 1 - \frac{q_{out}}{q_{in}}\;}, \qquad \mathrm{bwr} = \frac{w_p}{w_t}$$

Typical numbers: $\eta_{th} \approx 0.39$ at 8 MPa / 480 °C to a 10 kPa condenser,
with $\mathrm{bwr} \approx 0.0065$. **Superheat** raises the average temperature of
heat addition (better $\eta$, drier exit); **reheat** finishes the expansion after
a return trip through the boiler, keeping $x_4$ high so droplets don't erode
blades.

*From* [4.1](lessons/04-01-rankine-vapor-power-cycle.md)

### Otto and Diesel — reciprocating gas cycles

Both are closed-system, air-standard, states 1→2→3→4→1 with **isentropic**
compression (1→2) and expansion (3→4). They differ only in the heat-addition leg.

| | Otto | Diesel |
|---|---|---|
| 2→3 heat addition | constant **volume**: $q_{in} = c_v(T_3-T_2)$ | constant **pressure**: $q_{in} = c_p(T_3-T_2)$ |
| 4→1 heat rejection | $q_{out} = c_v(T_4-T_1)$ | $q_{out} = c_v(T_4-T_1)$ |
| ratios | $r = V_1/V_2 = V_{max}/V_{min}$ | also $r_c = V_3/V_2$ (cutoff) |
| typical $r$ | 8–11 (knock-limited) | 14–24 (nothing to knock) |

$$\boxed{\;\eta_{Otto} = 1 - \frac{1}{r^{\,k-1}}\;} \qquad\qquad \boxed{\;\eta_{Diesel} = 1 - \frac{1}{r^{\,k-1}}\left[\frac{r_c^{\,k}-1}{k\,(r_c-1)}\right]\;}$$

The bracket is **always greater than 1** for $r_c > 1$, so at equal $r$ the Diesel
loses to the Otto; as $r_c \to 1$ it collapses to 1 and the two formulas agree.
Diesels win in practice only by running a much higher $r$.

*From* [4.2](lessons/04-02-gas-power-cycles-otto-diesel.md)

### Brayton — the gas-turbine cycle

Steady-flow, air-standard, states 1→2→3→4→1: isentropic compression, constant-$p$
combustion, isentropic expansion, constant-$p$ rejection. Both isentropic legs
span the same pressure ratio, so both use the same factor $r_p^{(k-1)/k}$:

$$r_p = \frac{p_2}{p_1}, \qquad \frac{T_2}{T_1} = \frac{T_3}{T_4} = r_p^{(k-1)/k}$$

$$q_{in} = c_p(T_3-T_2), \quad w_{comp} = c_p(T_2-T_1), \quad w_{turb} = c_p(T_3-T_4)$$

$$\boxed{\;\eta_{Brayton} = 1 - \frac{1}{r_p^{(k-1)/k}}\;}, \qquad \mathrm{bwr} = \frac{T_2-T_1}{T_3-T_4} \approx 0.4\text{–}0.6$$

Efficiency rises with $r_p$ forever, but **net work per kilogram peaks** at
$r_p^{(k-1)/k} = \sqrt{T_3/T_1}$ (equivalently $T_2 = T_4 = \sqrt{T_1T_3}$), and
real designs sit near that peak. Because $w_{net}$ is a small difference of two
large works, component isentropic efficiencies matter far more here than in a
steam plant.

*From* [4.3](lessons/04-03-brayton-gas-turbine-cycle.md)

### Vapor-compression refrigeration and heat pumps

States: **1** evaporator exit (saturated vapor) → **2** compressor exit
(superheated, ideally $s_2 = s_1$) → **3** condenser exit (saturated liquid) → **4**
after the throttle.

| Leg | Device | Per kilogram |
|---|---|---|
| 1→2 | compressor | $w_{in} = h_2 - h_1$ |
| 2→3 | condenser | $q_H = h_2 - h_3$ (rejected) |
| 3→4 | throttle | $h_4 = h_3$ |
| 4→1 | evaporator | $q_L = h_1 - h_4$ (the cooling load) |

$$\mathrm{COP}_R = \frac{h_1-h_4}{h_2-h_1}, \qquad \mathrm{COP}_{HP} = \frac{h_2-h_3}{h_2-h_1}, \qquad q_H = q_L + w_{in} \;\Rightarrow\; \mathrm{COP}_{HP} = \mathrm{COP}_R + 1$$

Both are capped by the Carnot COPs above. The gap between real and Carnot is
mostly the throttle, which destroys the liquid's pressure energy instead of
recovering work — kept anyway because a valve is cheap and a two-phase turbine
is not.

*From* [4.4](lessons/04-04-refrigeration-heat-pump-cycles.md)

### Psychrometrics

$$\omega = \frac{m_v}{m_a} = 0.622\,\frac{p_v}{p - p_v}, \qquad \phi = \frac{p_v}{p_g(T)}, \qquad p_g(T_{dp}) = p_v$$

The $0.622$ is $M_v/M_a = 18.02/28.97$. $\omega$ is how much water is *actually*
there; $\phi$ is how close it is to raining out; $T_{dp}$ depends only on $p_v$,
so heating air changes $\phi$ but never $T_{dp}$. Read $p_g(T)$ off the same
saturation table as everything else.

*From* [4.5](lessons/04-05-psychrometrics-exergy.md)

### Exergy — the second-law scorecard

$$\psi = (h-h_0) - T_0(s-s_0) + \frac{V^2}{2} + gz, \qquad \boxed{\,X_{dest} = T_0\,S_{gen} \ge 0\,} \ \ (\text{Gouy–Stodola})$$

$$\eta_{II} = \frac{\text{exergy recovered}}{\text{exergy supplied}}$$

Heat $Q$ delivered at $T_H$ against a dead state $T_0$ carries exergy
$Q\left(1 - T_0/T_H\right)$ — exactly the Carnot work it could have produced.
Energy is conserved; exergy is *destroyed*. A first-law efficiency of 40 percent
can be a second-law efficiency of 80 percent if 40 was near the ceiling all along.

*From* [4.5](lessons/04-05-psychrometrics-exergy.md)

## Pitfalls

### Signs and conventions

- $Q - W = \Delta U$ with $W$ positive *out* is this course's convention; the physics/chemistry $Q + W = \Delta U$ is equally correct and not interchangeable mid-problem. *([1.5](lessons/01-05-work-and-heat.md), [2.1](lessons/02-01-first-law-closed-systems.md))*
- $W = \int p\,dV$ is signed: positive expanding, negative compressing. Feed the signed value into the balance rather than the magnitude. *([2.2](lessons/02-02-closed-system-processes.md))*
- Any temperature appearing in a ratio, power law, or logarithm must be **kelvin** — Carnot efficiency, COP, isentropic relations, $\Delta s$ logs, the Clausius integral. Only bare differences like $T_H - T_L$ survive Celsius. *([1.4](lessons/01-04-ideal-gas-model-limits.md), [3.1](lessons/03-01-second-law-carnot-limit.md), [3.2](lessons/03-02-entropy-clausius-inequality.md), [3.3](lessons/03-03-entropy-balance-tds-relations.md))*
- Pressure in $pv = RT$ is **absolute**, not gauge. *([1.4](lessons/01-04-ideal-gas-model-limits.md))*

### Boundaries and state

- The boundary is a *choice*, often imaginary (the plane across a pipe inlet), and "closed vs. control volume" is decided by the **process**, not the hardware — a capped bottle is closed until you pour. *([1.1](lessons/01-01-system-vs-control-volume-state.md))*
- Two properties fix a state only if they are **independent and intensive**. Inside the dome $T$ and $p$ are locked to each other and carry one fact, not two — you need $v$, $u$, $h$, $s$, or $x$. *([1.1](lessons/01-01-system-vs-control-volume-state.md), [1.2](lessons/01-02-phase-behavior-pure-substance.md), [1.3](lessons/01-03-property-tables-quality.md))*
- "Saturated" does not mean fully vaporized — it means *on the dome*. Saturated liquid (f) is 100 percent liquid; saturated vapor (g) is 100 percent vapor; both are saturated. *([1.2](lessons/01-02-phase-behavior-pure-substance.md))*
- Adding heat does not always raise temperature: across a phase change it buys latent heat at fixed $T$, and a gas doing more work than the heat supplied actually cools. *([1.2](lessons/01-02-phase-behavior-pure-substance.md), [2.1](lessons/02-01-first-law-closed-systems.md))*
- The compressed-liquid approximation keys on **temperature**: at 5 MPa and 80 °C use $v_f$ and $u_f$ read at 80 °C. *([1.3](lessons/01-03-property-tables-quality.md))*
- Never interpolate across the dome edge — properties kink there. Straddling rows means you opened the wrong table. *([1.3](lessons/01-03-property-tables-quality.md))*

### The ideal-gas model

- "High pressure" and "high temperature" mean nothing absolute — only $T_R$ and $p_R$, measured against *that substance's* critical point, decide whether $pv=RT$ is safe. Steam in a power cycle is not. *([1.4](lessons/01-04-ideal-gas-model-limits.md))*
- Only $R_u$ is universal; the specific $R = R_u/M$ changes with the gas. *([1.4](lessons/01-04-ideal-gas-model-limits.md))*
- $\Delta u = c_v\Delta T$ and $\Delta h = c_p\Delta T$ hold for an ideal gas in **any** process, not just constant-$V$ or constant-$p$ ones — $u$ and $h$ can only see temperature. Conversely $c_p$ is not a flow-only quantity: a piston under constant load uses it too. *([2.1](lessons/02-01-first-law-closed-systems.md), [2.2](lessons/02-02-closed-system-processes.md))*
- $n = 1$ is isothermal, $n = k$ is adiabatic — different curves, and the adiabat falls steeper because the gas cools as it expands. *([2.2](lessons/02-02-closed-system-processes.md))*

### Work, heat, and flow

- A system does not "contain" heat or work; they exist only while crossing a boundary. There is no $\Delta Q$ or $\Delta W$, which is exactly why they are path-dependent and $U$ is not. *([1.5](lessons/01-05-work-and-heat.md))*
- $\int p\,dV$ with the *system's own* pressure requires a quasi-equilibrium path. A free expansion into vacuum does zero boundary work, not $\int p_{gas}\,dV$. *([1.5](lessons/01-05-work-and-heat.md))*
- A flowing stream carries **enthalpy**, not internal energy — and flow work is already inside $h$, so never add it as a separate term. Use $u$ for closed systems, $h$ for anything with a port. *([2.3](lessons/02-03-mass-energy-balance-control-volumes.md), [2.4](lessons/02-04-steady-flow-devices-work.md))*
- Decide KE and PE per device rather than by habit: negligible for turbines, throttles, and boilers; the entire point for nozzles and diffusers. *([2.3](lessons/02-03-mass-energy-balance-control-volumes.md), [2.5](lessons/02-05-steady-flow-devices-no-work.md))*
- Turbine power comes from the **enthalpy** drop, not the pressure drop — two turbines with equal pressure drop and different exit qualities give different power. *([2.4](lessons/02-04-steady-flow-devices-work.md))*
- Convert $\Delta h$ to J/kg before pairing it with $V^2$ in a nozzle, or the exit speed comes out about 30 times too small. *([2.4](lessons/02-04-steady-flow-devices-work.md))*
- A throttle holds $h$, **not** $T$. Constant temperature follows only for an ideal gas; real fluids cool, which is the entire refrigeration industry. *([2.5](lessons/02-05-steady-flow-devices-no-work.md))*
- Pick one heat-exchanger boundary and stay in it: around both streams there is no $\dot Q$ term, around one stream there is. *([2.5](lessons/02-05-steady-flow-devices-no-work.md))*

### Entropy and the second law

- Rejected heat $Q_L > 0$ is required by the second law, not an engineering failure — the cooling tower is physics. And a hotter furnace helps only slowly: 1500 K against a 300 K sink still caps you at 0.80. *([3.1](lessons/03-01-second-law-carnot-limit.md))*
- Adiabatic is not isentropic. A real adiabatic turbine generates entropy, so its exit entropy is *higher* than its inlet. Isentropic = adiabatic **and** reversible. *([3.2](lessons/03-02-entropy-clausius-inequality.md), [3.4](lessons/03-04-isentropic-processes-efficiency.md))*
- Compute $\Delta s$ from endpoint properties (tables or the $Tds$ relations), never from the real irreversible $\int\delta Q/T$ — the shortfall between them *is* $S_{gen}$. The $Tds$ relations are property relations and hold on any path. *([3.2](lessons/03-02-entropy-clausius-inequality.md), [3.3](lessons/03-03-entropy-balance-tds-relations.md))*
- $\Delta s < 0$ for a system is legal (it rejected heat); $S_{gen} < 0$ is not. Always ask "generated," not "changed." *([3.3](lessons/03-03-entropy-balance-tds-relations.md))*
- The compressor efficiency is inverted relative to the turbine's, and both are only meaningful when the real and ideal exits share the **same exit pressure**. *([3.4](lessons/03-04-isentropic-processes-efficiency.md))*

### Cycles

- The pump work in Rankine is small but not conceptually optional — it is the whole reason a vapor cycle beats a gas cycle. Conversely, never drop the compressor in Brayton, where it eats 40 to 60 percent of the turbine output. *([4.1](lessons/04-01-rankine-vapor-power-cycle.md), [4.3](lessons/04-03-brayton-gas-turbine-cycle.md))*
- Get the turbine exit state from the **entropy** ($s_4 = s_3$, then quality), never from the temperature — inside the dome, temperature tells you nothing new. And expect it wet: $x_4 < 1$ is normal, and pushing it back up is what superheat and reheat are for. *([4.1](lessons/04-01-rankine-vapor-power-cycle.md))*
- Match the specific heat to the leg: constant-volume legs use $c_v$, the Diesel's constant-pressure burn uses $c_p$. *([4.2](lessons/04-02-gas-power-cycles-otto-diesel.md))*
- At equal compression ratio Diesel is *less* efficient than Otto, and a longer burn (larger $r_c$) makes it worse still. Diesels win by running a higher $r$, so always ask "at what $r$?" before comparing. *([4.2](lessons/04-02-gas-power-cycles-otto-diesel.md))*
- Higher $r_p$ raises ideal Brayton efficiency but net work per kilogram peaks and then falls, and component losses bite a thin $w_{net}$ margin far harder than they bite a steam plant. *([4.3](lessons/04-03-brayton-gas-turbine-cycle.md))*
- COP rises as $T_H - T_L$ shrinks — a colder freezer or a hotter day *lowers* it. And the refrigerator's prize is $q_L$ while the heat pump's is $q_H$; same machine, different numerator. *([4.4](lessons/04-04-refrigeration-heat-pump-cycles.md))*
- Relative humidity is not "how much water" — saturated 30 °C air is still over 95 percent dry air by mass. $\omega$ is the amount, $\phi$ is the closeness to condensing, and $T_{dp}$ ignores the current temperature entirely. *([4.5](lessons/04-05-psychrometrics-exergy.md))*
- Energy is conserved, exergy is destroyed — an ocean at $T_0$ holds enormous energy and zero work potential. *([4.5](lessons/04-05-psychrometrics-exergy.md))*
