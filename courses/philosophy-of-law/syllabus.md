# Philosophy of Law — Syllabus

> Politics & Society · Tier 2 · ~23 lessons · Prereqs: [`political-philosophy`](../political-philosophy/syllabus.md) (and through it [`ethics`](../ethics/syllabus.md) and [`philosophical-method`](../philosophical-method/syllabus.md)) · Roadmap id: `philosophy-of-law`

## Goal

This course owns jurisprudence for the whole library: what law is, how judges should decide the cases the rules do not settle, whether anyone owes the law obedience as law, what justifies punishing people, and what it means to have a legal right or to be legally responsible for a harm. You will learn the three great answers to "what is law?" at the strength their best defenders would recognize. Legal positivism runs from Austin's command theory through Hart's rule of recognition to Raz on authority and the inclusive/exclusive split. Natural law jurisprudence runs from Aquinas through Finnis and Fuller's inner morality of law. Dworkin's law as integrity is the third. Then the course runs those theories on the questions they were built for: hard cases, interpretation, civil disobedience, law under tyranny, whether international law is law, retributive and consequentialist punishment, mercy, rights, excuses and causation. The course works by close reading wherever the texts are public domain: Aquinas's treatise on law, Austin, Bentham, Holmes's "The Path of the Law", Kant on punishment, Hohfeld, and American judicial opinions (*Riggs v. Palmer*, *Palsgraf*). You will finish able to reconstruct each theory's central argument, run it on a fresh case, and name the premise two rivals actually disagree about. The course takes no side. It deliberately skips US constitutional doctrine and the justices' own interpretive theories ([`constitutional-law`](../constitutional-law/syllabus.md)), political obligation as a question about the state ([`political-philosophy`](../political-philosophy/syllabus.md) Module 1), natural law as an ethical theory ([`ethics`](../ethics/syllabus.md) 4.4-4.5), the metaphysics of free will and causation ([`metaphysics`](../metaphysics/syllabus.md)), the enforcement of morality debate (political-philosophy 3.3), law and economics beyond one formal deterrence model, critical legal studies and feminist jurisprudence beyond a mention, comparative law, and canon law (not in the library).

## How practice works in this course

Problems follow [HUMANITIES-BUILD-BRIEF.md](../../HUMANITIES-BUILD-BRIEF.md): every part declares its kind, and every solution is a rubric (must-hit moves, wrong turns) plus a model answer.

- **Exegetical parts are strict.** What Austin means by a command, what Hart's rule of recognition does that a habit of obedience cannot, which of Fuller's eight desiderata a regime violates, what Aquinas says a law contrary to natural law is, what *Riggs v. Palmer* held and on what ground, which Hohfeldian position is the correlative of a power, what the but-for test says about two merged fires. These have right answers.
- **Evaluative parts are verdict-neutral.** No rubric rewards concluding that positivism beats natural law, that Dworkin is right about hard cases, that there is or is not an obligation to obey the law, that retributivism is true, or that international law is law. The must-hit moves are ones either side has to make: state the theory precisely, run it on the case, name the premise the objection attacks, say whether the objection generalizes. Religious and secular positions (Aquinas and Finnis, Hart and Raz, Kant and Bentham) get the same treatment.
- **Close reading carries more weight here than in political-philosophy.** Public-domain texts are quoted and read closely: Aquinas (English Dominican Province translation), Austin's *Province of Jurisprudence Determined*, Bentham's *Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation*, Holmes's "The Path of the Law", Kant's *Philosophy of Law* (Hastie translation), Thoreau's "Civil Disobedience", Hohfeld's 1913 article, and American judicial opinions, which are public domain. Modern authors (Hart, Fuller, Dworkin, Raz, Finnis, Feinberg, Duff, Murphy, Moore) are paraphrased and cited, never quoted at length; so is Martin Luther King Jr.'s "Letter from Birmingham Jail", which is under copyright. Every module also uses apply-to-a-hard-case, find-the-crux and at least one of reconstruct, diagnose, counterexample or steelman-and-reply.
- **Formal parts are light:** an expected-sanction deterrence calculation (5.1) and Hohfeld's correlative and opposite relations as a small logic (6.1).

## Dangerous Checklist

When you finish, you can:

- [ ] State Austin's command theory precisely and give Hart's three main objections to it (power-conferring rules, the continuity of sovereignty, the gunman writ large), saying which one the theory can absorb
- [ ] Explain what the rule of recognition is, what the internal point of view adds to Holmes's bad man, and what must be true of officials and citizens for a legal system to exist
- [ ] Reconstruct Raz's argument from law's claim to authority to the sources thesis, and say what separates inclusive from exclusive positivism on a constitution with moral terms in it
- [ ] Say whether *lex iniusta non est lex*, in Aquinas and in Finnis, is a claim about legal validity, binding force, or the central case of law, and point to the text that decides it
- [ ] Diagnose which of Fuller's eight ways to fail to make law a regime commits, and state the positivist's reply that these are virtues of efficacy, not morality
- [ ] Run the Hart-Fuller debate on a case of law under tyranny, and say what Radbruch's formula would add
- [ ] Separate rules from principles in Dworkin's sense, run *Riggs v. Palmer* through Hart's discretion thesis and through law as integrity, and say what fit and justification each require
- [ ] Apply intentionalist, textualist and purposivist accounts of interpretation to a legal text, and say where each stops determining an answer
- [ ] Say whether there is a general obligation to obey the law as such, distinguish civil disobedience from conscientious objection, and assess how a legal system should respond to either
- [ ] Argue either side of whether international law is law, using Austin, Hart and at least one later view
- [ ] Run consequentialist, retributive and expressive theories of punishment on a sentencing case, compute an expected sanction, and state the paradox of mercy
- [ ] Classify a legal relation in Hohfeld's scheme and test the will and interest theories of rights on a case that splits them
- [ ] Tell a justification from an excuse, and decide a causation problem with the but-for test, the NESS test and proximate cause, saying where they diverge

## Modules

### Module 1: Law as social fact: from command to authority

The positivist tradition, built one objection at a time: Austin's commands, Hart's rules, Raz's authority.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1.1 | Austin: law as the command of a sovereign | State the command theory and see what it was built to exclude | Bentham as forerunner, command, duty and sanction, the sovereign and the habit of obedience, positive law vs positive morality, law's existence separated from its merit; *The Province of Jurisprudence Determined* (1832) |
| 1.2 | Being obliged vs having an obligation | Reconstruct Hart's case against the command theory and against Holmes's prediction theory | the gunman writ large, duty-imposing vs power-conferring rules, nullity is not a sanction, continuity and persistence of law across sovereigns, self-binding legislation; Holmes's bad man and law as prophecy of what courts will do ("The Path of the Law", 1897) |
| 1.3 | The rule of recognition and the internal point of view | Explain how a legal system identifies its own law and why that needs accepted rules | primary and secondary rules (recognition, change, adjudication), the defects of a regime of primary rules only, the internal vs external point of view, officials' acceptance and citizens' obedience as the minimum conditions of a legal system; Kelsen's basic norm named as contrast; Weber's legitimacy-as-belief cited to [`social-theory`](../social-theory/syllabus.md) 4.3 |
| 1.4 | Raz: authority and the sources thesis | Reconstruct the argument from law's claim to legitimate authority to the sources thesis | law claims authority, the normal justification thesis, the dependence thesis, pre-emptive reasons, the service conception, why an authority's directives must be identifiable without re-running the moral reasons (the sources thesis) |

**Boss problem 1:** (a) Close-read the passage in Austin's first lecture of *The Province of Jurisprudence Determined* (1832, public domain) where he defines a command and links command, duty and sanction. State the three notions and how each depends on the others, pointing to the words that decide it. *Exegetical.* (b) Close-read the passage in Holmes's "The Path of the Law" (1897, public domain) that introduces the bad man. Say what Holmes offers the bad man's standpoint as a test *for*, and whether the passage claims that law just is a prediction or only that a prediction is what the bad man cares about. *Exegetical.* (c) An invented case: a small island community has customary rules, no legislature and no courts, and a visiting official declares one of its customs void. Apply Hart's account of primary and secondary rules and say whether the community has a legal system, and what the official's declaration presupposes. *Exegetical, then evaluative where the facts leave it open.* (d) In 300-500 words: does Hart's internal point of view answer Holmes, or change the subject? Any verdict, if Hart's distinction between being obliged and having an obligation is stated precisely and the strongest defence of the predictive view is met. *Evaluative.*

### Module 2: Natural law jurisprudence

The rival tradition: that law is essentially connected to reason and the common good, in Aquinas's metaphysical version, Finnis's methodological one and Fuller's procedural one, and what happened when these met positivism over law under tyranny.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2.1 | Aquinas: an unjust law as a theory of law | Read the treatise on law as jurisprudence and decide what *lex iniusta non est lex* claims | the definition of law (ST I-II q.90 a.4), derivation of human law from natural law by conclusion and by determination (q.95 a.2), unjust laws and binding in conscience (q.96 a.4), Augustine's dictum as Aquinas uses it; validity vs binding force; the strong vs weak natural law thesis (Murphy); natural law as ethics cited to [`ethics`](../ethics/syllabus.md) 4.4, the historical and political reading to [`history-of-political-thought`](../history-of-political-thought/syllabus.md) 2.3 |
| 2.2 | Finnis: the central case of law | State Finnis's argument that describing law needs a practical, evaluative point of view | *Natural Law and Natural Rights* (1980), central case and focal meaning, the practically reasonable viewpoint, unjust law as law in a secondary sense, the positivist's "what's the disagreement?" reply; basic goods assumed from [`ethics`](../ethics/syllabus.md) 4.5 |
| 2.3 | Fuller: the inner morality of law | Diagnose the ways a lawmaker can fail to make law at all, and ask whether those failures are moral | *The Morality of Law* (1964), Rex's eight failures (generality, promulgation, non-retroactivity, clarity, consistency, possibility of compliance, constancy, congruence of official action with declared rule), morality of duty vs aspiration, procedural natural law, Hart's "morality of poisoning" objection, Raz's rule of law as a virtue of efficacy |
| 2.4 | The Hart-Fuller debate and law under tyranny | Run the separation of law and morals on a case of wicked law and its aftermath | the 1958 exchange in the *Harvard Law Review*, the separation thesis, the grudge-informer case as each read it, retroactive punishment vs declaring the old statute invalid, fidelity to law, Radbruch's formula and its use in the post-reunification border-guard prosecutions; the Hart-Devlin debate cited to [`political-philosophy`](../political-philosophy/syllabus.md) 3.3 |

**Boss problem 2:** (a) Close-read ST I-II q.95 a.2 and q.96 a.4 (English Dominican translation, public domain). Does Aquinas say an unjust human law is no law at all, not law in the full sense, or law that does not bind in conscience? Point to the words that decide it, and say whether the two articles say the same thing. *Exegetical.* (b) Reconstruct Fuller's argument that a regime failing his desiderata badly enough has no legal system, in numbered premises, and name the premise Hart's poisoning objection denies. *Exegetical.* (c) An invented regime governs by secret decrees, some applied retroactively; after it falls, a new government must decide whether to prosecute an informer who acted under one decree. State what Hart (retroactive statute, honestly labelled), Fuller (the decree was not law), and Radbruch's formula each recommend, graded for fidelity to each view. *Exegetical.* (d) Name the crux between Hart and Fuller over the informer. *Exegetical.* (e) In 300-500 words: which response to the informer best respects the rule of law? Any verdict, if the answer engages both the cost of retroactivity and the cost of treating the decree as valid, and states the strongest rival view at full strength. *Evaluative.*

| 3.2 | Positivism answers: inclusive and exclusive | Say how each form of positivism handles principles and moral terms in law | inclusive (soft, incorporationist) positivism: Hart's Postscript (1994), Waluchow, Coleman; exclusive positivism from Raz's authority argument (1.4); moral terms in a constitution as the test case; directed-power reading of principles; what each concedes to Dworkin |
| 3.3 | Dworkin: law as integrity | State law as integrity and run fit and justification on a line of cases | *Law's Empire* (1986), the semantic sting, law as an interpretive concept, conventionalism vs pragmatism vs integrity, fit and justification, Hercules, the chain novel, checkerboard statutes, the one-right-answer thesis; the moral reading of constitutions cited to [`political-philosophy`](../political-philosophy/syllabus.md) 5.2 |

**Boss problem 3:** (a) Close-read *Riggs v. Palmer* (N.Y. 1889, public domain): the majority's use of the maxim that no one may profit from his own wrong, and the dissent's reply that the statute of wills is plain. Say which words of each opinion treat the maxim as law and which as something outside it. *Exegetical.* (b) Reconstruct Dworkin's argument from *Riggs* against the positivist model of rules in numbered premises, and name the premise inclusive positivism denies and the premise exclusive positivism denies. *Exegetical.* (c) An invented statute provides that "no vehicle may be driven on the town green"; an ambulance, a toddler's electric toy car and a decommissioned tank mounted as a memorial are each at issue. Say what an intentionalist, a textualist and a purposivist would each conclude, and where each stops giving a verdict. *Exegetical, then evaluative.* (d) In 300-500 words: in the toy-car case, does the judge have discretion in Hart's sense, or is there a right answer Hercules could find? Any verdict, if the answer uses fit and justification, states Hart's Postscript reply at full strength, and says what would count as evidence for the one-right-answer thesis. *Evaluative.*

**Boss problem 4:** (a) Close-read the passage of Thoreau's "Civil Disobedience" (1849, public domain) that asks whether unjust laws should be obeyed, amended while obeyed, or transgressed at once. ### Module 6: Rights, responsibility and causation

The analytic toolkit lawyers use on every case: what a right is, when a person is responsible, and when an act caused a harm.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 6.1 | Hohfeld's analysis of rights | Classify a legal relation and derive its correlative and opposite | Hohfeld's "Some Fundamental Legal Conceptions as Applied in Judicial Reasoning" (1913), claim-right and duty, privilege (liberty) and no-right, power and liability, immunity and disability, correlatives and opposites, first-order vs second-order positions, property as a bundle of Hohfeldian relations |
| 6.2 | Will and interest theories | Test both theories of what makes something a right on the cases that split them | Hart's will (choice) theory, the interest (benefit) theory (Raz, MacCormick), children and incapacitated adults, unwaivable rights, third-party beneficiaries, criminal law and rights against the state, Kramer, Simmonds and Steiner's *A Debate Over Rights* (1998), Wenar's several-functions view named |
| 6.3 | Responsibility, justification and excuse | Separate the senses of responsibility and classify a defense | Hart's four senses (role, causal, liability, capacity), *mens rea* and strict liability, justification (self-defense, necessity) vs excuse (duress, insanity), the M'Naghten rules (1843), omissions and duties to act (doing and allowing cited to [`ethics`](../ethics/syllabus.md) 3.1), intention vs foresight in law (double effect cited to [`ethics`](../ethics/syllabus.md) 3.2); capacity and free will cited to [`metaphysics`](../metaphysics/syllabus.md) 6.1-6.3 |
| 6.4 | Legal causation and moral luck | Decide a causation problem with the legal tests and ask whether results should matter to liability | the but-for test and its failure on overdetermination (the merged fires) and preemption (analysis cited to [`metaphysics`](../metaphysics/syllabus.md) 4.2), Wright's NESS test, proximate cause and foreseeability, *Palsgraf v. **Boss problem 6:** (a) Close-read the majority opinion by Cardozo and the dissent by Andrews in *Palsgraf* (N.Y. 1928, public domain). Say what question each treats as decisive (duty to this plaintiff, or proximate cause of her harm), and which words of each opinion decide it. *Exegetical.* (b) An invented case: two campers, acting independently, each carelessly start a fire; the fires merge and burn a cabin that either fire alone would have destroyed. Apply the but-for test and the NESS test and say whether each camper caused the loss. *Exegetical.* (c) Classify each of the following in Hohfeld's scheme, giving the correlative: a tenant's entitlement that the landlord not enter without notice; the landlord's ability to terminate the lease on breach; a passer-by's freedom to look at the house. Then say which of them the will theory and the interest theory each count as rights. *Formal, then exegetical.* (d) Find the crux between a critic who says a careless driver who kills should be punished no more than an equally careless driver who by luck kills no one, and a defender of the law's current practice. *Exegetical.* (e) In 300-500 words: should the second camper's liability depend on whether the first camper's fire happened to reach the cabin first? Any verdict, if the answer uses at least one causation test and one theory of punishment from Module 5, and states the moral-luck objection to your view at full strength. *Evaluative.*
