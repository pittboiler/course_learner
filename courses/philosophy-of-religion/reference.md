# Philosophy of Religion · Reference Card

> Open book. This card is meant to be open while you work — including during
> quizzes. Nothing here is something you're expected to recall cold.

The course weighs the arguments for and against the God of perfect-being theology, and asks whether belief needs such arguments at all. It takes no side. Each entry gives the argument as the lessons state it, the premise it turns on, who denies that premise and why, and the reply. Start with [Argument skeletons](#argument-skeletons) to find the weight-bearing premise fast; the [Formulas](#formulas-and-formal-steps) section has every modal step and odds calculation the lessons use. Two warnings before you use any entry: say whether an argument claims proof, persuasion or only a raised probability ([proof and probability](#proof-and-probability)), and remember that reaching a first cause or designer is not yet reaching God ([the gap problem](#the-gap-problem)). Where the build's checks corrected the syllabus, the card follows the lessons ([Conventions](#conventions)).

### Perfect-being theology

God is the greatest possible being, so God has every great-making property in the highest compossible degree.

Anselm's formula ("a being than which nothing greater can be conceived", *Proslogion* 2) used as a method; modern statements in Thomas Morris, *Our Idea of God* (1991), and Katherin Rogers, *Perfect Being Theology* (2000). It yields the standard list: omnipotent, omniscient, perfectly good, necessarily existent, creator of all else. It does not settle *which* properties are great-making, which is where [classical theism](#classical-theism) and [theistic personalism](#theistic-personalism) split. Later uses: the source of the ontological argument's description ([2.1](lessons/02-01-anselms-ontological-argument.md)); the hiddenness argument's premise that God perfectly loves every finite person ([4.5](lessons/04-05-divine-hiddenness.md)).

*Introduced:* [1.1](lessons/01-01-which-god-and-what-an-argument-must-do.md); used in [1.2](lessons/01-02-omnipotence-and-its-paradoxes.md), [2.1](lessons/02-01-anselms-ontological-argument.md), [4.5](lessons/04-05-divine-hiddenness.md)

### Classical theism

Perfect-being theology plus simplicity, immutability, impassibility and timelessness, on the ground that parts, change and being acted on are marks of limitation.

Augustine, Anselm, Maimonides, Aquinas. Most classical theists affirm that God knows and wills; what they deny is that God is a person *univocally* with us, or one subject among others.

*Introduced:* [1.1](lessons/01-01-which-god-and-what-an-argument-must-do.md); the package is tested in [1.4](lessons/01-04-eternity-immutability-and-simplicity.md)

### Theistic personalism

God as a person in much the sense we are, only without limits: a being who knows, wills, and may respond in time.

The label is Brian Davies's (*An Introduction to the Philosophy of Religion*); many so labelled reject it, and "neoclassical theism" is a common alternative. Swinburne (*The Coherence of Theism*, 1977) and Wolterstorff ("God Everlasting", 1975) hold God everlasting rather than timeless; Plantinga (*Does God Have a Nature?*, 1980) argues against simplicity.

*Introduced:* [1.1](lessons/01-01-which-god-and-what-an-argument-must-do.md)

### The gap problem

Most arguments reach some $X$ (a first cause, a necessary being, a designer, a moral ground); showing that $X$ is God is a second argument with premises of its own.

1. Stage one: $X$ exists.
2. Stage two, the bridge: anything that is $X$ is (or probably is) God: one, personal, omnipotent, omniscient, perfectly good.
3. ∴ God exists (or probably exists).

Both sides accept the frame; the dispute is how wide the gap is. **Critic** (Hume's Philo, *Dialogues* V, "why may not several deities combine"; Oppy): a first cause could be many, impersonal or indifferent, and naturalism can fill the same causal shape with an initial physical state. **Thomist bridge:** an uncaused cause is pure act, hence simple, infinite and unique; Aquinas's "and this everyone understands to be God" records usage, and ST I qq.3-11 do the bridging. **Swinburnian bridge:** a person with zero limits is the simplest hypothesis. ### Proof and probability

Three standards of success, often confused: soundness, dialectical success, and probabilistic support.

- **Soundness:** valid with true premises; a sound argument can persuade no one.
- **Dialectical success:** it should move a reasonable person who does not already accept the conclusion. Oppy (*Arguing about Gods*, 2006) sets the bar at *every* reasonable person and argues no argument for *or* against God meets it.
- **Probabilistic support:** Swinburne's **C-inductive** argument raises the probability of its conclusion; a **P-inductive** one makes it more probable than not.

Placement in the course: Mackie's logical problem of evil aims at proof, Draper's evidential argument at probability ([4.2](lessons/04-02-the-evidential-problem-of-evil.md)); Plantinga (1974) says the modal ontological argument does not prove its conclusion but shows it rationally acceptable ([2.2](lessons/02-02-the-modal-ontological-argument.md)); Kant's moral argument is a practical postulate and the abductive moral argument at most C-inductive ([3.3](lessons/03-03-moral-arguments.md)); religious experience raises probability inside a cumulative case ([3.4](lessons/03-04-the-argument-from-religious-experience.md)).

*Introduced:* [1.1](lessons/01-01-which-god-and-what-an-argument-must-do.md); used in [2.2](lessons/02-02-the-modal-ontological-argument.md), [3.3](lessons/03-03-moral-arguments.md), [3.4](lessons/03-04-the-argument-from-religious-experience.md), [4.2](lessons/04-02-the-evidential-problem-of-evil.md)

### Cumulative case

Several C-inductive arguments can jointly be P-inductive: multiply the likelihood ratios, each weighed given the items already counted.

See [Cumulative odds](#cumulative-odds) for the formula. Two arguments that share a premise count once, and every item, favourable or not, enters one product for one hypothesis $G$ (a design ratio counts for $G$ only to the extent $G$ predicts design better than rivals, including a limited designer). Used by theists (Swinburne, who puts religious experience last: unless other evidence makes theism very improbable, experience makes it probable) and atheists (Draper, theism vs naturalism on the total evidence). A theist's Moore shift in odds form must use evidence for $G$ independent of the distribution of suffering ([4.2](lessons/04-02-the-evidential-problem-of-evil.md)). Swinburne's case is also the evidentialist theist's denial of P4 of the [evidentialist objection](#evidentialist-objection) ([5.1](lessons/05-01-evidentialism-and-the-ethics-of-belief.md)).

*Introduced:* [1.1](lessons/01-01-which-god-and-what-an-argument-must-do.md); used in [3.4](lessons/03-04-the-argument-from-religious-experience.md), [4.2](lessons/04-02-the-evidential-problem-of-evil.md), [5.1](lessons/05-01-evidentialism-and-the-ethics-of-belief.md)

### Paradox of the stone

Can God make a stone so heavy He cannot lift it? Either answer seems to name something God cannot do.

1. Either God can make a stone God cannot lift, or not.
2. If God can, there is a possible task God cannot do: lift it.
3. If God cannot, there is a possible task God cannot do: make it.
4. An omnipotent being can do every possible task.
5. ∴ God is not omnipotent.

Turns on P3 (both P2 and P3 assume the tasks are possible). **Mavrodes** (1963): for an omnipotent maker "a stone its maker cannot lift" is a self-contradictory **pseudo-task**, like a square circle; Aquinas's "better to say that such things cannot be done, than that God cannot do them" makes the same point. **Savage** (1967), without assuming omnipotence: "x cannot make a stone x cannot lift" means only that whatever stone x can make, x can lift, which entails no limit. Dissent: J. L. Cowan (1965). Most philosophers, theist and atheist, take one of the replies to answer the paradox; the live pressure is on what definition replaces it.

*Introduced:* [1.2](lessons/01-02-omnipotence-and-its-paradoxes.md)

### Omnipotence

The power to do whatever is possible; the hard part is saying "possible" without counterexamples.

Aquinas, ST I q.25 a.3: power over everything "possible absolutely", that does not imply a contradiction in terms. Aquinas's examples turn on natures, so "absolutely possible" is closer to conceptual or metaphysical possibility than to formal consistency. The ladder:

| Rung | Definition | What broke it |
|---|---|---|
| D0 | anything at all, even the contradictory (Descartes, often read so) | the attribute rules nothing out |
| D1 | anything logically possible (roughly Aquinas) | "make a table God did not make"; "sin" |
| D2 | anything logically possible *for that agent* | [McEar](#mcear) |
| D3 | [maximal power](#maximal-power): no possible agent could out-do S | cost: exception clauses |

*Introduced:* [1.2](lessons/01-02-omnipotence-and-its-paradoxes.md)

### McEar

Plantinga's counterexample (*God and Other Minds*, 1967, ch. 7): a being necessarily such that the only thing he does is scratch his ear.

Everything possible for McEar he can do, so "can do whatever is possible for it" (D2) calls him omnipotent. It pushes the definition up to [maximal power](#maximal-power).

*Introduced:* [1.2](lessons/01-02-omnipotence-and-its-paradoxes.md)

### Maximal power

S is omnipotent iff S can bring about any state of affairs any agent could bring about in S's situation, holding fixed the past and what free creatures would freely do (simplified form).

Thomas Flint and Alfred Freddoso, "Maximal Power" (1983); related accounts by Hoffman and Rosenkrantz and by Wierenga (*The Nature of God*, 1989). In words: no possible agent could out-do S. McEar fails it; the table and the past are excluded by the "holding fixed" clauses. **Critic:** clauses written to fit God, and a definition patched for every counterexample suggests an unstable concept. **Defender:** no agent can change a fixed past or make another's free choice, so these are not limits on power, and no ordinary concept ("knows", "causes") has a clause-free definition either. On D3 the stone is answered by which state of affairs is specified: no agent can bring about "there exists a stone *an omnipotent being* cannot lift".

*Introduced:* [1.2](lessons/01-02-omnipotence-and-its-paradoxes.md)

### Impeccability

God cannot sin. The worry: sinning is something some agents can do, so a being who cannot sin seems not omnipotent.

1. A perfect being is omnipotent.
2. A perfect being is essentially morally perfect, so cannot sin.
3. Sinning (lying, say) is something some agents can do.
4. A being that cannot do what some agent can do is not omnipotent.
5. ∴ No being is both; perfect-being theology is incoherent.

| Reply | Premise denied | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| Aquinas (q.25 a.3 ad 2): to sin is to fall short, so the ability to sin is a liability, not a power; God cannot sin *because* omnipotent | P3 as a statement of power | needs all sin to be privation ([4.3](lessons/04-03-theodicies.md)) |
| Pike (1969): "God" is a title; the individual holding it can sin and freely refrains; needed for praiseworthiness | weakens P2 | God's goodness becomes contingent, which most classical theists reject |
| State-of-affairs reframing (D3): God can bring about every state of affairs a liar does except one whose description includes the agent's own wrongdoing | reframes P4 | one more exception clause |

In 3.3 the same question (is a necessarily good God praiseworthy?) bears on the theistic ground of morality.

*Introduced:* [1.2](lessons/01-02-omnipotence-and-its-paradoxes.md); used in [3.3](lessons/03-03-moral-arguments.md)

### Omniscience

Knowing every truth (open theists: every truth that can be known); for classical theism infallible and essential.

The foreknowledge problem arises only from **infallibility plus pastness**: a fallible forecaster's past judgment, however reliable, gives line 4 of the [foreknowledge argument](#foreknowledge-argument) nothing to transfer.

*Introduced:* [1.3](lessons/01-03-omniscience-and-human-freedom.md)

### Necessity of the consequence and of the consequent

Necessity on the link, $\Box(p \to q)$, is not necessity on the outcome, $p \to \Box q$; confusing them is the careless foreknowledge argument.

From $\Box(G \to S)$ and $G$ only $S$ follows; $\Box S$ needs $\Box G$ as well. Two-world countermodel in [Modal fallacy](#modal-fallacy). The scope distinction kills only the fallacious version; the valid one gets necessity onto the antecedent from the fixity of the past. The careful modal-collapse derivation of 1.4 does not trade on this confusion: it gets the necessity of the act itself (line 4). Scope distinction owned by [metaphysics 3.1](../metaphysics/lessons/03-01-kinds-of-necessity.md).

*Introduced:* [1.3](lessons/01-03-omniscience-and-human-freedom.md); used in [1.4](lessons/01-04-eternity-immutability-and-simplicity.md)

### Foreknowledge argument

The valid version (Pike 1965; Zagzebski's form, 1991): God's past belief is fixed, it strictly entails the act, so the act is as fixed as the belief.

$N p$ = $p$ is true and no one now has any choice about whether $p$. $G$ = God believed yesterday that Teo signs today; $S$ = Teo signs.

1. $G$ (infallible foreknowledge).
2. If $p$ is a fact about the past, $N p$ ([fixity of the past](#fixity-of-the-past)).
3. $N G$ (1, 2).
4. $\Box(G \to S)$ (infallibility).
5. If $N p$ and $\Box(p \to q)$ then $N q$ (transfer, strict-entailment form).
6. $N S$ (3, 4, 5).
7. If $N S$, Teo cannot do otherwise.
8. If Teo cannot do otherwise, he does not sign freely (alternate possibilities).
9. ∴ Teo does not sign freely.

Widely agreed valid, so every response denies a premise. It is [metaphysics 6.1](../metaphysics/lessons/06-01-determinism-and-the-consequence-argument.md)'s consequence argument with God's belief for P0; McKay and Johnson's coin case against Beta does not touch line 5's strict form. No causation is claimed: line 5 transfers lack of choice.

| Response | Denies | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| [Ockhamism](#ockhamism) | line 2 for God's belief | a principled [hard/soft](#soft-and-hard-facts) line |
| [Molinism](#molinism) (usually plus Ockhamism) | line 2, via a theory of how God knows | grounding objection |
| Timeless eternity (Boethius, Aquinas) | line 1 as written: no belief *yesterday* | Zagzebski's parallel argument with "in the timeless realm" for "past"; reply: that intuition is weaker |
| [Open theism](#open-theism) | line 1: future free acts not knowable | revised omniscience; risk-taking providence; prophecy |
| Theological compatibilism (Frankfurt cases) | line 8 | the libertarian freedom the [free will defense](#free-will-defense) relies on |

*Introduced:* [1.3](lessons/01-03-omniscience-and-human-freedom.md)

### Fixity of the past

If $p$ is a (hard) fact about the past, no one now has a choice about $p$: line 2 of the foreknowledge argument.

**Critic of the argument:** a belief whose content is about Teo's noon is as much about noon as "it was true that Teo would sign", which is plainly soft. **Defender:** ordinary believing is a mental state fixed when it occurs; if an infallible belief is soft, nearly every past fact turns out soft (the prophet case: the strict entailment runs only from "the prophet spoke *because God revealed it*", which the Ockhamist must also call soft). The dispute is over what makes a fact *about* a time.

*Introduced:* [1.3](lessons/01-03-omniscience-and-human-freedom.md)

### Soft and hard facts

A hard fact is wholly about the past and fixed; a soft fact about the past is partly about the future ("it was true yesterday that Teo would sign").

*Introduced:* [1.3](lessons/01-03-omniscience-and-human-freedom.md)

### Ockhamism

Denies the fixity of the past for God's past beliefs: they are soft facts, over which we have counterfactual power.

After William of Ockham; revived by Marilyn Adams (1967) and Plantinga ("On Ockham's Way Out", 1986). Teo can act so that, were he to act, God would always have believed otherwise. Cost: a principled hard/soft line; critics, Pike first among them, say a belief is a state of the believer at a time, as hard as anything gets.

*Introduced:* [1.3](lessons/01-03-omniscience-and-human-freedom.md)

### Molinism

God has **middle knowledge** of counterfactuals of creaturely freedom ("if Teo were in C, he would freely sign"), between his natural and his free knowledge; choosing C, God knows what Teo will do without causing it.

Luis de Molina, *Concordia* (1588). A theory of how God knows, not by itself a premise-denial; Molinists usually add Ockhamism. **Grounding objection** (Robert Adams 1977; Hasker 1989): what makes the counterfactual true before Teo exists? Not God's decree (then not free), not Teo (not yet existing). Reply: such counterfactuals need no grounds beyond themselves, like many ordinary conditionals. Plantinga states the free will defense with counterfactuals of freedom, and critics list the grounding objection against it ([4.1](lessons/04-01-the-logical-problem-of-evil.md)).

*Introduced:* [1.3](lessons/01-03-omniscience-and-human-freedom.md); used in [4.1](lessons/04-01-the-logical-problem-of-evil.md)

### Open theism

God knows every truth that can be known; future free acts are not (yet) knowable, so there is no infallible foreknowledge of them.

Clark Pinnock and others, *The Openness of God* (1994); Hasker (1989). Open theists differ on whether such propositions are not yet true or true but unknowable. Defenders say this redefines omniscience the way omnipotence excludes square circles; it does not deny it. Cost: revised omniscience, providence that takes risks, prophecy needing another explanation.

*Introduced:* [1.3](lessons/01-03-omniscience-and-human-freedom.md)

### Boethian eternity

Eternity is the whole, simultaneous possession of unending life: timeless, with no before and after.

Boethius; ST I q.10 a.1. The classical chain: simple, so immutable, so timeless. Fits the B-theory of time far more naturally than the A-theory ([metaphysics 5.1](../metaphysics/lessons/05-01-the-a-theory-and-the-b-theory.md)). Stump and Kretzmann ("Eternity", 1981) propose ET-simultaneity, under which every temporal moment is present to God without God being in time; its coherence is disputed. Against foreknowledge it denies there is *fore*knowledge at all ([1.3](lessons/01-03-omniscience-and-human-freedom.md)).

*Introduced:* [1.4](lessons/01-04-eternity-immutability-and-simplicity.md); used in [1.3](lessons/01-03-omniscience-and-human-freedom.md)

### Timeless and everlasting

Timeless God: no before and after. Everlasting God: in time, without beginning or end, living through a sequence.

Everlasting: Wolterstorff ("God Everlasting", 1975: a God who promises and then fulfils has a before and after), Swinburne (*The Coherence of Theism*, 1977). Hybrid: Craig (*Time and Eternity*, 2001), timeless without creation, temporal from creation on.

*Introduced:* [1.4](lessons/01-04-eternity-immutability-and-simplicity.md)

### Knowledge of tensed facts

Kretzmann (1966): an omniscient being knows what time it is now, what time it is changes, so an omniscient being changes; the classical package is inconsistent.

1. A perfect being is omniscient.
2. An omniscient being knows, at each time, what time it is now.
3. What time it is now changes.
4. So a being that always knows what time it is now changes in what it knows.
5. A perfect being is immutable.
6. ∴ No being is both perfect and omniscient-and-immutable.

**B-theorist** denies 2 as meant: "now" reports no fact beyond tenseless ones; God lacks only the located, indexical way of holding them (cost: Prior's "thank goodness that's over"). **Everlasting theist** denies strong 5: character, purposes and power never change; knowledge of the passing present does. **Timeless A-theorist** has the hardest job, which is why Craig moves God into time. Used by everlasting theists against classical theism, not an argument for atheism. Crux: does "now" add a fact or only a perspective?

*Introduced:* [1.4](lessons/01-04-eternity-immutability-and-simplicity.md)

### Divine simplicity

No composition in God at all, not even of subject and attribute: God does not *have* goodness or power but *is* them.

ST I q.3; derived in [thomistic-synthesis 3.4](../thomistic-synthesis/lessons/03-04-divine-simplicity-and-the-attributes.md), doctrinal standing in [trinity-and-god 1.3](../trinity-and-god/lessons/01-03-simplicity-as-doctrine.md); this course neither asserts nor denies it. Two objections: the [property objection](#property-objection) and [modal collapse](#modal-collapse).

*Introduced:* [1.4](lessons/01-04-eternity-immutability-and-simplicity.md)

### Property objection

Plantinga (*Does God Have a Nature?*, 1980): if God is identical with each of his properties, all his properties are one, and God is a property, abstract and causally inert.

1. God is identical with each of his properties.
2. Identity is transitive, so he has one property.
3. So God is a property.
4. Properties are abstract and causally inert.
5. ∴ The simple God cannot be the personal creator.

Replies deny 1 as Plantinga reads it: Stump and Kretzmann ("Absolute Simplicity", 1985), one reality under many descriptions, like morning star and evening star; Brower (2008), truthmaker reading: what makes "God is good" true is God himself; Mann, identity with concrete property *instances*. It refutes identity with an abstract universal, which no classical defender holds; whether the alternatives are intelligible is the live question.

*Introduced:* [1.4](lessons/01-04-eternity-immutability-and-simplicity.md)

### Modal collapse

If everything about God is necessary and God's act fixes which world exists, nothing could have been otherwise. Pressed against divine simplicity (1.4) and against the PSR (2.4).

**Against simplicity** (Moreland and Craig 2003; Leftow; Mullins 2013): God = his act of willing; God exists necessarily; so the act exists necessarily; necessarily, if the act exists this world exists (efficacy); so this world exists necessarily. Outline in [Modal collapse derivations](#modal-collapse-derivations). Tomaszewski (*Analysis*, 2019) argued a common formulation invalid; the lesson's version is valid, so the dispute is over simplicity, necessary existence and efficacy. **Main reply** denies efficacy: the act is necessary, its terminus contingent; "God wills this world" is an extrinsic denomination (ST I q.13 a.7; God wills creatures by supposition, q.19 a.3). **Critic's rejoinder:** the difference principle, a different effect requires a different cause; otherwise which world obtains is brute. **Defender:** that principle is the hidden premise, and free causation by a unique simple cause is where it fails. Others accept a form of the conclusion or drop strong simplicity (Plantinga, Craig).

**Against the PSR** (van Inwagen 1983): if every contingent truth is explained and explanation entails, the explanation of the [big conjunctive contingent fact](#big-conjunctive-contingent-fact) is necessary (so the BCCF is) or contingent (self-explanation). Replies: deny that explanation entails (Leibniz's inclining reasons; Pruss 2006: free actions explain without entailing), accept necessitarianism (Spinoza), reject the PSR.

*Introduced:* [1.4](lessons/01-04-eternity-immutability-and-simplicity.md); against the PSR in [2.4](lessons/02-04-cosmological-arguments-sufficient-reason.md)

## Module 2 Arguments from being

Arguments that reach God (or a first cause, or a necessary being) from concepts, dependence, contingency or a beginning. The modal and infinite-cardinal steps are in [Formulas and formal steps](#formulas-and-formal-steps).

### Anselms ontological argument

*Proslogion* 2: a reductio on the description "that than which nothing greater can be conceived". If it existed in the understanding alone, something greater could be conceived (the same being, existing in reality).

$G$ = [that than which nothing greater](#that-than-which-nothing-greater) can be conceived.

1. P1. $G$ exists in the understanding (the fool understands the phrase).
2. P2. $G$ can be conceived to exist in reality.
3. P3. Whatever exists in the understanding alone but can be conceived to exist in reality can be conceived to be greater than it is (the **greater-than premise**).
4. P4. Suppose, for reductio, $G$ exists in the understanding alone.
5. P5. Then a being greater than $G$ can be conceived (P2-P4).
6. P6. No being greater than $G$ can be conceived (definition).
7. ∴ $G$ exists in reality as well as in the understanding.

Valid with each term fixed; the debate is over P1, P3, and whether "conceive" is constant. Three classic replies:

| Reply | Target | Defender's answer |
|---|---|---|
| Understanding objection (Gaunilo: words only; Aquinas ST I q.2 a.1 ad 2: not all take "God" so, and only mental existence follows); today: is the description coherent? | P1 | the perfections have joint maxima and fit together (Module 1's question) |
| [Gaunilos island](#gaunilos-island) | the form (proves too much) | only the unrestricted description works; [intrinsic maximum](#intrinsic-maximum) |
| Kant, [existence is not a predicate](#existence-is-not-a-predicate) | P3 | P3 compares an instance with an empty concept, a claim about value, not concept contents |

**Where it turns:** critics attack P3 (existing is not a way of being better; the reductio compares something with nothing). Defenders: that rests on the Fregean thesis, itself disputed, and Q2 of chapter 3 survives it. The deeper vulnerability is P1 read as coherence, since every island repair leans on it. The SEP reports a widespread view, theists included, that no known ontological argument succeeds. **Proslogion 3** is the [necessary existence](#necessary-existence) version.

*Lessons:* [2.1](lessons/02-01-anselms-ontological-argument.md)

### That than which nothing greater

Anselm's description of God (*Proslogion* 2, Deane): "a being than which nothing greater can be conceived". Not "whatever I can think of exists": a parody must copy the description, not the slogan.

The source of [perfect-being theology](#perfect-being-theology)'s formula. The reductio is like "no largest whole number" run in reverse: the number reductio shows nothing fits its description; Anselm's claims something must, and the difference is P3.

*Introduced:* [2.1](lessons/02-01-anselms-ontological-argument.md)

### Gaunilos island

Gaunilo's parody: the same steps prove an island more excellent than all lands exists.

A **proves-too-much** objection: it shows something fails, not which premise. Replies: Anselm, his reasoning fits only the unrestricted description, never "the greatest of its kind"; Plantinga, the [intrinsic maximum](#intrinsic-maximum) reply moves the island's failure to P1. Critic's rejoinder: rebuild the parody from properties that *do* have maxima. Modal form: a "maximally pervasive island"; the stronger parody is a being omnipotent, omniscient and *maximally evil* in every world, which forces a substantive claim about why the attributes fit with goodness but not with evil ([2.2](lessons/02-02-the-modal-ontological-argument.md)).

*Introduced:* [2.1](lessons/02-01-anselms-ontological-argument.md); modal form in [2.2](lessons/02-02-the-modal-ontological-argument.md)

### Intrinsic maximum

A great-making property has one if it has a highest possible degree (knowing every truth, power over everything possible) and lacks one if it can always be increased (one more palm tree).

Plantinga, *The Nature of Necessity* (1974): there is no greatest island, so "the greatest conceivable island" is incoherent like "the largest number", and the island's P1 fails. Cost: greatness must have an intrinsic maximum and the maximal properties must be jointly possible.

*Introduced:* [2.1](lessons/02-01-anselms-ontological-argument.md)

### Existence is not a predicate

Kant, *Critique of Pure Reason* A598/B626: a hundred real thalers contain no more than a hundred possible ones; "exists" adds nothing to a concept.

The thesis and its Fregean sharpening are stated in [metaphysics 2.5](../metaphysics/lessons/02-05-essence-and-existence.md); here only its bearing. Against Anselm it targets P3, and works only if P3 needs existence to enlarge a concept. Malcolm (1960): it defeats chapter 2, not chapter 3, since necessary existence is a perfection even if existence is not. Against Plantinga it does not bite directly: P2 concerns instantiation across worlds, handled by the semantics. It returns as "is necessary existence something a concept can just contain?", which is the existence question again.

*Introduced:* [2.1](lessons/02-01-anselms-ontological-argument.md); used in [2.2](lessons/02-02-the-modal-ontological-argument.md)

### Necessary existence

*Proslogion* 3: a being that cannot be conceived not to exist is greater than one that can, so $G$ cannot be conceived not to exist.

1. Q1. A being that cannot be conceived not to exist can be conceived.
2. Q2. Such a being is greater than one that can be conceived not to exist.
3. Q3. So if $G$ could be conceived not to exist, something greater than $G$ could be conceived.
4. ∴ C3. $G$ cannot be conceived not to exist.

Malcolm (1960) and Hartshorne (1941) take this as the stronger argument. Its shape is *possible, therefore actual* (Anselm's *Reply* ch. 1: "if that being can be even conceived to be, it must exist in reality"). The bridge from C3 to existence needs "inconceivable" = impossible, and Q1 needs "conceivable" = possible, so Q1 becomes the [possibility premise](#possibility-premise).

*Introduced:* [2.1](lessons/02-01-anselms-ontological-argument.md); formalized in [2.2](lessons/02-02-the-modal-ontological-argument.md)

### Modal ontological argument

Plantinga (1974): if a maximally great being is possible, then, since maximal greatness includes existing in every world, it exists.

$G$ = a maximally great being exists; $\Box$ true in every world, $\Diamond$ true in some world.

1. P1. $\Diamond G$ ([possibility premise](#possibility-premise)).
2. P2. $\Box(G \to \Box G)$ (from the definition of [maximal greatness](#maximal-greatness)).
3. $\Diamond\Box G$ (P1, P2, by K).
4. $\Box G$ (3, by the S5 step).
5. ∴ $G$ (4, by T).

Derivations in [S5 steps](#s5-steps). Validity is the least contested part: B suffices for the theist's direction. **Turns on P1.** Critic (Mackie 1982, pressing Plantinga's own no-maximality): given P2, $\Diamond G$ is equivalent to $G$, so grounds for it can be no better than grounds for $G$, and the [reverse ontological argument](#reverse-ontological-argument) is equally conceivable. Defender: break the symmetry (show the perfections consistent, as Leibniz said Descartes needed; argue positive maximal properties are better candidates for possibility; support $\Diamond G$ independently, e.g. cosmologically), and note the critic assumes conceivability counts equally on both sides. Plantinga's own verdict: not a proof, but it shows belief rationally acceptable ([proof and probability](#proof-and-probability)).

*Lessons:* [2.2](lessons/02-02-the-modal-ontological-argument.md)

### Maximal greatness

Maximally excellent in a world = omnipotent, omniscient and morally perfect there; maximally great = maximally excellent in *every* world.

Plantinga, *The Nature of Necessity* (1974). A de re property of a being, so $\Box(G \to \Box G)$ holds by definition. In S5 with that premise, $\Diamond G$, $G$ and $\Box G$ are equivalent: a maximally great being is either necessary or impossible. Compare Goldbach's conjecture: asserting its possibility is asserting its truth.

*Introduced:* [2.2](lessons/02-02-the-modal-ontological-argument.md)

### S5 and the characteristic axiom

S5's characteristic axiom is 5: $\Diamond p \to \Box\Diamond p$ (what is possible is necessarily possible). Its dual form $\Diamond\Box p \to \Box p$ is the step the modal ontological argument uses.

Semantically, in S5 every world sees every world. B already proves $\Diamond\Box p \to p$, which suffices for the conclusion; the forward argument fails in S4. The reverse argument needs only K and T. S5 as a formal system belongs to [philosophy-of-language-and-logic](../philosophy-of-language-and-logic/syllabus.md). Steps in [S5 steps](#s5-steps).

*Introduced:* [2.2](lessons/02-02-the-modal-ontological-argument.md); used in [2.4](lessons/02-04-cosmological-arguments-sufficient-reason.md) (weak PSR)

### Possibility premise

$\Diamond G$: possibly a maximally great being exists. Given $\Box(G \to \Box G)$ it is exactly as strong as the conclusion, so it is the premise the modal argument turns on.

Introduced in 2.1 as where *Proslogion* 3 moves the weight (Q1). Conceivability is weak evidence for possibility ([metaphysics 3.1](../metaphysics/lessons/03-01-kinds-of-necessity.md)). "Possibly a maximally great being" is not like "possibly a unicorn".

*Introduced:* [2.1](lessons/02-01-anselms-ontological-argument.md); owned by [2.2](lessons/02-02-the-modal-ontological-argument.md)

### Reverse ontological argument

Plantinga's own "no-maximality" premise: possibly no being is maximally great. With P2 it yields $\neg G$, validly in K plus T.

1. $\Diamond\neg\Box G$.
2. $\Box(G \to \Box G)$.
3. ∴ $\neg G$ (steps in [S5 steps](#s5-steps)).

It begs the question exactly as much as P1, no more and no less; that symmetry is the objection. Defenders' attempts to break it are listed under the [modal ontological argument](#modal-ontological-argument).

*Introduced:* [2.2](lessons/02-02-the-modal-ontological-argument.md)

### Per se and per accidens series

Per se (essentially ordered): each intermediate exercises its power now only while the member above exercises its own (derived power). Per accidens: each member owns what it has.

The distinction is owned by [metaphysics 4.4](../metaphysics/lessons/04-04-ordered-causal-series.md). Aquinas allowed an infinite per accidens series (man begetting man) and denied an infinite per se one (ST I q.46 a.2 ad 7). Repeater towers: each retransmits only what it receives, while it receives it. The kalam's past is per accidens, the kind the regress allows to be infinite ([2.5](lessons/02-05-cosmological-arguments-the-kalam.md)).

*Used:* [2.3](lessons/02-03-cosmological-arguments-the-per-se-regress.md)

### Per se regress argument

A series of borrowers needs a lender: if every member's power were derived, nothing would be lent.

1. P1. Some effect is being produced now by an essentially ordered series.
2. P2. Every intermediate member's power is derived, held only while the member above exercises its own.
3. P3 (regress premise). A series all of whose members have only derived power produces no effect.
4. C1. So the series has a member with underived power in that series (P1-P3).
5. P4. If that member's power is derived from another series, apply P1-P3 to that series.
6. ∴ At least one being has causal power underived from anything.

Neutral skeleton recognized by defenders (Second Way read with ad 7; Feser, *Five Proofs*, 2017) and critics (Oppy). Time-indifferent: it is about vertical dependence now. "First" means underived, and C1 gives it only relative to one series. **Turns on P3**: the [infinite-intermediaries reply](#infinite-intermediaries-reply). Physics no longer supplies P1 (a coasting body needs no mover), so P1 becomes a metaphysical claim about derived power. Then [the gap problem](#the-gap-problem): no uniqueness, personhood, or exclusion of a natural first member.

*Lessons:* [2.3](lessons/02-03-cosmological-arguments-the-per-se-regress.md)

### Infinite-intermediaries reply

Denies the regress premise: in an infinite per se series each member is explained by the next, so no member is unexplained and no first member is needed.

Oberle (2022): affirming an infinite series is not removing the first member of a finite one (against the Second Way's "to take away the cause is to take away the effect", read literally). Demanding a first member is demanding an explanation of the whole series, which is the PSR the argument promised not to need. **Defender** (Feser's line; Oppy 2019 and Feser 2021 exchange in *Religious Studies*): the reply treats derived power as owned, explaining each relay as a source, so it denies the per se distinction rather than answering it. **Crux:** is a derived-power explanation a deferral, never paid out by an infinite regress, or a complete local explanation, paid out at every step? Same fork as infinitism vs foundationalism ([epistemology 2.1](../epistemology/lessons/02-01-the-regress-problem.md)).

*Introduced:* [2.3](lessons/02-03-cosmological-arguments-the-per-se-regress.md)

### Argument from contingency

Thomist form: things whose existence is not part of what they are must be given existence now, and by the regress that series has a first member with underived existence.

1. K1. Some things exist whose existence is not part of what they are.
2. K2 (conservation). Whatever does not have existence of itself is, at each moment it exists, being given existence by something.
3. K3. By the per se regress, a series of existence-givers that are themselves given existence has a first member.
4. ∴ Something exists whose existence is underived.

Avoids the physics trouble (existing is not moving); pays with K2, which [existential inertia](#existential-inertia) denies. The Third Way's first half ("each corruptible thing at some time is not", so "at some time nothing was") is a quantifier shift (see [Quantifier shift in the Third Way](#quantifier-shift-in-the-third-way)); modern Thomists drop it, so refuting it does not refute K. Leibnizian PSR form: [Leibnizian cosmological argument](#leibnizian-cosmological-argument).

*Introduced:* [2.3](lessons/02-03-cosmological-arguments-the-per-se-regress.md)

### Existential inertia

Persisting is the default and needs no sustaining cause.

Beaudoin, Oppy, Schmid. Denies K2 of the Thomist contingency argument; without K2 there is no per se series of existence-givers for the regress. Not Newtonian inertia ([metaphysics 4.4](../metaphysics/lessons/04-04-ordered-causal-series.md)).

*Introduced:* [2.3](lessons/02-03-cosmological-arguments-the-per-se-regress.md)

### Principle of sufficient reason

Every truth (or every contingent truth, or every fact possibly) has an explanation; the strengths are tabulated in [metaphysics 3.3](../metaphysics/lessons/03-03-brute-facts-and-necessary-existence.md).

The Leibnizian argument needs at least the contingency row applied to the [big conjunctive contingent fact](#big-conjunctive-contingent-fact), and must survive [modal collapse](#modal-collapse). The kalam's [causal principle](#causal-principle) is weaker, but the quiet-past case forces it to borrow some PSR ([2.5](lessons/02-05-cosmological-arguments-the-kalam.md)). In 3.3 the theist presses a demand for explanation against [brute ethical facts](#brute-ethical-facts).

*Used:* [2.4](lessons/02-04-cosmological-arguments-sufficient-reason.md); also [2.5](lessons/02-05-cosmological-arguments-the-kalam.md), [3.3](lessons/03-03-moral-arguments.md)

### Leibnizian cosmological argument

Explaining each contingent thing by another, even forever, never explains why there is a chain of contingent things at all; so the reason lies in a necessary being.

1. Every contingent fact has an explanation (PSR, contingency row).
2. There is a big conjunctive contingent fact (BCCF), and it is contingent.
3. So the BCCF has an explanation.
4. No contingent fact can explain it (any such fact is one of its conjuncts).
5. So it is explained by a necessary fact: the existence or activity of a necessary being.
6. That necessary being is God.

Leibniz, "On the Ultimate Origination of Things" (1697; the eternal geometry book: *why books at all*, and *why written in this way*) and *Monadology* (1714) §§36-39; Clarke, *A Demonstration of the Being and Attributes of God* (1705). Both grant an eternal series. Objections: [Hume-Edwards](#hume-edwards-objection) (3, 4); [modal collapse](#modal-collapse) (premise 1 with "explanation entails"); [the gap problem](#the-gap-problem) (6: why not a necessary physical state?). Theist's rejoinder ties gap to collapse: only a free agent explains without necessitating, so whatever escapes the collapse is already personal. **Where it turns:** premise 1 must apply to the BCCF and survive collapse; the critic says that requires denying entailment, after which a reason may not answer *why this rather than another*; the defender says entailment would make every free action inexplicable. Crux: does a strong PSR demand contrastive explanation? Variant: [weak PSR](#weak-psr).

*Lessons:* [2.4](lessons/02-04-cosmological-arguments-sufficient-reason.md)

### Big conjunctive contingent fact

The BCCF: the conjunction of all contingent truths, the whole story of the contingent world as one fact.

The fact whose explanation the Leibnizian argument needs and van Inwagen's collapse targets. Hume-Edwards denies it is a further fact over its conjuncts.

*Introduced:* [2.4](lessons/02-04-cosmological-arguments-sufficient-reason.md)

### Hume-Edwards objection

Explain each member of a collection and you have explained the collection.

Cleanthes, *Dialogues* IX; Edwards (1959): five Inuit on a New York street corner, each with his own reason. Rowe (1975): explaining why each dependent being exists is not explaining why there are dependent beings at all. Test: do the members' explanations ever cite anything outside the collection? Edwards's do (climate, a job); Leibniz's copied books never do. Not merely a composition-fallacy charge: it is a claim about what facts there are, and if the BCCF is a genuine fact the PSR already applies to it.

*Introduced:* [2.4](lessons/02-04-cosmological-arguments-sufficient-reason.md)

### Weak PSR

Gale and Pruss (1999): possibly, the actual BCCF has an explanation.

1. W1. Possibly the actual BCCF has an explanation.
2. W2. Where it does, the explanation cannot draw on contingent beings, so it is the intentional act of a necessary being.
3. W3. So possibly a necessary being exists; by the S5 step, actually.

Oppy (2000): for a fact as total as the BCCF the weak principle entails the strong one, so the advantage disappears.

*Introduced:* [2.4](lessons/02-04-cosmological-arguments-sufficient-reason.md)

### Kalam cosmological argument

Whatever begins to exist has a cause; the universe began to exist; so the universe has a cause.

1. P1. Whatever begins to exist has a cause ([causal principle](#causal-principle)).
2. P2. The universe began to exist.
3. ∴ C1. The universe has a cause.

al-Ghazali (*The Incoherence of the Philosophers*, First Discussion: Saturn completes 1/30 as many turns as the sun, yet both totals would be infinite; is the total odd or even?); revived by Craig (1979). Weight on P2, supported by (a) no actual infinite ([actual and potential infinite](#actual-and-potential-infinite), [Hilberts Hotel](#hilberts-hotel)), (b) the [traversal argument](#traversal-argument), (c) cosmology ([Borde-Guth-Vilenkin theorem](#borde-guth-vilenkin-theorem)); these are independent supports. Bridge (Craig): a timeless cause with sufficient conditions would give a timeless effect (always below freezing, always ice), the effect is not eternal, so the cause is a free agent; Morriston (2000): a timeless intention is just as sufficient; Craig: the agent must also exercise its power; Morriston: exercising a power is temporal. Stated on an A-theory; Grünbaum doubts a B-block with an edge "comes into being"; Loke says traversal works on either view. **Where it turns:** P2a's move from counterintuitive to impossible; symmetry objection (Oppy, Morriston: an endless future of angels' praises raises the same puzzles); defender: mathematical consistency is not real possibility ($x=-2$ is not minus two people).

*Lessons:* [2.5](lessons/02-05-cosmological-arguments-the-kalam.md)

### Actual and potential infinite

Actual: a completed totality with infinitely many members. Potential: a collection that can always grow but is finite at every stage.

Craig: a beginningless past would be actual; the future (on an A-theory) only potential. P2a: an actual infinite cannot exist in reality; P2b: a beginningless past would be one; so the past had a first member.

*Introduced:* [2.5](lessons/02-05-cosmological-arguments-the-kalam.md)

### Hilberts Hotel

A full hotel with a room for every positive integer still takes in one, or infinitely many, new guests.

Hilbert, lecture of January 1924 (unpublished); popularized by Gamow (1947). Maps and the checkout case in [Hilberts Hotel maps](#hilberts-hotel-maps). Set theory has no complaint: an infinite set is one in one-to-one correspondence with a proper part ([mathematical-logic 4.3](../mathematical-logic/lessons/04-03-cardinals-cantors-theorem.md)). Craig accepts the mathematics and says a *real* hotel, or past, behaving so would be absurd; critics say counterintuitive, not contradictory. The dispute is whether finite part-whole intuitions are necessary truths about concrete collections.

*Introduced:* [2.5](lessons/02-05-cosmological-arguments-the-kalam.md)

### Traversal argument

Even if an actual infinite could exist, it could not be formed by successive addition, so a beginningless past could never have reached today.

The man who finishes counting down from minus infinity (why today, not yesterday?); Russell's Tristram Shandy (*Principles of Mathematics*, 1903), who takes a year to write up each day. Reply (Morriston): at every point infinitely many events have already elapsed; no starting point is needed. A quiet past (static forever, then expanding) raises the question whether there is any series of events to traverse.

*Introduced:* [2.5](lessons/02-05-cosmological-arguments-the-kalam.md)

### Borde-Guth-Vilenkin theorem

A spacetime whose average expansion rate along a past-directed path is positive cannot be extended indefinitely into the past along that path (*Physical Review Letters*, 2003).

Vilenkin reads it as evidence of a beginning. Critics (Carroll): it is classical, says nothing about quantum gravity, and "a past boundary of the expanding region" is not "nothing before"; models with zero average past expansion (Ellis and Maartens's emergent universe, 2004) fall outside it. The Big Bang establishes expansion from a hot, dense state about 13.8 billion years ago, not an absolute beginning.

*Introduced:* [2.5](lessons/02-05-cosmological-arguments-the-kalam.md)

### Causal principle

Whatever begins to exist has a cause: the kalam's P1, weaker than the PSR because it asks only that beginnings have causes.

Objections: (i) Hume, cause and beginning are separable ideas (*Treatise* 1.3.3); (ii) quantum events (Craig: a vacuum is a structured physical state, not nothing; Morriston: what makes merely necessary conditions a cause?); (iii) induction from within-universe beginnings to the universe, the Hume-Edwards point.

*Introduced:* [2.5](lessons/02-05-cosmological-arguments-the-kalam.md)

## Module 3 Arguments from the world and experience

Probabilistic arguments: each is at most C-inductive and goes into [odds form](#odds-form). In every one, find the term the argument turns on; it is almost always a likelihood, not the prior.

### Design argument

Paley's argument from adapted organs, read either as an analogy (watch and eye) or, following Sober (2003), as a likelihood argument: adaptation is far likelier on design than on chance.

1. P1. $E$: organisms have parts intricately adapted to functions.
2. P2. $P(E \mid D)$ is not very small.
3. P3. $P(E \mid C)$ is extremely small.
4. ∴ $E$ strongly favours $D$ over $C$ and raises the odds of design by the likelihood ratio.

Read as analogy (reconstruction owned by [philosophical-method 2.2](../philosophical-method/lessons/02-02-arguments-from-analogy.md)), it is only as good as the relevance premise linking artifacts to organisms, which Philo attacks with rival analogies. The likelihood reading escapes two of Hume's objections (we need not have watched worlds being made; the world need not resemble a house); the lesson attributes it to Sober and does not settle Paley's exegesis. Paley, *Natural Theology* (1802) ch. III: the eye was made for vision as the telescope for assisting it. **Darwin** (1859) replaced the rival with chance plus selection, which predicts adaptation, so P3's ratio collapses toward 1 while the prior and P2 are untouched: the general way to defeat a likelihood argument. Paley fenced off the origin of the laws themselves, which is where [fine-tuning](#fine-tuning-argument) goes.

*Lessons:* [3.1](lessons/03-01-from-design-to-fine-tuning.md)

### Likelihood principle

$E$ favours $H_1$ over $H_2$ iff $P(E \mid H_1) > P(E \mid H_2)$, and the ratio measures how strongly.

$$\frac{P(E \mid D)}{P(E \mid C)} > 1$$

It says nothing about which hypothesis to believe; that needs the prior ([odds form](#odds-form)). A tiny $P(E \mid C)$ alone does not make chance improbable: every bridge hand is improbable; thirteen spades is suspicious only by comparison.

*Introduced:* [3.1](lessons/03-01-from-design-to-fine-tuning.md); used in [3.2](lessons/03-02-fine-tuning-the-objections.md)

### Fine-tuning argument

The constants take life-permitting values; that is very improbable on single-universe chance and not very improbable on design.

1. P1. $E$: the constants take life-permitting values.
2. P2. $P(E \mid D)$ is not very small (a designer would plausibly want embodied, morally significant agents).
3. P3. $P(E \mid C)$ is extremely small (life-permitting values occupy a tiny part of the possible range).
4. P4. So $E$ strongly favours $D$ over $C$ (likelihood principle).
5. ∴ $E$ raises the odds of design over single-universe chance by a large factor; not a proof.

"Fine-tuned" is a physicist's description of a theory (observation matched only in a narrow parameter range), neutral about design. Examples (Collins 2009; Barnes 2012): the cosmological-constant mismatch, usually quoted as about $10^{120}$ but cutoff-dependent (SEP: $10^{50}$ to $10^{123}$); the strong force (on one standard estimate, more than about 50% stronger burns almost all hydrogen early); gravity, the weak force, quark masses, density fluctuations. Flatness is now usually explained by inflation. **Where it turns:** P3, attacked by three of five objections ([normalizability](#normalizability-problem), [multiverse](#multiverse-objection), dynamics), plus [observation selection](#observation-selection-effect); and P2 ([inscrutable designer](#inscrutable-designer-objection)). Defender: each objection rests on something its holder would not accept elsewhere (physicists' naturalness judgments; the firing squad; a multiverse with a disputed prior and possibly tuned mechanism). No objection targets the prior. Then [the gap problem](#the-gap-problem). Numbers: [Design and fine-tuning odds](#design-and-fine-tuning-odds).

*Lessons:* [3.1](lessons/03-01-from-design-to-fine-tuning.md), [3.2](lessons/03-02-fine-tuning-the-objections.md)

### Normalizability problem

No uniform probability exists over an unbounded range of possible values, so $P(E \mid C)$ may be undefined rather than small.

McGrew, McGrew and Vestrup (*Mind*, 2001): a window of width 1 in a range of width $W$ gets $1/W$, which goes to 0 for **every** finite window as $W \to \infty$, so "coarse-tuning" would score as well as fine-tuning. Reply (Collins 2009): restrict to the **epistemically illuminated range**, the values for which current physics can say whether life is possible; it is finite. Cost: a probability relative to what we can calculate. It is a dilemma, not a quibble: a defined probability needs a range someone must justify.

*Introduced:* [3.1](lessons/03-01-from-design-to-fine-tuning.md); developed in [3.2](lessons/03-02-fine-tuning-the-objections.md)

### Multiverse objection

Replace single-universe chance $C$ with many universes $M$ of varied constants: then *some* universe is almost surely life-permitting, and observers exist only in those.

Attacks P3 by changing the rival. It concedes $E$ is improbable on a single chance universe, and must pay for $M$'s own prior (and possibly a tuned generating mechanism). Answered by the [inverse gamblers fallacy](#inverse-gamblers-fallacy) charge; rebutted by appeal to selection. Numbers in [Multiverse at least one](#multiverse-at-least-one).

*Introduced:* [3.2](lessons/03-02-fine-tuning-the-objections.md)

### Inverse gamblers fallacy

Inferring many trials from one remarkable outcome (a double six, so many rolls).

Hacking (*Mind*, 1987), aimed at Wheeler's sequential universes; White (*Noûs*, 2000) generalized: our evidence is that **this** universe is life-permitting, and other universes do not make this one likelier to be. Rebuttal (McGrath and Leslie, *Mind*, 1988; Bradley 2009): we are selected, so the evidence is that *some* universe is life-permitting, which $M$ does raise. The arithmetic is not in dispute; the description of the evidence is.

*Introduced:* [3.2](lessons/03-02-fine-tuning-the-objections.md)

### Observation selection effect

We could only observe life-permitting values, so conditioning on our existence makes $E$ certain on any hypothesis and the ratio 1.

Sober (2003): $P(E \mid D, \text{we observe}) = P(E \mid C, \text{we observe}) = 1$. Answered by the [firing squad case](#firing-squad-case). Note the direction: the selection effect is what the multiverse *defender* uses to escape the inverse-gambler charge, and what Sober uses against design.

*Introduced:* [3.2](lessons/03-02-fine-tuning-the-objections.md)

### Firing squad case

Leslie (*Universes*, 1989): a prisoner survives an execution in which every marksman missed. "I would only observe this if they missed" seems not to stop rational suspicion that the misses were arranged.

Sober accepts that the survivor should *not* infer it without independent evidence; critics (Leslie, Weisberg, Kotzen) find that verdict implausible. Sober's side: the intuition borrows background knowledge (how executions are staged) the formal case omits. Same structure as the universe, so whoever is right about the prisoner is right about fine-tuning.

*Introduced:* [3.2](lessons/03-02-fine-tuning-the-objections.md)

### Inscrutable designer objection

We know designers' aims only from human designers, so $P(E \mid D)$ is unassessable; and an omnipotent God need not work through finely balanced physics.

Sober (2003), Hume's older objection in new dress. Reply (Swinburne 2004; Collins): a perfectly good God has reason to create embodied, finite agents whose choices matter, which needs life-permitting physics; we infer designers in archaeology and SETI on modest assumptions about aims. Rejoinder: that imports perfect-being theology into P2. It is the mirror image of [skeptical theism](#skeptical-theism): a theist who says we cannot tell what God would allow faces the same modesty here.

*Introduced:* [3.2](lessons/03-02-fine-tuning-the-objections.md)

### Moral argument

Objective moral facts, especially obligations, and our knowledge of them are likelier on theism than on naturalism.

**Abductive form** (Adams 1979; Evans 2013; Linville 2009). $G$ theism, $N$ naturalism, $E$ the moral evidence:

1. There are objective moral facts, including obligations binding regardless of desire or convention.
2. $\Pr(E \mid G)$ is high (a good God would create persons and stand in obligation-grounding relations to them; for Adams, obligation is constituted by a good God's commands).
3. $\Pr(E \mid N)$ is low (objective action-guiding demands are, in Mackie's word, "queer").
4. ∴ $E$ confirms $G$ over $N$.

At most C-inductive. **Turns on** premise 3 ($\Pr(E \mid N)$, the most disputed term) and, for anti-realists, premise 1. **Mackie** (1982) granted 2-3 in substance and denied 1, so atheists divide: error theorists and constructivists deny the data; robust realists deny 3 ([brute ethical facts](#brute-ethical-facts)). Moral knowledge version (Linville): God as the third factor making faculties track; naturalist reply: Wielenberg's own third factor (capacities that ground rights and produce beliefs), and $\Pr(\text{God makes faculties track} \mid G)$ is as inscrutable as fine-tuning's design likelihood. Does not presuppose divine command theory (a natural-law theist fills $\Pr(E \mid G)$ through God's reason). God's goodness must be necessary, which raises Pike's praise worry ([impeccability](#impeccability)) and a parity worry (God is then a brute necessity too); theist: one necessary person explains goodness, obligation and moral agents together; atheist: the Euthyphro dilemma one level up. **Kant's practical form:** [postulate of practical reason](#postulate-of-practical-reason).

*Lessons:* [3.3](lessons/03-03-moral-arguments.md)

### Postulate of practical reason

Kant (*Critique of Practical Reason*, 1788): morality requires us to promote the highest good, which only a moral author of nature makes possible, so a moral agent must postulate God; a practical commitment, not a theoretical proof.

1. We are rationally required to promote the highest good: happiness proportioned to virtue.
2. Ought implies can.
3. Nature shows no tie between virtue and happiness, and finite agents cannot forge one.
4. The highest good is possible only if nature is governed by a cause with moral intention and the power to proportion happiness to virtue.
5. ∴ A rational moral agent must postulate that God exists.

Weak premise: 1 (critics: morality requires us to *strive* for the good, not believe it will be achieved). A critic who shows God's existence is not *known* this way agrees with Kant. Sidgwick saw the same gap (dualism of practical reason) without endorsing the argument.

*Introduced:* [3.3](lessons/03-03-moral-arguments.md)

### Grounding and knowing

The moral argument is about what makes moral facts obtain, not whether atheists can know them or be good.

"I'm moral without believing in God" answers a claim no serious defender makes; an argument that atheists cannot know right from wrong would be a different, much weaker argument.

*Introduced:* [3.3](lessons/03-03-moral-arguments.md)

### Brute ethical facts

Robust non-theist realism: basic ethical truths are necessary and need no ground (Wielenberg, *Robust Ethics*, 2014; Enoch, *Taking Morality Seriously*, 2011).

If so, both likelihoods are 1 and the ratio is 1. **Defender of the argument:** the probabilities are epistemic credences of agents who do not know which necessities hold (as with an unproved conjecture), so a necessary truth can still be evidence; and a brute necessity is an acceptable stopping point only if one rejects extending the demand for explanation, as in the [PSR](#principle-of-sufficient-reason) debates.

*Introduced:* [3.3](lessons/03-03-moral-arguments.md)

### Argument from religious experience

If seeming counts as evidence in perception, parity asks the same for experiences as of God.

Swinburne (*The Existence of God*, 1979; 2nd ed. 2004, ch. 13):

1. P1. [Principle of credulity](#principle-of-credulity).
2. P2. Many people have had experiences in which it seemed that God was present.
3. P3. For many, special considerations (i) and (ii) do not apply.
4. P4. Considerations (iii) and (iv) apply only if there is strong independent evidence that God does not exist (an omnipresent sustainer would be a cause of any such experience).
5. P5. There is no such strong evidence.
6. ∴ These experiences are probably of God, and by the [principle of testimony](#principle-of-testimony) others have reason to believe so.

Alston's version: [doxastic practice](#doxastic-practice). Two routes for the opponent: religious experience is unlike perception ([cross-checking](#cross-checking-objection), diversity: [5.5](lessons/05-05-religious-diversity.md)), or a defeater is present ([projection theory](#projection-theory)). **Turns on** P4 with the parity behind P1. Defender: a near-end causal story does not exclude God further back (neural causes of seeing a tree do not exclude the tree), and the projection theory assumes there is no God to perceive. Swinburne puts it last in his [cumulative case](#cumulative-case): it works as the last step, not as a free-standing proof. Brain correlates alone debunk nothing: every experience has one (James's "medical materialism"; Persinger's "God helmet", whose 2005 double-blind replication by Granqvist's group tracked suggestibility); a mechanism bears on God only if theism and naturalism predict it differently.

*Lessons:* [3.4](lessons/03-04-the-argument-from-religious-experience.md)

### Marks of mystical experience

James, *Varieties of Religious Experience* (1902), Lectures XVI-XVII: ineffability and noetic quality (jointly sufficient), transiency and passivity (usually present).

The noetic mark (the state presents itself as knowledge) makes the comparison with perception possible; ineffability makes it hard to say what proposition is supported. James's verdict: such states are authoritative for those who have them, create no duty for outsiders, and show sense-based consciousness is not the only kind. Swinburne's and Alston's arguments cover any experience as of God, not only mystical ones.

*Introduced:* [3.4](lessons/03-04-the-argument-from-religious-experience.md)

### Principle of credulity

Swinburne: absent special considerations, if it seems to a subject that $x$ is present, probably $x$ is present.

The four special considerations: (i) unreliable subject or conditions; (ii) similar claims in similar circumstances have proved false; (iii) background evidence makes $x$'s presence very improbable; (iv) the experience was probably caused by something else. A defeasible default, not "believe whatever seems true"; arguing over (iv) is arguing inside the principle. Swinburne's is about seemings, closer to phenomenal conservatism ([epistemology 2.2](../epistemology/lessons/02-02-foundationalism.md)); Reid's "principle of credulity" was about testimony.

*Introduced:* [3.4](lessons/03-04-the-argument-from-religious-experience.md)

### Principle of testimony

Swinburne: absent special considerations, other people's experiences are probably as they report them.

The step that carries the argument to James's outsiders; counterpart of Reid's anti-reductionist default ([epistemology 4.4](../epistemology/lessons/04-04-testimony.md)).

*Introduced:* [3.4](lessons/03-04-the-argument-from-religious-experience.md)

### Doxastic practice

Alston (*Perceiving God*, 1991): a socially established way of forming beliefs from some input and testing them; it is rational to engage in an established practice absent sufficient reason to think it unreliable.

Sense perception cannot be shown reliable without using it (epistemic circularity), so demanding that Christian mystical practice pass sense perception's tests is "epistemic imperialism", a double standard. Its own overriders: fit with doctrine, fruits in a life. On diversity, Alston calls rival mystical practices with incompatible outputs the severest difficulty for his position; his worst-case reply: it is rational to continue in one's own undefeated practice ([5.5](lessons/05-05-religious-diversity.md)). Swinburnean alternative: count only the shared core, at the cost of a much thinner conclusion.

*Introduced:* [3.4](lessons/03-04-the-argument-from-religious-experience.md); used in [5.5](lessons/05-05-religious-diversity.md)

### Cross-checking objection

There is no public, intersubjective test for experiences of God, as there is for seeing a tree, so they do not earn perception's default.

Pressed by Evan Fales among others. Alston: intersubjective testing works where the object shows regularities; a personal God revealing himself at will would show none, so the absence of tests is expected even if the practice is reliable.

*Introduced:* [3.4](lessons/03-04-the-argument-from-religious-experience.md)

### Projection theory

God as humanity's own essence, objectified and worshipped as a separate being.

Feuerbach, *The Essence of Christianity* (1841); his words were objectification and alienation, and "projection" is the later label. Later versions: Freud (1927), cognitive accounts of agency detection. The naturalist rival that triggers consideration (iv) without first proving atheism, attacking P4.

*Introduced:* [3.4](lessons/03-04-the-argument-from-religious-experience.md)

### Miracle

Three definitions: a violation of a law (event); an act of an agent outside nature (agent); an effect whose cause is hidden from all creatures.

| Definition | Source | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Violation of a law of nature | Hume, *Enquiry* X §90; Swinburne (*The Concept of Miracle*, 1970): a non-repeatable counter-instance | on a pure regularity view of laws nothing can be violated |
| Intrusion from outside the natural order | Hume's footnote ("by a particular volition of the Deity, or by the interposition of some invisible agent"); Mackie (1982): laws describe the world "left to itself" | builds God (or an agent) into the event |
| Cause hidden from all creatures | Aquinas, ST I q.105 a.7 ([thomistic-synthesis 5.2](../thomistic-synthesis/lessons/05-02-providence-and-secondary-causes.md)) | |

Two questions: did the event happen (evidence), and was it a divine act (the [gap problem](#the-gap-problem): an undetected natural process, a misdiagnosis, another agent)? The Catholic view that miracles are signs making revelation credible ([fundamental-theology 2.2](../fundamental-theology/lessons/02-02-credibility-and-the-preambles-of-faith.md)) needs both; it is one position among others.

*Introduced:* [3.5](lessons/03-05-miracles.md)

### Humes maxim on testimony

*Enquiry* X Part I: believe a miracle report only if its falsehood would be more miraculous than the event.

Part I is the in-principle argument: a miracle is contrary to a law, laws rest on uniform experience, so the prior is as small as experience can make anything, and testimony (whose credit also rests on experience) must beat it. Uniformity is built into the definition, which makes the prior small by stipulation. In odds form, the report's likelihood ratio must beat the event's tiny prior. **Critic** (Lewis, *Miracles*, 1947): experience is uniform only if every miracle report is false, so assuming uniformity assumes the answer. **Humean:** the reports are weighed, a few items against the mass of observation behind the law. **Price** (1767): improbability alone cannot defeat testimony (a newspaper's lottery number); **Babbage** (1837) and **Earman** (2000): [independent witnesses](#independent-witnesses) beat any nonzero prior, so Part I as a barrier fails; **Fogelin** (2003): Part I was never a barrier, only the standard Part II shows no report meets. Not "extraordinary claims need extraordinary evidence": the maxim compares two improbabilities. The reconstruction exercise is owned by [philosophical-method 2.4](../philosophical-method/lessons/02-04-weighing-evidence-in-odds-form.md).

*Introduced:* [3.5](lessons/03-05-miracles.md)

### Humes four considerations

*Enquiry* X Part II: no actual miracle report has ever been good enough.

1. P1. No miracle is attested by enough witnesses of good sense, integrity and reputation, testifying publicly where detection would be unavoidable.
2. P2. Love of surprise and wonder, plus religious zeal, make false reports of marvels unusually frequent.
3. P3. Miracle reports abound among uneducated peoples, or descend from them, and thin out as reliable records begin.
4. P4. Each religion's miracles oppose every rival religion's.
5. ∴ No testimony for a miracle has amounted even to a probability, nor can establish one as the foundation of a religion.

In odds form P1-P3 say $P(\text{report} \mid \neg\text{event})$ is high, so the ratio is small; P4 adds evidence against each rival miracle. Hume limits the conclusion (§99): testimony could establish a violation (eight days of darkness from 1 January 1600), not a miracle founding a religion. **Turns on** independence: P2 and P3 describe exactly the conditions (rumour, shared enthusiasm, one source retold) that break it (Ahmed 2015). Defender of a particular report: independence must be argued case by case, not ruled out wholesale.

*Introduced:* [3.5](lessons/03-05-miracles.md)

### Independent witnesses

Each independent witness multiplies the odds by its likelihood ratio $L$, so any $L > 1$ eventually overcomes any prior odds above zero.

Price (*Four Dissertations*, 1767), Babbage (1837), Earman (*Hume's Abject Failure*, 2000). Formula in [Witnesses and miracles](#witnesses-and-miracles). Requires conditional independence given the truth or falsity of the event; copies of one report add nothing after the first. Refutes only the in-principle reading of Part I; whether actual reports meet it is Part II's question.

*Introduced:* [3.5](lessons/03-05-miracles.md)

### Logical problem of evil

The claim that God (omnipotent, omniscient, wholly good) and evil are logically inconsistent.

Mackie, "Evil and Omnipotence" (*Mind*, 1955), saw the core triad is not formally contradictory and added quasi-logical rules:

1. God is omnipotent, omniscient and wholly good.
2. A wholly good being eliminates evil as far as it can.
3. There are no (logically possible) limits to what an omnipotent being can do.
4. So if God exists, there is no evil (1-3).
5. Evil exists.
6. ∴ God, so conceived, does not exist.

Epicurus's questions, via Hume's Philo (*Dialogues* X; the attribution, from Lactantius, *On the Anger of God* 13, is uncertain). A reductio ([philosophical-method 4.1](../philosophical-method/lessons/04-01-objections-and-replies.md)) meeting all five reply types: R1 concede and restrict (finite God), R2 deny the objection's premise, R3 distinguish (omnipotence covers the possible only), R4 deny evil is real, R5 grant and outweigh; the live debate runs through R2, R3, R5. Mackie's strongest form: there is a possible world where all free creatures always do right; why not create it? **Where it turns:** line 2 needs a greater-good qualification; the atheist answers that for an omnipotent being the qualification bites only where the good-evil link is *logically* necessary, so the weight falls on line 3 and on whether F2's limit is real. Most philosophers, including many atheists, take the [free will defense](#free-will-defense) to answer it; Mackie (1982) conceded consistency, and the argument moved to probability. Philo already said "a mere possible compatibility is not sufficient".

*Lessons:* [4.1](lessons/04-01-the-logical-problem-of-evil.md); contrasted in [4.2](lessons/04-02-the-evidential-problem-of-evil.md)

### Defense and theodicy

A **defense** describes a *possible* situation in which God and evil coexist (enough for consistency; truth and plausibility not at issue). A **theodicy** claims to give God's actual, or probable, reason.

Plantinga (1974). To show two propositions consistent, find a third consistent with the first that together with it entails the second. A defense blocks the logical problem; the evidential problem sets the bar for a theodicy. The free will *theodicy* (freedom is God's reason) is a different claim from the *defense* (a reason of this shape is possible). Adams is closer to a defense with Christian content than a theodicy, since she denies knowing God's reasons for particular horrors.

*Introduced:* [4.1](lessons/04-01-the-logical-problem-of-evil.md); used in [4.3](lessons/04-03-theodicies.md)

### Free will defense

Plantinga (*The Nature of Necessity*; *God, Freedom, and Evil*, both 1974): assuming libertarian freedom, it is possible that God could not create moral good without moral evil.

- F1. Possibly a world with significantly free creatures doing more good than evil is better than any world without free creatures.
- F2. Possibly it was not within God's power to create a world containing moral good but no moral evil ([transworld depravity](#transworld-depravity)).
- F3. Possibly every natural evil is caused by the free choices of non-human persons (after Augustine).

With theism these make evil consistent, so line 3 of Mackie's argument, or the step to line 4, fails. F2 carries the weight. Contested premises it rests on (per its critics): libertarian freedom ([metaphysics 6.4](../metaphysics/lessons/06-04-libertarianism-and-its-critics.md)) and true counterfactuals of freedom without grounds ([Molinism](#molinism)'s grounding objection); Plantinga *states* it with counterfactuals of freedom. A compatibilist theist must answer Mackie otherwise. F3 may win consistency while doing nothing against the evidential problem; the objector replies a defense needs some reason to think it possible. Answers the logical, not the evidential, problem; consistency shows neither that God exists nor that evil is no evidence.

*Introduced:* [4.1](lessons/04-01-the-logical-problem-of-evil.md)

### Transworld depravity

A person suffers it if, for every world in which they are free and always do right, that world includes a situation in which, were God to create it, they would freely go wrong.

Plantinga's term. If possibly every creaturely essence suffers it, F2 is possible: Mackie's world of perfect free agents, though possible, is not one God could guarantee. Its target is **Leibniz's Lapse**, the assumption that an omnipotent God can actualize any possible world. Illustration: if Curley *would* freely take this bribe in these circumstances, God cannot actualize a world where Curley, in them, freely refuses; only the circumstances are up to God.

*Introduced:* [4.1](lessons/04-01-the-logical-problem-of-evil.md)

### Natural evil

Evil not caused by human free choice: disease, earthquakes, animal suffering.

The free will defense reaches it only via F3 (possibly non-human free persons cause it). Theodicies for it: [natural-law](#natural-law-theodicy), and the options for [animal suffering](#animal-suffering).

*Introduced:* [4.1](lessons/04-01-the-logical-problem-of-evil.md); theodicies in [4.3](lessons/04-03-theodicies.md)

### Evidential problem of evil

Evil makes God improbable rather than impossible; it survives the free will defense.

**Rowe** (*American Philosophical Quarterly*, 1979), case E1: a fawn trapped in a forest fire, in agony for days. $G$ = an omnipotent, omniscient, wholly good being exists.

1. P1. There are instances of intense suffering an omnipotent, omniscient being could have prevented without losing a greater good or permitting an equal or worse evil (gratuitous suffering).
2. P2. An omniscient, wholly good being would prevent any intense suffering it could, unless it could not do so without losing a greater good or permitting an equal or worse evil.
3. ∴ $\neg G$.

Valid; most theists accept P2 (some classical theists deny God's goodness consists in meeting obligations of that kind). The weight is on P1, supported by the [noseeum inference](#noseeum-inference). Replies: the [G. E. Moore shift](#g-e-moore-shift); [skeptical theism](#skeptical-theism) against the noseeum step; theodicies. **Draper** gives the comparative version: [Drapers argument from pain and pleasure](#drapers-argument-from-pain-and-pleasure). Hiddenness has an evidential form too ([4.5](lessons/04-05-divine-hiddenness.md)). A theist need not deny suffering is evidence: many grant it lowers theism's probability and argue other evidence outweighs it.

*Lessons:* [4.2](lessons/04-02-the-evidential-problem-of-evil.md); used in [4.5](lessons/04-05-divine-hiddenness.md)

### Noseeum inference

"We know of no good that justifies God in permitting this evil, so probably there is none."

- N1. After careful reflection, we know of no good that would justify God in permitting E1.
- N2. ∴ Probably there is none, so P1 is true.

The name is Wykstra's (1996), after an insect too small to see that still bites; the inference was criticized in his 1984 CORNEA paper. Rowe's defenders treat it as ordinary inference from surveyed to unsurveyed, strengthened by the number of cases. Attacked by [CORNEA](#cornea) and the [skeptical theses](#skeptical-theses). Draper's argument does not use it. The defender of theodicy says the critic's necessity-premise attack ("could God not have had the goods with less?") rests on it ([4.3](lessons/04-03-theodicies.md)).

*Introduced:* [4.2](lessons/04-02-the-evidential-problem-of-evil.md); assessed in [4.4](lessons/04-04-skeptical-theism.md)

### G. E. Moore shift

Keep Rowe's P2 and reason the other way: God exists, so no suffering is gratuitous.

1. $G$.
2. P2.
3. ∴ $\neg$P1.

Rowe's own name, after Moore's "here is a hand" ([epistemology 3.3](../epistemology/lessons/03-03-moore-and-the-dogmatist.md)). Valid; which argument is rational to accept depends on whether $G$ or P1 is better supported. Cogent for anyone whose grounds for $G$ do not rest on denying P1; begs the question only against someone with no such grounds. Rowe's **friendly atheism**: the evidence supports atheism, yet a theist with other grounds may rationally believe. In odds form the other evidence must be independent of how suffering is distributed, or it double-counts ([cumulative case](#cumulative-case)).

*Introduced:* [4.2](lessons/04-02-the-evidential-problem-of-evil.md)

### Drapers argument from pain and pleasure

Which hypothesis better predicts the pattern of pain and pleasure: theism, or indifference?

Draper, "Pain and Pleasure" (*Noûs*, 1989). $O$ = what we know about who feels pain and pleasure and when (moral agents and other sentient animals; biologically useful and not known to be useful). $T$ theism; HI the [hypothesis of indifference](#hypothesis-of-indifference).

1. D1. $O$ is known.
2. D2. $\Pr(O \mid \text{HI})$ is much greater than $\Pr(O \mid T)$.
3. D3. $T$ is not intrinsically much more probable than HI.
4. ∴ Other evidence held equal, $T$ is more probably false than true.

For D2: on HI pain and pleasure serve survival and reproduction, the pattern found; on $T$ pain needs a *moral* justification, with no antecedent reason it should track biological usefulness. Since HI entails $\neg T$, $\Pr(T) < \Pr(\text{HI})$ forces $\Pr(T) < \frac{1}{2}$. **Turns on** D2 at $\Pr(O \mid T)$ (can anyone say what a perfect being would permit? the [inscrutable likelihood](#inscrutable-likelihood)) and D3's prior parity ([epistemology 5.4](../epistemology/lessons/05-04-the-problem-of-priors.md)). Draper: D2 needs only a comparison, not a theory of divine reasons; inscrutability does not *raise* $\Pr(O \mid T)$ to match a well-understood rival ([4.4](lessons/04-04-skeptical-theism.md)). Numbers in [Draper odds](#draper-odds).

*Lessons:* [4.2](lessons/04-02-the-evidential-problem-of-evil.md); [4.4](lessons/04-04-skeptical-theism.md)

### Hypothesis of indifference

Draper (1989): neither the nature nor the condition of sentient beings on earth results from benevolent or malevolent actions by nonhuman persons.

Entails $\neg T$ but is not naturalism (it allows an indifferent creator). Its likelihood for the observed pattern is well understood, so inscrutability about theism's likelihood need not cancel the evidence ([4.4](lessons/04-04-skeptical-theism.md)).

*Introduced:* [4.2](lessons/04-02-the-evidential-problem-of-evil.md); used in [4.4](lessons/04-04-skeptical-theism.md)

### Greater-good theodicy skeleton

The common form of a theodicy for an evil $E$ God permits.

1. There is a good $G$ God could not secure without permitting $E$ or some evil as bad (**necessity premise**).
2. $G$ outweighs $E$ (**outweighing premise**).
3. A perfectly good being may permit an evil meeting 1 and 2.
4. So God's permitting $E$ is consistent with perfect goodness, and a reason is in view.

Adams adds 2′: $G$ defeats $E$ *within the sufferer's own life*. The surgeon analogy: the pain must be needed, worth it, and (many think) for the patient. **Where it turns:** for horrendous and animal suffering, the critic attacks necessity (could God not have had the goods with far less?; Draper's comparison is the probabilistic form); the defender says that attack rests on the noseeum inference. Adams attacks premise 3 from inside theism. Defenders run a cumulative case: each theodicy covers a region, and the test is whether the regions jointly cover the hard cases. Divinely commanded violence ([old-testament 3.1](../old-testament/lessons/03-01-joshua-judges-and-the-land.md)): hyperbole reading (Copan and Flannagan 2014), divine authority (Aquinas, [ethics 6.5](../ethics/lessons/06-05-god-and-morality-the-euthyphro-dilemma.md)), skeptical theism, each with its critic.

*Lessons:* [4.3](lessons/04-03-theodicies.md)

### Privation theory of evil

Augustine: evil is a lack of a good a thing ought to have, not a substance.

Texts in [ancient-medieval-philosophy 5.2](../ancient-medieval-philosophy/lessons/05-02-augustine-evil-and-the-will.md); doctrine in [thomistic-synthesis 2.4](../thomistic-synthesis/lessons/02-04-the-transcendentals.md). Explains evil's metaphysical status (God need not create evil as a thing; no second evil principle), not why God permits the lack, so it must be paired with a reason. Objection: pain and cruelty seem positive; reply: the evil of pain is the disorder it signals. Aquinas's impeccability reply needs it ([1.2](lessons/01-02-omnipotence-and-its-paradoxes.md)).

*Introduced:* [4.3](lessons/04-03-theodicies.md)

### Best possible world

Leibniz, *Theodicy* (1710) I §§8-9: a wise and good God must choose the best, or create nothing; the world is "all of one piece, like an ocean".

The claim is deduced from God's wisdom and goodness, not read off the world, so evil alone cannot refute it. Attacked by the [no-best-world objection](#no-best-world-objection).

*Introduced:* [4.3](lessons/04-03-theodicies.md)

### No-best-world objection

Rowe (*Can God Be Free?*, 2004): if every world has a better one, God is surpassable whatever he creates; if there is a best, God must create it and is not free.

Reply (Robert Adams, "Must God Create the Best?", 1972): creating a less than best world in which every creature's life is good on the whole wrongs no one, and may express grace.

*Introduced:* [4.3](lessons/04-03-theodicies.md)

### Soul-making theodicy

Hick (*Evil and the God of Love*, 1966; "Irenaean"): God makes persons who become good through struggle in a world of real dangers, a "vale of soul-making", at an **epistemic distance** that leaves faith free.

Objection: much suffering crushes rather than builds (infants, dementia, torture). Hick: if suffering were visibly apportioned to its use, compassion could not form; soul-making continues after death until all reach God. Rejoinder: the theodicy then rests on an eschatology the argument cannot independently support. Transposed to belief in 4.5 (seeking and hiddenness as goods: Pascal, Swinburne).

*Introduced:* [4.3](lessons/04-03-theodicies.md); used in [4.5](lessons/04-05-divine-hiddenness.md)

### Natural-law theodicy

Swinburne (*Providence and the Problem of Evil*, 1998): responsible choice needs knowledge of how to cause and prevent harm, which comes from regular laws that sometimes harm.

Objection (necessity premise): would a hundredth of this natural evil not teach the same? Why not reveal the knowledge directly? Swinburne: direct revelation would bring God so close that our choices would not be free (Hick's epistemic distance again).

*Introduced:* [4.3](lessons/04-03-theodicies.md)

### Horrendous evils

M. M. Adams (*Horrendous Evils and the Goodness of God*, 1999): evils whose doing or suffering gives prima facie reason to doubt the participant's life could be a great good to them on the whole.

Generic theodicies give at best [global defeat](#global-and-individual-defeat); divine love requires individual defeat. Her answer: intimacy with God is an incommensurable good that can make the horror part of a life that is a great good on the whole. She does not claim to know God's reasons for particular horrors and draws on specifically Christian resources; the critic says that content must earn its own probability.

*Introduced:* [4.3](lessons/04-03-theodicies.md)

### Global and individual defeat

Global: the world as a whole is better for the evil. Individual: the evil is defeated within the sufferer's own life.

Adams requires individual defeat of divine love, which is premise 2′ of the [theodicy skeleton](#greater-good-theodicy-skeleton).

*Introduced:* [4.3](lessons/04-03-theodicies.md)

### Animal suffering

Animal pain predates humans by hundreds of millions of years and builds no soul; Murray (*Nature Red in Tooth and Claw*, 2008) surveys the options without endorsing one as sufficient.

**Neo-Cartesian** (many animals not aware of pain in the morally relevant way; an empirical claim that does not transfer to higher animals), **fall** (a primordial rebellion), **nomic regularity** (price of a law-governed world), **chaos-to-order** (a universe developing to order is itself a great good). Critic: each is empirically doubtful or a natural-law theodicy with a larger bill; combined with a skeptical-theist claim it is no longer a theodicy. Defender: the case is cumulative.

*Introduced:* [4.3](lessons/04-03-theodicies.md)

### Skeptical theism

If God exists, not seeing God's reasons for permitting evil is what we should expect, so the noseeum inference fails.

1. P1. Rowe's premise rests on a noseeum inference.
2. P2. A noseeum inference requires the goods we know of to be a representative sample of the goods there are.
3. P3. We have no good reason to think they are ([skeptical theses](#skeptical-theses)).
4. ∴ Rowe's premise is not supported by the noseeum inference.

**Undercutting, not rebutting:** Rowe's premise might be true. Not a theodicy (names no reason) and not skepticism about God (a skeptical theist may be confident God exists). Wykstra's analogy: an infant at the vaccination clinic. Objections: [moral paralysis](#moral-paralysis-objection), [skepticism spreads](#skepticism-spreads-objection), Draper (no noseeum needed). **Where it turns:** P3 at its scope; the theses must block Rowe yet leave moral judgment, trust in revelation and theistic arguments standing. Defender: ST1-ST3 are a general modesty about sampling, and the symmetry demand fails because God's role and ours differ. Mirror image: the [inscrutable designer objection](#inscrutable-designer-objection) to fine-tuning. Applied to hiddenness, it denies the "always" in premise 2 (Howard-Snyder).

*Lessons:* [4.4](lessons/04-04-skeptical-theism.md); applied in [4.5](lessons/04-05-divine-hiddenness.md)

### CORNEA

Wykstra (1984), the Condition On ReasoNable Epistemic Access: from "it appears there is no X" you may infer "there is no X" only if, were there an X, things would likely appear different to you.

No dog in the garage: good inference. No flea: bad inference. A cousin of Nozick's sensitivity condition ([epistemology 1.2](../epistemology/lessons/01-02-sensitivity-and-safety.md)). The skeptical theist says God's reasons are fleas, not dogs. Critic of the parent analogy: the student has independent grounds to trust the oncologist, and the oncologist can explain afterwards, while God's silence is itself data ([4.5](lessons/04-05-divine-hiddenness.md)).

*Introduced:* [4.4](lessons/04-04-skeptical-theism.md)

### Skeptical theses

Bergmann (*Noûs*, 2001), answering Rowe's revised argument of 1996: we have no good reason to think that the goods (ST1), evils (ST2), and entailments between goods and permissions of evils (ST3) we know of are representative of those there are.

His later work adds ST4: no good reason to think the total value we perceive in a complex state of affairs reflects its real total value. Each thesis is about the representativeness of a sample, not about God; an atheist can accept them.

*Introduced:* [4.4](lessons/04-04-skeptical-theism.md)

### Inscrutable likelihood

The skeptical theist's claim that $\Pr(E \mid T)$ cannot be assessed, so we cannot say whether the likelihood ratio is below 1.

Averaged over a credence spread, it gives a definite (often still sub-1) ratio, so inscrutability shrinks the evidence without cancelling it (close to Draper's point). Held as an imprecise credence ([epistemology 5.1](../epistemology/lessons/05-01-credences-and-probabilism.md)), it gives no direction (the skeptical theist's point). Which treatment ignorance deserves is the problem of priors, not a question about God. See [Inscrutability averaged and imprecise](#inscrutability-averaged-and-imprecise).

*Introduced:* [4.4](lessons/04-04-skeptical-theism.md)

### Moral paralysis objection

Almeida and Oppy (2003): if I cannot judge that a child's suffering serves no unseen good, I cannot judge that I should prevent it.

A structural claim about inference, not a charge of callousness. Replies: our obligations depend on what *we* know and our role (a parent may permit a hurt a babysitter must prevent), and on God's commands to prevent harm (Bergmann and Rea 2005). The reply must break the parity between Rowe's judgment and the bystander's.

*Introduced:* [4.4](lessons/04-04-skeptical-theism.md)

### Skepticism spreads objection

If God's reasons are beyond us, so are God's possible reasons to deceive us: about promises, revelation, the external world.

Wielenberg ("Sceptical Theism and Divine Lies", 2010); Maitzen on divine commands; Hasker ("All Too Skeptical Theism", 2010). It spreads to natural theology: fine-tuning assumes we can judge what God would likely do. Bergmann: the theses concern only reasons for permitting evil, and external-world knowledge rests on externalist grounds. Critics: limiting the skepticism to evil is ad hoc.

*Introduced:* [4.4](lessons/04-04-skeptical-theism.md)

### Divine hiddenness argument

Schellenberg (1993; *The Hiddenness Argument*, 2015): a perfectly loving God would always be open to relationship, so there would be no nonresistant nonbelief; there is some; so no God.

1. Necessarily, if God exists, God perfectly loves every finite person.
2. Necessarily, if God perfectly loves finite persons, God is always open to a meaningful conscious relationship with each one capable of it.
3. Necessarily, if God is always so open, no capable person is ever in nonresistant nonbelief.
4. At least one capable finite person is, or was, in nonresistant nonbelief.
5. ∴ God does not exist.

Valid, deductive (modus tollens through necessary conditionals). Premise 1 is close to definitional ([perfect-being theology](#perfect-being-theology)).

| Response | Premise | Who |
|---|---|---|
| Love holds back for a time, for a good (freedom, mutual responsibility, non-coercion, seeking) | 2 | Swinburne (1979; 1998 ch. 11), Murray (2002), Pascal |
| Divine love is not parental love writ large | 2 | Rea (2018) |
| Unseen goods for which love would wait | 2 ("always") | skeptical theism (Howard-Snyder) |
| Relationship without belief (hope, trust, love of goodness) | 3 | Cullison (2010), Moser, Stump; Schellenberg: a *conscious* relationship needs belief |
| All nonbelief involves resistance | 4 | Reformed tradition ([sensus divinitatis](#sensus-divinitatis), Romans 1), Henry (2001), Pascal's distribution claim |

**Where it turns:** premise 2 (love compatible with purposeful distance?); Schellenberg: the goods of hiddenness are available inside the relationship. Second battleground: premise 4, empirical and hard to settle. Early humans with no concept of God (Marsh 2013) strain the premise-4 denial and freedom-based goods at once. Pascal (*Pensées* 430, Trotter) anticipated it: enough light for those who seek, enough obscurity for a contrary disposition. Not a demand for overwhelming proof: only evidence enough for belief, for those not resisting. Whether it is a species of the problem of evil: Schellenberg (2010) and van Inwagen say independent (a painless world could still argue from nonbelief; premises 1-4 never call nonbelief bad); theists reply the two problems share their best answers. Evidential form in [Hiddenness odds](#hiddenness-odds).

*Lessons:* [4.5](lessons/04-05-divine-hiddenness.md)

### Nonresistant nonbelief

Failing to believe that God exists without having brought this about oneself: no self-deception, no avoidance of evidence, no wish that God not exist.

Schellenberg's 2015 test; in 1993 he spoke of reasonable or inculpable nonbelief. Effort is not the test: someone who never considered God, through no avoidance of their own, counts.

*Introduced:* [4.5](lessons/04-05-divine-hiddenness.md)

## Module 5 Faith and reason

Whether belief in God needs arguments at all, whether practical reasons can license it, whether faith answers to reason, and what religious diversity does to confidence. These are de jure questions (is the belief rational?), kept apart from the de facto one (is it true?).

### Map of positions on faith and reason

| Position | Must belief in God rest on argument? | Answerable to defeaters? | Premise of the [evidentialist objection](#evidentialist-objection) it denies | Lesson |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Evidentialist atheism (Mackie) | yes | yes | none: defends P4 | [5.1](lessons/05-01-evidentialism-and-the-ethics-of-belief.md) |
| Evidentialist theism, natural theology (Swinburne; *Dei Filius* as a claim about reason's capacity) | yes | yes | P4 | [5.1](lessons/05-01-evidentialism-and-the-ethics-of-belief.md) |
| [Reformed epistemology](#properly-basic-belief) | no | yes | P2 | [5.2](lessons/05-02-reformed-epistemology.md) |
| Pragmatic arguments ([wager](#pascals-wager-as-a-reason-to-cultivate-belief), [will to believe](#will-to-believe-thesis)) | not always: practical reasons may decide some options | yes | limits P1 | [5.3](lessons/05-03-pragmatic-arguments-the-wager-and-the-will-to-believe.md) |
| [Fideism](#fideism) (Kierkegaardian, Wittgensteinian, traditionalist) | no | no | rejects P1 for faith | [5.4](lessons/05-04-fideism.md) |

The box that separates reformed epistemology from fideism is answerability to defeaters, not whether arguments are required.

### Evidentialism

A belief is justified only if it fits, in proportion, the believer's evidence.

Hume, *Enquiry* §10: "A wise man, therefore, proportions his belief to the evidence." Two versions: **Clifford's** (1877) is about duty and blame, you wrong others by believing badly; **Conee and Feldman's** ("Evidentialism", 1985; *Evidentialism*, 2004) is about synchronic epistemic justification, fit with the evidence you have now, experiences included, silent on blame and inquiry. Not an atheist doctrine: Locke and Swinburne hold it, and natural theology tries to meet it.

*Introduced:* [5.1](lessons/05-01-evidentialism-and-the-ethics-of-belief.md)

### Cliffords principle

"It is wrong always, everywhere, and for anyone, to believe anything upon insufficient evidence" (Clifford, "The Ethics of Belief", *Contemporary Review*, 1877).

The shipowner stifles his doubts about an old ship; he is guilty of the emigrants' deaths, and would be as guilty had she come home safe, because the wrong is in how the belief was formed, not whether it is true. Duty of inquiry: never lawful to stifle a doubt. Belief is moral because beliefs guide later action and careless belief trains credulity; no time to study means no time to believe. In "The Weight of Authority" he separates honesty from knowledge and limits testimony by verifiability. Neutral on content: it condemns the avoiding atheist as readily as the avoiding believer. James's "The Will to Believe" (1896) replies to it ([5.3](lessons/05-03-pragmatic-arguments-the-wager-and-the-will-to-believe.md)).

*Introduced:* [5.1](lessons/05-01-evidentialism-and-the-ethics-of-belief.md); used in [5.3](lessons/05-03-pragmatic-arguments-the-wager-and-the-will-to-believe.md)

### Evidentialist objection

Belief in God is irrational because it is neither properly basic nor supported by adequate evidence from what is.

1. P1 (evidentialism). S's belief that p is rational only if p is properly basic for S, or based on adequate evidence from S's properly basic beliefs.
2. P2 ([classical foundationalism](#classical-foundationalism)). p is properly basic for S only if self-evident, incorrigible, or evident to the senses for S.
3. P3. "God exists" is none of these for S.
4. P4. There is no adequate evidence for it from such beliefs.
5. ∴ S's belief in God is not rational.

Plantinga's label and reconstruction ("Reason and Belief in God", 1983); representative holders Scriven (1966) and Flew (1972). Valid, with a different opponent at each premise: evidentialist theists deny P4; reformed epistemologists deny P2; pragmatists limit P1; fideists reject P1 for faith. Clifford's principle becomes anti-theistic only by adding P4. **Where it turns:** P2 (self-reference; cannot ground beliefs about other minds, the past, the external world). Evidentialist reply: swap P2 for modest foundationalism or Conee-Feldman fit; the objection becomes "on your total evidence, experiences included, belief does not fit", relocating the dispute to P4 and to whether [religious experience](#argument-from-religious-experience) is evidence. Reformed reply: the relocated objection has given up the claim that theistic belief needs argument.

*Lessons:* [5.1](lessons/05-01-evidentialism-and-the-ethics-of-belief.md)

### Classical foundationalism

A belief is properly basic only if it is self-evident, incorrigible, or evident to the senses.

General theory in [epistemology 2.2](../epistemology/lessons/02-02-foundationalism.md). Plantinga's self-referential problem: the criterion is not itself self-evident, incorrigible or sensory, and no one has produced adequate evidence for it from such beliefs, so by its own standard it is not rationally believed. It refutes classical foundationalism as a criterion of basicality, not Conee-Feldman evidentialism.

*Introduced:* [5.1](lessons/05-01-evidentialism-and-the-ethics-of-belief.md)

### Properly basic belief

Held without inference from other beliefs, yet rational; grounded in occasioning circumstances (not groundless), and open to defeat.

Plantinga (1981, 1983). "God made all this" under a starry sky, "God disapproves of what I did" after wronging a friend, are occasioned as "there is a tree" is by seeing one.

1. P1. A belief may be rational without inferential support if formed in appropriate circumstances with no undefeated defeaters (modest foundationalism).
2. P2. Beliefs like "God made all this", so formed, are of this kind.
3. P3. They self-evidently entail that God exists.
4. ∴ Belief in God can be rational without arguments.

**Turns on** P2 and its successor ("if God exists, the belief is probably warranted"): critic, without an independent check on which faculties are reliable, "properly" adds nothing to "basic" ([Great Pumpkin](#great-pumpkin-objection)); defender, no one has a non-circular check on memory or perception either, so demanding one for religious belief alone is a double standard ([parity argument](#parity-argument)). Gives no one else a reason to believe and is not a proof; answers the de jure question only. Not fideism if genuinely answerable to [defeaters](#defeaters) ([5.4](lessons/05-04-fideism.md)).

*Introduced:* [5.2](lessons/05-02-reformed-epistemology.md); used in [5.4](lessons/05-04-fideism.md), [5.5](lessons/05-05-religious-diversity.md)

### Sensus divinitatis

Calvin, *Institutes* I.iii (paraphrased): a natural sense of the divine in every human being, triggered by circumstances and dulled by sin.

In Plantinga's Aquinas/Calvin model (*Warranted Christian Belief*, 2000), the faculty that produces basic belief in God. With Romans 1, it grounds the Reformed denial that nonresistant nonbelief exists ([4.5](lessons/04-05-divine-hiddenness.md)).

*Introduced:* [5.2](lessons/05-02-reformed-epistemology.md); used in [4.5](lessons/04-05-divine-hiddenness.md)

### De jure and de facto objections

De facto: theism is false. De jure: belief in God is irrational or unwarranted whether or not God exists.

Plantinga (WCB, 2000): there is no viable de jure objection independent of the de facto one. If God exists, the A/C model (or something like it) is probably true and the belief probably warranted; if not, probably not. So a critic who would show the belief unwarranted must first argue God does not exist. A projection theory, for instance, undercuts only if belief comes from wish-fulfilment *rather than* a sensus divinitatis, which presupposes atheism; critic: a naturalistic account explaining the whole pattern of religious belief is evidence against God, so it can undercut and bear on the de facto question together. What counts as an "independent" de jure objection is itself disputed.

*Introduced:* [5.2](lessons/05-02-reformed-epistemology.md)

### Parity argument

Plantinga (*God and Other Minds*, 1967): the analogical argument for other minds and the design argument share the same weakness; belief in other minds is rational, so belief in God is too.

The 1981-83 work extends the parity class to memory and perception.

*Introduced:* [5.2](lessons/05-02-reformed-epistemology.md)

### Great Pumpkin objection

If belief in God can be properly basic, why not Linus's belief that the Great Pumpkin returns every Halloween?

Plantinga raised it against himself (*Noûs*, 1981). Replies: criteria for proper basicality are reached **bottom up** from paradigm cases; basic beliefs need grounds, and the Pumpkin belief has none of the right kind. **Son of Great Pumpkin** (Michael Martin, *Atheism: A Philosophical Justification*, 1990): concedes the believer's belief need not be arbitrary, but the *method* lets any community certify its own paradigms (a voodoo community). Plantinga (WCB): warrant, unlike 1983 rationality, depends on truth, so a false community belief gets none. Critic: then the theist's warrant depends on exactly what was in dispute. Pressed in 5.4 as the charge that reformed epistemology is fideism in practice.

*Introduced:* [5.2](lessons/05-02-reformed-epistemology.md); used in [5.4](lessons/05-04-fideism.md)

### Defeaters

A rebutting defeater is a reason to think the belief false; an undercutting defeater is a reason to distrust its source (Pollock's distinction).

Candidates for theism: evil (Module 4), hiddenness ([4.5](lessons/04-05-divine-hiddenness.md)), projection theories ([3.4](lessons/03-04-the-argument-from-religious-experience.md)), diversity ([5.5](lessons/05-05-religious-diversity.md)). Responses: a **defeater-defeater** (a defense or theodicy), or Plantinga's **intrinsic defeater-defeater** (the basic belief has more warrant than the defeater, as a vivid memory beats someone else's evidence). Answerability to defeaters is the test separating reformed epistemology from fideism ([5.4](lessons/05-04-fideism.md)).

*Introduced:* [5.2](lessons/05-02-reformed-epistemology.md); used in [5.4](lessons/05-04-fideism.md), [5.5](lessons/05-05-religious-diversity.md)

### Warrant and proper function

Warrant is what turns true belief into knowledge; on Plantinga's account a belief has it if produced by faculties functioning properly, in the environment they were designed for, by a good design plan aimed at truth.

The general theory is [epistemology 2.4](../epistemology/lessons/02-04-reliabilism.md). The A/C model applies it: *if* God made us with a sensus divinitatis, belief so formed is warranted and could be knowledge without argument. Plantinga presents the model as possibly true and denies philosophy can show it true.

*Introduced:* [5.2](lessons/05-02-reformed-epistemology.md)

### Pascals wager as a reason to cultivate belief

The decision-theoretic wager gives a prudential reason to work toward belief in God.

1. W1. Reason cannot settle whether God exists.
2. W2. One must live as a believer or an unbeliever; not choosing is an option.
3. W3. Given the stakes, wagering for God maximizes expected utility ([decision-theory 6.1](../decision-theory/lessons/06-01-pascals-wager-as-a-decision-problem.md), granted with its objections).
4. W4. Wagering for God means coming to believe, or working to.
5. W5. One can do what W4 describes.
6. W6. The belief so produced is the kind the payoff rewards.
7. ∴ One has a prudential reason to cultivate belief in God.

W5: [doxastic involuntarism](#doxastic-involuntarism) and Pascal's [indirect voluntarism](#indirect-voluntarism). **Turns on** W6: the [mercenary-belief objection](#mercenary-belief-objection). Defender: the motive changes along the way. Whether cultivated belief is rational turns on whether the regimen *caused* the belief or *cleared the way* for grounds it now rests on. Pascal, *Pensées* (1670), §233 (Trotter). James calls the option dead for anyone with no prior leaning toward masses and holy water, a clincher for those already leaning.

*Lessons:* [5.3](lessons/05-03-pragmatic-arguments-the-wager-and-the-will-to-believe.md)

### Doxastic involuntarism

We cannot believe at will as we raise an arm at will.

Williams ("Deciding to Believe", 1970): belief aims at truth, so a state known to be willed regardless of truth could not be regarded as a belief. Alston (*Epistemic Justification*, 1989): we lack the voluntary control over belief that talk of epistemic duty seems to presuppose. Pascal grants it (*Pensées* §233): the interlocutor accepts the calculation and cannot believe. So it does not refute the wager.

*Introduced:* [5.3](lessons/05-03-pragmatic-arguments-the-wager-and-the-will-to-believe.md)

### Indirect voluntarism

You cannot choose a belief, but you can choose actions that reliably cause it, as training causes fitness.

Pascal's advice: act "as if they believed, taking the holy water, having masses said" (*Pensées* §233, Trotter); "deaden your acuteness" renders *vous abêtira*, more bluntly "make you dull". The obstacle is the passions, not a shortage of proofs.

*Introduced:* [5.3](lessons/05-03-pragmatic-arguments-the-wager-and-the-will-to-believe.md)

### Mercenary-belief objection

A belief cultivated for a reward is not the faith rewarded, or a good God would not reward it.

Raised by James himself: faith adopted after "such a mechanical calculation would lack the inner soul of faith's reality". Targets W6. Reply: the calculation is where the journey starts; Pascal's regimen aims to change the motive, as a friendship begun for advantage need not stay one.

*Introduced:* [5.3](lessons/05-03-pragmatic-arguments-the-wager-and-the-will-to-believe.md)

### Genuine option

James (1896): a choice between hypotheses that is **living** (both live for you), **forced** (no standing place outside), and **momentous** (unique, high stakes, irreversible).

Contrasts: dead, avoidable, trivial. "Live" is a relation to a thinker, not a property of the hypothesis. Most options fail at least one test, and there James defers to the evidence (a lab catalyst: avoidable and trivial, so suspend and test, as Clifford would).

*Introduced:* [5.3](lessons/05-03-pragmatic-arguments-the-wager-and-the-will-to-believe.md)

### Will to believe thesis

James: "Our passional nature not only lawfully may, but must, decide an option between propositions, whenever it is a genuine option that cannot by its nature be decided on intellectual grounds."

1. J1. There are two duties, believe truth and shun error, and no rule of the intellect fixes their weights.
2. J2. Clifford's rule weighs avoiding error above everything, itself a passional preference.
3. J3. In a forced option, suspending loses the truth as surely as rejecting does, so suspension is not neutral.
4. J4. So in a genuine option the intellect cannot decide, and declining is itself a passional decision.
5. ∴ In such an option hope rather than fear may decide.

Plus faith-that-creates-the-fact cases (meeting someone half-way), and the possibility that evidence is withheld from those who refuse to meet the hypothesis half-way, so a rule forbidding belief first could block a real truth. A claim about permission, not maximizing; James rejects Pascal's calculation. **Turns on** J3 ("forced"): one can practise, hope and act without believing; and the thesis seems to license wishful thinking wherever evidence runs out. Defender: the critic's rule rests on J2's preference, no more intellectually required than James's.

*Introduced:* [5.3](lessons/05-03-pragmatic-arguments-the-wager-and-the-will-to-believe.md)

### Fideism

Religious faith is not answerable to rational evaluation: evidence and argument neither ground it nor can properly overturn it.

Plantinga's definition (1983, adopted by the SEP): exclusive or basic reliance on faith, with reason disparaged. **Suprarational** (faith concerns what reason cannot reach) vs **contrarational** (faith embraces what reason judges false or absurd). Grounds: passion and decision (Kierkegaard), practice-internal criteria (Wittgensteinian), handed-down revelation (traditionalism, [fundamental-theology 2.3](../fundamental-theology/lessons/02-03-fideism-and-rationalism.md)). Not fideist: "faith exceeds reason" (Aquinas, Vatican I, most natural theologians); "belief in God need not be inferred" (reformed epistemology). The test is **immunity** to defeaters. Critics: *secular*, a belief no evidence could count against cannot track truth, and rival faiths cannot be ranked (Hume's irony: "Our most holy religion is founded on Faith, not on reason"); *religious*, faith reason cannot assess cannot be told from credulity; Catholic teaching rejects fideism (*Dei Filius*, 1870, defines a capacity of reason, not a proof, and does not use the word "fideism"; its target was traditionalism). Tertullian never wrote *credo quia absurdum* ([patristics 2.3](../patristics/lessons/02-03-tertullian.md)).

*Introduced:* [5.4](lessons/05-04-fideism.md)

### Kierkegaards leap

Faith as passionate, unconditional commitment held fast in objective uncertainty.

Johannes Climacus (*Philosophical Fragments*, 1844; *Concluding Unscientific Postscript*, 1846); interpreters warn against equating Climacus with Kierkegaard.

1. Objective inquiry yields at best approximation.
2. Faith is an unconditional commitment.
3. An unconditional commitment cannot be proportioned to, or wait upon, an approximation.
4. The object of Christian faith (the god in time) is the absolute paradox: reason takes offense or yields.
5. ∴ Faith cannot rest on objective reasoning; it is a leap.

**Turns on** 3: critic, proportioning commitment to evidence is what responsible belief is, and this is what Clifford condemned; defender, marriage, vocation and loyalty are unconditional commitments under uncertainty we do not call irrational. Evans reads Kierkegaard as at most a "responsible" fideist.

*Introduced:* [5.4](lessons/05-04-fideism.md)

### Wittgensteinian fideism

Meaning is use within a form of life, and standards of evidence and mistake are internal to practices, so treating religious belief as a hypothesis misunderstands it.

1. Meaning is fixed by use within a practice.
2. Standards of evidence, reason and mistake are internal to practices.
3. "God exists", prayer, "eternal life" function within religious practice, not as explanatory hypotheses.
4. ∴ Outside-evidence criticism misunderstands its object; religion is neither supported nor threatened by it.

Nielsen's critical label (*Philosophy*, 1967); D. Z. Phillips (1970, 1976) rejected it. **Turns on** 2: practice-internal criteria would equally protect astrology or witchcraft; and most believers mean "God exists" as true regardless of practice, so 3 may reinterpret religion rather than defend it. Phillips: practices are not sealed off; religion can be confused, superstitious or shaken by life, but not refuted as a failed theory.

*Introduced:* [5.4](lessons/05-04-fideism.md)

### Exclusivism inclusivism pluralism

Race's typology (1983): **exclusivism**, one tradition is right where traditions conflict; **inclusivism**, one tradition is normative but its truth or saving power reaches others; **pluralism**, the major religions are roughly equally valid responses to one ultimate reality.

Keep the **truth** axis and the **salvation** axis apart: Plantinga's exclusivism is about truth and compatible with inclusivism about salvation. Logic forces some truth-exclusivism on almost everyone (triune or not; enduring self or not). Inclusivist positions cited, not asserted: Rahner's "anonymous Christian" (1966); *Nostra Aetate* 2 (1965) and *Lumen Gentium* 16 (1964); how they square with older formulas is [apologetics-foundations](../apologetics-foundations/syllabus.md) 8.2's work. Pluralist: Hick. Pluralism is a first-order thesis most adherents of every tradition reject, not the neutral default.

*Introduced:* [5.5](lessons/05-05-religious-diversity.md)

### The Real

Hick (*An Interpretation of Religion*, 1989): the Real *an sich* vs the Real as humanly experienced, through cultural concepts as personae (Yahweh, the Trinity, Allah, Vishnu) and impersonae (Brahman, the Dharmakaya, Nirvana).

1. H1. The great traditions produce, at roughly equal rates, the same transformation from self-centredness to Reality-centredness.
2. H2. If one tradition alone were in contact with the ultimate, that parity would be inexplicable, and morally troubling given that belief tracks birthplace.
3. H3. A Kantian-type distinction explains the parity.
4. ∴ The traditions are different authentic responses to one Real that none describes literally; doctrines are "mythologically" true.

Objections: **contentlessness** (if no substantive concept applies to the Real, nothing explains why the personae should be good or count as responses to it; Plantinga, "Ad Hick", 1997; Hick: only formal properties apply); **pluralism is a rival creed** (it says Christians and Theravada Buddhists are both wrong on literal points, a covert exclusivism, D'Costa; Hick replied, 1997). "Copernican revolution" image: *God and the Universe of Faiths* (1973).

*Introduced:* [5.5](lessons/05-05-religious-diversity.md)

### Arbitrariness charge

Against the exclusivist in Plantinga's **condition C** (knows the other traditions and their saints, has no argument that would convince them): she favours her own beliefs over similar ones for no relevant reason.

Plantinga ("Pluralism: A Defense of Religious Exclusivism", 1995): on reflection she does not think the cases similar (the belief seems true, its rival false); compare certainty that bigotry is wrong despite disagreement; the birthplace argument cuts against the pluralist too. The separate **moral** charge (arrogance) meets the **self-referential reply**: believing, withholding and pluralism all put her at odds with thoughtful people, so the charge convicts the pluralist too. Concession: awareness of diversity can lower degree of belief and with it warrant. Critic: the reply works equally for every party.

*Introduced:* [5.5](lessons/05-05-religious-diversity.md)

### Religious disagreement

Conciliationism applied to religion: knowing peers disagree should substantially lower your confidence.

1. D1. Many adherents of incompatible traditions are epistemic peers on the question: shared evidence, equal competence.
2. D2. Conciliationism with Independence: knowingly disagreeing with a peer, with no dispute-independent reason to think she erred, you should substantially lower your confidence.
3. D3. The believer typically has no such reason.
4. ∴ D4. She should substantially lower her confidence (Feldman 2007: suspend).

General theory: [epistemology 4.5](../epistemology/lessons/04-05-peer-disagreement.md). **Turns on** D1's shared-evidence clause: Plantinga, Alston and others deny religious evidence is fully shared (experience, the internal witness); critic: every party can say that, which brings back arbitrariness; defender: that assumes evidence one cannot hand over cannot be weighed from inside. D2 is contested (steadfast and total-evidence views; self-undermining). Symmetric: it applies equally to atheism, agnosticism and conciliationism itself. Diversity as a defeater for experience: Alston's worst-case reply under [doxastic practice](#doxastic-practice).

*Introduced:* [5.5](lessons/05-05-religious-diversity.md)

## Formulas and formal steps

The modal and probabilistic machinery the lessons use, compact. Symbols: $\Box$ true in every possible world, $\Diamond$ true in some world; $P(A \mid B)$ the probability of $A$ given $B$; odds $O = P(H)/P(\neg H)$; likelihood ratio $L = P(E \mid H)/P(E \mid \neg H)$. Odds form itself is owned by [philosophical-method 2.4](../philosophical-method/lessons/02-04-weighing-evidence-in-odds-form.md).

### Odds form

Where you end is where you started, times how much more expected the evidence is on the hypothesis than on its rival.

$$\frac{P(H_1 \mid E)}{P(H_2 \mid E)} = \frac{P(H_1)}{P(H_2)} \times \frac{P(E \mid H_1)}{P(E \mid H_2)}$$

Instances in the course, with the term each argument turns on:

| Lesson | $H_1$ vs $H_2$ | $E$ | Disputed term |
|---|---|---|---|
| [3.1](lessons/03-01-from-design-to-fine-tuning.md), [3.2](lessons/03-02-fine-tuning-the-objections.md) | design $D$ vs single-universe chance $C$ | life-permitting constants | $P(E \mid C)$; also $P(E \mid D)$ |
| [3.3](lessons/03-03-moral-arguments.md) | theism $G$ vs naturalism $N$ | objective obligations | $\Pr(E \mid N)$ |
| [3.4](lessons/03-04-the-argument-from-religious-experience.md) | $G$ vs $\neg G$ | widespread experiences as of God | $P(E \mid \neg G)$ |
| [3.5](lessons/03-05-miracles.md) | event vs no event | testimony | $P(\text{report} \mid \neg\text{event})$; independence |
| [4.2](lessons/04-02-the-evidential-problem-of-evil.md), [4.4](lessons/04-04-skeptical-theism.md) | $T$ vs HI | pattern of pain and pleasure | $\Pr(O \mid T)$; prior parity |
| [4.5](lessons/04-05-divine-hiddenness.md) | $T$ vs $N$ | pattern of nonbelief | $P(E \mid T)$ |

To convert odds $a:b$ to a probability: $a/(a+b)$. To stay at even odds after evidence with ratio $L$, prior odds must be $1/L$.

### Cumulative odds

Several items of evidence combine by multiplying ratios, each computed given the items already counted.

$$\frac{P(G \mid E_1 \wedge E_2)}{P(\neg G \mid E_1 \wedge E_2)} = \frac{P(G)}{P(\neg G)} \times L_1 \times L_2'$$

$L_1 = P(E_1 \mid G)/P(E_1 \mid \neg G)$; $L_2'$ is the ratio for $E_2$ given $E_1$. Items sharing a premise count once; unfavourable items enter the same product. *From* [1.1](lessons/01-01-which-god-and-what-an-argument-must-do.md); see [cumulative case](#cumulative-case).

### Modal fallacy

Necessity of the consequence is not necessity of the consequent.

Valid (T, then modus ponens):

$$\Box(G \to S),\ G \ \vdash\ S$$

Invalid:

$$\Box(G \to S),\ G \ \nvdash\ \Box S$$

Countermodel: world $w_1$ has $G$ and $S$; world $w_2$ has neither. $\Box(G \to S)$ holds, $G$ holds at $w_1$, $S$ fails at $w_2$, so $\Box S$ fails at $w_1$. Adding $\Box G$ makes $\Box S$ follow by K. Reading the premise as $G \to \Box S$ makes it valid but false. *From* [1.3](lessons/01-03-omniscience-and-human-freedom.md); used in [1.4](lessons/01-04-eternity-immutability-and-simplicity.md).

### Transfer of necessity

The foreknowledge argument's line 5: lack of choice passes along strict entailment.

$$N p,\ \Box(p \to q) \ \vdash\ N q$$

$N p$: $p$ is true and no one now has any choice about it. The strict-entailment form avoids McKay and Johnson's coin case against Beta. *From* [1.3](lessons/01-03-omniscience-and-human-freedom.md).

### Modal collapse derivations

Both at outline level; full statements in the lessons.

**Against simplicity** ([1.4](lessons/01-04-eternity-immutability-and-simplicity.md)). $G$ God, $A$ God's act of willing creation, $C$ the created world as it is.

1. $G = A$ (simplicity).
2. $\Box(G \text{ exists})$ (necessary existence).
3. $\Box(G = A)$ (necessity of identity).
4. $\Box(A \text{ exists})$.
5. $\Box(A \text{ exists} \to C)$ (efficacy).
6. $\Box C$ (K).

Valid; the dispute is over 1, 2 and 5. It does not use the [modal fallacy](#modal-fallacy): line 4 gets the necessity of the act itself.

**Against the PSR** ([2.4](lessons/02-04-cosmological-arguments-sufficient-reason.md); van Inwagen 1983). $p$ the BCCF, $q$ its explanation.

1. Every contingent truth has an explanation (strong PSR).
2. $p \wedge \Diamond\neg p$.
3. Some true $q$ explains $p$.
4. If $q$ explains $p$, $\Box(q \to p)$ (E: explanation entails).
5. If $\Box q$, then $\Box p$, contradicting 2.
6. If $q$ is contingent, it is a conjunct of $p$ and helps explain itself.
7. So reject 1 or E (or deny any contingent truth).

### S5 steps

The principles 2.2 uses, and which system each direction needs.

| Principle | Statement |
|---|---|
| K | from $\Box(A \to B)$ and $\Diamond A$, infer $\Diamond B$ |
| T | $\Box p \to p$ |
| Duality | $\Diamond A \leftrightarrow \neg\Box\neg A$ |
| B validates | $\Diamond\Box p \to p$ |
| Axiom 5 (S5) | $\Diamond p \to \Box\Diamond p$ |
| Dual form of 5 | $\Diamond\Box p \to \Box p$ |

**Forward** (Plantinga): $\Diamond G$ and $\Box(G \to \Box G)$ give $\Diamond\Box G$ by K; the dual form of 5 gives $\Box G$; T gives $G$. B suffices for $G$ directly; the argument fails in S4.

**Reverse** (no-maximality): from $\Diamond\neg\Box G$ and $\Box(G \to \Box G)$, contraposition inside the box and K give $\Diamond\neg G$, i.e. $\neg\Box G$; T on the premise gives $G \to \Box G$; so $\neg G$. Valid with K and T alone.

With $\Box(G \to \Box G)$ in S5: $\Diamond G$, $G$ and $\Box G$ are equivalent. *From* [2.2](lessons/02-02-the-modal-ontological-argument.md).

### Quantifier shift in the Third Way

That each corruptible thing fails to exist at some time does not entail a time when all fail to exist.

$$\forall x\, \exists t\; \neg E(x,t) \;\nvdash\; \exists t\, \forall x\; \neg E(x,t)$$

$E(x,t)$: $x$ exists at $t$. Each repair (an infinite past, a restricted domain) adds an unstated premise; the textual reading is in [ancient-medieval-philosophy 7.3](../ancient-medieval-philosophy/lessons/07-03-the-five-ways-as-text.md). *From* [2.3](lessons/02-03-cosmological-arguments-the-per-se-regress.md).

### Hilberts Hotel maps

A full hotel with rooms $1, 2, 3, \dots$ makes room without anyone leaving.

| Arrival | Map | Freed |
|---|---|---|
| one new guest | $n \mapsto n+1$ | room 1 |
| infinitely many new guests | $n \mapsto 2n$ | all odd rooms |

Checkout: the odd rooms leave ($\aleph_0$ leave, $\aleph_0$ stay); rooms 4 and up leave ($\aleph_0$ leave, 3 stay). So $\aleph_0 - \aleph_0$ has no single value, which is why cardinal subtraction is undefined for infinite cardinals; $\aleph_0 + \aleph_0 = \aleph_0$ ([mathematical-logic 4.4](../mathematical-logic/lessons/04-04-continuum-cardinal-arithmetic.md)). Craig: absurd for concrete things; critics: counterintuitive, not contradictory. *From* [2.5](lessons/02-05-cosmological-arguments-the-kalam.md).

### Design and fine-tuning odds

$$L = \frac{P(E \mid D)}{P(E \mid C)}$$

$$\text{posterior odds} = \frac{P(D)}{P(C)} \times L$$

A uniform chance hypothesis over a range of width $W$ with a life-permitting window of width $w$ gives $P(E \mid C) = w/W$, so the result scales with the assumed $W$ and with the assumed $P(E \mid D)$, neither of which physics supplies. As $W \to \infty$, $w/W \to 0$ for every finite window ([normalizability problem](#normalizability-problem)). Darwin's pattern: replace $C$ by chance plus selection with a likelihood near $P(E \mid D)$ and $L \to 1$, prior and P2 untouched. *From* [3.1](lessons/03-01-from-design-to-fine-tuning.md), [3.2](lessons/03-02-fine-tuning-the-objections.md).

### Multiverse at least one

With $N$ independent universes, each life-permitting with probability $p$:

$$P(\text{some universe life-permitting} \mid M) = 1 - (1 - p)^N$$

$$P(\text{this universe life-permitting}) = p \ \text{ on both } C \text{ and } M$$

The first line favours $M$ strongly; the second gives ratio 1. The multiverse dispute is which line describes our evidence (Bradley: the first, since observers are selected; White: the second). *From* [3.2](lessons/03-02-fine-tuning-the-objections.md).

### Witnesses and miracles

With prior odds $O$ and $n$ independent witnesses, each with likelihood ratio $L$:

$$\text{posterior odds} = O \times L^n$$

On a log scale each witness adds $\log_{10} L$. Any $L > 1$ beats any $O > 0$ eventually (Babbage, Earman). Hume's P2 lowers $L$; P3 (one source retold) breaks independence, so later copies have ratio 1. Price's lottery: what matters is how improbable *this exact report* would be if false, not how improbable the event is. *From* [3.5](lessons/03-05-miracles.md).

### Draper odds

$$\frac{\Pr(T \mid O)}{\Pr(\text{HI} \mid O)} = \frac{\Pr(T)}{\Pr(\text{HI})} \times \frac{\Pr(O \mid T)}{\Pr(O \mid \text{HI})}$$

Since HI entails $\neg T$, $\Pr(T) + \Pr(\text{HI}) \le 1$, so $\Pr(T) < \Pr(\text{HI})$ forces $\Pr(T) < \frac{1}{2}$. The odds are $T$'s share of the two-way contest only. The Moore shift in odds form: the theist and critic share the multiplier and differ in prior odds, which must come from evidence independent of how suffering is distributed. *From* [4.2](lessons/04-02-the-evidential-problem-of-evil.md).

### Inscrutability averaged and imprecise

If $\Pr(E \mid T)$ is uncertain over a range, two treatments diverge.

- **Averaged** (law of total probability): use the mean of the range; the ratio is definite, usually shrunk toward 1 but not cancelled.
- **Imprecise credence:** the ratio is the whole interval $[\min/\Pr(E \mid \text{HI}),\ \max/\Pr(E \mid \text{HI})]$; if it straddles 1 the evidence has no determinate direction.

Which treatment ignorance deserves is the [problem of priors](../epistemology/lessons/05-04-the-problem-of-priors.md). *From* [4.4](lessons/04-04-skeptical-theism.md).

### Hiddenness odds

$$\frac{P(T \mid E)}{P(N \mid E)} = \frac{P(T)}{P(N)} \times \frac{P(E \mid T)}{P(E \mid N)}$$

$E$: the observed pattern of nonbelief. Maitzen (2006): cultural clustering of belief is far likelier on naturalism. Greater-good responses raise $P(E \mid T)$; skeptical theism declines to estimate it; Baker-Hytch (2016): dependence on testimony produces clustering on either hypothesis. *From* [4.5](lessons/04-05-divine-hiddenness.md).

## Pitfalls

Every lesson's "Watch out", deduped and grouped by theme.

### Proof versus probability and the gap

- Thinking an argument that persuades no atheist has failed: that is dialectical failure; soundness and probabilistic support are separate questions. *([1.1](lessons/01-01-which-god-and-what-an-argument-must-do.md))*
- Thinking closing the gap is cheap: stage two is a full argument; score stage one and stage two separately. A regress gives an underived first member, not one personal God. *([1.1](lessons/01-01-which-god-and-what-an-argument-must-do.md), [2.3](lessons/02-03-cosmological-arguments-the-per-se-regress.md))*
- Thinking "first" means earliest: it means underived in the order of dependence, and only relative to one series. *([2.3](lessons/02-03-cosmological-arguments-the-per-se-regress.md))*
- Thinking a tiny $P(E \mid C)$ makes chance improbable: every bridge hand is improbable; evidence favours design only by comparison with $P(E \mid D)$, and belief also needs the prior. *([3.1](lessons/03-01-from-design-to-fine-tuning.md))*
- Thinking "fine-tuned" means "designed": it describes a theory matching observation only in a narrow parameter range. *([3.1](lessons/03-01-from-design-to-fine-tuning.md))*
- Thinking Darwin refuted the design argument: he defeated one premise for one body of evidence with a rival that predicts it, and said nothing about the laws. *([3.1](lessons/03-01-from-design-to-fine-tuning.md))*
- Thinking the multiverse objection denies there is evidence: it concedes $E$ is improbable on one chance universe and must pay for its own prior. *([3.2](lessons/03-02-fine-tuning-the-objections.md))*
- Treating the selection effect and the inverse gambler's fallacy as one point: they pull opposite ways. *([3.2](lessons/03-02-fine-tuning-the-objections.md))*
- Calling normalizability a quibble: it is a dilemma between a justified finite range and no probability at all. *([3.2](lessons/03-02-fine-tuning-the-objections.md))*
- Thinking Kant's moral argument is a weaker proof: it is a practical postulate, not a theoretical argument. *([3.3](lessons/03-03-moral-arguments.md))*
- Thinking the experience argument is a free-standing proof: it is the last step of a cumulative case. *([3.4](lessons/03-04-the-argument-from-religious-experience.md))*
- Thinking Earman refutes Hume's conclusion: Babbage's theorem refutes only the in-principle reading of Part I. *([3.5](lessons/03-05-miracles.md))*
- Thinking "extraordinary claims need extraordinary evidence" is Hume's maxim: the maxim compares two improbabilities, and Price's lottery is established by ordinary evidence. *([3.5](lessons/03-05-miracles.md))*
- Thinking establishing the event establishes a miracle: event and agent are separate hypotheses. *([3.5](lessons/03-05-miracles.md))*
- Thinking answering a paradox about an attribute shows God exists: it shows at most that the concept is not obviously incoherent. *([1.2](lessons/01-02-omnipotence-and-its-paradoxes.md))*

### Logical versus evidential

- Thinking the free will defense answers the evidential problem: it shows only consistency, and a consistent hypothesis can be improbable. *([4.1](lessons/04-01-the-logical-problem-of-evil.md), [4.2](lessons/04-02-the-evidential-problem-of-evil.md))*
- Thinking answering the logical problem shows God exists, or that evil is no evidence: consistency is the weakest result there is. *([4.1](lessons/04-01-the-logical-problem-of-evil.md))*
- Thinking a theist must deny suffering is evidence: many grant it lowers theism's probability and argue other evidence outweighs it. *([4.2](lessons/04-02-the-evidential-problem-of-evil.md))*
- Thinking the Moore shift begs the question: it is valid and cogent for anyone whose grounds for $G$ do not rest on denying P1. *([4.2](lessons/04-02-the-evidential-problem-of-evil.md))*
- Thinking the hiddenness argument demands overwhelming proof: it asks only for evidence enough for belief, for those not resisting. *([4.5](lessons/04-05-divine-hiddenness.md))*
- Thinking any reply that works for suffering works for hiddenness: the good must require withholding the relationship itself. *([4.5](lessons/04-05-divine-hiddenness.md))*

### Defense versus theodicy

- Reading the free will defense as saying evil exists *because* humans are free: that is the free will *theodicy*; the defense asserts no actual reason. *([4.1](lessons/04-01-the-logical-problem-of-evil.md))*
- Thinking compatibilists can simply reject the defense: that is its cost, not a refutation; a compatibilist theist needs another answer to Mackie. *([4.1](lessons/04-01-the-logical-problem-of-evil.md))*
- Thinking the privation account answers the problem of evil: it answers "what is evil?"; "why permit the lack?" remains. *([4.3](lessons/04-03-theodicies.md))*
- Thinking a theodicy must justify every evil alone: defenders run a cumulative case, region by region. *([4.3](lessons/04-03-theodicies.md))*
- Calling Adams's view a theodicy: she denies knowing God's reasons, which is closer to a defense with Christian content. *([4.3](lessons/04-03-theodicies.md))*
- Treating skeptical theism as skepticism about God, or as showing Rowe's premise false: it is skepticism about our sample of goods, and its conclusion undercuts rather than rebuts. *([4.4](lessons/04-04-skeptical-theism.md))*
- Hearing the moral-paralysis objection as a charge of callousness: it is a structural claim about inference, and the reply must break the parity. *([4.4](lessons/04-04-skeptical-theism.md))*

### Conceivability versus possibility

- Thinking the ontological argument says "I can think of God, so God exists": it is a reductio on one description; a parody must copy the description. *([2.1](lessons/02-01-anselms-ontological-argument.md))*
- Thinking Gaunilo's island locates the false premise: a parody only shows something proves too much. *([2.1](lessons/02-01-anselms-ontological-argument.md))*
- Thinking Kant's slogan refutes every ontological argument: Malcolm accepted it against chapter 2 and denied it touches chapter 3. *([2.1](lessons/02-01-anselms-ontological-argument.md))*
- Thinking $\Diamond G$ is the weak, safe premise: given $\Box(G \to \Box G)$ it is as strong as $\Box G$. *([2.2](lessons/02-02-the-modal-ontological-argument.md))*
- Thinking critics attack the S5 step: the logic is least contested; B suffices, and the serious objections target the possibility premise. *([2.2](lessons/02-02-the-modal-ontological-argument.md))*
- Thinking the reverse argument assumes atheism: it begs the question exactly as much as P1, which is the objection. *([2.2](lessons/02-02-the-modal-ontological-argument.md))*
- Thinking Hilbert's Hotel is a contradiction: both sides accept the arithmetic; the dispute is whether finite part-whole intuitions bind concrete collections. *([2.5](lessons/02-05-cosmological-arguments-the-kalam.md))*

### Consequence versus consequent

- Thinking the foreknowledge problem is that knowledge causes the act: the valid version transfers lack of choice, not causal power. *([1.3](lessons/01-03-omniscience-and-human-freedom.md))*
- Thinking the scope distinction answers the foreknowledge argument: it answers only the fallacious version; the valid one gets necessity from the fixity of the past. *([1.3](lessons/01-03-omniscience-and-human-freedom.md))*
- Thinking modal collapse trades on the same fallacy: the careful version gets the necessity of the act itself. *([1.4](lessons/01-04-eternity-immutability-and-simplicity.md))*
- Thinking the property objection refutes simplicity: it refutes identity with an abstract universal, which no classical defender holds. *([1.4](lessons/01-04-eternity-immutability-and-simplicity.md))*
- Thinking modal collapse refutes the Leibnizian argument outright: it refutes the PSR *together with* "explanation entails"; which to drop is the dispute. *([2.4](lessons/02-04-cosmological-arguments-sufficient-reason.md))*

### Concepts of God

- Thinking classical theists deny God is personal: most affirm God knows and wills, and deny only univocal personhood. *([1.1](lessons/01-01-which-god-and-what-an-argument-must-do.md))*
- Thinking the stone is the big threat to omnipotence: the hard cases are counterexamples to the repair, above all sin. *([1.2](lessons/01-02-omnipotence-and-its-paradoxes.md))*
- Thinking "God cannot do the impossible" concedes a limit: Aquinas and Mavrodes deny there is a task to fail at. *([1.2](lessons/01-02-omnipotence-and-its-paradoxes.md))*
- Thinking open theism denies omniscience: it redefines it as knowing every knowable truth. *([1.3](lessons/01-03-omniscience-and-human-freedom.md))*
- Thinking the tensed-knowledge argument is an atheist argument: everlasting theists use it against the classical package. *([1.4](lessons/01-04-eternity-immutability-and-simplicity.md))*

### Cosmological arguments

- Thinking the per se regress or the Leibnizian argument needs a beginning: both allow an eternal series; a beginning is the kalam's claim. *([2.3](lessons/02-03-cosmological-arguments-the-per-se-regress.md), [2.4](lessons/02-04-cosmological-arguments-sufficient-reason.md))*
- Thinking refuting the Third Way's quantifier shift refutes the contingency argument: the modern form drops that half. *([2.3](lessons/02-03-cosmological-arguments-the-per-se-regress.md))*
- Thinking Hume-Edwards is only a composition-fallacy charge: it is a claim about what facts there are. *([2.4](lessons/02-04-cosmological-arguments-sufficient-reason.md))*
- Thinking the Big Bang settles the kalam's P2: it establishes expansion from a hot dense state, not an absolute beginning. *([2.5](lessons/02-05-cosmological-arguments-the-kalam.md))*
- Thinking the kalam is a per se regress argument: its series is per accidens, the kind the regress allows to be infinite. *([2.5](lessons/02-05-cosmological-arguments-the-kalam.md))*
- Thinking a refutation of P2a refutes P2: the traversal argument and the physics are independent supports. *([2.5](lessons/02-05-cosmological-arguments-the-kalam.md))*

### Morality and experience

- Thinking the moral argument says atheists cannot be moral: it concerns grounding, not knowledge or behaviour. *([3.3](lessons/03-03-moral-arguments.md))*
- Thinking the moral argument presupposes divine command theory: it needs only that theism explains $E$ better than naturalism. *([3.3](lessons/03-03-moral-arguments.md))*
- Thinking a brain correlate debunks an experience: every experience has one; a mechanism matters only if the hypotheses predict it differently. *([3.4](lessons/03-04-the-argument-from-religious-experience.md))*
- Thinking the principle of credulity says believe whatever seems true: it is a defeasible default with four named defeaters. *([3.4](lessons/03-04-the-argument-from-religious-experience.md))*
- Thinking the experience argument covers mystical states only: it covers any experience as of God. *([3.4](lessons/03-04-the-argument-from-religious-experience.md))*

### Faith and reason

- Thinking evidentialism is an atheist doctrine: it is a norm many theists accept; what is contested is P4. *([5.1](lessons/05-01-evidentialism-and-the-ethics-of-belief.md))*
- Thinking the self-referential problem refutes evidentialism: it refutes classical foundationalism as a criterion of basicality. *([5.1](lessons/05-01-evidentialism-and-the-ethics-of-belief.md))*
- Thinking Clifford's shipowner is about believing something false: the verdict holds if the ship comes home; the target is how the belief was formed. *([5.1](lessons/05-01-evidentialism-and-the-ethics-of-belief.md))*
- Thinking "properly basic" means "held without reasons": basic beliefs have grounds and are open to defeat. *([5.2](lessons/05-02-reformed-epistemology.md))*
- Thinking reformed epistemology argues God exists: it argues only that *if* God exists, belief can be warranted without argument. *([5.2](lessons/05-02-reformed-epistemology.md))*
- Thinking the Great Pumpkin objection says the belief is arbitrary: its strongest form attacks the method, not the believer. *([5.2](lessons/05-02-reformed-epistemology.md))*
- Thinking doxastic involuntarism refutes the wager: Pascal grants it and answers with indirect voluntarism. *([5.3](lessons/05-03-pragmatic-arguments-the-wager-and-the-will-to-believe.md))*
- Thinking James licenses believing whatever you like: only a living, forced, momentous, intellectually undecidable option qualifies. *([5.3](lessons/05-03-pragmatic-arguments-the-wager-and-the-will-to-believe.md))*
- Thinking James's argument is a wager: it is about permission, and he rejects Pascal's calculation. *([5.3](lessons/05-03-pragmatic-arguments-the-wager-and-the-will-to-believe.md))*
- Thinking fideism means believing without arguments: that is reformed epistemology's claim; fideism is immunity to rational assessment. *([5.4](lessons/05-04-fideism.md))*
- Thinking "faith exceeds reason" is fideist: Aquinas, Vatican I and most natural theologians hold it. *([5.4](lessons/05-04-fideism.md))*
- Thinking Kierkegaard and Phillips called themselves fideists: critics applied the label, and Phillips rejected it. *([5.4](lessons/05-04-fideism.md))*
- Thinking exclusivism is a claim about who is damned: Plantinga's is about truth and compatible with inclusivism about salvation. *([5.5](lessons/05-05-religious-diversity.md))*
- Thinking pluralism is the neutral default: it is a first-order thesis most adherents of every tradition reject. *([5.5](lessons/05-05-religious-diversity.md))*
- Thinking conciliationism only threatens religious belief: it applies equally to atheism, agnosticism and itself. *([5.5](lessons/05-05-religious-diversity.md))*

## Conventions

- **Verdict-neutral.** Entries give each argument, the premise it turns on, who denies it and why, and the reply; never a verdict. Theist and atheist arguments get the same treatment.

- **Boss problems** are referred to at outline level only; the card does not reconstruct their answers.
- **No prose dollar signs.**
