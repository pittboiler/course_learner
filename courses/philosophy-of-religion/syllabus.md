# Philosophy of Religion — Syllabus

> Philosophy · Tier 1 · ~24 lessons · Prereqs: [`metaphysics`](../metaphysics/syllabus.md), [`epistemology`](../epistemology/syllabus.md) · Roadmap id: `philosophy-of-religion`

## Goal

This course owns the philosophical case for and against God for the whole library. It asks whether the concept of God is coherent, whether any argument makes theism true or probable, whether evil or divine hiddenness makes it false or improbable, and whether religious belief needs arguments at all. You will finish able to reconstruct every major argument at the strength its best defenders would recognize: the ontological, cosmological, design and fine-tuning, moral and experiential arguments; the logical and evidential problems of evil; divine hiddenness; Hume on miracles; and evidentialism, reformed epistemology, fideism and religious diversity. You will be able to find the premise each one turns on, and put the probabilistic ones into odds form, where a hidden prior or an unassessable likelihood can be seen. The course takes no side. Theist and atheist cases get equal footing, and `apologetics-foundations` builds its argued case for theism on top of this neutral one. It deliberately skips Aquinas's Five Ways inside the Thomist metaphysical framework (`thomistic-synthesis`), the divine attributes as Catholic dogma (`trinity-and-god`), faith and reason as Catholic doctrine ([`fundamental-theology`](../fundamental-theology/syllabus.md)), the resurrection as history (`apologetics-foundations`), and comparative religion as a descriptive discipline.

## How practice works in this course

Problems follow [HUMANITIES-BUILD-BRIEF.md](../../HUMANITIES-BUILD-BRIEF.md). The field is Philosophy, so the stance is **verdict-neutral**, and this course is held to that rule strictly because a Catholic Theology course builds on it. **Exegetical** parts are graded strictly: what Anselm's *Proslogion* 2 infers, which of Philo's four circumstances a case falls under, what Plantinga's free will defense claims and what it does not claim. **Evaluative** parts are graded on must-hit moves, and every must-hit move is one either side must make. "Say whether the reply generalizes to natural evil" is a move; "conclude that skeptical theism leads to moral skepticism" is a verdict and never appears in a rubric. **Formal** parts are ordinary math: the S5 step in the modal ontological argument, and odds-form updates for fine-tuning and the evidential problem of evil. The wager's decision theory is formal work owned by [`decision-theory`](../decision-theory/syllabus.md). There are **no apologetic parts**. Steelman-and-reply problems are evaluative, and across each module the course sets them for both sides. Close reading uses public-domain texts: Anselm and Gaunilo (Deane), Boethius (H. R. James), Aquinas (English Dominican Province), Hume's *Dialogues* and *Enquiry*, Paley, Kant, Pascal (Trotter), Clifford, and William James. Plantinga, Rowe, Swinburne, Mackie and other modern authors are paraphrased and cited by work.

## Dangerous Checklist

When you finish, you can:

- [ ] Test a proposed definition of omnipotence or omniscience against the classic paradoxes, and say which attribute a given incoherence argument actually threatens
- [ ] Diagnose the modal fallacy in the foreknowledge dilemma, and state what Ockhamism, Molinism, timeless eternity and open theism each give up to answer the version that survives
- [ ] Reconstruct Anselm's and Plantinga's ontological arguments, carry out the S5 step, and run the parody or reverse argument that shows where the weight really sits
- [ ] Say which cosmological arguments need a per se series and which need the PSR, and where the gap from "first cause" or "necessary being" to "God" opens
- [ ] State what Big Bang cosmology does and does not establish about a beginning, and use that to assess the kalam's second premise
- [ ] Put the fine-tuning argument in odds form, and locate the normalizability, multiverse and inscrutable-likelihood objections as attacks on specific terms
- [ ] Reconstruct the moral argument and the argument from religious experience as inferences to the best explanation, and name the naturalist rival each must beat
- [ ] Separate Hume's in-principle argument against miracles from his practical one, and assess each with the independent-witness point
- [ ] Distinguish a defense from a theodicy, state the free will defense exactly, and say why most philosophers now think it answers the logical problem of evil while the evidential problem stays open
- [ ] Run Rowe's and Draper's evidential arguments, and state the skeptical theist reply together with the strongest objection to it
- [ ] Reconstruct Schellenberg's hiddenness argument and say which premise each major response denies
- [ ] Place a religious believer's position among evidentialism, reformed epistemology, pragmatic belief and fideism, and say what religious diversity does to each

## Modules

### Module 1: The concept of God and the divine attributes

Before asking whether God exists, fix what is being asked about, and see whether the concept holds together. The incoherence arguments against theism live here.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1.1 | Which God, and what an argument must do | Fix the concept at issue and say what counts as success for a theistic or atheistic argument | perfect-being theology, classical theism vs theistic personalism, the gap problem, proof vs raising probability, cumulative cases |
| 1.2 | Omnipotence and its paradoxes | Define omnipotence so that it survives the stone paradox, and see what the definition costs | the paradox of the stone (Mavrodes, Savage), logical possibility (ST I q.25 a.3), omnipotence and impeccability, maximal power definitions |
| 1.3 | Omniscience and human freedom | Find the modal fallacy in the foreknowledge argument and state the version that survives | necessity of the consequence vs of the consequent, the fixity of the past, Ockhamism and soft facts, Molina's middle knowledge, open theism |
| 1.4 | Eternity, immutability, and simplicity | Say what a timeless God would be and whether simplicity collapses into contradiction or modal collapse | Boethian eternity, timeless vs everlasting, knowledge of tensed facts, divine simplicity (ST I q.3), the property objection, the modal-collapse objection |

**Boss problem 1:** Take Boethius, *The Consolation of Philosophy* Book V (H. R. James translation, public domain): prose 3, where the prisoner argues that divine foreknowledge makes free choice impossible, and prose 6, where Philosophy replies. (a) Reconstruct the prisoner's argument in standard form. (b) Close-read prose 6: state Philosophy's definition of eternity and her distinction between two kinds of necessity, with the example she uses. (c) Write the fatalist inference with modal operators and show which reading is invalid and which is valid but has a disputed premise. (d) In 300–500 words, assess whether timeless eternity answers the valid version or only moves the problem, and whether that answer is available to a God who is also simple and immutable; state the strongest case against your verdict. Parts (a)–(b) are exegetical; (c) is formal; (d) is evaluative.

### Module 2: Ontological and cosmological arguments

The a priori argument and its modal revival, then three ways of arguing from the existence of anything at all to a first cause or necessary being.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2.1 | Anselm's ontological argument | Reconstruct *Proslogion* 2–3 and assess the classic replies | "that than which nothing greater can be conceived", existence in the understanding vs in reality, Gaunilo's island, whether Kant's predicate objection (stated in [`metaphysics`](../metaphysics/syllabus.md) 2.5) defeats it, *Proslogion* 3 and necessary existence |
| 2.2 | The modal ontological argument | Carry out Plantinga's S5 argument and locate its weight in the possibility premise | maximal greatness, S5 and the characteristic axiom, the possibility premise, the reverse ontological argument, parody and symmetry, dialectical vs logical success |
| 2.3 | Cosmological arguments I: the per se regress | Say why a regress of dependent causes is supposed to need a first member, and test that claim | per se order assumed from [`metaphysics`](../metaphysics/syllabus.md) 4.4, the regress premise and the infinite-intermediaries reply, the argument from contingency in Thomist form (framework ceded to `thomistic-synthesis`), what "first" does and does not give you |
| 2.4 | Cosmological arguments II: sufficient reason | Run the Leibnizian argument to its conclusion and test the steps after the PSR | Leibniz and Clarke, the PSR assumed from [`metaphysics`](../metaphysics/syllabus.md) 3.3, van Inwagen's modal-collapse objection developed here, the Hume–Edwards objection (explain the parts, explain the whole), why a necessary being would have to be God, the weak-PSR versions (Pruss and Gale) |
| 2.5 | Cosmological arguments III: the kalam | Assess both premises of the kalam, using the mathematics of infinity and the physics of the Big Bang | al-Ghazali and Craig, the actual infinite and Hilbert's Hotel ([`mathematical-logic`](../mathematical-logic/syllabus.md) 4.3), traversing an infinite past, Big Bang evidence vs an absolute beginning ([`cosmology`](../cosmology/syllabus.md)), the causal principle, from cause to personal cause |

**Boss problem 2:** Take Hume, *Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion* Part IX (public domain), where Demea gives an a priori argument for a necessarily existent being and Cleanthes objects. (a) Reconstruct Demea's argument in standard form. (b) State Cleanthes's objections to it, including his point about explaining each of twenty particles, and say which premise of (a) each objection targets. (c) Using S5, show that "possibly, necessarily G" entails "necessarily G", then show that "possibly, not necessarily G" entails "not G"; say what these two results together mean for Plantinga's possibility premise. (d) In 300–500 words, assess whether the Leibnizian argument of 2.4 survives the objection in (b) and van Inwagen's modal collapse ([`metaphysics`](../metaphysics/syllabus.md) 3.3, whose Boss 3 derives the collapse), or whether a weak PSR escapes both at an acceptable cost; state the strongest case for the other verdict. Parts (a)–(b) are exegetical; (c) is formal; (d) is evaluative.

### Module 3: Arguments from design, morality, experience, and miracles

Arguments from particular features of the world. Every one of them has the same odds-form skeleton, and each is weakest where a likelihood is hardest to assess.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3.1 | From design to fine-tuning | Recast the design argument as a likelihood argument and see why physics replaced biology as its evidence | Paley's watch, Hume's objections (the analogy form is assumed from [`philosophical-method`](../philosophical-method/syllabus.md) 2.2), Darwin and the lost biological likelihood gap, the likelihood principle, fine-tuned constants and the cosmological-constant problem ([`cosmology`](../cosmology/syllabus.md) 4.4) |
| 3.2 | Fine-tuning: the objections | Assign each objection to the term of the odds form it attacks | the multiverse and the inverse gambler's fallacy (Hacking, White), observation selection effects and Leslie's firing squad, the normalizability problem (McGrew, McGrew and Vestrup), inscrutable divine preferences, fine-tuning later explained by dynamics (flatness and inflation, [`cosmology`](../cosmology/syllabus.md) 4.1–4.2) |
| 3.3 | Moral arguments | Reconstruct the moral argument as an inference to the best explanation and state the naturalist realist's rival | Kant's moral argument as a postulate of practical reason, the abductive moral argument, grounding vs knowing, non-naturalist realism without God (Wielenberg, Enoch), debunking cutting both ways ([`ethics`](../ethics/syllabus.md) 6.4–6.5 assumed) |
| 3.4 | The argument from religious experience | Assess whether religious experience justifies belief the way perception does | William James's marks of mystical experience, Swinburne's principle of credulity (compare Reid on testimony, [`epistemology`](../epistemology/syllabus.md) 4.4) and its special considerations, Alston's doxastic practices, cross-checking, conflicting experiences, neuroscientific debunking |
| 3.5 | Miracles | Separate Hume's in-principle and practical arguments and assess each | miracle as violation vs as divine act, "a firm and unalterable experience", Part II's four considerations, Price's reply, Earman's critique, multiple independent witnesses, whether a miracle could be identified as one |

**Boss problem 3:** (a) Close-read the opening of Paley's *Natural Theology* chapter 1 (public domain): say whether the text supports reading his inference as an argument from analogy or as an inference to the best explanation, and name the sentences that decide it, including what Paley says about a watch that sometimes goes wrong. (b) A physicist says a constant could have taken any value in a range of width W, and life requires it to fall in a window of width w. Write the likelihood ratio for design against chance under a uniform distribution, compute the posterior odds for stated prior odds, and show what happens to the calculation when the range is unbounded. (c) Using [`cosmology`](../cosmology/syllabus.md) 4.1–4.2 and 4.4, explain why the flatness of the early universe is no longer usually cited as fine-tuning, and why the cosmological-constant problem still is. (d) The fine-tuning argument, the moral argument (3.3) and a miracle report (3.5) share the odds-form skeleton. For each, name the term whose value is most disputed, and in 300–500 words argue whether the multiverse objection defeats the fine-tuning argument, meeting the inverse-gambler reply or its rebuttal, whichever is against you. Parts (a) and (c) are exegetical; (b) is formal; (d) is evaluative.

### Module 4: Evil and hiddenness

The strongest arguments against theism: that evil is logically incompatible with God, that it makes God improbable, and that God's absence from some sincere seekers does the same. Then the theist replies, stated at full strength.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4.1 | The logical problem of evil | State the incompatibility claim exactly and assess the free will defense | Epicurus's questions as Hume's Philo restates them (*Dialogues* X), Mackie's "Evil and Omnipotence", defense vs theodicy, Plantinga's free will defense and transworld depravity, natural evil |
| 4.2 | The evidential problem of evil | Run Rowe's and Draper's arguments and put Draper's in odds form | Rowe's fawn and the noseeum inference, the G. E. Moore shift, Draper's hypothesis of indifference, evil as evidence vs as proof |
| 4.3 | Theodicies | State the major theodicies and test each on horrendous and animal suffering | evil as privation (Augustine), the best possible world (Leibniz), soul-making (Hick), the free will theodicy, natural-law theodicy (Swinburne), horrendous evils (Marilyn McCord Adams), animal suffering |
| 4.4 | Skeptical theism | State the skeptical theist's theses and the strongest objections to them | Wykstra's CORNEA, Bergmann's skeptical theses, the inscrutable likelihood, the moral-paralysis objection, the skepticism-spreads objection, Draper's reply |
| 4.5 | Divine hiddenness | Reconstruct Schellenberg's argument and match each response to the premise it denies | nonresistant nonbelief, a perfectly loving God and relationship, Pascal on the hidden God, responses from freedom and soul-making, whether hiddenness is a species of the problem of evil |

**Boss problem 4:** Take Hume, *Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion* Parts X–XI (public domain). (a) Close-read Part XI: list Philo's four circumstances on which natural evil depends, and for each say whether a natural-law theodicy (4.3) is a reply to it. (b) Reconstruct Rowe's evidential argument in standard form and say which premise the noseeum inference supports. (c) Given stated likelihoods for a body of suffering under theism and under Draper's hypothesis of indifference, and stated prior odds, compute the posterior odds; then show formally that the skeptical theist's claim amounts to refusing to assign the likelihood under theism, and say what that does to the posterior. (d) In 300–500 words, assess whether skeptical theism answers the evidential argument without also undermining ordinary moral reasoning; steelman the side you reject before replying to it. Parts (a)–(b) are exegetical; (c) is formal; (d) is evaluative.

### Module 5: Faith, evidence, and the many religions

Does religious belief need arguments at all? Evidentialism says yes; reformed epistemology, pragmatic arguments and fideism each say no in a different way; religious diversity presses on all of them.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5.1 | Evidentialism and the ethics of belief | State the evidentialist objection to theistic belief and the classical-foundationalist view behind it | Clifford's shipowner and principle, the evidentialist objection, classical foundationalism ([`epistemology`](../epistemology/syllabus.md) 2.2) and its self-referential problem, the Catholic claim that God can be known by reason as one evidentialist-friendly position ([`fundamental-theology`](../fundamental-theology/syllabus.md) 1.2, cited) |
| 5.2 | Reformed epistemology | Explain how belief in God could be properly basic and what would defeat it | properly basic belief, Plantinga's sensus divinitatis and warrant (proper functionalism from [`epistemology`](../epistemology/syllabus.md) 2.4), the parity argument with other minds and memory, the Great Pumpkin objection, defeaters and defeater-defeaters |
| 5.3 | Pragmatic arguments: the wager and the will to believe | Say whether practical reasons can justify religious belief, and read James's reply to Clifford | the wager's decision logic assumed from [`decision-theory`](../decision-theory/syllabus.md) 6.1, doxastic involuntarism and Pascal's advice to act as if believing, whether a wagered belief is the one rewarded, James's live, forced and momentous options |
| 5.4 | Fideism | Say what fideism is, tell its forms apart, and state what its critics, religious and secular, object to | Kierkegaard's leap and the paradox (paraphrased), Wittgensteinian fideism (Phillips), whether reformed epistemology is fideist, fideism as condemned in Catholic teaching ([`fundamental-theology`](../fundamental-theology/syllabus.md) 2.3, cited) |
| 5.5 | Religious diversity | Assess exclusivism, inclusivism and pluralism, and say what disagreement does to religious belief | Hick's pluralist hypothesis and the Real, Plantinga's defense of exclusivism, the arbitrariness charge, conciliationism applied (general theory in [`epistemology`](../epistemology/syllabus.md) 4.5), diversity as a defeater for religious experience (3.4) |

**Boss problem 5:** Take Clifford, "The Ethics of Belief" (1877) and William James, "The Will to Believe" (1896), both public domain. (a) Close-read Clifford's opening case of the shipowner and state his general principle, with the words that make it universal. (b) State James's conditions for a genuine option and the claim he makes about when our passional nature may decide, and say which premise of Clifford's he denies. (c) Take the wager's decision logic as settled in [`decision-theory`](../decision-theory/syllabus.md) 6.1. Pascal advises someone who cannot yet believe to act as believers do until belief comes. Say whether that answers the objection that belief cannot be chosen, and whether James's conditions for a genuine option would count the wager as one, in 150 words. (d) In 300–500 words, assess whether a reformed epistemologist's properly basic belief in God can survive awareness of religious diversity (5.5) without becoming arbitrary; state the Great Pumpkin objection at full strength and the best reply, then give your verdict and the strongest case against it. Parts (a)–(b) are exegetical; (c)–(d) are evaluative.

## Notes

- **Neutrality, and why it is checked.** `apologetics-foundations` lists this course as a prerequisite and argues for theism, citing the argument skeletons from this course. So every skeleton here must be one a leading atheist philosopher and a leading theist philosopher would both accept as a fair statement. The pairing of the Sources of truth (Oppy and Mackie beside Swinburne and Plantinga) is the test for that. Lessons may report where professional opinion sits when the report is accurate. The most common such report is that the free will defense is widely taken to answer the *logical* problem of evil, which is why the checklist states it. They never argue for a verdict in the lesson's voice.
- **Where this goes next.** `apologetics-foundations` takes Modules 2–4 and argues for theism against naturalism, and it takes up miracles (3.5) as history in its treatment of the resurrection. `thomistic-synthesis` takes the per se regress (2.3) into act and potency and the Five Ways in full. `trinity-and-god` takes the attributes of Module 1 as dogma. `philosophy-of-science` takes the likelihood principle and observation selection effects (3.1–3.2) as general confirmation theory. [`fundamental-theology`](../fundamental-theology/syllabus.md) Module 2 gives the Catholic doctrinal account of faith that 5.1 and 5.4 cite as one position.
- **The physics bridge.** 2.5 and 3.1–3.2 lean on the built [`cosmology`](../cosmology/syllabus.md) lessons: 4.1 for the flatness and horizon problems, 4.2 for inflation as a dynamical explanation of apparent fine-tuning, and 4.4 for the cosmological-constant problem. Lessons should state carefully what the physics shows. Big Bang models describe expansion from a hot dense state; whether that state was an absolute beginning is a further question on which cosmologists disagree. Singularity theorems such as Borde–Guth–Vilenkin (2003) are cited together with the disputes over what they imply.
- **Accuracy discipline.** Modern works are cited by title and year only where these are certain, and are paraphrased. No section numbers are given for Leibniz, Pascal or Augustine unless checked against the translation used. Quotations attributed to Epicurus come through Hume's Philo, whose restatement is public domain, and the historical attribution is flagged as uncertain.
- **Lesson count.** The roadmap estimated ~20; this runs 24 (+20%, inside the ±25% band). Three topics the steering lists as owned here would otherwise be squeezed into other lessons. Cosmological arguments take three lessons because the per se regress, the PSR and the kalam fail or succeed for unrelated reasons. The problem of evil takes four, separating the logical problem, the evidential problem, theodicy and skeptical theism, plus hiddenness. The attributes take four because the incoherence arguments against theism belong to them. Nothing is crammed: Hume on miracles fits one lesson because [`philosophical-method`](../philosophical-method/syllabus.md) Boss 2 has already done the reconstruction and odds form.
