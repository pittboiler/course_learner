# Philosophy of Religion · Lesson 3.5: Miracles

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 3: Arguments from design, morality, experience, and miracles · Builds on: [philosophical-method 2.4](../../philosophical-method/lessons/02-04-weighing-evidence-in-odds-form.md), [epistemology 4.4](../../epistemology/lessons/04-04-testimony.md), [3.4 The argument from religious experience](03-04-the-argument-from-religious-experience.md) · Unlocks: [4.1 The logical problem of evil](04-01-the-logical-problem-of-evil.md)

## Why this matters

Most religions rest on reported events: a sea parted, a man raised, a sick child healed at a shrine. Nobody alive saw the founding ones, so the question is always about **testimony**: can reports ever make it reasonable to believe that something happened against the course of nature, and that God did it? Hume's *Enquiry* §X (1748) says no testimony has ever done so. Its odds-form skeleton is already yours from [philosophical-method 2.4](../../philosophical-method/lessons/02-04-weighing-evidence-in-odds-form.md) and that course's Boss 2. This lesson is about the substance: what a miracle is, what Hume's two arguments actually claim, and the eighteenth-century and modern replies.

## The idea

Two questions hide in "could you ever believe a miracle report?"

1. **Did the event happen?** A woman certified dead walks out of the morgue. That is a question about evidence for an *event*.
2. **Was it a miracle?** Even granting the event, was it a divine act and not a freak of physics we don't yet understand? That is a question about *cause*, and it is the [gap problem](../reference.md#the-gap-problem) of [1.1](01-01-which-god-and-what-an-argument-must-do.md) again.

Hume's essay has two parts, aimed mostly at the first question. **Part I is the in-principle argument.** A miracle is by definition contrary to a law, and laws rest on uniform experience. So the event's prior improbability is as great as experience can make anything, and testimony, whose credit also rests only on experience, has to beat it. That is where the [maxim](../reference.md#humes-maxim-on-testimony) comes in: believe the report only if its being false would be even more improbable than the event. **Part II is the practical argument.** Even if testimony *could* in principle win, no actual miracle report has ever been good enough.

The best reply is the one physics and courtrooms use every day. Independent reports **multiply**. A tiny prior is not a wall. It is a number, and a big enough product of likelihood ratios gets over any number.

## Source

Hume, *Enquiry* §X Part I (§90 in the numbered editions):

> "A miracle is a violation of the laws of nature; and as a firm and unalterable experience has established these laws, the proof against a miracle, from the very nature of the fact, is as entire as any argument from experience can possibly be imagined. … Nothing is esteemed a miracle, if it ever happen in the common course of nature. It is no miracle that a man, seemingly in good health, should die on a sudden … But it is a miracle, that a dead man should come to life; because that has never been observed in any age or country. There must, therefore, be a uniform experience against every miraculous event, otherwise the event would not merit that appellation."

Read the last sentence carefully. The uniformity of experience is built into the *definition*: if experience weren't uniform against the event, it wouldn't be a miracle at all. That makes the prior small by stipulation. It also invites the circularity objection below.

## The argument

**Three definitions of a miracle.** In the passage, Hume defines a miracle by the *event*, as a violation. A footnote to the same section adds the *agent*, calling a miracle "a transgression of a law of nature by a particular volition of the Deity, or by the interposition of some invisible agent." Richard Swinburne (*The Concept of Miracle*, 1970) makes the event notion precise: a miracle is a **non-repeatable counter-instance** to a law. If the exception recurred under like conditions, we would revise the law. If it never recurs, keeping the law is still the best science. J. L. Mackie (*The Miracle of Theism*, 1982) makes the agent notion precise. Laws describe how the world works *when left to itself*, and a miracle is an intrusion from outside the natural order. Aquinas offers a third route, defining it by a cause hidden from *all* creatures ([thomistic-synthesis 5.2](../../thomistic-synthesis/lessons/05-02-providence-and-secondary-causes.md)). The definitions matter. On a pure regularity view of laws ([metaphysics 4.3](../../metaphysics/lessons/04-03-powers-dispositions-and-laws.md)), an exceptionless regularity can't be "violated": any counter-instance just shows it was never a law. The agent definitions avoid that, at the cost of building God into the description of the event. See [miracle](../reference.md#miracle).

**Hume's practical argument (Part II)**, in standard form:

- **P1.** No miracle in history is attested by enough witnesses of unquestioned good sense, integrity and reputation, testifying publicly where detection would be unavoidable.
- **P2.** People take pleasure in surprise and wonder, and religious zeal adds motives to deceive and to be deceived. So for marvels, the rate of false reports is unusually high.
- **P3.** Miracle reports abound chiefly among uneducated peoples, or come down from them, and they thin out as reliable records begin.
- **P4.** Every religion's miracles are offered to establish that religion against the others, so the testimony for each is opposed by the testimony for all the rest.
- **∴ C.** No testimony for a miracle has ever amounted even to a probability. No miracle can be proved "so as to be the foundation of a system of religion" (§99).

*In words:* P1 and P3 say the witnesses are weak. P2 says false reports of marvels are cheap. P4 says the reports cancel each other out. These are Hume's [four considerations](../reference.md#humes-four-considerations). In odds form, P1–P3 all argue that $P(\text{report}\mid\neg E)$ is high for miracle reports, which makes the likelihood ratio small. P4 adds evidence *against* each rival miracle.

Hume himself limits the conclusion. In §99 he grants that a violation could be established by testimony. His example is universal, uniform reports of eight days of total darkness from 1 January 1600. What testimony could never do is establish a miracle as the foundation of a religion.

**The replies.**

- **Price (1767).** Richard Price's *Four Dissertations* argued that an event's improbability alone can't defeat testimony, because we credit improbable reports all the time. A newspaper's report of which number won a large lottery is a report of a million-to-one event, and we believe it.
- **Babbage and Earman.** Charles Babbage (*Ninth Bridgewater Treatise*, 1837) and John Earman (*Hume's Abject Failure*, 2000) made the point general. If witnesses are **independent** and each is more often right than wrong, then for any prior short of zero there is a number of witnesses whose agreement makes the event probable. With $n$ witnesses, each with likelihood ratio $L$ and prior odds $O$:

$$\text{posterior odds} = O \times L^n$$

In words: each [independent witness](../reference.md#independent-witnesses) multiplies the odds by $L$, so any $L > 1$ overcomes any $O > 0$ eventually. Earman concludes that Part I, read as an in-principle barrier, fails. Robert Fogelin (*A Defense of Hume on Miracles*, 2003) replies that Part I was never meant as a barrier, only as the standard of evidence that Part II then shows no actual report meets.

**Where the argument is weakest.** For Part I, the critic attacks the premise that experience is *uniform* against the miracle. On this objection (pressed by C. S. Lewis, *Miracles*, 1947), experience is uniform only if every report of a miracle is false, so assuming uniformity assumes the answer. The Humean replies that the reports are not excluded but weighed. They are a few items of testimony set against the whole mass of observation behind the law. For Part II, the weight sits on the **independence** of witnesses. Babbage's theorem needs reports that are independent given the truth or falsity of the event. Hume's P2 and P3 describe exactly the conditions (contagious rumour, shared enthusiasm, one source retold) that break independence. Arif Ahmed (2015) presses exactly this assumption against the Babbage-style reply. The defender of a particular report answers that independence is a question about *that* report's history and has to be argued case by case, not ruled out wholesale.

## The odds

![Line chart of log posterior odds against number of witnesses from a prior of one in a million. With likelihood ratio 90 per independent witness the odds cross even at four witnesses; with ratio 18 they cross at five; copies of a single report stay flat after the first.](assets/03-05-fig1.svg)

On a log scale each independent witness adds the same step, $\log_{10} L$. Lowering $L$ (Hume's P2) makes the line shallower. Breaking independence makes it flat.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean case: Price's lottery).** A lottery has 1,000,000 tickets. The newspaper reports that ticket 417,302 won. Prior odds that this ticket won: $1 : 999{,}999$. Suppose the paper prints a wrong number one time in 1,000, and a misprint could be any of the 999,999 other numbers. Then

$$L = \frac{0.999}{0.001/999{,}999} \approx 9.99 \times 10^{8}$$

$$\text{posterior odds} \approx \frac{1}{999{,}999} \times 9.99 \times 10^{8} = 999 : 1$$

The event was a million to one, and you are now 99.9% sure of it. That is Price's point. The question is never how improbable the event is, only how improbable *this exact report* would be if the event hadn't happened. A false report naming that particular number is itself a million-to-one coincidence.

**Example 2 (hard case: a raised woman).** Invented: four unconnected witnesses report that a woman certified dead walked out of a mortuary. Take prior odds $1 : 1{,}000{,}000$. Suppose each witness would report it 90% of the time if it happened and 1% of the time if it didn't, so $L = 0.9/0.01 = 90$.

- $n=1$: $10^{-6} \times 90 = 9 \times 10^{-5}$
- $n=2$: $10^{-6} \times 8{,}100 = 0.0081$
- $n=3$: $10^{-6} \times 729{,}000 = 0.729$, about $42\%$
- $n=4$: $10^{-6} \times 65{,}610{,}000 = 65.6 : 1$, about $98.5\%$

Now push Hume's P2. For religious marvels the false-report rate is 5%, not 1%, so $L = 18$. Four witnesses reach $10^{-6} \times 18^4 \approx 0.105$, and it takes five to pass even ($18^5 \times 10^{-6} \approx 1.89$). Push P3 instead: if the four are retelling one source, the later reports have ratio $1$ and the odds stay at $9 \times 10^{-5}$. And even at $65.6 : 1$, what is probable is the *event*. The step to "God raised her" still needs ruling out an undetected natural process, a misdiagnosed death, or some agent other than God (Hume's footnote allows "some invisible agent"). Each of these is a hypothesis with its own prior.

## Watch out

- **You might think Earman refutes Hume's conclusion, but actually** Babbage's theorem refutes only the in-principle reading of Part I. Whether any actual report has independent witnesses with $L > 1$ is Part II's question, and the theorem is silent on it.
- **You might think "extraordinary claims need extraordinary evidence" is Hume's maxim, but actually** the maxim compares two improbabilities (the event and the report's falsehood). Price's lottery shows that an extraordinary event can be established by quite ordinary evidence, provided the report would be extraordinary if false.
- **You might think establishing the event establishes a miracle, but actually** the event and the agent are separate hypotheses. The Catholic position that miracles are signs that make revelation credible ([fundamental-theology 2.2](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/02-02-credibility-and-the-preambles-of-faith.md)) needs both, and it is one position among others here.

## One-liner

> Hume's Part I makes the prior tiny and Part II makes the witnesses weak; independent witnesses multiply past any prior, so the live questions are whether real reports are independent and whether an established event is God's act.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢) *(Exegetical)*** (a) Using Hume's criteria in the Source and Part I, classify each invented report as a *miracle* or merely *marvellous or unusual*, with the deciding reason in one line each: (i) islanders who have never seen ice are told by a sailor that, far to the north, lakes harden enough to walk on; (ii) a fit marathon runner of 40 dies suddenly at the finish line; (iii) a man buried for three days is seen walking and eating by his family. (b) A healing is reported only by the healer's own disciples, who are recruiting new followers. A rival sect reports the same healing of its own founder, and each sect cites its healing as proof that its faith alone is true. Name the two of Part II's four considerations that these details are written to trigger. 80 words or fewer for (b).

**P2 (🟡) *(Formal (a)–(c) · Evaluative (d))*** Invented: villagers in Varena report that a stone statue rose a metre into the air. Take prior odds $1 : 10^{10}$. Each witness would report it with probability $0.75$ if it happened and $0.005$ if it didn't, and witnesses are conditionally independent. (a) Find $L$ and the posterior odds for $n = 1, 2, 3$. (b) Find the smallest $n$ for which the posterior odds exceed $1 : 1$. (c) A Humean argues that for religious marvels the false-report rate is $0.05$. Recompute $L$ and the smallest such $n$. (d) In 100 words or fewer, name the assumption of the calculation the Humean should attack next, and give the best answer a defender of the Varena report could make.

**P3 (🔴, optional) *(Evaluative)*** Steelman and reply. As its best defender would, state the objection that Hume's Part I argues in a circle by appealing to "uniform experience". Name the premise it targets, then give the best Humean reply. 150 words or fewer.

<details>
<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict (a):**

- (i) Marvellous, not a miracle. It is outside the islanders' experience, not contrary to uniform experience in like circumstances. This is Hume's frost case.
- (ii) Not a miracle. Sudden death in apparent health happens in the common course of nature and "has yet been frequently observed".
- (iii) A miracle. Return from death "has never been observed in any age or country", so uniform experience is against it.

**Must hit, strict (b):**

- The **first** consideration: the witnesses are not of such undoubted integrity, or with such reputation to lose, as to be beyond suspicion. The disciples have an interest in the report.
- The **fourth**: contrary religions' miracles oppose and cancel each other, since each is offered to establish a rival system.

**Wrong turns:** calling (i) a miracle because the islanders find it incredible. Hume's distinction is contrary to experience, not merely outside it. In (b), naming the second consideration (love of wonder) instead of the first or fourth. It bears on every marvel, but nothing in these details is written to trigger it.

**Model answer (b):** The first consideration, because interested disciples are not witnesses beyond suspicion of a design to deceive. The fourth, because each sect's miracle is offered to establish its own faith against the other's, so the testimonies are contrary and destroy each other's credit.

---

**P2** *(Formal (a)–(c) · Evaluative (d))*

(a) $L = 0.75/0.005 = 150$.

- $n=1$: $10^{-10} \times 150 = 1.5 \times 10^{-8}$
- $n=2$: $10^{-10} \times 22{,}500 = 2.25 \times 10^{-6}$
- $n=3$: $10^{-10} \times 3{,}375{,}000 = 3.375 \times 10^{-4}$

(b) $n=4$: $150^4 = 5.0625 \times 10^{8}$, giving odds $0.0506$, which is not enough. $n=5$: $150^5 = 7.59 \times 10^{10}$, giving odds $7.59 : 1$ (about 88%). Smallest $n = 5$.

(c) $L = 0.75/0.05 = 15$. $15^8 = 2.56 \times 10^{9}$, giving odds $0.256$, and $15^9 = 3.84 \times 10^{10}$, giving odds $3.84 : 1$. Smallest $n = 9$.

**Must hit, any verdict (d):**

- Name **conditional independence**: the calculation assumes each report adds a fresh factor of $L$ whether or not the event happened.
- Say what would break it (shared source, mutual influence, collective enthusiasm, Hume's P2/P3), and give a defender's answer that bears on the history of *this* report.

**Wrong turns:** attacking the prior instead. The calculation already works with any prior short of zero, and lowering it only raises $n$. Treating (c) as refuting the report rather than as raising the bar.

**Model answer (d), one of several:** The Humean should attack independence. Villagers talk to each other before anyone records them, so the fifth report may only echo the first, and its ratio given the others is near 1. The defender's best answer is evidential, not general. Show that the witnesses gave statements separately before contact, that they differ in incidental detail the way independent accounts do, and that some had motives to deny the event. That earns the multiplication case by case.

---

**P3** *(Evaluative)*

**Must hit, any verdict:**

- Target: the premise that "a firm and unalterable", **uniform** experience stands against every miracle.
- The objection as a dilemma: if reports of miracles count as experience, experience is not uniform; if they are excluded, they are excluded because they are assumed false, which is the conclusion.
- A real Humean reply that does not just repeat the premise: for example, that the reports are not excluded but weighed as testimony against the vast body of observation behind the law; or that §99 admits testimony could establish a violation, so the uniformity premise sets a prior and is not a ban.

**Wrong turns:** answering with Babbage's theorem, which is a different objection (to the weighing, not to the uniformity premise). Calling the objection decisive or empty without stating the reply.

**Model answer, one of several:** Hume says experience is uniform against every miracle. But reports of miracles exist in every age. If they count as experience, experience is not uniform. If they don't, that can only be because they are presumed false, and that presumption is the conclusion. So Part I targets the uniformity premise and either fails or begs the question. The Humean reply is that nothing is excluded. "Uniform" names the observations that ground the law: countless deaths, none reversed under examination. Miracle reports are then weighed as testimony against that body of observation, just as the maxim says. §99 confirms this reading, since there Hume admits that sufficiently uniform testimony could establish eight days of darkness. The premise fixes how small the prior is. It does not stop evidence from being counted.

</details>

## Flashback

**From Lesson [3.3](03-03-moral-arguments.md) (Moral arguments):** *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)* Counterexample to Kant. An invented volunteer, Ines, says: "I am obliged to work toward a world where no child dies of leukaemia. I am also certain that world will never fully arrive. There is no contradiction in that." (a) Which premise of Kant's moral argument does Ines's case put pressure on, and how does it read the duty differently from Kant? Two sentences. (b) Does the case refute that premise? Give the best answer a Kantian could make and the critic's best rejoinder, and say what follows for Kant's conclusion even if the Kantian wins. 120 words or fewer, any verdict.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):**

- **Premise 1**, together with how premise 2 applies to it: that we are rationally required to promote the highest good *as a goal whose achievement must be possible*.
- The case reads the duty as a duty to **strive**: ought implies can applies to the striving, which is always possible, not to the goal's ever being achieved. That is the critics' objection the lesson names.

**Must hit, any verdict (b):**

- A Kantian reply that finds a disanalogy, for example: Ines's goal comes in degrees, and each cure is a real piece of it she can achieve, whereas the highest good is the *final* end of all moral action, and a rational agent cannot make an end her final end while holding it impossible.
- A critic's rejoinder that meets the reply, for example: virtue and happiness can be brought into closer proportion locally, by degrees, so promoting the highest good is possible without anyone guaranteeing the whole; or: rational striving needs only that the end is not known to be impossible, not belief that it is possible.
- Even if the Kantian wins, the conclusion is a **postulate of practical reason**, not a theoretical proof that God exists.

**Wrong turns:** treating Ines's case as an attack on premise 3 or 4 (she says nothing about whether nature ties virtue to happiness); answering "Kant proved God exists" or "the argument is refuted" without engaging the striving reading.

**Model answer (b), one of several:** The Kantian says Ines can achieve parts of her goal, so her case is unlike the highest good, a final end that a rational agent cannot pursue while holding it impossible. The critic replies that the highest good also comes in degrees: we can make the good less often losers, here and now, which is possible on any hypothesis, so ought implies can is met without a guarantor. Either way, Kant's conclusion is only that a moral agent must postulate God, not that God exists.

</details>
