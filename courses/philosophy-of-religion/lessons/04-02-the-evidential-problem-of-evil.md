# Philosophy of Religion · Lesson 4.2: The evidential problem of evil

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 4: Evil and hiddenness · Builds on: [4.1 The logical problem of evil](04-01-the-logical-problem-of-evil.md), [3.5 Miracles](03-05-miracles.md) · Unlocks: [4.3 Theodicies](04-03-theodicies.md), [4.4 Skeptical theism](04-04-skeptical-theism.md)

## Why this matters

[4.1](04-01-the-logical-problem-of-evil.md) ended with the standard report: most philosophers, many atheists among them, take Plantinga's free will defense to show that God and evil are *logically consistent*. That settles less than it seems. Consistency is cheap: it is consistent that every coin you ever toss lands heads. The question that survives is whether the evil we actually find makes God **improbable**. That is the [evidential problem of evil](../reference.md#evidential-problem-of-evil), and it is the form in which the problem is argued today. This lesson runs its two classic versions: William Rowe's, which argues from particular horrors, and Paul Draper's, which argues from the overall pattern of pain and pleasure and goes naturally into odds form.

## The idea

Suppose a hospital administrator says every painful procedure on her ward serves the patient's recovery. You walk the ward and find a patient in agony from a procedure you cannot connect to any benefit. That does not *prove* her wrong; some benefit may exist that you cannot see. But it is *evidence* against her, and the more such cases you find, the stronger it gets.

Rowe's argument has this shape. Theism says a perfectly good, all-powerful, all-knowing being permits suffering only for some sufficient reason. We find suffering for which, after hard looking, we see no reason. So probably there is none, and so probably no such being exists.

Draper changes the question. Instead of asking whether this or that evil is justified, he asks which hypothesis better *predicts* the pattern we see: theism, or a rival on which nobody is looking after sentient creatures at all. That is a likelihood comparison, and the odds form of [3.5](03-05-miracles.md) handles it: posterior odds equal prior odds times the likelihood ratio ([philosophical-method 2.4](../../philosophical-method/lessons/02-04-weighing-evidence-in-odds-form.md)).

## The argument

**A. Rowe's argument** (William Rowe, "The Problem of Evil and Some Varieties of Atheism", *American Philosophical Quarterly*, 1979). His case, E1: in a distant forest, lightning sets a fire; a fawn is trapped, badly burned, and lies in agony for several days before it dies. The case is chosen so that no human good obviously hangs on it. Write $G$ for "there exists an omnipotent, omniscient, wholly good being."

1. **P1.** There are instances of intense suffering that an omnipotent, omniscient being could have prevented without losing some greater good or permitting some evil equally bad or worse. *In words:* some suffering is pointless; call it **gratuitous**.
2. **P2.** An omniscient, wholly good being would prevent any intense suffering it could, unless it could not do so without losing a greater good or permitting an equal or worse evil.
3. **∴ C.** $\neg G$: there is no omnipotent, omniscient, wholly good being.

The argument is valid, and P2 is accepted by most theists. The weight is on P1, and P1's support is an inductive step, the [noseeum inference](../reference.md#noseeum-inference):

- **N1.** After careful reflection, we know of no good that would justify God in permitting E1.
- **N2.** **∴ Probably** there is no good that justifies God in permitting E1, and so P1 is true.

*In words:* we look and see none, so probably none is there. The name is Stephen Wykstra's (1996), from the no-see-um, an insect too small to see that still bites. Rowe's defenders treat N1 to N2 as an ordinary inference from what we have surveyed to what we have not, made stronger by the sheer number of cases. This is what makes the argument evidential: P1 is not claimed to be certain, only probable, so $\neg G$ is only made probable too.

**The [G. E. Moore shift](../reference.md#g-e-moore-shift).** Rowe himself named the theist's best reply after G. E. Moore's answer to the skeptic, who reasoned from "here is a hand" back against the skeptic's premises (compare [epistemology 3.3](../../epistemology/lessons/03-03-moore-and-the-dogmatist.md)). Keep P2 and run the argument in reverse:

1. **G.** There exists an omnipotent, omniscient, wholly good being.
2. **P2.** (as above)
3. **∴ ¬P1.** No intense suffering is gratuitous.

Both arguments are valid and share P2. Which is rational to accept depends on which is better supported, $G$ or P1. Rowe drew a conclusion his title advertises: his "friendly atheism" holds that the evidence supports atheism, yet a theist with other grounds for $G$ may be rational in believing it.

**B. Draper's argument** (Paul Draper, "Pain and Pleasure: An Evidential Problem for Theists", *Noûs*, 1989). Let $O$ be what we know about who feels pain and pleasure and when. That covers moral agents and other sentient animals, pain and pleasure known to be biologically useful, and some not known to be. Let $T$ be theism and HI the [hypothesis of indifference](../reference.md#hypothesis-of-indifference): neither the nature nor the condition of sentient beings on earth results from benevolent or malevolent actions by nonhuman persons. Write $\Pr(O \mid T)$ for the probability of $O$ supposing $T$.

1. **D1.** $O$ is known.
2. **D2.** $\Pr(O \mid \text{HI})$ is much greater than $\Pr(O \mid T)$. *In words:* $O$ is much less surprising if nobody is looking after sentient creatures.
3. **D3.** $T$ is not intrinsically much more probable than HI. *In words:* before $O$, neither hypothesis is far ahead.
4. **∴ C.** Other evidence held equal, $T$ is more probably false than true.

Draper's case for D2: if HI is true, pain and pleasure will be whatever serves survival and reproduction, which is the pattern we find. If $T$ is true, pain needs a *moral* justification, and there is no antecedent reason why morally justified pain should line up so closely with biological usefulness, in animals that are not moral agents as much as in us. Note that HI is not naturalism: it allows an indifferent creator. And because HI entails $\neg T$, showing $T$ less probable than HI is enough to show $T$ less probable than not.

**Where the argument is weakest.** For Rowe, the critic attacks the step from N1 to N2. Wykstra's objection ([4.4](04-04-skeptical-theism.md)) is that "we see none" is evidence of "there is none" only if we would expect to see such goods were they there. Given the gap between a divine mind and ours, he argues, we should not. The defender replies that this move proves too much: it would also cut off our grounds for trusting God's promises or our own moral judgments. A smaller group of critics attack P2 instead: some classical theists ([1.1](01-01-which-god-and-what-an-argument-must-do.md)) deny that God's goodness consists in meeting obligations of the kind P2 states. For Draper, the critic attacks D2 at $\Pr(O \mid T)$. Can anyone say what a perfect being would be likely to permit? A second line attacks D3's prior parity ([epistemology 5.4](../../epistemology/lessons/05-04-the-problem-of-priors.md) on why priors resist settling). Draper's side replies that D2 needs only a comparison, not a full theory of divine reasons.

## The odds

Prior one to one gives fifty percent each; multiplying by one fifth gives one to five, about seventeen percent theism. A prior of eight to one gives about eighty-nine percent; multiplying by the same one fifth gives eight to five, about sixty-two percent.](assets/04-02-fig1.svg)

The bars show $T$'s share of the two-way contest between $T$ and HI only, not $T$'s probability against every rival. The multiplier is the same in both pairs; the verdicts differ because the starting points do.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean case: Draper in odds form).** The numbers are invented for illustration. Suppose $O$ is five times more expected on HI than on $T$, so the likelihood ratio for $T$ against HI is

$$L = \frac{\Pr(O \mid T)}{\Pr(O \mid \text{HI})} = \frac{1}{5}.$$

Take D3 at its simplest, prior odds $T : \text{HI} = 1:1$. Then

$$\text{posterior odds} = 1 \times \tfrac{1}{5} = 1:5.$$

So $T$ gets $\frac{1}{6} \approx 17\%$ of the two-way contest. Since HI entails $\neg T$, $\Pr(T) + \Pr(\text{HI}) \le 1$, and $\Pr(T) < \Pr(\text{HI})$ forces $\Pr(T) < \frac{1}{2}$. That is Draper's conclusion, with no claim about $T$'s other rivals. To stay at even odds after $O$, a theist would need prior odds of $5:1$ before it, the reciprocal of $L$.

**Example 2 (hard case: the Moore shift in odds form).** A theist grants Example 1's ratio but says the arguments of Modules 2 and 3 already put her at $8:1$ for $T$ over HI before she weighs $O$. The same $O$ takes her to

$$8 \times \tfrac{1}{5} = 8:5,$$

about $62\%$. This is the Moore shift with numbers: the critic and the theist share the multiplier and differ in what they bring to it. It strains in two places. First, the $8:1$ must come from evidence independent of how suffering is distributed. If any of it already assumed that God's goodness explains the world's evils, counting it and then $O$ double-counts ([cumulative case](../reference.md#cumulative-case)). Second, the shift is only as good as that other evidence. Rowe grants that it can make theism rational for someone who has it. He denies that it touches the evidential force of E1 itself. Both sides now agree on what the dispute is: total evidence, not whether suffering counts at all.

## Watch out

- **You might think the evidential argument is answered by the free will defense, but** the defense shows only that $G$ and evil are consistent. A consistent hypothesis can still be improbable; the [logical problem of evil](../reference.md#logical-problem-of-evil) and the evidential one need different answers.
- **You might think a theist must deny that suffering is evidence against God, but** many grant that it lowers the probability of theism and argue that other evidence outweighs it. Raising or lowering a probability is not proving ([proof and probability](../reference.md#proof-and-probability)). Others deny that it is evidence at all, which is [4.4](04-04-skeptical-theism.md)'s position.
- **You might think the Moore shift begs the question, but** it is valid, and it is cogent for anyone whose grounds for $G$ do not rest on denying P1. It begs the question only against someone with no such grounds.

## One-liner

> The evidential problem does not say God and evil are inconsistent; it says suffering we cannot explain is more to be expected if no one is looking after us. The argument turns on the noseeum step, and in odds form on $\Pr(O \mid T)$.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢) *(Formal (a)–(b) · Evaluative (c))*** A philosopher takes a different datum, $P$: *in nonhuman animals, pleasure attaches almost entirely to biologically useful activity: feeding, mating, and play that trains survival skills.* She grants prior odds $T : \text{HI} = 2:1$, and judges $P$ three times more expected on HI than on $T$.

(a) Compute the posterior odds $T : \text{HI}$ and $T$'s share of the two-way contest as a fraction.
(b) What prior odds would a theist need for the posterior to be exactly even? Then show, in one line, why a posterior below even for $T$ against HI implies $\Pr(T) < \frac{1}{2}$.
(c) A theist replies: "A God who creates through natural selection would make pleasure track biological usefulness, so the ratio is 1, not one third." Say which term of the odds form the reply attacks, give the new posterior, and state the strongest rejoinder from Draper's side. 100 words or fewer for (c).

**P2 (🟡) *(Exegetical)*** An invented exchange at a university debate:

> **Imogen:** A flash flood fills a badger's sett and it drowns slowly over hours, alone. I have thought hard about what good that could serve and found none. So probably there is none, and a perfectly good God who could prevent it would have.
>
> **Dev:** I accept your second step: a perfectly good God would prevent pointless suffering. But I have better grounds for thinking God exists than you have for thinking that flood was pointless. So I conclude that it was not pointless, though I cannot say what it was for.

(a) Name the premise both accept, and the single claim on which their disagreement turns. (b) Name one kind of evidence or argument that would move Imogen, and one that would move Dev, saying which is which. 120 words or fewer in total.

**P3 (🔴, optional) *(Exegetical)*** Two posts in an invented online forum thread:

> **Post 1:** "Draper's paper proves God doesn't exist. Pain lines up with biology exactly as atheism predicts. Case closed."
>
> **Post 2:** "Plantinga refuted the problem of evil back in 1974. Draper is just restating an argument that was answered fifty years ago."

Diagnose each post: name the distinction it misses and say what is actually true. Two sentences per post.

<details>
<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Formal (a)–(b) · Evaluative (c))*

(a) $L = \frac{1}{3}$ for $T$ against HI. Posterior odds $2 \times \frac{1}{3} = 2:3$. $T$'s share is $\frac{2}{2+3} = \frac{2}{5} = 40\%$.

(b) Even odds need prior $\times \frac{1}{3} = 1$, so prior odds $3:1$. Since HI entails $\neg T$, $\Pr(T) + \Pr(\text{HI}) \le 1$; if $\Pr(T) < \Pr(\text{HI})$ then $2\Pr(T) < 1$, so $\Pr(T) < \frac{1}{2}$.

**Must hit, any verdict (c):**

- The reply attacks the likelihood $\Pr(P \mid T)$, raising it to equal $\Pr(P \mid \text{HI})$; with $L = 1$ the posterior stays at the prior, $2:1$, a two-thirds share.
- A rejoinder that engages the reply: the auxiliary claim "God creates through selection and lets pleasure track usefulness" is not part of $T$. $\Pr(P \mid T)$ is raised only in proportion to how probable that auxiliary is given $T$. Writing it into the hypothesis lowers the prior of the stronger hypothesis instead.

**Wrong turns:** computing $2 - \frac{1}{3}$ or using $L = 3$ for $T$; treating the reply as an attack on the prior; saying the reply is skeptical theism (it claims to *predict* $P$, not that the likelihood is unknowable).

**Model answer (c), one of several:** It attacks $\Pr(P \mid T)$. If the ratio is 1, the posterior is the prior, $2:1$. Draper's rejoinder: theism alone does not say God works through selection or wants pleasure to track usefulness. If that auxiliary has probability one half given $T$, and $P$ is very unlikely on $T$ without it, $\Pr(P \mid T)$ is about half of $\Pr(P \mid \text{HI})$, not equal to it. Building the auxiliary into the hypothesis just moves the cost into a lower prior.

---

**P2** *(Exegetical, strict)*

**Must hit, strict (a):**

- Shared premise: Rowe's P2, that a wholly good, omnipotent, omniscient being would prevent intense suffering it could prevent without losing a greater good or allowing an equal or worse evil.
- Crux: the comparative claim of which is better supported, $G$ (God exists) or P1 (this suffering is gratuitous), and so whether the noseeum step from "I see no good" to "there is none" outweighs Dev's grounds for $G$. Dev is running the G. E. Moore shift.

**Must hit, strict (b):** something that changes the balance and is attributed to the right side. For Imogen: strong independent evidence for $G$ (a cumulative case from Modules 2–3), or a reason to think goods of this kind would be invisible to us (Wykstra's point). For Dev: evidence weakening his grounds for $G$, or a case that the noseeum inference is reliable here, such as a demonstration that the goods theists propose cannot apply to a solitary animal's suffering.

**Wrong turns:** naming "God exists" alone as the crux, when the dispute is comparative; calling Dev's argument invalid or circular, since it is valid and question-begging only if his grounds for $G$ presuppose denying P1.

**Model answer:** (a) Both accept that a perfectly good, all-powerful God would prevent suffering that serves no greater good. They split over whether Imogen's evidence that the badger's suffering is pointless is stronger than Dev's evidence that God exists. (b) Imogen would be moved by a strong independent case for God, or by reason to think such goods would be beyond our sight. Dev would be moved by the collapse of his grounds for theism, or by an argument that every candidate good is ruled out for a solitary animal.

---

**P3** *(Exegetical, strict)*

**Must hit, strict:**

- Post 1 conflates raising a probability with proof: Draper's argument is evidential and its conclusion is conditional ("other evidence held equal"); it also compares $T$ with HI, not with atheism in general, and HI allows an indifferent creator.
- Post 2 conflates the logical and evidential problems: the free will defense (1974) answers the claim that God and evil are inconsistent; Draper argues improbability, which consistency does not touch.

**Wrong turns:** saying Post 1 is wrong because pain does not line up with biology (that is a premise dispute, not the diagnosis); saying Post 2 is wrong because Plantinga's defense fails (the standard report is that it succeeds against the logical problem).

**Model answer:** Post 1: Draper argues that $O$ is better predicted by indifference than by theism, which lowers theism's probability "other evidence held equal"; it is not a proof, and its rival is HI, which even allows an indifferent creator. Whether theism ends up improbable depends on the rest of the evidence. Post 2: Plantinga's defense is widely taken to show that God and evil are consistent, which answers the logical problem. Draper's is an evidential argument, and a consistent hypothesis can still be made improbable by what we observe.

</details>

## Flashback

**From Lesson [3.5](03-05-miracles.md) (Miracles):** *(Exegetical.)* Invented case. In a monastery laboratory, a sealed flask of water at room temperature and normal pressure is filmed boiling for ten minutes, then stopping. The instruments confirm it, physicists agree it contradicts the laws as known, and years of attempts under identical conditions never reproduce it. (a) In one sentence each, say whether the event counts as a miracle, or as a violation of a law, on (i) Swinburne's definition, (ii) a pure regularity view of laws, (iii) the agent definition of Hume's footnote and Mackie. (b) The abbot announces: "The flask broke the laws of nature, so God boiled it." In two sentences, name the question he has skipped and two rival hypotheses he must still exclude.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):**

- (i) Swinburne: yes, as an event. It is a counter-instance that does not recur under like conditions, so keeping the law is still the best science and the event is a non-repeatable counter-instance.
- (ii) Regularity view: no violation is possible. A law just is an exceptionless regularity, so the counter-instance shows the regularity was never a law; nothing was broken.
- (iii) Agent definition: not settled by the observations. It is a miracle only if God (or, in Hume's footnote, some invisible agent) produced it as an intrusion into the natural order, and the instruments record the event, not its cause.

**Must hit, strict (b):**

- The skipped question is the second one: granting the event, was it a divine act? That is the gap problem of [1.1](01-01-which-god-and-what-an-argument-must-do.md), now about cause, not occurrence.
- Two rivals, each with its own prior: an undetected natural process (physics not yet understood), and an agent other than God (Hume's "some invisible agent").

**Wrong turns:** saying Swinburne's definition fails because the event never recurs, when non-recurrence is exactly what his definition requires. Offering "the witnesses are mistaken" as a rival in (b): that bears on whether the event happened, which the case stipulates, not on what caused it.

**Model answer:** (a) (i) On Swinburne's definition it is a violation: a counter-instance that never recurs, so we keep the law. (ii) On a pure regularity view nothing was violated; the supposed law was simply not exceptionless, so it was never a law. (iii) On the agent definition it is a miracle only if God (or some invisible agent) did it, which the footage cannot show. (b) The abbot skips from "did it happen?" to "who did it?" without argument. He still has to exclude a natural process we do not yet understand and an agent other than God.

</details>
