# Philosophy of Religion · Lesson 3.1: From design to fine-tuning

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 3: Arguments from design, morality, experience, and miracles · Builds on: [philosophical-method 2.2 Arguments from analogy](../../philosophical-method/lessons/02-02-arguments-from-analogy.md), [philosophical-method 2.4 Weighing evidence in odds form](../../philosophical-method/lessons/02-04-weighing-evidence-in-odds-form.md), [1.1 Which God, and what an argument must do](01-01-which-god-and-what-an-argument-must-do.md) · Unlocks: [3.2 Fine-tuning: the objections](03-02-fine-tuning-the-objections.md)

## Why this matters

The design argument is the oldest argument for God that ordinary people find moving, and it has survived two near-death experiences. Hume attacked it as a weak analogy; Darwin removed its best evidence. It came back in the late twentieth century with new evidence, the apparently life-permitting values of physical constants, and a new logical form: not "the world resembles a machine" but "this evidence is far more expected if there is a designer than if there is not." This lesson makes that change of form explicit. It shows why the change answers Hume's analogy objections but not his deeper one, and why physics, not biology, now carries the argument.

## The idea

Deal a bridge hand and get thirteen spades. Every hand is equally improbable, so the improbability alone proves nothing. What makes the spade hand suspicious is a *comparison*: a rigged deal makes that hand far more likely than a fair one does. Evidence favours whichever hypothesis made it less surprising.

That is the whole modern design argument. It does not need the world to look like a watch. It needs one feature of the world, $E$, such that $E$ is much more probable if a designer exists than if not. Then everything turns on two numbers: how probable is $E$ on design, and how probable on the rival?

Darwin changed the second number for biology. Physics seems to make it small again, one level down, for the constants in the laws themselves.

## The argument

**From analogy to likelihood.** Paley's watch and Hume's analogy objections are reloaded from [philosophical-method 2.2](../../philosophical-method/lessons/02-02-arguments-from-analogy.md): read as an analogy, the argument is only as good as the relevance premise linking artifacts to organisms, and Philo attacks that premise with rival analogies. Elliott Sober ("The Design Argument", 2003) proposed reading Paley differently, as a contest between two hypotheses. The tool is the [likelihood principle](../reference.md#likelihood-principle):

$$\frac{P(E \mid D)}{P(E \mid C)} > 1$$

Here $D$ is the design hypothesis, $C$ the rival (blind chance, or later chance plus selection), and $E$ the observed feature. *In words:* $E$ favours $D$ over $C$ exactly when $E$ is more probable on $D$, and the ratio measures how strongly.

The likelihood principle says nothing about which hypothesis to believe. That needs [odds form](../../philosophical-method/lessons/02-04-weighing-evidence-in-odds-form.md), reloaded and not re-derived:

$$\text{posterior odds} = \frac{P(D)}{P(C)} \times \frac{P(E \mid D)}{P(E \mid C)}$$

*In words:* where you end up is where you started, times how much more expected the evidence is on design.

Paley himself argues as though the comparison were obvious. Of the eye he writes (*Natural Theology*, 1802, ch. III):

> there is precisely the same proof that the eye was made for vision, as there is that the telescope was made for assisting it.

On Sober's reading, Paley's case needs only that adapted organs are far likelier on design than on chance. That escapes two of Hume's objections: we need not have watched worlds being made, and the world need not closely resemble a house.

**The [design argument](../reference.md#design-argument) as a likelihood argument.**

- **P1.** $E$: organisms have parts intricately adapted to functions.
- **P2.** $P(E \mid D)$ is not very small: a designer with the relevant aims would plausibly make adapted organisms.
- **P3.** $P(E \mid C)$ is extremely small: random assembly almost never yields an eye.
- **C.** $E$ strongly favours $D$ over $C$, and raises the odds of design by the likelihood ratio.

*In words:* the eye is evidence because it would be unsurprising if designed and miraculous if not.

**Darwin and the lost biological gap.** After *On the Origin of Species* (1859) the live rival to $D$ is not $C$ but $C_s$, chance variation plus natural selection, a process that predictably accumulates adaptation. $P(E \mid C_s)$ is not tiny, so P3's ratio collapses toward 1. Note what Darwin did *not* do: he left the prior and P2 untouched. He replaced the rival hypothesis with one that predicts the evidence. That is the general pattern for defeating any likelihood argument.

**[Fine-tuning](../reference.md#fine-tuning-argument).** Selection works within fixed physical laws. Paley himself fenced off that level, in the same chapter:

> I speak not of the origin of the laws themselves; but such laws being fixed, the construction in both cases is adapted to them.

Modern fine-tuning arguments (Robin Collins's "The Teleological Argument", in *The Blackwell Companion to Natural Theology*, 2009, is the standard statement) go to exactly that level. Physicists call a theory *fine-tuned* when it reproduces what we observe only if certain free parameters lie in a narrow range. The term describes the theory and implies nothing about a designer. The examples are surveyed in Luke Barnes's 2012 review in the *Publications of the Astronomical Society of Australia*:

- **The cosmological constant.** A naive quantum estimate of vacuum energy exceeds the observed dark-energy density by a factor usually quoted as about $10^{120}$ ([cosmology 4.4](../../cosmology/lessons/04-04-dark-energy-cosmic-acceleration.md)). The exponent depends on the cutoff assumed; the SEP's "Fine-Tuning" entry gives estimates from $10^{50}$ to $10^{123}$. A much larger positive value would have driven expansion fast enough to stop galaxies forming, an anthropic bound Steven Weinberg drew in 1987.
- **The strong force.** On one standard estimate, had it been more than about 50% stronger, almost all hydrogen would have been burned in the early universe.
- **The strength of gravity, the weak force, the quark masses**, and the size of early density fluctuations are further entries on the list.

Not every apparent fine-tuning lasts: the flatness of the early universe is now usually explained dynamically by inflation, which [3.2](03-02-fine-tuning-the-objections.md) takes up. The fine-tuning argument replaces P1 with $E_{\mathrm{ft}}$, "the constants take life-permitting values", and P3 with "$P(E_{\mathrm{ft}} \mid C)$ is extremely small, because life-permitting values occupy a tiny part of the range the constants could have taken." P2 becomes "a designer would plausibly want embodied, morally significant agents, so $P(E_{\mathrm{ft}} \mid D)$ is not very small."

**Where the argument is weakest.** Both likelihoods, for opposite reasons. Against P3, the critic says $P(E_{\mathrm{ft}} \mid C)$ requires a probability distribution over values a constant "could have taken", and physics supplies neither the range nor the measure; in the worst case no uniform distribution exists at all (the [normalizability problem](../reference.md#normalizability-problem), 3.2). Against P2, Sober's deepest objection is Hume's older one in new dress: we have no independent access to a designer's aims and abilities, so $P(E \mid D)$ is not assessable, and a ratio with an unassessable numerator gives nothing. Defenders reply that this proves too much. We infer designers in archaeology and in the search for extraterrestrial intelligence on modest assumptions about aims, and Collins argues that a good God has reason to create embodied moral agents. The critic's rejoinder is that this assumption imports perfect-being theology into P2 without arguing for it. The design conclusion also leaves [the gap problem](../reference.md#the-gap-problem) of [1.1](01-01-which-god-and-what-an-argument-must-do.md) open: a designer of the constants is not yet the God of classical theism.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean case: the eye before and after Darwin).** Invented numbers. Say a designer with Paley's aims makes eye-like organs with probability $\tfrac{1}{10}$, and random assembly yields one with probability $10^{-15}$. Then

$$L = \frac{10^{-1}}{10^{-15}} = 10^{14}$$

which is overwhelming. Now replace the rival with chance plus selection, which yields eye-like organs with probability $\tfrac{1}{20}$ over evolutionary time:

$$L = \frac{1/10}{1/20} = 2$$

The evidence is now barely worth mentioning, and no premise about God changed. A theist can still hold design on other grounds, or say selection is the designer's means. But the eye no longer does argumentative work on its own.

**Example 2 (hard case: a constant).** Invented numbers. Suppose a constant could, on the chance hypothesis, take any value from 0 to $10^9$ in some unit, uniformly, and life needs a window of width 100. Then $P(E \mid C) = 100/10^9 = 10^{-7}$. Grant $P(E \mid D) = 10^{-2}$, since a designer might have many aims. With prior odds $1:1{,}000$ against design:

$$L = \frac{10^{-2}}{10^{-7}} = 10^5$$

$$\text{posterior} = \frac{1}{1{,}000} \times 10^5 = 100:1$$

That is a probability of $100/101 \approx 99\%$. Now see where it strains (the figure above). Why a range of $10^9$? Take $10^5$ and $P(E \mid C) = 10^{-3}$, so $L = 10$, and the posterior is $1:100$, about $1\%$. Keep the range but let a sceptic about divine aims set $P(E \mid D) = 10^{-6}$, and $L = 10$ again. The arithmetic is trivial, and the conclusion is fixed by two inputs that physics alone does not supply.

## Watch out

- **You might think "fine-tuned" means "designed", but** it is a physicist's description of a theory: observation is matched only in a narrow parameter range. Theists, multiverse theorists and those who expect a deeper theory all accept the description.
- **You might think a tiny $P(E \mid C)$ makes chance improbable, but** that is the transposed conditional of [philosophical-method 2.4](../../philosophical-method/lessons/02-04-weighing-evidence-in-odds-form.md). Every specific bridge hand has tiny probability. Evidence favours design only by comparison with $P(E \mid D)$, and belief also needs the prior.
- **You might think Darwin refuted the design argument, but** he defeated one premise for one body of evidence by supplying a rival that predicts it. He said nothing about the laws selection runs on, which is where the argument went.

## One-liner

> The design argument became a likelihood argument: evidence favours design exactly as far as it is likelier on design than on its best rival. Darwin raised the rival's likelihood for organisms, and fine-tuning bets that nothing does the same for the constants.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢) *(Formal (a)–(b) · Exegetical (c).)*** Surveyors on a remote plateau find twelve stones arranged in a near-perfect circle. Take prior odds of $1:20$ that people built it (most such sites are natural). Suppose the arrangement has probability $0.3$ if built by people and $0.0006$ if left by glacial deposition alone.

(a) Compute the likelihood ratio, the posterior odds, and the posterior probability that people built it, as an exact fraction.
(b) A geologist shows that freeze-thaw cycles sort stones into rings, so the natural rival now gives the arrangement probability $0.15$. Recompute all three.
(c) Which move in the lesson does (b) reproduce, and which term of the odds form did it change? Two sentences.

**P2 (🟡) *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)*** From a parish newsletter, written for this problem and attributed to no one:

> "Physicists tell us the cosmological constant is fine-tuned to one part in $10^{120}$. So the odds that our universe arose by chance are one in $10^{120}$, and the odds that it was designed are $10^{120}$ to one. No rational person bets against odds like that."

(a) Name two distinct errors in the inference, each in one sentence. (b) A reader replies: "Even if the number is off by a hundred orders of magnitude, any sensible fill-in still favours design." Say what would have to be true for the reply to work, and whether it is safe. 100 words or fewer for (b).

**P3 (🔴, optional) *(Evaluative.)*** A sceptic argues: "The design argument from biology looked unanswerable until Darwin. Every design argument rests on a gap in current science, and gaps close. So fine-tuning should be given little weight." Steelman this argument in odds-form terms, then give the best reply a fine-tuning defender can make. 150 words or fewer.

<details>
<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Formal (a)–(b) · Exegetical (c))*

(a) $L = \dfrac{0.3}{0.0006} = 500$. Posterior odds $= \dfrac{1}{20} \times 500 = 25:1$. Probability $= \dfrac{25}{26} \approx 96.2\%$.

(b) $L = \dfrac{0.3}{0.15} = 2$. Posterior odds $= \dfrac{1}{20} \times 2 = 1:10$. Probability $= \dfrac{1}{11} \approx 9.1\%$. The ratio fell by a factor of 250.

**Must hit, strict (c):**

- It reproduces the Darwin move: a rival hypothesis that predicts the evidence replaces one that did not.
- The term changed is the likelihood on the rival, the denominator of the likelihood ratio; the prior and the likelihood on design are untouched.

**Wrong turns:** saying the geologist lowered the prior for design; treating (b) as showing the circle was not built, when the posterior is $1:10$, not zero.

**Model answer (c):** It is the lesson's Darwin move: a natural process that predicts the pattern replaces one that made it a fluke. Only the denominator of the likelihood ratio changed, from 0.0006 to 0.15; the prior and $P(E \mid \text{built})$ stayed fixed.

---

**P2** *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b))*

**Must hit, strict (a):**

- **Not a probability.** "One part in $10^{120}$" describes a precision, or a theory-observation mismatch; turning it into $P(E \mid C)$ needs a range of possible values and a probability measure over them, which the newsletter assumes without stating.
- **Transposed conditional.** Even if $P(E \mid C) = 10^{-120}$, that is the probability of the evidence given chance, not of chance given the evidence. Posterior odds also need $P(E \mid D)$ and the prior odds, and the newsletter omits both.

A strong answer adds that $10^{120}$ is the figure for one constant on one estimate, which depends on the cutoff assumed.

**Must hit, any verdict (b):**

- The reply needs $P(E \mid D)$ not to be comparably tiny, so it needs some assessable claim about a designer's aims.
- It needs the prior odds not to be so low that they absorb the ratio.
- It needs the range-and-measure problem to have some sensible answer at all; if no distribution is defensible, there is nothing to fill in.
- Any verdict, if these three are weighed.

**Wrong turns:** answering with the multiverse, which attacks a different term and belongs to 3.2; calling the reply safe or unsafe without naming its assumptions.

**Model answer (b), one of several:** The reply works only if three things hold: some defensible distribution makes $P(E \mid C)$ very small; $P(E \mid D)$ is not comparably small, which needs a claim about what a designer would want; and the prior is not low enough to swallow the ratio. A huge margin protects against error in the size of $P(E \mid C)$, not against its being undefined or against an unassessable $P(E \mid D)$. So it is safe against the newsletter's arithmetic, and not safe against the two objections the lesson calls weakest.

---

**P3** *(Evaluative)*

**Must hit, any verdict:**

- **Steelman in odds terms.** The track record should lower confidence that $P(E_{\mathrm{ft}} \mid \text{no design})$ is truly small: past small denominators were artifacts of missing theory (the eye; flatness, per 3.2), so a rational agent discounts the current estimate.
- **Reply.** The defender must say what is different now, for example that selection-style explanations need prior laws, so an explanation of the constants must work at the level of the laws; or that the induction cuts both ways, because a deeper theory might itself need fine-tuned inputs.
- State whether the reply blunts the induction or only limits it.

**Wrong turns:** steelmanning it as "science will explain everything", which is a promise, not an argument; replying that the sceptic assumes naturalism, when the argument is an induction from cases.

**Model answer, one of several:** Steelman: the argument does not deny that $P(E_{\mathrm{ft}} \mid C)$ is currently estimated as small. It says our estimates of such denominators have a bad record. The eye looked improbable without design until selection, and early-universe flatness looked fine-tuned until inflation. So we should put real credence on a future theory that makes $E_{\mathrm{ft}}$ expected, and that shrinks the effective ratio. Reply: Darwin's success came from a mechanism operating *within* laws, and no analogous mechanism is known for the laws' own parameters. A deeper theory may also move the fine-tuning into its own inputs instead of removing it. The induction justifies humility about the ratio's size, not treating it as 1.

</details>

## Flashback

**From Lesson [2.4](02-04-cosmological-arguments-sufficient-reason.md) (Cosmological arguments II: sufficient reason):** *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)* An invented exchange from a debate night:

> **Naturalist:** Grant your necessary foundation. Make it a necessary initial physical state, governed by indeterministic laws. The state is necessary, but the world arises from it by chance, so nothing forces the world to be necessary. No god required.
> **Theist:** Then nothing explains why *this* world arose rather than another. Only a free choice gives a reason without necessitating its outcome.

(a) Which premise of the lesson's six-step Leibnizian skeleton is under dispute here, and which objection's escape route is the naturalist copying? Two sentences. (b) Find the crux: name the single claim the theist's last line needs, and say what consideration would move each side on it. 100 words or fewer.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):**

- Premise 6, "that necessary being is God": the gap problem, with the naturalist offering a necessary physical foundation, the rival the lesson attributes to Oppy.
- The naturalist copies the escape from modal collapse: deny E (explanation entails), here by a chance explanation instead of a free choice, so the necessary foundation does not make the world necessary.

**Must hit, any verdict (b):**

- The crux: a non-entailing explanation is *sufficient* only if it is given by an agent's reasons; chance explanation does not count. Equivalently: does the PSR demand an answer to the contrastive question, and can a free choice give one when chance cannot?
- Parity pressure: a libertarian choice also leaves "why this rather than that?" open in the lesson's Ilse case, so the theist owes a disanalogy (reasons make the outcome intelligible; a probability only makes it expected).
- One mover for each side, and a verdict either way.

**Wrong turns:** treating this as Hume–Edwards, which disputes whether the BCCF needs explaining at all, while both speakers grant it does; saying the naturalist's foundation entails the world, when indeterministic laws are exactly what stop it from doing so.

**Model answer (b), one of several:** The theist needs the claim that only reason-giving explanations can be non-entailing yet sufficient. The naturalist answers that a chance outcome is explained by its law and initial state just as a choice is explained by its reasons, and that neither settles the contrast. The theist should move if the reasons-versus-chance difference cannot be stated without assuming that a choice settles the contrast. The naturalist should move if it can: if acting for a reason makes an outcome intelligible in a way a probability does not, then the escape from collapse really is personal.

</details>
