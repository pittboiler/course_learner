# Modern Philosophy · Lesson 5.1: The critical question

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 5: Kant I: the conditions of experience · Builds on: [4.1 Impressions, ideas and the fork](04-01-impressions-ideas-and-the-fork.md), [4.2 Causation and necessary connection](04-02-causation-and-necessary-connection.md), [4.5 Reid: common sense against the way of ideas](04-05-reid-common-sense-against-the-way-of-ideas.md) · Unlocks: [5.2 Space and time: the Transcendental Aesthetic](05-02-space-and-time-the-transcendental-aesthetic.md)

## Why this matters

Hume left the necessity in "cause" as a felt determination of the mind ([4.2](04-02-causation-and-necessary-connection.md)). Immanuel Kant (1724–1804) says, in the preface to the *Prolegomena* (1783; Carus trans.), that "the suggestion of David Hume was the very thing, which many years ago first interrupted my dogmatic slumber". He did not answer Hume about cause alone. He generalized the question: how can any judgment both *extend* what we know and be known *independently of experience*? The *Critique of Pure Reason* (1781; second edition 1787) is built around that question. Its answer, that the mind supplies forms which experience must take, is the setup for this module and the backdrop for [`phenomenology-and-personalism`](../../phenomenology-and-personalism/syllabus.md).

## The idea

Kant asks two separate questions about any judgment.

**How is it known?** Knowledge is [a priori](../reference.md#a-priori-and-a-posteriori) when it is independent of *all* experience, not just of this or that experience. Kant's example: a man who undermines his house might be said to know *a priori* that it will fall. But only relatively so, since he learned from experience that bodies are heavy. The marks of the a priori are **necessity** and **strict universality** (B3–4). Experience teaches that something *is* so, never that it could not be otherwise. Its universality is only comparative: so far, no exception.

**What joins the predicate to the subject?** A judgment is [analytic](../reference.md#analytic-and-synthetic) when the predicate is already contained, perhaps covertly, in the concept of the subject; analysis just makes it explicit. Kant calls these *explicative*. It is synthetic when the predicate adds something the concept did not contain: *augmentative*. His examples (Introduction §IV): *all bodies are extended* is analytic, since extension is part of what we think in "body". *All bodies are heavy* is synthetic, since weight is not.

Cross the two questions and you get four cells (see the picture). One is empty. An analytic judgment needs nothing beyond its concept, so grounding it on experience would be absurd. Two are easy. Analytic judgments rest on the principle of contradiction. Synthetic a posteriori judgments are joined by experience. The fourth cell is the puzzle. In a [synthetic a priori](../reference.md#synthetic-a-priori) judgment, the concept does not contain the predicate, and experience cannot supply necessity. So what joins them? Kant calls it *the unknown = X*.

Kant claims the cell is occupied. Arithmetic and geometry live there. So do the principles of pure physics, such as conservation of the quantity of matter. And metaphysics *wants* to live there: *the world must have a beginning* claims necessity and goes beyond any concept. Hence the problem of the whole *Critique* (Introduction §VI): "How are synthetical judgements à priori possible?" On the answer depends whether metaphysics is a science or a delusion.

## Source

Kant, *Critique of Pure Reason*, Preface to the Second Edition (1787; Bxvi), Meiklejohn trans.:

> It has hitherto been assumed that our cognition must conform to the objects; but all attempts to ascertain anything about these objects *à priori*, by means of conceptions, and thus to extend the range of our knowledge, have been rendered abortive by this assumption. Let us then make the experiment whether we may not be more successful in metaphysics, if we assume that the objects must conform to our cognition. [...] We here propose to do just what Copernicus did in attempting to explain the celestial movements. When he found that he could make no progress by assuming that all the heavenly bodies revolved round the spectator, he reversed the process, and tried the experiment of assuming that the spectator revolved, while the stars remained at rest.

Three things to notice. **"Experiment"** appears twice. The [Copernican revolution](../reference.md#copernican-revolution) is offered as a hypothesis, to be judged by whether it explains what the old assumption could not. **The analogy is about the observer, not about centrality.** Copernicus explained the apparent motions by the spectator's own motion. Kant proposes that some features of objects as we know them come from the knower. **What the words do not settle:** the next lines speak of objects as given in experience, so whether "objects" here means things as they are in themselves, or only objects of possible experience, is the question of [6.1](06-01-transcendental-idealism-and-the-thing-in-itself.md).

## The argument

The critical question, reconstructed from the Introduction and the two prefaces.

1. **Metaphysics aims at judgments that are a priori and that extend knowledge**: God, freedom, immortality, a beginning of the world. *In words:* it claims necessity about things no concept contains and no experience shows.
2. **Analytic judgments only explicate concepts.** *In words:* they clarify what we already think; they never add to it.
3. **Experience yields only comparative universality, never necessity.** So no a posteriori judgment can be metaphysical.
4. **∴ Metaphysics is possible only if synthetic a priori judgments are.**
5. **Hume showed, for one concept, that neither analysis nor experience yields the necessity it claims.** He concluded that the necessity in "cause" is the subjective necessity of habit (*Prolegomena*, preface; Introduction §VI).
6. **Pure mathematics is synthetic a priori too.** So Hume's reasoning, carried through, would destroy mathematics with metaphysics, which Kant thinks Hume would have found absurd.
7. **Pure mathematics and pure natural science exist.** Introduction §VI argues that their reality shows they must be possible.
8. **∴ Synthetic a priori knowledge is actual, and the question is how it is possible**, and whether metaphysics can share it.
9. **If cognition must conform to objects, a priori knowledge of them is unintelligible; if objects, as known, conform to our cognition, it is intelligible.** *In words:* we can know in advance only what we ourselves put into experience.
10. **∴ Test the second hypothesis** by asking what the a priori conditions of any experience are.

That inquiry is the [transcendental method](../reference.md#transcendental-method). Kant says: "I apply the term transcendental to all knowledge which is not so much occupied with objects as with the mode of our cognition of these objects, so far as this mode of cognition is possible à priori" (Introduction §VII).

**Where the argument is weakest.** Premises 6–7. Premise 7 is regressive: it assumes the sciences that Hume's principles call into question. Kant's reply (*Prolegomena* §4) is that only the *Prolegomena* start from the given sciences; the *Critique* treats the question synthetically, from reason itself, resting on no such fact. Premise 6 drew the stronger attack. Gottlob Frege (*The Foundations of Arithmetic*, 1884) argued that arithmetic is analytic, derivable from logic plus definitions, while agreeing with Kant about geometry. A Kantian replies that Frege changed the test: Kant's analyticity is containment in the concept as thought, and Frege's is provability. Whether that is a refutation or a change of subject belongs to [`epistemology` 4.1](../../epistemology/lessons/04-01-a-priori-knowledge.md). Geometry's turn comes with non-Euclidean geometry in [5.2](05-02-space-and-time-the-transcendental-aesthetic.md).

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean: the geometer and the philosopher).** In the Doctrine of Method (Discipline of Pure Reason, ch. I, sect. 1), Kant gives a philosopher the concept of a triangle and asks how its angles relate to two right angles. The philosopher has only the concept of a figure enclosed by three straight lines, and so with three angles. So *a triangle has three angles* is **analytic a priori**: analysis yields it. However long he analyses "straight line", "angle" and "three", he never finds the angle sum. The geometer instead *constructs* a triangle, extends a side, draws a parallel, and reads the result off the construction. So *the angles of a triangle equal two right angles* is **synthetic a priori**. It is necessary and strictly universal, and no surveyor's measurement could establish that, so it is a priori. It is not in the concept, so it is synthetic. Kant's name for what the geometer uses is intuition, which [5.2](05-02-space-and-time-the-transcendental-aesthetic.md) explains.

**Example 2 (hard: 7 + 5 = 12).** Kant concedes (Introduction §V) that this *looks* analytic. His case: the concept of the sum of seven and five contains only their uniting into one number, not *which* number. To get twelve you must add the units one at a time in intuition, with fingers or with points as in Segner's arithmetic. Try large numbers, he says, and it becomes evident that no analysis of the concepts gives the sum.

The strain is in "contained". If containment means whatever a given person happens to think, analyticity varies from person to person. Kant anticipates this. The question, he says, is not what we *must* join to a concept but what we actually think in it, though obscurely. That keeps the test conceptual but leaves it hard to apply. Quine later complained ("Two Dogmas of Empiricism", 1951) that containment never gets past metaphor, and Frege's provability test is one way to replace it. Inside Kant's own terms, the argument stands or falls with his claim that the concept of a sum does not fix its value.

## Watch out

- **You might think Kant's a priori means innate, but it does not.** The Introduction opens by granting that all knowledge *begins* with experience; Kant denies only that all of it *arises out of* experience. A priori marks how a judgment is justified, not when the knower acquired it. In the deduction (B167–168) Kant considers the view that the categories are aptitudes implanted at our creation. He rejects it because their necessity would then be merely subjective, "just what the sceptic wants". Contrast Leibniz's veined marble in [3.1](03-01-locke-against-innate-ideas.md), and see [innate ideas](../reference.md#innate-ideas).
- **You might think Kant's grid is Hume's fork relabelled, but it is not.** Hume's [fork](../reference.md#humes-fork) has two bins, sorted by how a claim is known. Kant has two distinctions that cut across each other. Hume put all of arithmetic and geometry among relations of ideas, knowable by thought alone (*Enquiry* §20). Kant's quarrel is precisely that they are synthetic. Reading Kant's "analytic" as "true by meaning" is a twentieth-century gloss. Kant's test is containment in a subject concept, stated for affirmative subject–predicate judgments.
- **You might think "transcendental" means "beyond experience", but that is "transcendent".** Kant draws the line himself in the Transcendental Dialectic: a transcendent principle oversteps experience, and a transcendental inquiry asks about the a priori conditions of experience. The *Critique* is a transcendental inquiry, and one of its results is that theoretical knowledge cannot be transcendent.

## One-liner

> Kant turns Hume's question about cause into one about all knowledge: how can a judgment add to its concept and still be necessary? He bets that it can only if objects, as we know them, conform to our cognition.

**P1 (🟢) *(Exegetical.)*** Classify each judgment in *both* of Kant's distinctions, as he would, with his reason in one line. Then (e): why does none of them, and no judgment at all, fall in the analytic a posteriori cell? One sentence.

**P2 (🟡) *(Exegetical (a)–(b) · Evaluative (c).)*** Close reading and crux. Kant, *Prolegomena*, Preamble (§4 in Carus's edition), Carus trans.:

> Hume being prompted (a task worthy of a philosopher) to cast his eye over the whole field of a priori cognitions in which human understanding claims such mighty possessions, heedlessly severed from it a whole, and indeed its most valuable, province, viz., pure mathematics; for he thought its nature, or, so to speak, the state-constitution of this empire, depended on totally different principles, namely, on the law of contradiction alone; and although he did not divide Judgments in this manner formally and universally as I have done here, what he said was equivalent to this: that mathematics contains only analytical, but metaphysics synthetical, a priori judgments. In this, however, he was greatly mistaken, and the mistake had a decidedly injurious effect upon his whole conception.

For comparison, Hume (*Enquiry* §20–21) says relations of ideas include "Geometry, Algebra, and Arithmetic", are "discoverable by the mere operation of thought", and that the contrary of every matter of fact "can never imply a contradiction".

(a) What does Kant attribute to Hume, and which words show he knows it is a reconstruction rather than Hume's own classification? Two sentences. (b) Name the single premise Hume, as Kant reads him, accepts and Kant denies. One sentence. (c) Is "what he said was equivalent to this" fair to *Enquiry* §20–21? Any verdict; 100 words or fewer.

<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical, strict.)*

**Must hit, strict:**

- (a) **Analytic a priori.** Kant says the concept of gold *is* "a yellow metal", so the judgment only analyses it. The concept is empirical, but the judgment needs no experience beyond the concept (*Prolegomena* §2b).
- (b) **Synthetic a priori.** It claims necessity, so it is a priori. The concept of matter contains only its presence in space, not its permanence, so it is synthetic (Introduction §V.2).
- (c) **Analytic a priori.** Kant lists it among the few geometers' principles that are "really analytical", resting on the principle of contradiction. They serve as links in mathematical method and are admitted only because they can be presented in intuition (Introduction §V.1).
- (d) **Synthetic a posteriori.** A contingent report joined by observation.
- (e) An analytic judgment needs nothing beyond its concept, so appeal to experience is pointless. Kant calls grounding one on experience absurd (Introduction §IV).

**Wrong turns:** calling (a) a posteriori because we learned what gold looks like. Forming the concept took experience, but the judgment does not. Calling (c) synthetic because it is a mathematical axiom: Kant treats it as the exception.

**Model answer:** (a) analytic a priori: "yellow metal" is the concept of gold. (b) synthetic a priori: necessary, yet permanence is not in "matter". (c) analytic a priori: "greater than its part" is contained in "whole". (d) synthetic a posteriori: observed and contingent. (e) Analysis never needs experience, so none can be analytic a posteriori.

---

**P2** *(Exegetical (a)–(b) · Evaluative (c).)*

**Must hit, strict (a):**

- Kant says Hume held that pure mathematics rests on the law of contradiction alone, so it contains only analytic a priori judgments, while metaphysics would need synthetic ones.
- The hedge: "although he did not divide Judgments in this manner formally and universally as I have done here, what he said was equivalent to this". Kant is translating Hume into his own distinction.

**Must hit, strict (b):**

- The premise is that *whatever is known a priori, by thought alone, is true by the principle of contradiction alone* (equivalently, a priori implies analytic). Hume, on Kant's reading, accepts it; Kant denies it, with mathematics as the counterexample.

**Must hit, any verdict (c):**

- Use what §21 does say: matters of fact can be denied without contradiction, which implies that relations of ideas cannot. That supports Kant.
- Use what it does not say: Hume sorts by how a claim is known ("mere operation of thought", intuition or demonstration). He offers no test of predicate containment. So "law of contradiction alone" is Kant's inference, not Hume's words.

**Wrong turns:** treating "relations of ideas" and "analytic" as synonyms, which is the question at issue. In (b), naming "whether cause is a priori": both agree it is not analytic. The split is over mathematics.

**Model answer (c), one of several:** Partly fair. Section 21 says matters of fact can be denied without contradiction, so by contrast denying a relation of ideas yields one. That is close to "the law of contradiction alone". But Hume's category is defined by its route: intuition or demonstration, by thought. Nothing in §20 says what makes the proposition true, and no containment test appears. Kant has supplied the bridge from *knowable by thought* to *true by contradiction*, and that bridge is the very premise he then denies. So the reconstruction is fair as a diagnosis, but it is a diagnosis, not a report.

---

**P3** *(Exegetical, strict.)*

**Accept:** any three of the four errors below, each tied to its correcting text.

**Must hit, strict:**

- **"Born with"**: a priori concerns justification, not origin in time. All knowledge *begins* with experience (Introduction §I).
- **"Brains built to count" as the source of necessity**: Kant rejects implanted aptitudes, since they would yield merely subjective necessity, "just what the sceptic wants" (B167–168).
- **"The mind makes its world, as in a dream"**: the Bxvi passage is about the *form* of objects as known, offered as an experiment to explain a priori knowledge. Knowledge still begins with objects that affect our senses (Introduction §I). The status of things in themselves is 6.1's question.
- **"Sided with the rationalists"**: a later textbook label. Kant rejects both dogmatic metaphysics and Hume's empirical derivation of necessity.

**Wrong turns:** correcting "born with" by saying a priori means "learned early". Kant's point concerns justification, not timing. Treating the dream analogy as merely too strong, when the text says objects must be *given*.

**Model answer:** First, a priori is not innate: knowledge begins with experience, and a priori concerns how a judgment is justified, by necessity and strict universality. Second, wiring cannot ground necessity. Kant rejects implanted aptitudes because they would give only subjective necessity, "just what the sceptic wants". Third, the Copernican experiment concerns the a priori form of objects as known, not their production. Objects still affect our senses and are given.

## Flashback

**From Lesson [4.4](04-04-the-self-belief-and-the-passions.md) (The self, belief and the passions):** *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)* Apply to a hard case. Hume, *Treatise* II.iii.3 (Selby-Bigge text, Project Gutenberg), drawing the consequences of his two senses of "unreasonable":

> [I]t is impossible, that reason and passion can ever oppose each other, or dispute for the government of the will and actions. The moment we perceive the falshood of any supposition, or the insufficiency of any means our passions yield to our reason without any opposition. I may desire any fruit as of an excellent relish; but whenever you convince me of my mistake, my longing ceases.

An invented case. Leo is furious with his neighbour, whom he believes dented his car. The building's camera shows a delivery van did it. His anger at the neighbour drains away within a minute, though he is still annoyed about the dent. A classmate says: "Reason just defeated a passion, so Hume's premise that reason alone can never oppose a passion is false." (a) Describe what happened in Hume's terms, and say why, by the passage, it is not reason opposing a passion. Three sentences. (b) The classmate replies: "'Yield without any opposition' is a relabelling that makes Hume's premise impossible to refute." Is it? Any verdict; 100 words or fewer.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):**

- Leo's anger was founded on a false supposition, that the neighbour dented the car: Hume's first sense in which a passion is called unreasonable. Strictly, what was unreasonable was the judgment, not the anger.
- When the judgment was corrected, the passion lost what it was founded on and ceased, as the longing for the fruit ceases. Reason supplied no contrary impulse, and for Hume only a contrary impulse can oppose a passion.
- The leftover annoyance confirms the point. It rests on no false belief (the car really is dented), so the discovery leaves it untouched. Reason acts on passions only through the beliefs they depend on.

**Must hit, any verdict (b):**

- State the distinction: to *oppose* is to act on the will as a rival force; to make a passion *yield* is to remove the belief about existence or means it was founded on.
- Say what would refute the premise: a case where grasping a truth moves the will, or quells a passion, without changing any belief that passion rests on. Clarke's line offers one: seeing that an act is unfitting moves me to refrain. Hume reclassifies such cases as calm passions.
- A verdict. "Unfalsifiable" if every apparent counter-motive is reclassified as a calm passion by definition. "Contentful" if calm passions can be identified independently, and the thesis is a claim about what discovering a truth can do on its own.

**Wrong turns:** calling Leo's anger itself contrary to reason; by the passage it is the judgment. Saying reason did nothing here. It corrected the belief, which is the job Hume gives it: informing us of what exists and of means to ends.

**Model answer (b), one of several:** Not quite. The distinction has content: correcting Leo's belief worked by removing the anger's ground, and that predicts that his annoyance about the dent, which has no false ground, survives, as it does. The premise would fail if a bare truth, changing no belief about objects or means, could move or check the will. The danger is in Hume's reply to such cases. If whatever moves us without a passion in view is renamed a "calm passion" just because it moves us, the premise becomes true by definition. It stays testable only if calm passions can be picked out some other way.

</details>
