# Modern Philosophy · Lesson 5.2: Space and time: the Transcendental Aesthetic

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 5: Kant I: the conditions of experience · Builds on: [5.1 The critical question](05-01-the-critical-question.md), [2.3 Leibniz: sufficient reason and monads](02-03-leibniz-sufficient-reason-and-monads.md) · Unlocks: [5.3 Concepts and the categories](05-03-concepts-and-the-categories.md), [6.1 Transcendental idealism and the thing in itself](06-01-transcendental-idealism-and-the-thing-in-itself.md)

## Why this matters

[5.1](05-01-the-critical-question.md) ended with a question: how can we know [synthetic a priori](../reference.md#synthetic-a-priori) truths, such as the theorems of geometry, before experience and yet about the world? The Transcendental Aesthetic, the opening of the *Critique of Pure Reason*, gives Kant's first answer and his first argument for transcendental idealism: space and time are neither things nor relations among things, but the forms in which anything can be given to us. It is also the part of Kant that later mathematics and physics hit hardest.

## The idea

Kant splits the mind's contribution into two stems. **Sensibility** is receptive: through it objects are *given*. **Understanding** is spontaneous: through it objects are *thought*. Each stem has its own kind of representation. An **intuition** relates to its object immediately and is singular: *this* patch of red in front of you. A **concept** relates to objects only mediately, through a mark that many things can share: "red", "tower", "body". (Kant's "graduated list" in the Dialectic, Book I §1; see [intuition and concept](../reference.md#intuition-and-concept).)

Now ask where space belongs. In Kant's day there were two answers. Newton's followers held that space is a real, self-standing container. Leibniz ([2.3](02-03-leibniz-sufficient-reason-and-monads.md)) held that it is the order of relations among coexisting things, which we form a concept of. Kant opens the Aesthetic (§2) by setting out both and adding a third: space belongs only to the form of intuition, the way an outer sense like ours must receive whatever affects it.

Why "intuition"? Take the concept *dog*. Particular dogs fall *under* it, and each can be thought without the others. Now take space. "This space" and "that space" are not instances of a kind. They are *parts* of one space, and you can only think them as cut out of it. That is how an intuition behaves, not a concept.

Geometry then gets its explanation. You cannot prove that two sides of a triangle together exceed the third by unpacking the concept *triangle*. You have to construct a triangle, on paper or in imagination, and see. If space is the form of our outer sense, that construction tells you in advance how every triangle you could ever meet must be. The price is the conclusion: it tells you nothing about things as they are in themselves.

## Source

Kant, *Critique of Pure Reason*, Transcendental Aesthetic §3, "Transcendental Exposition of the Conception of Space" (Meiklejohn trans., second edition, 1787). "Conception" is Meiklejohn's word for concept; "apodeictic" means necessarily true.

> Geometry is a science which determines the properties of space synthetically, and yet à priori. What, then, must be our representation of space [...]? It must be originally intuition, for from a mere conception, no propositions can be deduced which go out beyond the conception, and yet this happens in geometry. [...] But this intuition must be found in the mind à priori [...], consequently must be pure, not empirical, intuition. For geometrical principles are always apodeictic [...]. Now, how can an external intuition anterior to objects themselves, and in which our conception of objects can be determined à priori, exist in the human mind? Obviously not otherwise than in so far as it has its seat in the subject only, as the formal capacity of the subject's being affected by objects [...]; consequently, only as the form of the external sense in general.

Three things to notice. The argument runs *backwards*, from a fact Kant takes as given (geometry exists) to what must be true for it to be possible: the [transcendental method](../reference.md#transcendental-method). Each property of geometry buys one property of the representation: synthetic buys *intuition*, a priori buys *pure*. And the last step ("Obviously not otherwise") infers to the only explanation Kant can see; it is not a deduction.

## The argument

Kant gives two expositions of space, then draws conclusions (§§2–4).

**A. The metaphysical exposition (§2).** In the second edition there are four arguments. Each shows something about the representation of space.

- **M1. Not empirical.** To represent sensations as outside me, and as beside one another in different places, I must already have the representation of space. *In words:* you can't learn space from outer experience, because you need it to have outer experience.
- **M2. Necessary.** "We never can imagine or make a representation to ourselves of the non-existence of space, though we may easily enough think that no objects are found in it." *In words:* objects depend on space, not space on objects.
- **M3. Not a concept but an intuition.** There is only one space; "spaces" are its parts, made by limiting it, not building blocks that come first. *In words:* space relates to its parts the way a whole does, not the way a kind does.
- **M4. An infinite given magnitude.** A concept has infinitely many representations *under* it, but none *in* it. Space contains infinitely many parts within it. *In words:* only an intuition could be given as infinite.

**B. The transcendental exposition (§3, the Source).**

1. **T1.** Geometry determines the properties of space synthetically and a priori.
2. **T2.** From a mere concept no proposition can be derived that goes beyond the concept. *So* geometry rests on an intuition of space.
3. **T3.** Geometrical principles are necessary, and experience cannot teach necessity. *So* that intuition is pure, prior to any perception of objects.
4. **T4.** An outer intuition that comes before its objects, and fixes in advance how they must be, is possible only if it is the form of the subject's way of being affected.
5. **∴ C.** Space is the form of outer sense, so geometry holds a priori of everything that can appear to us outwardly.

*In words:* the only way to know beforehand how anything you meet must be laid out is that the layout comes from your way of meeting things.

**C. Conclusions (§4).** Space represents no property of things in themselves, and no relation among them. It is the subjective condition of outer intuition. So Kant claims [empirical reality and transcendental ideality](../reference.md#empirical-reality-and-transcendental-ideality) at once: "We maintain, therefore, the empirical reality of space in regard to all possible external experience, although we must admit its transcendental ideality". Everything we can meet outwardly really is in space; abstract from our sensibility and space is nothing ([6.1](06-01-transcendental-idealism-and-the-thing-in-itself.md)).

**Time runs in parallel (§§5–7).** Its metaphysical arguments mirror M1–M4. Time is the form of *inner* sense, and so the formal condition of all appearances, since every representation is a state of the mind. Its transcendental exposition explains the a priori doctrine of motion: only in time can one thing be here and not here, one after the other.

**A further argument: incongruent counterparts** (*Prolegomena* §13). A right hand and its mirror image agree in every internal respect a concept could describe, yet the glove of one will not fit the other. In a 1768 essay Kant had used such cases for Newton's absolute space against Leibniz. Here they serve the new view: the difference shows up only in each hand's relation to the whole of space, and parts possible only through a whole mark an intuition, not things as the understanding alone would know them.

**Where the argument is weakest.** Grant the expositions: space is an a priori form of our intuition. Why must it not *also* be a feature of things in themselves? Early critics, H. A. Pistorius among them, and then F. A. Trendelenburg in the mid-nineteenth century pressed this as the "neglected alternative": space could be both our form *and* real in itself. Kuno Fischer defended Kant, and the dispute ran for decades. Kant's answer is in §4(a): no determination of a thing in itself can be intuited before that thing is given, so a priori knowledge could never be knowledge of things in themselves. The critic replies that this shows only that we could not *know* things in themselves are spatial. It does not show that they are not, yet Kant says space is nothing at all outside our sensibility. Whether that stronger claim is earned is a live dispute that [6.1](06-01-transcendental-idealism-and-the-thing-in-itself.md) takes up.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean: Kant's own theorem).** In M3 Kant cites the principle that in a triangle two sides together exceed the third.

- *Analytic route.* The concept *triangle* is a figure bounded by three straight lines. Nothing in it mentions comparing lengths of sides. Analysis alone never yields the inequality, so the judgment is synthetic ([5.1](05-01-the-critical-question.md)).
- *Empirical route.* Measuring triangles gives "so far, always", not "must"; the judgment is held as necessary.
- *Kant's route.* Construct a triangle in pure intuition. The construction is singular, but nothing in it depends on the lengths you drew, so what you see holds of any triangle that can be given to you.
- *The price.* The theorem is guaranteed for every triangle that can appear to us. It says nothing about how things are in themselves.

**Example 2 (hard: non-Euclidean geometry).** Kant knew only Euclid, and his *Prolegomena* says appearances "can never contain anything but what geometry prescribes to them" (§13, Remark I, Carus trans.). Then came consistent non-Euclidean geometries (Bolyai and Lobachevsky around 1830, Riemann in 1854), and in 1915 general relativity described physical space-time as curved: light-ray triangles near a mass need not have angles summing to two right angles. The responses depend on what Kant is taken to claim.

- **Strong reading.** Every outer appearance, measured physical space included, must be Euclidean. On this reading general relativity is a counterexample.
- **Pure versus physical geometry.** The intuited space of construction is Euclidean a priori; which geometry fits measured light rays and rods is an empirical question. (This is the moderate rationalist's reply in [epistemology 4.1](../../epistemology/lessons/04-01-a-priori-knowledge.md).) Critics answer that it cuts the tie the *Prolegomena* insists on, between pure geometry and appearances.
- **A thinner a priori.** What intuition supplies is less than Euclid's metric. Hermann von Helmholtz (1870) kept space as a form of intuition while making the metric axioms empirical. Hans Reichenbach (1920) proposed a "relativized" a priori: principles that constitute experience within a theory but can change with it.
- **The critics.** Henri Poincaré (*Science and Hypothesis*, 1902) held geometrical axioms to be neither synthetic a priori nor experimental facts but conventions. The later Reichenbach (1928) held that once measurement is defined, physical geometry is simply empirical.

This course does not adjudicate; [`philosophy-of-physics`](../../philosophy-of-physics/syllabus.md) 4.4 argues conventionalism. Notice that M1–M4 never mention Euclid.

## Watch out

- **You might think space and time are two of Kant's categories. Actually** they are forms of *intuition*, on the receptive stem. The categories are pure concepts of the understanding ([5.3](05-03-concepts-and-the-categories.md)). Mixing them up erases the two stems.
- **Anachronism: "Kant says the brain is hard-wired for Euclidean space."** "Hard-wired" is a modern psychological gloss. Kant asks what must hold for experience of objects to be possible, not how a nervous system produces it.
- **You might think transcendental ideality makes bodies illusions. Actually** Kant claims empirical reality for space in the same breath. In §8 he argues the reverse: treating space as a real thing in itself is what would excuse Berkeley for making bodies mere illusion. Whether Kant's view finally differs from Berkeley's is a question for [6.1](06-01-transcendental-idealism-and-the-thing-in-itself.md), not settled here.

## One-liner

> Space and time are the forms of our receiving anything at all, so geometry holds necessarily of everything we can meet, and of nothing as it is in itself.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢) *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)*** Reconstruct. Kant's third argument of the metaphysical exposition of space (Meiklejohn trans., §2):

> Space is no discursive, or as we say, general conception of the relations of things, but a pure intuition. For, in the first place, we can only represent to ourselves one space, and, when we talk of divers spaces, we mean only parts of one and the same space. Moreover, these parts cannot antecede this one all-embracing space, as the component parts from which the aggregate can be made up, but can be cogitated only as existing in it. Space is essentially one, and multiplicity in it, consequently the general notion of spaces, of this or that space, depends solely upon limitations.

(a) Put the argument for "space is not a discursive concept but an intuition" into numbered premises in Kant's terms, making explicit the unstated premise about how a concept relates to what falls under it. (b) A Leibnizian ([2.3](02-03-leibniz-sufficient-reason-and-monads.md)) holds that space is the order of relations among coexisting things. Which of your premises must the Leibnizian deny, and does either side beg the question against the other? Any verdict; 100 words or fewer for (b).

**P2 (🟡) *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)*** Apply to a hard case. An invented lecture note from a physics course:

> "Triangles of light rays near massive bodies have angle sums that differ from two right angles. So geometry is empirical, Kant's synthetic a priori geometry is refuted, and his idealism goes with it: space is a real thing out there after all."

(a) Using T1–T4 and M1–M4 as numbered in this lesson, say which premise the measurement bears on, and what it refutes on the strong reading and on the pure-versus-physical reading. (b) Does "space is a real thing out there" follow? Any verdict; 150 words or fewer for (b).

**P3 (🔴, optional) *(Exegetical.)*** Close reading. Transcendental Aesthetic §4 (Meiklejohn trans.):

> But, with the exception of space, there is no representation, subjective and referring to something external to us, which could be called objective à priori. For there are no other subjective representations from which we can deduce synthetical propositions à priori, as we can from the intuition of space. [...] Therefore, to speak accurately, no ideality whatever belongs to these, although they agree in this respect with the representation of space, that they belong merely to the subjective nature of the mode of sensuous perception; [...] by means of the sensations of colour, sound, and heat, but which, because they are only sensations and not intuitions, do not of themselves give us the cognition of any object [...]. My purpose [...] is merely this: to guard any one against illustrating the asserted ideality of space by examples quite insufficient, for example, by colour, taste, etc.

An invented study guide says: "Kant extends Locke's point about secondary qualities ([3.2](03-02-locke-qualities-substance-and-essence.md)) to space: as the rose's colour is in the mind and not in the rose, so is its shape." (a) What does the passage say space and colour have in common? (b) Name the two differences it draws, and the words that state each. (c) Is the study guide's analogy one Kant accepts? Two sentences per part.

<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b))*

**Accept:** any ordering that makes the unity premise, the part–whole premise and the concept premise explicit and draws the conclusion from them.

**Must hit, strict (a):**

- P1: We can represent only one space; "divers spaces" are parts of that one space.
- P2: The parts do not come before the whole as components it is built from; they are thought only *in* it, as limitations of it.
- P3 (unstated): A discursive concept relates to many representations as instances *under* it, which can each be represented apart from it; it is not a single whole whose parts are given only through it.
- P4: A representation of a single object whose parts are given only through the whole is an intuition. (Kant states this for time in §5.4: what "can only be given by a single object is an intuition".)
- ∴ C: The original representation of space is an intuition, not a discursive concept.

**Must hit, any verdict (b):**

- Locate the denial: the Leibnizian denies P2. For him the related things, and so the particular spatial relations, come first, and "one space" is the order abstracted from them.
- Assess the charge: either Kant begs the question (P2 is just intuitive realism about space's priority), or Kant has independent support (M1: you cannot represent things as beside one another without space already; incongruent counterparts), or the Leibnizian begs it in turn by assuming the relations can be represented without space.

**Wrong turns:** leaving P3 implicit, so that "one space" seems to prove "not a concept" by itself (a concept like *the sun* also applies to one thing). Making the Leibnizian deny P1: Leibniz agrees there is one order of coexistence.

**Model answer (b), one of several:** The Leibnizian denies P2. The relations among things are prior, and the one space is the order we abstract from them. Taken alone, P2 looks question-begging: it asserts the priority the Leibnizian denies. But Kant does not rest on it alone. M1 argues that to represent things as beside one another at all we already need space, so the Leibnizian's "relations among things" are already spatial relations. Whether that is a fair point or begs the question in turn is the real dispute.

---

**P2** *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b))*

**Must hit, strict (a):**

- The measurement bears on **T1**, the claim that geometry (Kant's Euclidean geometry) determines the properties of space a priori, and through it on the transcendental exposition's support for C.
- Strong reading: Kant claimed that every outer appearance, measured space included, must be Euclidean. The measurement refutes that.
- Pure versus physical reading: only the empirical claim that physical space is Euclidean is refuted; pure geometry in intuition is untouched. The cost is the *Prolegomena*'s claim that appearances contain only what geometry prescribes.
- M1–M4 do not depend on which geometry is true.

**Must hit, any verdict (b):**

- Separate "geometry is empirical" from "space is a thing in itself". Kant already held that space is empirically real, so measurable structure is no news to him.
- Say what is actually lost if the strong reading falls: the transcendental exposition's route to ideality via T1. Then say whether the metaphysical exposition (or a thinner a priori) still supports ideality.

**Wrong turns:** treating the measurement as refuting the whole Aesthetic, M1–M4 included. Saying Kant would accept that space is a real thing because it is "empirically real": empirical reality is not reality in itself.

**Model answer (b), one of several:** It does not follow. At most the measurement shows that the metric of physical space is learned by experience. That removes T1 on the strong reading, and with it the transcendental exposition's argument for ideality. But "space is a real thing out there" is ambiguous. In the empirical sense Kant already holds it. In the sense of a feature of things in themselves, nothing in a measurement reaches it, since every measurement is of appearances. What the critic can fairly claim is that the case for ideality now rests on M1–M4 alone, and those were always the contested part.

---

**P3** *(Exegetical, strict)*

**Must hit, strict:**

- (a) Both "belong merely to the subjective nature of the mode of sensuous perception": both depend on how we are affected.
- (b) First difference: space is a representation from which "we can deduce synthetical propositions à priori"; colour yields none. Second: colours "are only sensations and not intuitions", so they do not "give us the cognition of any object".
- (c) No. Kant names exactly this illustration as one he wants "to guard any one against": colour is "quite insufficient" to illustrate the ideality of space, and "to speak accurately, no ideality whatever belongs to" sensations in his sense.

**Wrong turns:** denying that the passage grants any common ground; it grants subjectivity. Reading "no ideality whatever belongs to these" as "colours are real properties of the rose": the point is that they lack the a priori, objective role that makes space's ideality *transcendental*.

**Model answer:** (a) Both depend on the subjective make-up of our sensibility. (b) Space yields synthetic a priori propositions and colour does not; and colours "are only sensations and not intuitions", giving no cognition of any object. (c) No: Kant writes this remark precisely to stop readers illustrating the ideality of space by colour, which he calls "quite insufficient".

## Flashback

**From Lesson [4.5](04-05-reid-common-sense-against-the-way-of-ideas.md) (Reid: common sense against the way of ideas):** *(Exegetical.)* Close reading. Reid, *Inquiry* V.3, "Of Natural Signs" (1818 edition):

> The first class of natural signs comprehends those whose connection with the thing signified is established by nature, but discovered only by experience. [...] What we commonly call natural causes, might [...] be called natural signs [...]; and all we can certainly affirm is, that nature hath established a constant conjunction between them and the things called their effects [...]. A third class of natural signs comprehends those which, though we never before had any notion or conception of the things signified, do suggest it [...], and at once give us a conception, and create a belief of it.

(a) Press your hand on a table. In which class is the sensation, taken as a sign of the table's hardness, and which words of the passage keep it out of the first class? Two sentences. (b) The first-class sentences sound like Hume. Name two points on which they agree with Hume's account of cause, and say why the third class is something Hume's copy principle cannot admit. Three sentences.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):**

- The third class. Reid says next that the notion of hardness, and the belief in it, are got "in a similar manner", annexed to the sensation by an original principle of our nature.
- First-class signs link a sign to a thing signified that experience *discovers* the connection with, so both must already be conceived. Hardness is something of which "we never before had any notion or conception" until the sensation suggested it, so the sensation gives us the conception itself, and the belief, "at once".

**Must hit, strict (b):**

- Agreements (any two): in natural causes we can affirm only a constant conjunction, Hume's "conjoined, but never connected" and his first definition of cause; we know of no efficiency in them ("all we can certainly affirm"); and these connections are discovered only by experience, not by reason.
- The copy principle requires every simple idea to be copied from a preceding impression it resembles. A conception suggested by a sensation it does not resemble, of something never before conceived, is for Hume either a copy of some impression or no idea at all. Reid's third class is exactly the case the copy principle rules out, and it is the doctrine of 4.5's premise 5.

**Wrong turns:** putting the hardness sensation in the first class because we learn by experience that pressing tables feels firm. What is learned that way is a connection between things already conceived; the conception of hardness is not learned. Reading the first-class sentences as Reid accepting Hume's view of causation everywhere. The claim is about natural, bodily causes; Reid keeps real efficiency for agents with active power.

**Model answer:** (a) The third class: the sensation suggests a conception of hardness we "never before had", and gives us that conception and the belief in it at once. First-class signs only reveal, through experience, a connection between things already conceived. (b) Like Hume, Reid says that in natural causes we can affirm only a constant conjunction, with no efficiency known, and that experience alone discovers the connections. But the copy principle makes every simple idea a copy of an impression it resembles. A conception suggested by a sensation it does not resemble, of something never conceived before, is precisely what that principle says cannot exist.
