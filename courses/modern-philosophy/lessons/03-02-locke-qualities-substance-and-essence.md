# Modern Philosophy · Lesson 3.2: Locke: qualities, substance and essence

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 3: Locke and Berkeley · Builds on: [3.1 Locke: against innate ideas](03-01-locke-against-innate-ideas.md), [1.2 The cogito and the wax](01-02-the-cogito-and-the-wax.md) · Unlocks: [3.4 Berkeley: immaterialism](03-04-berkeley-immaterialism.md)

## Why this matters

[3.1](03-01-locke-against-innate-ideas.md) gave Locke a mind stocked only by sensation and reflection. Now he asks what those ideas tell us about bodies. His answer is three verdicts in one book. Some of our ideas resemble their causes and some do not. The "substance" under the qualities is a support we suppose but cannot describe. And the inner constitution that makes gold gold is hidden from us, so we sort things by ideas of our own making. Each verdict became a target. Berkeley took the first ([3.4](03-04-berkeley-immaterialism.md)), and Kant's idealism was announced as Locke's treatment of colour extended to everything.

## The idea

Locke wrote the *Essay* (1689, dated 1690 on the title page) as a self-described labourer clearing ground for the scientists. In the Epistle to the Reader he names Boyle and Sydenham, Huygens and Newton. The science he took over was the [corpuscular hypothesis](../reference.md#corpuscular-hypothesis), most fully set out by Robert Boyle in *The Origin of Forms and Qualities* (1666). Bodies are made of particles too small to see. The particles have size, shape, motion and solidity and nothing else, and they act only by pushing.

Run a red porphyry slab through that picture. Its particles have sizes, shapes and an arrangement: their *texture*. When light bounces off that texture into your eye, motions run up the nerves and you have an idea of red. Now ask what in the slab is like your idea. The shape of the slab is, since a square in the mind and a square in the stone are both square. But nothing in a texture of particles is *like* redness as you see it. What the stone has is a texture apt to produce that idea in you: a **power**. This is the distinction of [primary and secondary qualities](../reference.md#primary-and-secondary-qualities).

The same diagnosis then runs deeper. Ask what holds the qualities together and you reach a "support" you cannot picture. Ask what makes this lump gold and you reach a constitution you cannot see. Locke's position throughout is the same. There is something there, and our ideas do not reach it.

## Source

Locke, *Essay* II.viii.14–15 (Gutenberg text, which follows the standard section numbers). Section 14 has just extended to tastes and sounds what Locke said of colours and smells. The opening "which" refers to all of these.

> [...] which, whatever reality we by mistake attribute to them, are in truth nothing in the objects themselves, but powers to produce various sensations in us [...]. From whence I think it easy to draw this observation,—that the ideas of primary qualities of bodies are resemblances of them, and their patterns do really exist in the bodies themselves, but the ideas produced in us by these secondary qualities have no resemblance of them at all. There is nothing like our ideas, existing in the bodies themselves. They are, in the bodies we denominate from them, only a power to produce those sensations in us: and what is sweet, blue, or warm in idea, is but the certain bulk, figure, and motion of the insensible parts, in the bodies themselves, which we call so.

Read the grammar before the doctrine. "Nothing in the objects themselves, but powers" means *nothing except* powers. The same clause denies something is in the object and locates something there. "There is nothing like our ideas" denies **resemblance**, not existence. And "what is sweet, blue, or warm *in idea*" shows that a colour word has two jobs, naming an idea in us and a power in the thing.

Readers split over which job Locke means when he denies colour to bodies. The **powers reading** cites §8 (a quality *is* the power to produce an idea), §14, and §23's definition of secondary qualities as powers. The **in-the-mind reading** cites §17 (light and colours are no more in fire or snow than sickness is in manna) and §19, where the porphyry has no colour in the dark. Notice §17's qualifier: colours vanish *as particular ideas*. And §8 warns that when Locke speaks of ideas as in things he means the qualities that cause them. Which reading the chapter better supports is [Boss 3](../syllabus.md)'s question, so it is left open here.

## The argument

**A. Qualities** (II.viii; section numbers as in the Gutenberg text, whose §10 is a placeholder).

1. **Every particle of matter, however divided, keeps solidity, extension, figure and mobility (§9).** *In words:* grind a grain of wheat to dust and each speck still has these. These are the **primary** qualities.
2. **Bodies act on one another, and on our organs, only by impulse (§11).** *In words:* no action at a distance, only pushing.
3. **So bodies produce ideas in us only through the bulk, figure and motion of particles, carried by the nerves to the brain (§§12–13).**
4. **Ideas of colour, taste and warmth contain nothing of bulk, figure or motion (§25), and God has annexed them to motions they in no way resemble, as pain is annexed to the motion of a blade cutting flesh (§13).**
5. **A circle or square is the same in the idea as in the body (§18).**
6. **∴ Ideas of primary qualities resemble something in bodies. Ideas of secondary qualities resemble nothing there; what corresponds to them is a power grounded in the primary qualities (§§14–15).** This is the [resemblance thesis](../reference.md#resemblance-thesis).

The famous cases follow the conclusion. Fire warms at one distance and pains at another, yet nobody puts the pain in the fire (§16). Manna (§18), a sweet white purgative, is the next case: it makes us sick, and nobody puts the sickness in the manna either. The porphyry is colourless in the dark (§19), and water feels hot to one hand and cold to the other (§21). Locke opens §21 with "Ideas being thus distinguished and understood, we may be able to give an account how" the water case works. So the theory explains the cases, and the cases do not prove the theory. §23 adds a third sort, powers to change other bodies, such as the sun's power to whiten wax.

**B. Substance** (II.xxiii.1–4). We find simple ideas going constantly together. We cannot conceive how they could subsist alone or in one another, so we suppose a [substratum](../reference.md#substratum) they subsist in. Our only idea of it is relational: whatever supports them. Asked what it is, we are like the Indian whose world rested on an elephant, the elephant on a tortoise, and the tortoise on something he could not name (§2). Locke denies that we have a clear idea of substance. He does not deny that there is one. In his replies to Bishop Stillingfleet in the late 1690s he insisted he had never discarded it.

**C. Essence** (III.iii.15; III.vi.2, 9, 35). The **real essence** of gold is the constitution of its insensible parts on which its observable qualities depend. The **nominal essence** is the complex idea the word stands for: yellow, heavy, malleable, fusible, fixed. We sort by the nominal essence, because the real one is hidden. We see it no better than a countryman gazing at the Strasbourg clock sees its wheels (§9). Hence §35: "men make sorts of things". That is [real and nominal essence](../reference.md#real-and-nominal-essence).

**Where the argument is weakest.** Premise 5 and Book IV share one exposure. Locke grants that the mind knows things only through its ideas (IV.iv.3), and asks himself: "How shall the mind, when it perceives nothing but its own ideas, know that they agree with things themselves?" If only ideas are perceived, the resemblance in premise 5 can never be checked by comparison. Berkeley pressed this as the claim that an idea can be like nothing but an idea ([3.4](03-04-berkeley-immaterialism.md)). Jonathan Bennett later named the picture the "veil of perception" (*Locke, Berkeley, Hume: Central Themes*, 1971). Defenders reply that Locke never claims to compare. Resemblance is a conclusion of the corpuscular hypothesis, which Locke calls the most intelligible account while declining to rule on its truth (IV.iii.16). His IV.iv.4 answer is that simple ideas, which the mind cannot make, conform to their causes as regular effects. Since A. D. Woozley (1964), readers have also asked whether Locke holds the picture theory at all.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean: a cherry in the cellar).** At noon a cherry gives you ideas of round, red and sweet. At midnight, in the cellar, it gives you nothing. Run Locke's account. Its roundness and the size and arrangement of its particles are there at midnight, and your idea of round resembles them (primary). Its redness, *as an idea*, is nowhere at midnight. But the texture that would produce redness when light returns is there all along: that is the power, the secondary quality (§19). Its juice can stain a linen cloth, a power to change another body, which is the third sort (§23). Whether "the cherry is red at midnight" is true depends on whether "red" names the idea or the power: the reading dispute in the Source.

**Example 2 (hard: gold that will not take the hammer).** Locke's own case (III.vi.35): a body turns up with every quality of gold except malleability. Is it gold? Locke says it depends whose abstract idea you consult. It is gold to anyone whose nominal essence leaves out malleability, and not to anyone whose idea includes it. He adds that refiners already know brittle gold. Leibniz's *New Essays* (written around 1704, published 1765) answers point by point (III.vi.35, Langley's translation): "Not at all; they would determine only the name." Such a find would teach us that malleability is not necessarily connected to gold's other qualities, which is a fact about nature. And brittle gold, says Leibniz, owes its brittleness to admixtures that the refiner's tests remove. Here is the **crux**. Does a sort-name stand only for our abstract idea (Locke)? Or does it aim, through provisional definitions, at the hidden constitution itself (Leibniz)? Locke calls it an abuse of words to make the word gold stand for a real essence nobody knows (III.x.17–18). Leibniz replies that ordinary usage is right to intend it. The later causal theory of natural-kind terms is a modern gloss on Leibniz's side, and it belongs to [`philosophy-of-language-and-logic`](../../philosophy-of-language-and-logic/syllabus.md) 3.3.

## Watch out

- **"Tertiary qualities" is not Locke's phrase.** You might think Locke has three kinds of quality named primary, secondary and tertiary. His §23 lists three sorts of qualities and calls the second and third alike only powers. "Tertiary" is a later label.
- **Anachronism: the "veil of ideas" and "sense data".** Both are later critics' vocabulary. A Lockean idea is whatever the mind perceives in itself, the immediate object of thought (II.viii.8), and it covers sensations, images and concepts alike ([3.1](03-01-locke-against-innate-ideas.md)). Whether Lockean ideas are objects we look at or the acts by which we perceive things is the dispute that the [veil of ideas](../reference.md#veil-of-ideas) label presupposes, so it cannot be used to settle it.
- **Locke did not deny substance.** You might read the elephant and the tortoise as a refutation. It is a complaint about the clarity of our idea. Locke goes on to use "substance" to frame personal identity ([3.3](03-03-locke-personal-identity.md)).

## One-liner

> Bodies have size, shape and motion like our ideas of them, and powers unlike our ideas of colour and taste. Under both lie a support and a constitution that are really there and that no idea of ours reaches.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢) *(Exegetical.)*** Close reading. *Essay* II.xiii.19 (Gutenberg), from the chapter on space. Locke has just argued that calling God, spirits and bodies all "substance" hides three different ideas under one word.

> They who first ran into the notion of accidents, as a sort of real beings that needed something to inhere in, were forced to find out the word substance to support them. Had the poor Indian philosopher [...] but thought of this word substance, he needed not to have been at the trouble to find an elephant to support it, and a tortoise to support his elephant [...]. And he that inquired might have taken it for as good an answer from an Indian philosopher,—that substance, without knowing what it is, is that which supports the earth, as take it for a sufficient answer and good doctrine from our European philosophers,—that substance, without knowing what it is, is that which supports accidents. So that of substance, we have no idea of what it is, but only a confused obscure one of what it does.

(a) Whose doctrine is the target of the joke, and what exactly is said to be wrong with it? (b) From the last sentence, what does Locke deny we have, and what does he grant we have? (c) Does the passage say there is no substance? Name the words that decide it. Two sentences per part.

**P2 (🟡) *(Exegetical (a)–(b) · Evaluative (c).)*** Apply to a hard case (invented). In 1700 an Amsterdam assayer receives bars that pass every test in the guild's manual: colour, weight, fusibility, fixedness in the fire, malleability and solubility in aqua regia. He stamps them gold. In 1720 a new test, unknown in 1700, separates them from all coin gold. The assayer writes: "We were deceived. They were never gold."

(a) On Locke's account (III.vi), were the bars gold in 1700, and what decides it? (b) What would Leibniz say, and name the single premise on which he and Locke split. (c) Can Locke make sense of "we were deceived" on his own premises? Any verdict; 120 words or fewer for (c).

**P3 (🔴, optional) *(Exegetical.)*** Diagnose. An invented study guide says:

> "Locke proves that secondary qualities are subjective with his hot-and-cold water experiment: since the same water feels hot to one hand and cold to the other, heat cannot be in the water. It follows that colours, tastes and sounds are illusions. Snow is not really white; it only seems white to us."

Name two errors, and for each point to the words in II.viii (or IV.iv) that show it. 100 words or fewer.

<details>
<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical, strict.)*

**Must hit, strict (a):**

- The target is "our European philosophers", the scholastic doctrine of accidents as "real beings" that need a subject to inhere in.
- The fault is that "substance" is a word filling a gap. It names a role (whatever supports accidents) without saying what fills it. As an answer it is exactly as good as the Indian's: "substance, without knowing what it is", supports accidents just as it supports the earth.

**Must hit, strict (b):**

- Denied: any idea of *what substance is*.
- Granted: "a confused obscure" idea of *what it does*, namely support. The idea is relational and functional.

**Must hit, strict (c):**

- No. Locke's complaint is about our *idea*, and about a *word* pressed into service as an answer (the scholastics "were forced to find out the word substance").
- "We have no idea of what it is" is a claim about knowledge, and granting an idea "of what it does" presupposes a supporter. The passage is consistent with II.xxiii's supposed support and with Locke's later insistence to Stillingfleet that he never discarded substance.

**Wrong turns:** reading the passage as Berkeley's or Hume's conclusion that there is no substance. Taking the Indian to be Locke's target, when he is the foil and the European philosophers are the target.

**Model answer:** (a) The scholastics, who made accidents into real beings needing something to inhere in. Their "substance" is a word that answers "what supports them?" without saying what it is, and is no better than the elephant. (b) He denies we know what substance is. He grants an obscure idea of what it does, namely support. (c) No. The words "we have no idea of what it is" concern our idea, and "of what it does" assumes something doing it.

---

**P2** *(Exegetical (a)–(b) · Evaluative (c).)*

**Must hit, strict (a):**

- Yes, for anyone whose nominal essence of gold is the guild's list. The bars matched it, and on III.vi.35 the abstract idea the name is annexed to "makes the species".
- What decides it is the nominal essence, not the real essence, which nobody in 1700 (or 1720) knows (III.vi.9).

**Must hit, strict (b):**

- Leibniz: they were never gold. The tests were a provisional definition, and the name aimed at the hidden constitution. The new test refines the definition and does not change the species (Leibniz imagines just this, a counterfeit gold caught by a later test, in *New Essays* III.vi).
- Crux: whether a sort-name signifies only our abstract idea (Locke) or intends the real essence behind it (Leibniz). Locke calls that intention an abuse of words (III.x.17–18).

**Must hit, any verdict (c):**

- State what Locke must say: in 1720 the guild adds a quality to its nominal essence, so "never gold" is true only relative to the new idea.
- Then test it. Does that capture what "deceived" means, or does the assayer's sense of having been *wrong*, rather than of having changed his definition, favour Leibniz? Or can Locke explain it as a discovery that the qualities he used were not reliable signs of the others?

**Wrong turns:** answering (a) with "no, because real essence decides". Locke says we cannot sort by it. Using modern chemistry to answer (c). The question is what Locke's premises allow.

**Model answer (c), one of several:** Locke can say something. In 1720 the guild learns that the six qualities do not always go together with a seventh, and it enlarges its nominal essence. "Never gold" then means "never gold by our improved idea". What he cannot easily say is that the 1700 stamp was *false*, since it matched the idea then annexed to the name. The assayer's "deceived" sounds like a discovery about the bars, not a revision of a word. That favours Leibniz's reading of usage, unless Locke reinterprets "deceived" as having trusted signs that turned out to be imperfect. Either verdict passes if it states Locke's commitment and names this strain.

---

**P3** *(Exegetical, strict.)*

**Accept:** any two of the three errors below, each tied to the text.

**Must hit, strict:**

- **The water case is not the proof.** §21 opens "Ideas being thus distinguished and understood, we may be able to give an account how" the water feels hot and cold. The case is *explained* by the distinction, whose grounds are the corpuscular account of §§9–13.
- **Not illusions.** Secondary qualities are real powers in bodies (§14: "nothing ... but powers"; §23). Simple ideas of them "exactly" answer those powers and have "all the real conformity" knowledge needs (IV.iv.4). The error Locke corrects is thinking the idea *resembles* something in the body (§15), not believing the body causes it.
- **"Snow is not really white" is ambiguous.** Snow really has the power to produce the idea of white (§8 uses the snowball as its example). What it lacks is anything *like* the idea.

**Wrong turns:** accepting "subjective" as a fair gloss. The power is in the snow whether or not anyone perceives it. Calling the relativity argument Berkeley's and therefore wrong for Locke. Berkeley does lean on it ([3.4](03-04-berkeley-immaterialism.md)), but the error here is about Locke's order of argument.

**Model answer:** First, the water case is not Locke's proof. §21 introduces it as something the distinction, once made, lets us "give an account" of, and the grounds are corpuscular (§§9–13). Second, colours are not illusions. They are powers really in bodies (§14), and our ideas of them really conform to those powers (IV.iv.4). What is false is that the idea resembles anything in the snow (§15).

## Flashback

**From Lesson [2.4](02-04-interaction-and-causation.md) (The problem they left: interaction and causation):** *(Exegetical.)* Diagnose. An invented forum post:

> *Malebranche in five claims.* (i) Malebranche denies that we are causes of our arm's motion in any sense; "cause" is a word for God alone. (ii) His problem is Elisabeth's: mind and body are too unlike to act on each other, though one body really can push another. (iii) Since God moves everything by a fresh act at each moment, nature has no fixed laws, and tomorrow anything might happen. (iv) God could easily have given creatures real power of their own; Malebranche just thinks he chose not to. (v) His test for a true cause is about what the mind perceives: a cause is true only if the mind sees that the effect must follow from it.

Which claims misread Malebranche? For each, name the error and the text or step of his argument that corrects it. One sentence per claim.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict:**

- (i) Wrong. We are the "natural" or occasional cause of the arm's motion (*Search* VI.ii.3: "we may be call'd, in that sense, the natural cause"); what we are not is its *true* cause.
- (ii) Wrong. The test fails body acting on body just as badly: our idea of body is extension, which contains no force, so a hammer is only the occasion on which God moves the nail.
- (iii) Wrong. God acts by general volitions, which constitute the order of nature, so occasionalists expect the same regularities as anyone else; they dispute where the efficacy lies, not whether nature is orderly.
- (iv) Wrong. To give a creature power, God would have to will that a body move whenever the creature wills, so God's will would still carry the necessity and the creature's would still be the occasion. Malebranche says it "seems to imply a Contradiction" that men should be true causes.
- (v) Right. "A true Cause is that betwixt which and its Effect, the Mind perceives a necessary connexion", offered as a stipulation ("for so I understand it").

**Wrong turns:** marking (v) wrong because necessary connection sounds like a fact about things, not about the mind. Malebranche's definition is framed in what the mind perceives. Marking (ii) right because Malebranche does answer Elisabeth; he answers her by turning her question into one about every cause.

**Model answer:** (i) misreads the distinction between natural and true causes: we are natural or occasional causes of our arm's motion, just not true ones. (ii) narrows the problem to unlike substances, when Malebranche's test denies that bodies act on bodies too. (iii) ignores God's general volitions, which make the laws of nature. (iv) misses the argument that a power God gave would still be God's will acting on an occasion, so finite true causes seem contradictory. (v) is correct.

</details>

## Connections

- **Backward:** the "ideas of sensation" being analysed are those of [3.1](03-01-locke-against-innate-ideas.md). Descartes's wax ([1.2](01-02-the-cogito-and-the-wax.md)) had already stripped the colour and smell from what the mind grasps of a body, though by intellect rather than by corpuscular science. Locke's God who annexes ideas to motions they do not resemble (§13) touches the causal puzzle of [2.4](02-04-interaction-and-causation.md), and Locke declines to resolve it.

- **Sideways:** substratum and essence as live positions are [`metaphysics` 2.1](../../metaphysics/lessons/02-01-substance-and-accident.md) and [2.4](../../metaphysics/lessons/02-04-essence-and-grounding.md).
