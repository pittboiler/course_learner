# Modern Philosophy · Lesson 5.5: The Second Analogy: Kant's reply to Hume

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 5: Kant I: the conditions of experience · Builds on: [4.2 Causation and necessary connection](04-02-causation-and-necessary-connection.md), [5.3 Concepts and the categories](05-03-concepts-and-the-categories.md), [5.4 The transcendental deduction](05-04-the-transcendental-deduction.md) · Unlocks: [6.1 Transcendental idealism and the thing in itself](06-01-transcendental-idealism-and-the-thing-in-itself.md), [6.2 The Dialectic](06-02-the-soul-and-the-antinomies.md)

## Why this matters

Hume looked for the impression of [necessary connection](../reference.md#necessary-connection) and found only the mind's felt passage from an object to its usual attendant ([4.2](04-02-causation-and-necessary-connection.md)). In the *Prolegomena* preface (headed "Introduction" in Carus's translation) Kant reports Hume's verdict: the concept of cause is the offspring of imagination, which "mistook the subjective necessity of habit for an objective necessity arising from insight." The Second Analogy of the *Critique of Pure Reason* (A189/B232 ff.) is Kant's answer for cause in particular. Habit cannot be the source, he argues, because without the concept of cause there would be no experience of anything *happening*, and so no sequences for habit to work on. Whether causation is regularity or power is argued live in [`metaphysics` 4.1](../../metaphysics/lessons/04-01-humes-challenge-and-the-regularity-theory.md). Here: what the argument is, and what it does and does not prove.

## The idea

Walk around a house: roof, walls, foundation. Watch a ship drift downstream. Both are successions of perceptions, since you always take in a manifold part by part. Yet you judge that the house's parts coexist and the ship's positions succeed. What tells you? Not a comparison with time itself, since time cannot be perceived: there is no clock in the sky against which to date your perceptions.

Kant's answer starts from a difference. In the house case you could have run the series the other way; in the ship case you could not. The ship's order *binds* your apprehension. But your perceptions are yours, and nothing in them says *this order and no other*. The binding must come from the object. For an appearance, as opposed to a thing in itself, being an object comes to this: the manifold stands under a rule that fixes how it must be connected ([5.4](05-04-the-transcendental-deduction.md)). To take the ship's lower position as *following* its upper one, rather than merely being perceived after it, is to take it as determined by something in the preceding state according to a rule. A rule by which one state determines the next in time is the concept of cause, under its [schema](../reference.md#schematism) from [5.3](05-03-concepts-and-the-categories.md): succession subject to a rule.

So the order of explanation runs the other way from Hume's. Hume: perceive sequences, repeat them, form a habit, project necessity. Kant: there is no perceived sequence *in the object* until the causal rule is already at work. The *Prolegomena* (§30) sums up the method: the categories are vindicated "not by deriving them from experience, but by deriving experience from them."

The Second Analogy is one of three [Analogies of Experience](../reference.md#analogies-of-experience), one for each mode of time. Substance persists through change (permanence), every alteration has a cause (succession), and coexisting substances act on each other (coexistence). Their shared principle: experience is possible only through the representation of a necessary connection of perceptions. The second, in Meiklejohn's translation of the 1787 text:

> All changes take place according to the law of the connection of Cause and Effect.

The 1781 wording, paraphrased: everything that happens presupposes something on which it follows according to a rule.

## Source

Kant, *Critique of Pure Reason*, Transcendental Analytic, "Second Analogy" (Meiklejohn, public domain). Kant has just said that apprehending an event is one perception following another, which is equally true of the house.

> For example, I see a ship float down the stream of a river. My perception of its place lower down follows upon my perception of its place higher up the course of the river, and it is impossible that, in the apprehension of this phenomenon, the vessel should be perceived first below and afterwards higher up the stream. Here, therefore, the order in the sequence of perceptions in apprehension is determined; and by this order apprehension is regulated. In the former example, my perceptions in the apprehension of a house might begin at the roof and end at the foundation, or vice versa; or I might apprehend the manifold in this empirical intuition, by going from left to right, and from right to left. Accordingly, in the series of these perceptions, there was no determined order, which necessitated my beginning at a certain point, in order empirically to connect the manifold.

Three things to notice. "It is impossible" is said of *apprehension*: so far the claim concerns perceptions, not causes. "By this order apprehension is regulated": the event dictates to the perceiver, not the reverse. And "no determined order" for the house does not make the house disordered. Its parts have places in space; what they lack is a before and after. The next paragraph draws the moral:

> I must, therefore, in the present case, deduce the subjective sequence of apprehension from the objective sequence of phenomena

So what fixes the event's order?

## The argument

The [Second Analogy](../reference.md#second-analogy), B-edition proof, in Kant's terms.

1. **Apprehension of any manifold is always successive.** *In words:* house or ship, perceptions come one after another.
2. **Time itself cannot be perceived.**
3. **So succession in apprehension leaves it undetermined whether the states succeed one another in the object.** *In words:* from the perceptions alone, house and ship look alike.
4. **To represent an [objective succession](../reference.md#objective-succession), an event, is to represent an order of states that binds apprehension: B cannot be apprehended before A.**
5. **For appearances, an order binding on apprehension can come only from a rule: something in the preceding state on which the later state follows always and necessarily.** *In words:* if neither time nor the perceptions fix the order, a rule must.
6. **A rule by which a preceding state determines a following one in time is the concept of cause and effect, schematized as succession subject to a rule.**
7. **∴ Experience of any event is possible only if all alterations of appearances stand under the law of cause and effect.** *In words:* "every event has a cause" is a condition of experiencing events at all, so it holds a priori of every object of possible experience, and only of those.

Premise 5 applies the deduction's thesis ([5.4](05-04-the-transcendental-deduction.md)) to time order: for us, an object is whatever in the manifold makes a rule necessary.

**The reply to Hume.** Kant grants Hume half his result: no necessity shows up in perceptions, and reason cannot see why one thing's existence requires another's (*Prolegomena* §27). He denies the other half. Hume assumed we first perceive successions of objects, then get the idea of necessity from repeating them. If Kant is right, that first step already uses the concept, so repetition cannot be its source.

**What it does not show.** The principle, not particular laws. The B Deduction says particular laws stand under the categories but cannot be wholly derived from them; experience must be added to know them (B165). The Second Analogy says that this water's boiling follows by some rule on something in the preceding state. Which state, and by what law, is for physics to find. Whether that leaves the induction problem of [4.3](04-03-induction-and-the-sceptical-solution.md) untouched is disputed and left for the module's boss problem. The principle also holds of appearances only, which is what leaves room for the freedom of the third antinomy ([6.2](06-02-the-soul-and-the-antinomies.md)).

**Where the argument is weakest.** The step from 4 to 5. Arthur Lovejoy ("On Kant's Reply to Hume", 1906) had argued, roughly, that we tell a moving ship from a standing house without appeal to causal law. P. F. Strawson (*The Bounds of Sense*, 1966) called the step a gross non sequitur, a slide between two necessities. Given that the ship did move downstream, my perceptions *had* to come in that order. That is a conditional necessity, which follows from the description of the event. It is not the necessity with which the lower position follows from the earlier state by causal law. Defenders reply that Kant is not inferring causation from irreversibility. Henry Allison (*Kant's Transcendental Idealism*, 1983) reads the argument as asking what it takes to *judge* a succession objective when time cannot be perceived. On that reading irreversibility is how such a judgment shows up in apprehension, and the rule is what makes the judgment possible. Paul Guyer (*Kant and the Claims of Knowledge*, 1987) reads it epistemically: we can know an objective succession only through a causal law governing it. That reading saves the step, at the price of a strong claim: dating events requires knowing laws.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean: cups and cooling tea).** Look along three cups on a shelf, left to right, then back. Then watch tea in one cup go from steaming to still. Both are successive apprehensions (step 1), and nothing in either series of perceptions marks it as an event (step 3). But you could have looked right to left. You could not have perceived the still tea first and the steaming tea after, given that it cooled (step 4). To take the cooling as something that *happened* is to give the still state a place after the steaming one that no choice of yours could reverse. Only a rule can give it that place: something in the earlier state (hot tea, cooler air) on which the later state follows (steps 5–7). Notice what you do *not* get. The principle does not say the cause was heat loss to the air, still less Newton's law of cooling. Those come from experience, under the principle. And "given that it cooled" is exactly Strawson's conditional necessity.

**Example 2 (hard: the falling tile).** Arthur Schopenhauer (*On the Fourfold Root of the Principle of Sufficient Reason*, §23 of the 1847 edition) granted that every change has a cause but denied that causality is our only way of knowing an order as objective. Things can follow *upon* one another without following *from* one another. A tile falls just as I step out of my house; day follows night. Each order is objective and not up to me, yet neither item causes the other. A Kantian reply: premise 5 says the later state follows *something in the preceding state* by a rule, not that the earlier perceived item is its cause. The tile's fall is fixed in time by its own antecedents, my exit by mine, and both belong to one causally connected nature. The reply has a cost. The "something" may lie anywhere in the preceding state of the world, so in any given case the principle says very little, which is the point of "What it does not show".

## Watch out

- **You might think Kant proves that everything whatever has a cause, but actually** the principle holds only of appearances. The *Prolegomena* (§30) says that the pure concepts have no signification when referred to things in themselves.
- **You might think Kant agrees with Hume that necessity is "in the mind", but actually** the two locate it differently. For Hume, necessity is a feeling produced by repetition. For Kant, it is a rule of the understanding that is a condition of there being objects of experience at all, valid before any repetition. In *Prolegomena* §27 Kant expressly denies that it is an illusion bred by long habit. "Mind-imposed structure" and "projection" are modern glosses; name them as such if you use them.
- **"Regulative" here is not the Dialectic's sense.** Kant calls the Analogies regulative, as against the constitutive mathematical principles. He means that they cannot construct the related term. An analogy gives a priori the *relation* to a fourth member, not the fourth member itself. The [regulative use](../reference.md#regulative-and-constitutive) of the ideas of reason in [6.2](06-02-the-soul-and-the-antinomies.md) is a different doctrine.

## One-liner

> Hume: we perceive sequences, and habit teaches us necessity. Kant: without a rule fixing what follows what, there would be no sequence in objects to perceive.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢) *(Exegetical.)*** Close reading. Kant, *Critique*, Second Analogy, near its end (Meiklejohn).

> The principle of the connection of causality among phenomena is limited in our formula to the succession thereof, although in practice we find [...] that cause and effect may be simultaneous. [...] Here it must be specially remembered that we must consider the order of time and not the lapse thereof. The relation remains, even though no time has elapsed. [...] If, for example, I consider a leaden ball, which lies upon a cushion and makes a hollow in it, as a cause, then it is simultaneous with the effect. But I distinguish the two through the relation of time of the dynamical connection of both. For if I lay the ball upon the cushion, then the hollow follows upon the before smooth surface; but supposing the cushion has, from some cause or another, a hollow, there does not thereupon follow a leaden ball.

(a) What distinction lets Kant keep a principle stated for succession when cause and effect are simultaneous? (b) In the ball case, what fixes which is cause and which effect, and how does that test differ from the ship test in the Source? Two sentences per part.

**P2 (🟡) *(Exegetical.)*** Diagnose. An invented study note:

> "The Second Analogy refutes Hume. Hume said necessity is only in the mind; Kant proves a priori that effects follow necessarily from their causes. So we can know without any experiment that heating water boils it, as surely as we know 7 + 5 = 12. Still, Kant only moves necessity from habit to the categories, which are also in the mind, so in the end he agrees with Hume about where necessity lives."

Name the two misreadings and cite the text that corrects each. 120 words or fewer.

**P3 (🔴, optional) *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)*** Steelman and reply. (a) Using the ship, state the two necessities Strawson's charge says Kant runs together. (b) Give the strongest Kantian reply, and say whether it answers the charge or relocates the burden to another premise. Any verdict. 150 words or fewer in total.

<details>
<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical, strict.)*

**Must hit, strict (a):**

- The **order** of time versus its **lapse**. The interval between cause and effect may vanish, but which one is determined by which still has a place in time ("the relation remains, even though no time has elapsed").

**Must hit, strict (b):**

- The asymmetry of dependence. Lay the ball on the cushion and a hollow follows; a cushion that already has a hollow is not followed by a ball. That is the "dynamical connection".
- The difference from the ship. There Kant read the order off an irreversible series of perceptions across an interval. Here there is no interval to watch, so the order is fixed by what follows when each state is posited. It is still a criterion of succession: the hollow "follows upon the before smooth surface". The next lines of the text call succession the only empirical criterion of effect.

**Wrong turns:** saying Kant gives up temporal order for simultaneous causes; he keeps order and gives up only lapse. Reading "order of time" as a short measured interval. Calling this a causal theory of time: that is a modern label, and the passage says only that "the relation remains".

**Model answer:** (a) He separates the order of time from its lapse. Cause and effect can share a moment, yet which determines which still has a direction in time. (b) The direction is fixed by dependence: positing the ball is followed by the hollow, and positing a hollow is not followed by a ball. In the ship case the order was read off an irreversible sequence of perceptions over an interval; here there is no interval, so Kant reads it off what follows when one state is posited.

---

**P2** *(Exegetical, strict.)*

**Must hit, strict:**

- **Principle versus particular law.** The Second Analogy proves only that every alteration follows *something* in the preceding state by *some* rule. That heating boils water is a particular law. Particular laws stand under the categories but "cannot be entirely deduced" from them, and experience "must be superadded" (B165). An analogy gives the relation to a fourth term, not the term.
- **Kant's necessity is not Hume's.** Hume's is a felt habit produced by repetition. Kant's is a rule that is a condition of experiencing objects at all, valid for all appearances before any repetition. Kant says Hume mistook "the subjective necessity of habit" for objective necessity (*Prolegomena* preface), and denies that the necessity is "a mere illusion produced in us by long habit" (§27).

**Wrong turns:** saying Kant holds particular laws to be mere contingent regularities. He holds that a law, once found by experience, is thought as necessary; the note's error is the "without any experiment". Treating "refutes Hume" as the misreading to diagnose: whether Kant succeeds is a verdict, not an exegetical error.

**Model answer:** First, the note turns a principle into a particular law. The Second Analogy shows that every alteration follows some prior state by some rule, not which rule. B165 says particular laws need experience "superadded", so the boiling of water is learned by experiment, not known like 7 + 5 = 12. Second, it runs together two senses of "in the mind". Hume's necessity is a habit bred by repetition; Kant's is a condition of there being any experienced objects, valid before repetition. Kant charges Hume with mistaking "the subjective necessity of habit" for objective necessity, and calls himself "very far from" treating it as an illusion of habit (*Prolegomena* §27).

---

**P3** *(Exegetical (a) · Evaluative (b).)*

**Must hit, strict (a):**

- **Necessity 1:** given that the ship moved downstream, my perceptions *had* to come upstream-first. This is a conditional necessity that follows from the description of the event and the fact that I watched it.
- **Necessity 2:** the lower position follows *necessarily, by a rule*, from something in the preceding state. This is causal necessity.
- The charge: Kant infers 2 from 1, and 1 would hold even if the ship's movement had no cause.

**Must hit, any verdict (b):**

- State the reply. Kant's premise is not irreversibility taken as a datum. It is that apprehension alone cannot tell whether any succession is objective (premises 1–3), so the question is what *judging* it objective requires. Irreversibility is how that judgment shows up in apprehension, not the ground it is inferred from (Allison's line).
- Then decide whether the burden has moved. If the reply works, everything rests on premise 5, that *only* a rule could fix the order, and so on the deduction's account of an object. One should say whether the reply answers Strawson or makes the argument stand or fall with 5.4 and transcendental idealism.

**Wrong turns:** reading the charge as a denial that perceptions of events are irreversible; Strawson grants it. Arguing whether causation is real, a live question this course does not grade. Putting invented words in quotation marks as Strawson's.

**Model answer (b), one of several:** The Kantian reply is that the ship argument does not start from irreversibility as a given fact. It starts from a problem: perceptions are always successive and time cannot be perceived, so nothing in apprehension tells me any succession is objective. To judge that the ship *moved* is already to treat the order as binding, and only a rule can bind it. Irreversibility is that judgment's trace, not its premise. This does block the charge as Strawson put it, since no inference from Necessity 1 to Necessity 2 is made. But it relocates the burden to premise 5, that nothing but a rule could fix the order. That premise needs 5.4's account of an object, so a reader who rejects it gets Strawson's objection back in a new place.

## Flashback

**From Lesson [5.3](05-03-concepts-and-the-categories.md) (Concepts and the categories):** *(Exegetical.)* Close reading. Kant, *Critique of Pure Reason*, opening of "Of the Schematism of the Pure Conceptions of the Understanding" (Meiklejohn, public domain):

> In all subsumptions of an object under a conception, the representation of the object must be homogeneous with the conception [...]. But pure conceptions of the understanding, when compared with empirical intuitions [...], are quite heterogeneous, and never can be discovered in any intuition. [...] Now a transcendental determination of time is so far homogeneous with the category [...] that it is universal and rests upon a rule à priori. On the other hand, it is so far homogeneous with the phenomenon, inasmuch as time is contained in every empirical representation of the manifold.

(a) Why do the categories, unlike empirical concepts, raise a special problem about being applied to appearances at all? Two sentences. (b) In what two respects is a "transcendental determination of time" homogeneous, one with each side? Two sentences. (c) 5.3 gave the schema of cause as succession subject to a rule. Show in one sentence that it has both features from (b).

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):**

- Subsuming an object under a concept requires homogeneity: the concept must contain what is represented in the object (Kant's gloss in the same sentence).
- Empirical concepts are drawn from intuitions and so share content with them. The categories are pure, taken from the understanding's functions of judgment (5.3), and "never can be discovered in any intuition": no one sees causality. So something must mediate between category and appearance, and that is the question the schematism answers.

**Must hit, strict (b):**

- With the category: a time-determination is "universal and rests upon a rule à priori".
- With the appearance: time "is contained in every empirical representation of the manifold", so a determination of time is present in whatever is given.

**Must hit, strict (c):**

- The rule makes the schema universal and a priori (the category's side). Succession is an order in time, which every manifold given to inner sense has (the appearance's side).

**Wrong turns:** reading "heterogeneous" as "the categories cannot apply to appearances". Kant's conclusion is nearly the reverse: through schemata they apply, and only to appearances. Treating the schema as an image or picture of causation; the same chapter says the schema "is clearly distinguishable from the image". Making space the mediator: the passage turns on time, which is contained in every empirical representation.

**Model answer:** (a) Subsumption needs the concept to contain what the object presents, and an empirical concept does because it was drawn from intuitions. A category is pure and can never be found in any intuition, so its application to appearances needs a third, mediating representation. (b) A transcendental determination of time is like the category in being universal and resting on an a priori rule. It is like the appearance because time is contained in every empirical representation. (c) Succession subject to a rule is a rule, and so universal and a priori, governing succession, an order in time that anything given to the senses must have.

</details>
