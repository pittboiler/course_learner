# Modern Philosophy · Reference Card

> Open book. This card is meant to be open while you work — including during
> quizzes. Nothing here is something you're expected to recall cold.

The course reads Descartes to Nietzsche as one argument about what the mind can know and how it stands to the world. Descartes doubts everything to find a foundation and rebuilds it with God; Spinoza, Leibniz and Malebranche each pay a different price for the mind-body union he could not explain; Locke, Berkeley and Hume run the "way of ideas" until Hume finds no impression of necessity, of the self, or of a reason for induction; Reid rejects the way of ideas itself; Kant asks how necessary knowledge of experience is possible and answers that objects, as known, conform to our forms, at the price of the thing in itself; Hegel and Nietzsche refuse that price in opposite directions. The card is **historical and verdict-neutral**: each entry states what a thinker held and argued, in his own terms, and the main reply; where scholars divide on a reading, it gives the readings, not a winner. The standing discipline is [anachronism](#anachronism): "rationalist", "empiricist", "Hume's fork", "veil of ideas", "parallelism", "direct realism", "transcendental ego" and "thesis-antithesis-synthesis" are later labels. Live versions of these debates belong to their owner courses (see [Assumed, not taught here](#assumed-not-taught-here)). Use the card mid-problem to look up a term, a position, an argument's premises, who said it where, and which premise a reply denies. Problems ask you to *use* these, not recite them.

### Method of doubt

A one-time procedure for finding a foundation: withhold assent from whatever is uncertain, as from the false, and see what survives.

Meditation I (1641). The image is architectural: a man may pull down his own house when its foundations are unsure (*Discourse* Part II, 1637). Two economies: a **ground for doubt** suffices (not proof of falsity), and the attack goes to the **principle** a class of beliefs rests on, not to each belief. The aim is "not action but knowledge", so practical life runs on the [provisional morality](#provisional-morality). Skeleton:

- **M1.** The sciences need a certain, indubitable foundation.
- **M2.** Withhold assent from the not-entirely-certain as carefully as from the manifestly false.
- **M3.** A ground for doubt in a class's principle is enough to withhold assent from the class.
- **M4.** My surest beliefs came from or through the senses.

Then three waves (senses; [dreaming argument](#dreaming-argument); [deceiving God argument](#deceiving-god-argument)) give **C:** nothing I formerly believed is beyond doubt.

**Objections.** Gassendi (Fifth Objections): feigning all false swaps an old prejudice for a new one, and is insincere. Bourdin (Seventh Objections): it uses the doubtful to reach the certain; Descartes answers with the apple basket. Descartes to Clerselier (1646): judging is an act of will, the supposition counters habit. Hume (*Enquiry* §12): a doubt reaching the faculties themselves would be "entirely incurable", which becomes the [Cartesian circle](#cartesian-circle). The pressure falls on M2 and M3 together with wave 3.

*Lessons:* [1.1](lessons/01-01-the-method-of-doubt.md), [1.2](lessons/01-02-the-cogito-and-the-wax.md) (P1 of the cogito). Foundationalism as a live theory: [epistemology 2.2](../epistemology/lessons/02-02-foundationalism.md).

### Simple and universal things

What even a dream must be built from, so what survives the dream wave.

Meditation I's painters analogy: dream images, like painted sirens, are composed of elements that must be real: corporeal nature and extension, figure, quantity or magnitude, number, place, time. So the sciences of composites (physics, astronomy, medicine) fall to the dream, while arithmetic and geometry hold "whether I am awake or dreaming". Whether the painters' "reality" means *existence* (so that some extended thing exists) is not settled by the text.

*Lessons:* [1.1](lessons/01-01-the-method-of-doubt.md)

### Evil demon

A supposition of the will that keeps the doubt vivid; not a premise of the doubt.

Veitch: "some malignant demon, who is at once exceedingly potent and deceitful" (Latin *genius malignus*, often "evil genius"; say which translation you quote). It arrives *after* Meditation I has concluded, "of deliberate design", as a counterweight to habit, and adds no new ground for doubt. Its list is sky, air, earth, colours, figures, sounds, external things, the body: arithmetic is not on it. The arithmetic doubt belongs to the [deceiving God](#deceiving-god-argument) (Meditation III traces it "for no other reason than" a God who might have given me an erring nature). **Readings divide:** the demon silently inherits that reach (Meditation II's deceiver is unrestricted), or it is a narrower device that keeps the doubt about bodies alive without making God the deceiver. In Meditation II the cogito survives it: "I exist, since I am deceived". Not a brain in a vat (Putnam 1981 is a modern gloss).

*Lessons:* [1.1](lessons/01-01-the-method-of-doubt.md), [1.2](lessons/01-02-the-cogito-and-the-wax.md)

### Provisional morality

How to live while judgment is suspended: by "three or four maxims".

*Discourse* Part III. The first maxim: obey the laws and customs of one's country. It explains why the doubt can be confined to knowledge: Meditation I's end is "not action but knowledge".

*Lessons:* [1.1](lessons/01-01-the-method-of-doubt.md)

### Cogito

The one thing the doubt cannot touch: thinking "I am not" requires being.

**Texts.** Meditation II: "I am, I exist, is necessarily true each time it is expressed by me, or conceived in my mind" (Veitch); no "cogito ergo sum" there. *Discourse* IV (1637): "I think, hence I am" (French *je pense, donc je suis*). *Principles* I.7 (1644) and the Second Replies have the Latin *ego cogito, ergo sum*; the Replies quote it to deny it is a syllogism. Outline as the lesson gives it:

1. Whatever admits the slightest doubt is set aside as if false ([method of doubt](#method-of-doubt)).
2. Any attempt to doubt, or be deceived about, my existence is itself a thought of mine.
3. It is impossible to conceive that what thinks does not exist while it thinks (*Principles* I.7: "a repugnance").
4. So "I am, I exist" cannot be doubted whenever I think it.

**Pressure point: premise 2.** Lichtenberg (notebooks, posthumous): the evidence licenses only an impersonal "it thinks", like "it is lightning"; Nietzsche, *Beyond Good and Evil* §§16-17 ([critique of the subject](#critique-of-the-subject)); Hume's bundle ([4.4](lessons/04-04-the-self-belief-and-the-passions.md)). Descartes's in-system reply: nothing has no properties, so where there is thinking there is a thing that thinks (*Principles* I.11). Whether premise 3 is reasoned *from* is the [three readings](#three-readings-of-the-cogito) dispute. Augustine's earlier "si fallor, sum" answered a different opponent (the Academics).

*Lessons:* [1.2](lessons/01-02-the-cogito-and-the-wax.md)

### Three readings of the cogito

Is "I exist" inferred from "I think", seen in it, or secured by the act of thinking it? The texts pull more than one way.

| Reading | Claim | Textual support |
|---|---|---|
| **Inference** | "I think" a premise, "I exist" a conclusion, premise 3 a suppressed general premise | Meditation II's "since I was persuaded", "since I am deceived"; the *ergo* of *Principles* I.7; *Principles* I.10 (one must already know "that, in order to think it is necessary to be"); Gassendi's reading (any action would do) |
| **Intuition** | Existence grasped *in* the thinking, in one act | Second Replies (Haldane-Ross): not deduced "by a syllogism, but, by a simple act of mental vision"; the mind forms general propositions from particular cases |
| **Performance** | "I do not exist" defeats itself whenever thought or said | Hintikka, "Cogito, Ergo Sum: Inference or Performance?" (1962); fits "each time it is expressed by me, or conceived" |

**What the words settle.** The Second Replies rule out a *syllogism* with a prior major premise, not every inference; Kenny (*Descartes*, 1968): intuited from one point of view, deduced from another. *Principles* I.10 shows the general truth was *known*, not that it was a *prior* premise. Each reading must explain away the others' texts. Kant's reading (the two are identical, "I exist" not inferred, [paralogisms](#paralogisms)) is Kant's, not a verdict on Descartes.

*Lessons:* [1.2](lessons/01-02-the-cogito-and-the-wax.md)

### Thinking thing

What the I is, so far as it is known after Meditation II: a thing that thinks, in the broad sense.

"A thing that doubts, understands, [conceives], affirms, denies, wills, refuses; that imagines also, and perceives" (Veitch; the bracket is his addition from the French). Perceiving counts as *seeming* to perceive, which survives the dream. Meditation II explicitly does **not** yet claim the I is nothing but this: whether the doubted bodies might be no different from himself he "cannot yet determine". The [real distinction](#real-distinction) waits for Meditation VI, and needs God. Hobbes (Third Objections): a thinking thing may be corporeal; "assumed, not proved".

*Lessons:* [1.2](lessons/01-02-the-cogito-and-the-wax.md), [1.4](lessons/01-04-the-real-distinction-and-the-interaction-problem.md)

### Truth rule

"All that is very clearly and distinctly apprehended (conceived) is true" (Meditation III, Veitch).

Floated in Meditation III from the inventory of what made "I am" certain; not yet secured, because the deceiving God could make me err even there. Secured by God's veracity in Meditation IV, where error is traced to a will that judges beyond what the intellect clearly perceives. Used in Meditation V (the [ontological argument](#cartesian-ontological-argument)'s first premise) and VI (premise 1 of the [conceivability argument](#conceivability-argument)). Whether it can be secured without circularity is the [Cartesian circle](#cartesian-circle).

*Lessons:* [1.3](lessons/01-03-god-clear-and-distinct-ideas-and-the-circle.md), [1.4](lessons/01-04-the-real-distinction-and-the-interaction-problem.md)

### Clear and distinct perception

Clear: present and manifest to an attentive mind. Distinct: clear, and so sharply separated from everything else that it contains only what is clear.

*Principles* I.45. Pain can be clear but not distinct, because we confusedly judge it to be in the limb (I.46). The cogito is the first clear and distinct perception; the [truth rule](#truth-rule) generalizes from it. Premise 1 of the [conceivability argument](#conceivability-argument) and the reply to Arnauld rest on it, so the [circle](#cartesian-circle) worry follows it into Meditation VI.

*Lessons:* [1.3](lessons/01-03-god-clear-and-distinct-ideas-and-the-circle.md), [1.4](lessons/01-04-the-real-distinction-and-the-interaction-problem.md)

### Formal and objective reality

Every idea has two kinds of being: what it has as a thing (formal), and what it has by representing (objective).

**Formal reality** of an idea: being a mode of a mind; in that respect all ideas are equal. **Objective reality:** the being a thing has *as represented* in the intellect (the scholastic sense, the reverse of modern "objective" = mind-independent; modern "objective" is nearer Descartes's *formal*). Hierarchy of reality: modes < finite substances < infinite substance, so an idea of God represents the most. **Eminent containment:** containing a perfection in a higher form (see Terms).

*Lessons:* [1.3](lessons/01-03-god-clear-and-distinct-ideas-and-the-circle.md)

### Causal adequacy principle

A cause must have at least as much reality as its effect; for ideas, as much as the idea represents.

Meditation III, by the natural light: "there must at least be as much reality in the efficient and total cause as in its effect". For ideas: the cause must contain *formally or eminently* at least as much reality as the idea contains *objectively*. First Replies (to Caterus): the idea of an ingenious machine needs a cause such as a real machine seen, mechanical knowledge, or ingenuity. A painting of a palace needs a cause rich enough for the palace in it. Premise 3 of the [trademark argument](#trademark-argument).

*Lessons:* [1.3](lessons/01-03-god-clear-and-distinct-ideas-and-the-circle.md)

### Real distinction

Two things are really distinct when each *could* exist without the other: separability, not present separation.

Holds only between substances: a shape cannot exist without something extended, so shape and body are not really distinct. Compatible with the closest union: however tightly God joined soul and body, they stay really distinct because God keeps the power to separate them (*Principles* I.60). Each substance has one principal attribute (I.53): thought for mind, extension for body. Argued by the [conceivability argument](#conceivability-argument), confirmed by the [divisibility argument](#divisibility-argument). Whether dualism is true: `philosophy-of-mind` (syllabus).

*Lessons:* [1.4](lessons/01-04-the-real-distinction-and-the-interaction-problem.md)

### Substantial union

Mind and body, though really distinct, "compose a certain unity": I feel pain, I do not observe it.

Meditation VI: I am "not only lodged in my body as a pilot in a vessel" (Veitch); a pilot would notice a wound as a hole in the hull, whereas I feel pain, hunger, thirst. Sensations are confused modes of thinking that arise from the union. Known obscurely by the intellect but very clearly by the senses, in ordinary life (to Elisabeth, 28 June 1643). The pilot picture is the one Descartes calls insufficient; it is not his view. "Substantial union" is a later label.

*Lessons:* [1.4](lessons/01-04-the-real-distinction-and-the-interaction-problem.md)

### Interaction problem

How can an unextended mind move, or be moved by, an extended body? A later label; the seventeenth century asked how the soul moves the body.

**Gassendi** (Fifth Objections): no motion without contact, no contact without body (citing Lucretius); Descartes: that submits to imagination what does not fall under it. **Elisabeth** (May 1643): every determination of motion comes from impulsion, from the manner of pushing, or from the quality and shape of the mover's surface; the first two need contact, the third extension, both denied to the soul. Her alternative: "easier" to concede matter and extension to the soul. Descartes's answer: the [primitive notions](#primitive-notions). It is a demand *inside shared contact mechanics*, not the conservation-of-energy objection and not the hard problem.

**The four answers** compared on one case (raising an arm), each with its price:

| Answer | Who | Price |
|---|---|---|
| Interaction | Descartes | The union is opaque: primitive, not explained |
| [Occasionalism](#occasionalism) | Malebranche | No finite thing is a true cause |
| [Parallelism](#parallelism) | Spinoza | No contingency; no causation across attributes |
| [Pre-established harmony](#pre-established-harmony) | Leibniz | No real interaction between substances |

Spinoza refuses the question (one substance; no causation across attributes, *Ethics* V preface). The live objection: `philosophy-of-mind` (syllabus).

*Lessons:* [1.4](lessons/01-04-the-real-distinction-and-the-interaction-problem.md), [2.2](lessons/02-02-spinoza-mind-body-and-necessity.md), [2.4](lessons/02-04-interaction-and-causation.md)

### Primitive notions

Three basic notions, each understood on its own terms: extension (body), thought (soul), and the union of the two.

Descartes to Elisabeth, 21 May and 28 June 1643. Error comes from explaining one by another: explaining the union by the notion of body, or carrying the union's notion over to bodies, as in the scholastic picture of heaviness as a quality moving a body without contact. The union is known clearly by the senses, through ordinary life rather than metaphysical meditation. Descartes's defence of the distinction: failing to explain the union through the other two notions shows nothing against them. Whether that answers Elisabeth or relocates the problem is left open.

*Lessons:* [1.4](lessons/01-04-the-real-distinction-and-the-interaction-problem.md)

**Arguments in Module 1**

### Dreaming argument

Wave 2: there are no certain marks of waking, so any particular sensory judgment might be a dream.

1. I sleep, and in dreams I have represented these same things.
2. There are "no certain marks by which the state of waking can ever be distinguished from sleep".
3. So every particular sensory judgment (I have hands, I am by the fire) has a ground for doubt.
4. But dream images are composed of [simple and universal things](#simple-and-universal-things) that must be real.
5. So physics, astronomy, medicine (sciences of composites) are doubtful; arithmetic and geometry hold.

*Removes:* particular bodies and the sciences of composites. *Leaves:* simple natures and mathematics. Just before, the madness case is set aside as "extravagant" (Foucault 1961 and Derrida 1963 dispute why). Live skeptical version, and the closure argument: [epistemology 3.1](../epistemology/lessons/03-01-the-closure-argument.md).

*Lessons:* [1.1](lessons/01-01-the-method-of-doubt.md)

### Deceiving God argument

Wave 3: the God who made me could make me err even in adding two and three.

1. An all-powerful God made me; he could arrange that I perceive earth and sky with none existing, and that I err "each time I add together two and three, or number the sides of a square".
2. *Reply:* God is good. *Rejoinder:* his goodness evidently permits occasional deception, so it does not obviously forbid constant deception.
3. If instead I came about by fate, chance or a chain of causes, the less powerful my origin, the likelier my nature is defective.
4. So on either horn my faculty itself is in doubt.

This, not the [evil demon](#evil-demon), reaches arithmetic. Meditation III calls the doubt "very slight, and, so to speak, metaphysical". Answered by the proofs of God and God's veracity in Meditations III-IV ([trademark argument](#trademark-argument), [truth rule](#truth-rule)).

*Lessons:* [1.1](lessons/01-01-the-method-of-doubt.md)

### Wax argument

Melted wax changes every sensible quality and stays the same wax, so bodies are known by the intellect, and the mind better than body.

1. After melting, every quality known by the five senses has changed.
2. The same wax remains.
3. So what I knew of the wax was not any sensible quality.
4. What remains is extended, flexible, movable, capable of more shapes than imagination can run through.
5. So the wax is grasped by "inspection of the mind alone" (*solius mentis inspectio*); bodies "are not properly perceived by the senses nor by the faculty of imagination, but by the intellect alone".
6. Every judgment that the wax exists implies more evidently that I who judge exist; so the mind is better known than the body.

Companion case: men in hats and cloaks seen from a window are judged, not seen, to be men.

*Lessons:* [1.2](lessons/01-02-the-cogito-and-the-wax.md)

### Trademark argument

My idea of an infinite God has more represented reality than I, a finite thing, could cause; so God exists as its cause.

1. I have an idea of God: infinite, independent, all-knowing, all-powerful creator.
2. That idea has more objective reality than any other (it represents infinite being).
3. The cause of an idea must contain formally or eminently as much reality as the idea contains objectively ([causal adequacy](#causal-adequacy-principle)).
4. I am finite, so I do not contain infinite reality, formally or eminently.
5. The idea is not built by negating the finite (I know my limits only by comparison with the infinite, which is positive and prior) and not supplied by potential perfections (growing knowledge never becomes actually infinite).
6. So an actually infinite being, God, exists as the cause of my idea.

"The mark of the workman impressed on his work" is Descartes's gloss *after* the argument; "trademark" is a later label, and "God stamped the idea, so God exists" would beg the question. Second Meditation III argument: conservation needs the same power as creation, so something keeps me in existence. Then: God is perfect, deception a defect, so God is no deceiver. **Pressure:** Gassendi (Fifth Objections) on premise 5: we build the idea by amplifying finite perfections. Hume's version of that reply: [4.1](lessons/04-01-impressions-ideas-and-the-fork.md).

*Lessons:* [1.3](lessons/01-03-god-clear-and-distinct-ideas-and-the-circle.md), [4.1](lessons/04-01-impressions-ideas-and-the-fork.md)

### Cartesian ontological argument

Existence belongs to the nature of a supremely perfect being as the angle sum belongs to the triangle.

Meditation V:

1. Whatever I clearly and distinctly perceive to belong to a thing's true nature belongs to it (the [truth rule](#truth-rule)).
2. I clearly and distinctly perceive that existence belongs to the nature of a supremely perfect being.
3. So God exists.

**Descartes's own objection:** a mountain without a valley is inconceivable, yet no mountain need exist. **Reply:** there the necessity binds two ideas; here it lies in the thing itself, not imposed by my thought. Meditation V runs *on* the truth rule and ends by saying even remembered geometry is doubtful until one knows God. Spinoza's *Ethics* I p11 (existence belongs to the nature of substance) is a relative. Kant's critique ([being is not a real predicate](#being-is-not-a-real-predicate)) is aimed at it. Whether ontological arguments succeed: [philosophy-of-religion 2.1](../philosophy-of-religion/lessons/02-01-anselms-ontological-argument.md), [2.2](../philosophy-of-religion/lessons/02-02-the-modal-ontological-argument.md).

*Lessons:* [1.3](lessons/01-03-god-clear-and-distinct-ideas-and-the-circle.md), [2.1](lessons/02-01-spinoza-one-substance.md), [6.3](lessons/06-03-the-critique-of-the-proofs-of-god.md)

### Cartesian circle

Clear perception is certain only given God, and God is known only by clear perception. The dispute is over what Descartes's answer means.

**Arnauld** (Fourth Objections; Mersenne's Second Objections, compiled from various theologians and philosophers, pressed a version first):

1. I am certain that what I clearly and distinctly perceive is true only if I am certain God exists.
2. I am certain God exists only because I clearly and distinctly perceive the premises of the proofs.
3. So I must be certain of the rule before I can be certain of it: circular.

**Readings of the reply:**

- **Memory** (Doney 1955): premise 1 is false as stated. Present, attended perception compels assent and needs no guarantee; God secures *remembered* conclusions. Texts: Fourth Replies; Second Replies' third point; the close of Meditation V.
- **Unshakeable conviction** (Frankfurt, *Demons, Dreamers, and Madmen*, 1970): the rule is not certified from outside; following it leads to a God who removes every reason for doubt, leaving "a conviction so strong that nothing can remove it"; a falsity "absolutely speaking" need not concern us (Second Replies).
- **General rule vs particular perceptions** (Van Cleve 1979): particular clear perceptions are known before the general rule is.

**Where it turns:** whether present clear perception is beyond doubt. Meditation III seems to deny it (a God could make me err where my evidence is greatest); the defender: the doubt arises only when I turn from the proposition to God's power. Second pressure point: the proof of God is itself long, so memory may enter the proof meant to secure memory. The conviction reading's price: conviction proof against doubt may not be truth. The atheist geometer perceives clearly but lacks "science". Descartes printed both objections with the *Meditations* (1641) and answered both.

*Lessons:* [1.3](lessons/01-03-god-clear-and-distinct-ideas-and-the-circle.md), [1.4](lessons/01-04-the-real-distinction-and-the-interaction-problem.md)

### Conceivability argument

I can clearly conceive mind without body, and God can make whatever I so conceive, so mind can exist without body.

Meditation VI; restated *Principles* I.60.

1. **(God premise, from 1.3)** Whatever I clearly and distinctly conceive can be produced by God exactly as I conceive it.
2. So if I clearly and distinctly conceive A apart from B, A can exist without B.
3. Things that can exist apart are really distinct (definition of [real distinction](#real-distinction)).
4. I have a clear and distinct idea of myself as only thinking and unextended, and a distinct idea of body as only extended and unthinking.
5. So my mind is really distinct from my body and can exist without it.

**How much work God does:** on one reading God's power is ineliminable (no route from conceiving to possibility without it); on another the theology states a modal principle ("it matters not by what power this separation is made"). "Because I know" names God as the stated warrant. The God premise inherits the [circle](#cartesian-circle). **Premise 4** is where it turns. Hobbes: assumed, not proved. Arnauld (Fourth Objections) grants premise 1 and denies that premise 4 delivers what premise 2 needs: one can clearly conceive a triangle inscribed in a semicircle without conceiving that it is right-angled, yet it cannot exist without that property. Descartes's reply adds "complete thing" (Fourth Replies): the question becomes whether completeness is perceived or presupposed. Run backwards: if interaction is certain but unintelligible on these premises, it tells against premise 4 (Elisabeth's "easier to concede"). Conceivability as defeasible evidence of possibility: [metaphysics 3.1](../metaphysics/lessons/03-01-kinds-of-necessity.md).

*Lessons:* [1.4](lessons/01-04-the-real-distinction-and-the-interaction-problem.md)

### Divisibility argument

Body is always divisible; the mind, as thinking, has no parts; so they differ in nature.

Meditation VI, offered as confirmation (it would suffice alone had the point not already been shown). Body is divisible even in thought (*Principles* II.20). Cut off a foot and nothing is taken from the mind; willing, understanding, perceiving are not parts but one mind acting. **Kant's reply** (second paralogism): the I is logically simple in thought, but that does not show a simple substance distinct from divisible body ([paralogisms](#paralogisms)).

*Lessons:* [1.4](lessons/01-04-the-real-distinction-and-the-interaction-problem.md), [6.2](lessons/06-02-the-soul-and-the-antinomies.md)

### Geometrical method

Spinoza's Euclidean order: definitions, axioms, then numbered propositions each proved from earlier ones.

Used in the *Ethics* (published posthumously, 1677) and earlier in his geometrical version of Descartes's *Principles* (1663). It makes dependencies traceable, so when a conclusion shocks you, you can find which definition or step produced it. It does **not** make the result certain: it guarantees only that the result follows from the definitions, and a Cartesian can refuse Definition III's "conceived through itself" and Definition VI's "infinite attributes".

*Lessons:* [2.1](lessons/02-01-spinoza-one-substance.md)

### Substance

What depends on nothing else: Descartes's definition, which Spinoza takes at its word.

**Descartes** (*Principles* I.51): what "stand[s] in need of nothing beyond itself in order to its existence"; strictly only God, so created minds and bodies are substances in a weaker sense, needing only God's concourse (I.52); each has one principal attribute (I.53). **Spinoza** (*Ethics* I def III): "that which is in itself, and is conceived through itself"; the second clause, about *conceiving*, is stronger than Descartes's existence test. Spinoza refuses the weaker sense, so only God qualifies (I p14); everything else is a mode. Image: you stand to God as a wave to the sea. Aristotelian background (substance and "present in a subject"): [metaphysics 2.1](../metaphysics/lessons/02-01-substance-and-accident.md). Leibniz's road back to many substances: [monad](#monad). Locke's substratum: [substratum](#substratum).

*Lessons:* [2.1](lessons/02-01-spinoza-one-substance.md)

### Attribute and mode

Attribute: what the intellect perceives as constituting a substance's essence. Mode: what is in, and conceived through, something else.

*Ethics* I def IV, def V. Thought and extension are two of God's infinitely many attributes (def VI: God consists in infinite attributes; God's essence contains "whatever expresses reality"). Minds and bodies, this idea and this body, are modes. Axiom I leaves no third category. Example: extension is an attribute of God, so God is extended, but substance as such is indivisible (I p13 cor.), and what we cut are bodies, which are modes; so God is extended and has no body (I p15 schol.).

*Lessons:* [2.1](lessons/02-01-spinoza-one-substance.md), [2.2](lessons/02-02-spinoza-mind-body-and-necessity.md)

### Natura naturans and naturata

Nature viewed as active (God's attributes, God as free cause) versus nature viewed as passive (all the modes, which are in God).

*Ethics* I p29 scholium. Bears on the pantheism question, which follows the reading of "in God" (I p15). **Inherence** (Bayle, *Dictionary*, "Spinoza", 1697): modes are God's properties, so when one army kills another, God modified one way kills God modified another; evidence: modes are "modifications" (*affectiones*). **Causal dependence** (Curley, *Spinoza's Metaphysics*, 1969): modes depend on God as effects on a cause; evidence: I ax 4, def V's pairing of "in" with "conceived through", p18's "indwelling" cause. On Curley's reading God is *natura naturans*, and *natura naturata* depends on God without being identical to God. The words do not settle it.

*Lessons:* [2.1](lessons/02-01-spinoza-one-substance.md)

### Deus sive Natura

"God or Nature": the famous tag is not in Part I.

In Elwes it first appears in the Part IV preface ("the eternal and infinite Being, which we call God or Nature") and in IV p4's proof ("power of God or of Nature"). Even there it names an eternal and infinite being, not the sum of bodies. Do not cite it as the climax of I p14-p15.

*Lessons:* [2.1](lessons/02-01-spinoza-one-substance.md)

### Mind as idea of the body

Your body is a mode of extension; your mind is the idea of that same body, in God.

*Ethics* II p13: "the object of the idea constituting the human mind is the body ... actually existing, and nothing else". Spinoza's example: a circle in nature and the idea of that circle in God are one thing displayed through different attributes. Every body has an idea in God, and all individuals are "animated" in different degrees (II p13 schol.; "panpsychism" is a later term). Not mind-brain identity theory, and not "dual-aspect monism" in the modern sense: the mind is the idea of the *whole* body, and ideas are not neural states.

*Lessons:* [2.2](lessons/02-02-spinoza-mind-body-and-necessity.md)

### Parallelism

"The order and connection of ideas is the same as the order and connection of things" (*Ethics* II p7). The label is later; Spinoza never uses it.

**Spinoza's argument** (as the lesson reconstructs it):

1. One substance, God; thought and extension among its attributes (I p14; II p1-2).
2. Each attribute is conceived through itself (I p10).
3. Knowing an effect involves knowing its cause (I ax 4); things with nothing in common cannot cause one another (I p3).
4. So modes of each attribute are caused by God only as considered under that attribute (II p6): bodies by bodies, ideas by ideas.
5. What follows under extension follows in the same order under thought (II p7 cor.).
6. So II p7, and (II p7 schol.) "a mode of extension and the idea of that mode are one and the same thing, though expressed in two ways".
7. The human mind is the idea of the body (II p13).
8. So "body cannot determine mind to think, neither can mind determine body to motion or rest" (III p2).

**The label's trouble:** "parallel" suggests two series kept in step (Leibniz's picture; Leibniz used "perfect parallelism" of his *own* view in a 1702 essay quoted in Latta's notes to *Monadology* §79), while II p7 schol. speaks of one thing expressed two ways. How to weigh the two descriptions is a reading question. Spinoza's Part V preface mocks Descartes's pineal union as worse than occult qualities and keeps Elisabeth's premise ("no common standard of volition and motion"); what he drops is the two substances. Price: contingency ([necessitarianism](#necessitarianism)).

*Lessons:* [2.2](lessons/02-02-spinoza-mind-body-and-necessity.md), [2.4](lessons/02-04-interaction-and-causation.md)

### Necessitarianism

Nothing could have been otherwise; "contingent" reports a gap in our knowledge.

*Ethics* I p29 (nothing contingent) and I p33 (things could not have been produced in any other manner or order); a thing is called contingent only "in relation to the imperfection of our knowledge" (I p33 schol. 1). Skeleton:

1. God exists necessarily; whatever is, is in God (I p11, p15).
2. Infinitely many things follow from God's nature, as theorems from a definition (I p16).
3. Every finite thing is determined by another finite cause, without end (I p28).
4. So nothing is contingent (I p29).
5. If things could have been otherwise, God's nature could have been otherwise, and that nature would exist necessarily too: two Gods, against I p14 (I p33 dem.).
6. So things could not have been produced otherwise (I p33).

**Readings:** strict (Garrett 1991; "in any manner or in any order") vs moderate (Curley 1988; I p28 and II ax 1, "a man may or may not exist"). **Turns on premise 5.** Leibniz (*Theodicy*, 1710, §§173-174): the possible is what implies no contradiction (an unwritten novel; Bayle's Spinoza dying at Leiden), so the world is necessary only morally, not metaphysically. Spinoza's defender (I p35): whatever is in God's power necessarily exists, so Leibniz's possibles beg the question. The crux: does God act by choice among possibles (I p17 schol. denies it)? Not fatalism: our efforts are among the causes. The "modal collapse": [philosophy-of-religion 2.4](../philosophy-of-religion/lessons/02-04-cosmological-arguments-sufficient-reason.md).

*Lessons:* [2.2](lessons/02-02-spinoza-mind-body-and-necessity.md)

### Conatus

Each thing, insofar as it is in itself, strives to persevere in its being, and that striving is its actual essence.

*Ethics* III p6-7. Not a final cause, and not made pointless by necessity: conatus is the thing's essence, and our ideas and efforts are among the causes through which what is necessary happens.

*Lessons:* [2.2](lessons/02-02-spinoza-mind-body-and-necessity.md)

### Spinozan freedom

Free = determined by your own nature alone, not exempt from causes.

I def 7: "That thing is called free, which exists solely by the necessity of its own nature, and of which the action is determined by itself alone". So God is the only free cause (I p17 cor. 2), and there is no free will (II p48). People think themselves free because they know their desires and not their causes (I Appendix); Letter 58 (to Schuller, 1674): a stone set in motion, if it could think, would believe it moved freely. Human freedom is real but partial: we act when we are the adequate cause (III def 2), and grow freer as passions become clear and distinct ideas (V p3). Compatibilism as a live position: [metaphysics 6.2](../metaphysics/lessons/06-02-compatibilism.md).

*Lessons:* [2.2](lessons/02-02-spinoza-mind-body-and-necessity.md)

### Principle of contradiction

What involves a contradiction is false.

*Monadology* §31 (Latta). It grounds **truths of reasoning**, whose opposite is impossible and which analysis reduces to identities (§§33-35). Kant keeps it as the principle of analytic judgments ([analytic and synthetic](#analytic-and-synthetic)).

*Lessons:* [2.3](lessons/02-03-leibniz-sufficient-reason-and-monads.md)

### Principle of sufficient reason

Nothing is or is true without a reason why it is so and not otherwise.

*Monadology* §32 (Latta): "there can be no fact real or existing, no statement true, unless there be a sufficient reason, why it should be so and not otherwise, although these reasons usually cannot be known by us". Grounds **truths of fact**, whose opposite is possible (§33). In the 1686 letter to Arnauld, a corollary of [predicate-in-subject](#predicate-in-subject-principle). Spinoza uses a version in the second proof of I p11. In Kant's critique, it grounds the cosmological proof and *Monadology* §40's step from a necessary being to one "illimitable" containing "as much reality as is possible" ([conversion objection](#conversion-objection)). Live version: [metaphysics 3.3](../metaphysics/lessons/03-03-brute-facts-and-necessary-existence.md); Leibniz's cosmological argument: [philosophy-of-religion 2.4](../philosophy-of-religion/lessons/02-04-cosmological-arguments-sufficient-reason.md).

*Lessons:* [2.3](lessons/02-03-leibniz-sufficient-reason-and-monads.md), [6.3](lessons/06-03-the-critique-of-the-proofs-of-god.md)

### Predicate in subject principle

In every true affirmative proposition, necessary or contingent, the predicate's notion is contained in the subject's.

To Arnauld, 1686 (Latta): "Always in every true affirmative proposition, whether necessary or contingent, universal or singular, the notion of the predicate is in some way comprehended in that of the subject, *praedicatum inest subjecto*; otherwise I know not what truth is". His "great principle", with the PSR among its corollaries. A truth's reason is the containment a full analysis would expose. **Russell (1900) and Couturat (1901)** read the metaphysics as following from this logic; others note the *Discourse* opens with God's perfection and that the *Monadology*'s route to simples never uses it. The letter supports logic-first *justification* of the PSR, not logic-first *discovery*. Kant keeps containment only as the mark of *analytic* judgments.

*Lessons:* [2.3](lessons/02-03-leibniz-sufficient-reason-and-monads.md), [5.1](lessons/05-01-the-critical-question.md)

### Complete individual concept

The concept of an individual substance contains every predicate true of it, past and future, relations included.

*Discourse* §8: God sees in Alexander's concept that he will conquer Darius and Porus. Derivation (*Discourse* §§8-9, 13-14):

1. [Principle of contradiction](#principle-of-contradiction) grounds truths of reasoning.
2. [Principle of sufficient reason](#principle-of-sufficient-reason) grounds truths of fact.
3. [Predicate-in-subject](#predicate-in-subject-principle).
4. An individual substance is a subject of predicates not itself predicated of anything (the traditional, merely nominal definition until 3 is added).
5. So its concept contains every predicate true of it.
6. So no two substances differ only in number ([identity of indiscernibles](#identity-of-indiscernibles)).
7. So whatever happens to a substance arises from its own nature (§14's heading).
8. Each concept includes relations to all others, so each expresses the whole universe (§9).
9. So all substances agree without interaction ([pre-established harmony](#pre-established-harmony)).

**Arnauld's objection** (March 1686, on the *Discourse*'s headings): if Adam's concept contains his descendants and their deeds, all happens by a necessity "more than fatal". **Turns on premise 3 applied to contingent truths.** Leibniz's replies: [hypothetical and absolute necessity](#hypothetical-and-absolute-necessity) and infinite analysis. Possible-worlds semantics and Lewis's "counterparts" are modern glosses; Leibniz's possible universes are ideas in God's understanding (*Monadology* §53).

*Lessons:* [2.3](lessons/02-03-leibniz-sufficient-reason-and-monads.md)

### Identity of indiscernibles

No two substances are alike in every respect.

*Discourse* §9; *Monadology* §9. Derived from the complete concept and from sufficient reason (fifth letter to Clarke); "To suppose two things indiscernible, is to suppose the same thing under two names" (fourth letter, §6). The converse of the near-uncontested **indiscernibility of identicals** (Leibniz's law); this direction is the substantive one. Echoes Aquinas's claim that each angel is its own species, extended to every substance. Black's two spheres: [metaphysics 2.1](../metaphysics/lessons/02-01-substance-and-accident.md); [metaphysics 5.3](../metaphysics/lessons/05-03-coincidence-and-the-ship-of-theseus.md).

*Lessons:* [2.3](lessons/02-03-leibniz-sufficient-reason-and-monads.md)

### Monad

A simple substance: no parts, no extension or figure, no windows; its states are perceptions and appetitions.

*Monadology* (1714) §§1-15: there are compounds, so there are simples (§2); simples have no parts, so no extension or figure (§3); no created thing alters them from outside, "no windows" (§7); they must differ in quality (§§8-9); change comes from an internal principle (§11), as perception and appetition (§§14-15). Perception need not be conscious; Leibniz faults the Cartesians for counting unnoticed perceptions as nothing (§14). **Windowless means no influence, not no representation:** every monad mirrors the whole universe (§56). The mill (§17): [philosophy-of-mind](../philosophy-of-mind/syllabus.md).

*Lessons:* [2.3](lessons/02-03-leibniz-sufficient-reason-and-monads.md)

### Pre-established harmony

Substances do not act on one another; each unfolds from its own nature, and God chose them so that their states agree.

"Just as if there were a mutual influence" (Third Explanation of the New System, 1696). *Monadology* §81 (Latta): "bodies act as if (to suppose the impossible) there were no souls, and souls act as if there were no bodies, and both act as if each influenced the other". Follows from the [complete concept](#complete-individual-concept), fixed when God chose which concepts to make actual. Price: real interaction between created substances. Contrast: Spinoza's one thing expressed two ways needs no coordinating God. Hume uses the phrase himself of nature and our ideas (*Enquiry* ¶44).

*Lessons:* [2.3](lessons/02-03-leibniz-sufficient-reason-and-monads.md), [2.4](lessons/02-04-interaction-and-causation.md)

### Hypothetical and absolute necessity

What follows from God's free decree is certain ("assured") but necessary only given the decree; its contrary implies no contradiction in itself.

*Discourse* §13 (Caesar and the Rubicon): "assured, but ... not therefore necessary", impossible *ex hypothesi* but not "in itself". God's choice of the best has a reason that "inclines without necessitating" (to Arnauld, 1686); its necessity is moral, not metaphysical. Paired with **infinite analysis** (*On Contingency*, *On Freedom*, late 1680s): a contingent truth's analysis never terminates, like the incommensurable ratio of a square's side and diagonal. **Critics:** infinite analysis may show only that *we* cannot finish the proof; and hypothetical necessity is only as contingent as its hypothesis, so if God's goodness guarantees the best, the series looks necessary after all (Spinoza's conclusion). Leibniz: a worse world is rejected for its imperfection, not its impossibility. Adams (*Leibniz: Determinist, Theist, Idealist*, 1994) weighs both accounts.

*Lessons:* [2.3](lessons/02-03-leibniz-sufficient-reason-and-monads.md)

### Occasionalism

God's will is the only true cause; finite things, minds and bodies alike, are the occasions on which he acts by general laws.

Malebranche, *The Search after Truth* VI.ii.3 (Taylor, 1700). Not irregularity: God acts by general laws, so nature stays lawful; the dispute is where the efficacy lies. Applies to body on body (the hammer and nail) as much as mind on body, so it turns Elisabeth's question about *unlike* things into a question about causation as such. Roots: Descartes's conservation as continuous creation; the *Dialogues on Metaphysics* (1688) use that route. Price: finite causation. Strain case: a sinful choice; Malebranche leaves the mind a sliver of activity ("I doubt whether it can be call'd a Power"), which Hume calls a mere pretext (*Treatise* I.iv.5). Rejects Aquinas's concurrentism ([thomistic-synthesis 5.2](../thomistic-synthesis/lessons/05-02-providence-and-secondary-causes.md)).

*Lessons:* [2.4](lessons/02-04-interaction-and-causation.md)

### True cause

"A true Cause is that betwixt which and its Effect, the Mind perceives a necessary connexion" (Malebranche, *Search* VI.ii.3).

The **no-necessary-connection argument**:

1. A true cause is one between which and its effect the mind perceives a necessary connection (stipulated: "for so I understand it").
2. Our idea of body contains no force: body is extension.
3. Between a finite will and any motion the mind perceives no necessary connection (and most people do not know their nerves and spirits, yet move their arms deftly).
4. Between the will of an infinitely perfect being and its effect the mind does perceive one.
5. So only God is a true cause.
6. So finite things are natural or occasional causes.

Could God *give* creatures power? Then God's will still carries the necessity. **Critics cut at opposite ends.** Leibniz denies **premise 1**: a genuine cause is a thing whose effects follow from the nature God gave it ([perpetual miracle objection](#perpetual-miracle-objection)). Hume keeps premises 1 and 3 and denies **premise 4**: no impression of power in God's will either (*Enquiry* §7 Part I); his *Treatise* I.iii.14 footnote points to "Book vi. Part 2, chap. 3" ([necessary connection](#necessary-connection)).

*Lessons:* [2.4](lessons/02-04-interaction-and-causation.md), [4.2](lessons/04-02-causation-and-necessary-connection.md)

**Arguments in Module 2**

### The one-substance deduction

Take substance as what depends on nothing, give God every attribute, forbid two substances one attribute, and only God is left.

*Ethics* I p1-15 (Spinoza's numbers):

1. Everything is in itself (substance) or in another (mode) (ax I, def III, def V).
2. Substance is prior in nature to its modes (p1).
3. Distinct things differ in attributes or in modes (p4).
4. So two substances cannot share an attribute (p5): set modes aside, and nothing distinguishes them.
5. One substance cannot produce another (p6).
6. So existence belongs to the nature of substance (p7).
7. Every substance is infinite (p8).
8. God, the substance with every attribute, necessarily exists (def VI, p11; relative of [Meditation V](#cartesian-ontological-argument), with a second proof from the demand for a reason).
9. Any other substance would share an attribute with God, against 4 (p14 proof).

So "besides God no substance can be granted or conceived" (p14), and whatever is, is in God (p15). **Turns on premise 4** ([shared-attribute objection](#shared-attribute-objection)); premise 8 inherits the questions about Meditation V. p6's proof restates p5's content rather than citing it.

*Lessons:* [2.1](lessons/02-01-spinoza-one-substance.md)

### Shared-attribute objection

Substance A has attributes c and d, B has d and e: they differ in attributes yet share d, so p5 fails.

Leibniz, in notes on the *Ethics*, against I p5, whose proof assumes one attribute per substance though Spinoza's God has infinitely many. **Replies:** (1) Def IV: an attribute *constitutes the essence* of its substance, so a shared attribute is a shared essence; (2) Della Rocca (*Spinoza*, 2008): each attribute is conceived through itself (p10), so A and B under d are indiscernible, and Spinoza's rationalism forbids an unexplained difference. Critics: both replies use more than p5's proof states. p6, p8 and p12-p14 depend on p5.

*Lessons:* [2.1](lessons/02-01-spinoza-one-substance.md)

### Perpetual miracle objection

If God moves bodies continually, every event is a miracle, "not popularly ... but philosophically, as that which exceeds the forces of created beings".

Leibniz against occasionalism, in his reply to Bayle's article "Rorarius" (Langley's appendix to the *New Essays*). Denies Malebranche's premise 1 ([true cause](#true-cause)): a genuine cause acts from the nature God gave it. **Occasionalist reply** (pressed by Bayle against Leibniz): God acts by general laws, and a miracle is a *particular* volition. **Leibniz:** a general decree still needs a natural means of execution. "Perpetual miracle" is Leibniz's charge, not the occasionalists' admission.

*Lessons:* [2.4](lessons/02-04-interaction-and-causation.md)

### Two clocks

Two clocks can agree by influence, by assistance, or by being made to agree from the start.

Leibniz, Third Explanation of the New System (1696, Latta). **Influence** = the common philosophy (Descartes), but nothing conceivable passes between substances; **assistance** = occasionalism, a *Deus ex machina* in an ordinary matter; **pre-established agreement** = his own [harmony](#pre-established-harmony). The simile was older: Geulincx used it, and Latta says Leibniz had it from Simon Foucher, who probably hit on it independently. Leibniz's contribution is the three-way sorting. Spinoza fits none of the three: on his view there is one series conceived twice, not two clocks.

*Lessons:* [2.4](lessons/02-04-interaction-and-causation.md)

### Innate ideas

Principles or ideas "stamped upon the mind" at its first being, before any experience: the doctrine *Essay* Book I attacks.

Locke's targets are innate *propositions* (the maxims "Whatsoever is, is" and "It is impossible for the same thing to be and not to be", and moral rules) and the *ideas* composing them; he grants innate faculties, which "nobody ... ever denied". **The argument** (I.ii):

1. An innate principle is received in the soul's "very first being" (I.ii.1).
2. The argument for them is universal assent (I.ii.2).
3. Universal assent proves nothing if it can be reached another way (I.ii.3), and Book II shows how.
4. There is no universal assent: children and "idiots" (Locke's word) have no thought of the maxims (I.ii.5).
5. Nothing is in the mind that it was never conscious of (I.ii.5: "never yet knew").
6. If "in the mind" means only that the mind can come to know it, every truth is innate and the claim is trivial (I.ii.5, I.ii.22).
7. "Known when men come to the use of reason" fails: either theorems are innate too (I.ii.8), or the timing is false (I.ii.12).
8. So there are no innate speculative principles.

I.iv adds: the ideas *impossibility* and *identity* are lacking in many grown men (I.iv.1-3). **Turns on premises 5 and 6** ([veined marble](#veined-marble)). Descartes had already given a capacity answer to Hobbes (Third Replies, Obj. X): innate means "we possess the faculty of summoning up this idea", a faculty tied to one content. The only nativist Book I names is Lord Herbert of Cherbury (I.iii.15); Yolton (1956) argued cruder nativism was common in England. Kant's a priori is not innate either: B167-168 rejects a "preformation-system" of implanted aptitudes because it gives only subjective necessity ([a priori and a posteriori](#a-priori-and-a-posteriori)).

*Lessons:* [3.1](lessons/03-01-locke-against-innate-ideas.md), [5.1](lessons/05-01-the-critical-question.md)

### Veined marble

Leibniz: the mind is marble whose veins already outline Hercules; innate truths are dispositions, brought out by work, not a bare capacity.

*New Essays on Human Understanding*, Preface (finished c. 1704, shelved at Locke's death, published 1765; Langley's translation). Truths are innate "as inclinations, dispositions, habits, or natural potentialities, and not as actions". **Against Locke's premise 5:** Locke himself says ideas not being contemplated are "in our understandings" when we *can* revive them (II.x.2). **Against premise 6:** a disposition is not a bare capacity; the veins favour Hercules "rather than other figures". We use general maxims unconsciously, like the unstated premise of an enthymeme, as we use them "in walking and leaping" (*New Essays* I.i §19); the propositions of arithmetic and geometry are of this kind. **Lockean replies:** "never yet" leaves room for memory of what was once perceived; and what are the veins, other than a name for the truths the mind finds necessary, which intuition (IV.ii.1) handles without innate content? Not Platonic recollection: Leibniz calls reminiscence "wholly fabulous". Modern gloss only: a cousin of inductive bias ([statistical-learning 1.4](../statistical-learning/lessons/01-04-no-free-lunch-and-inductive-bias.md)).

*Lessons:* [3.1](lessons/03-01-locke-against-innate-ideas.md)

### Tabula rasa

Not Locke's phrase: the *Essay* says "white paper, void of all characters" (II.i.2) and "empty cabinet" (I.ii.15).

It is Leibniz who calls Locke's view the *tabula rasa* "of Aristotle", whose image of the intellect as an unwritten writing tablet is *De Anima* III.4, and rejects it. Leibniz's amendment to the scholastic maxim that nothing is in the intellect not first in the senses: *nisi ipse intellectus*, "except the intellect itself" (*New Essays* II.i; Langley: "you must except the soul itself and its affections").

*Lessons:* [3.1](lessons/03-01-locke-against-innate-ideas.md)

### Ideas of sensation and reflection

The only two "fountains" of ideas: the senses reporting external objects, and the mind noticing its own operations.

*Essay* II.i.2-5. **Sensation:** the senses reporting external things. **Reflection:** the mind's notice of its own operations, such as perceiving, doubting, willing ("internal sense"). "Idea" is broad: whatever is the object of the understanding when a man thinks (I.i.8). Hume's copy principle makes this thesis into a test ([4.1](lessons/04-01-impressions-ideas-and-the-fork.md)); Locke's consciousness, in personal identity, is reflection ([consciousness criterion](#consciousness-criterion)).

*Lessons:* [3.1](lessons/03-01-locke-against-innate-ideas.md)

### Simple and complex ideas

A simple idea is "one uniform appearance", received passively; complex ideas are built by combining simple ones.

II.ii.1-2: simple ideas (the coldness of ice) cannot be invented or destroyed by the mind. II.xii.1: complex ideas (beauty, a man, an army, gratitude) are made by combining. Hume's simple and complex perceptions follow the same division ([copy principle](#copy-principle)). The child who refuses wormwood and takes sugar perceives that two particular ideas differ; that is knowledge "about ideas, not innate, but acquired" (I.ii.15).

*Lessons:* [3.1](lessons/03-01-locke-against-innate-ideas.md)

### Rationalism and empiricism

Later textbook labels that fit the seventeenth century badly.

Locke grants innate faculties and grounds all certainty in intuition (IV.ii.1); Leibniz grants that experience is needed to occasion every thought and worked on dynamics, mining engineering, microscope observations; Berkeley says he proved his case "a priori" (*Principles* §21). The 3.1 split is over what "in the mind" means and what grounds necessity, not over whether experience matters. Use the labels as glosses and name the actual premise in dispute. Kant's a priori is about validity, not about when knowledge arrives.

*Lessons:* [3.1](lessons/03-01-locke-against-innate-ideas.md), [2.3](lessons/02-03-leibniz-sufficient-reason-and-monads.md), [3.4](lessons/03-04-berkeley-immaterialism.md)

### Corpuscular hypothesis

Bodies are insensible particles with bulk, figure, motion or rest and solidity, acting only by impulse.

Boyle's mechanical philosophy, *The Origin of Forms and Qualities* (1666); Locke names Boyle, Sydenham, Huygens and Newton in the Epistle to the Reader and describes himself as a labourer clearing ground for them. Bodies act on one another and on our organs only by impulse (II.viii.11-13). Locke calls it the most intelligible account but declines to rule on its truth (IV.iii.16), so the [resemblance thesis](#resemblance-thesis) is a conclusion drawn from it, not an observation.

*Lessons:* [3.2](lessons/03-02-locke-qualities-substance-and-essence.md)

### Primary and secondary qualities

Primary qualities are in bodies as we perceive them; secondary qualities are powers in bodies, grounded in the primary ones, to produce ideas (colour, taste, warmth) that resemble nothing there.

*Essay* II.viii. **Primary:** solidity, extension, figure, mobility (the grain of wheat, §9, each speck keeps them); lists elsewhere add bulk, number, situation, texture (§§17, 23). **Secondary:** "nothing in the objects themselves, but powers to produce various sensations in us" (§14): "nothing ... but" means *nothing except*. **Third sort:** powers to change other bodies (sun whitens wax, §23). Gutenberg's §10 is a placeholder. **Cases** (explained by the distinction, not its proof; §21 opens "Ideas being thus distinguished and understood"): fire warmth vs pain (§16); manna's sickness (§18); porphyry has no colour in the dark (§19); almond (§20); water hot to one hand, cold to the other (§21).

**Two readings of "colours are not in bodies":** **powers** (§8: a quality *is* the power; §14; §23) vs **in-the-mind** (§17: colours no more in snow than sickness in manna; §19). Note §17's qualifier (colours vanish *as particular ideas*) and §8's warning that "ideas in things" means the qualities that cause them. The lesson leaves the verdict open. A colour word has two jobs: naming an idea in us and a power in the thing. Kant presents his idealism as doing to extension what Locke's age did to colour (*Prolegomena* §13, Remark II).

*Lessons:* [3.2](lessons/03-02-locke-qualities-substance-and-essence.md)

### Resemblance thesis

Ideas of primary qualities resemble them, "and their patterns do really exist in the bodies"; ideas of secondary qualities resemble nothing there.

II.viii.15. Argued (as the lesson reconstructs it):

1. Every particle keeps solidity, extension, figure, mobility (§9).
2. Bodies act only by impulse (§11).
3. So bodies produce ideas only through the bulk, figure and motion of particles, carried by the nerves (§§12-13).
4. Ideas of colour, taste, warmth contain nothing of bulk, figure or motion (§25); God annexed them to motions they do not resemble, as pain to a cutting blade (§13).
5. A circle or square is the same in the idea as in the body (§18).
6. So primary-quality ideas resemble; secondary-quality ideas do not, and what corresponds is a power (§§14-15).

**Turns on premise 5**, which can never be checked by comparison if only ideas are perceived ([veil of ideas](#veil-of-ideas)). Berkeley: an idea can be like nothing but an idea ([likeness principle](#likeness-principle)).

*Lessons:* [3.2](lessons/03-02-locke-qualities-substance-and-essence.md), [3.4](lessons/03-04-berkeley-immaterialism.md)

### Substratum

An unknown support we suppose for qualities that go constantly together, because we cannot conceive them subsisting alone.

*Essay* II.xxiii.1-4; "a supposed I know not what". Our idea is relational only: whatever supports them. The Indian whose world rested on an elephant, the elephant on a tortoise, the tortoise on something he could not name (II.xiii.19, II.xxiii.2). Locke denies we have a *clear idea* of substance, not that there is one (replies to Stillingfleet, late 1690s), and he uses "substance" to frame personal identity. Berkeley removes it. Live problem: [metaphysics 2.1](../metaphysics/lessons/02-01-substance-and-accident.md).

*Lessons:* [3.2](lessons/03-02-locke-qualities-substance-and-essence.md), [3.3](lessons/03-03-locke-personal-identity.md), [3.4](lessons/03-04-berkeley-immaterialism.md)

### Real and nominal essence

Real essence: the hidden constitution on which a thing's observable qualities depend. Nominal essence: the complex idea its name stands for.

III.iii.15, III.vi.2. Gold's nominal essence: yellow, heavy, malleable, fusible, fixed. The real essence is unknown, as the Strasbourg clock's wheels to a gazing countryman (III.vi.3, 9). We sort by nominal essence: "men make sorts of things" (III.vi.35). **Gold that will not take the hammer** (III.vi.35): gold or not depends on whose abstract idea you consult (Locke). Leibniz (*New Essays* III.vi.35): "Not at all; they would determine only the name"; such a find teaches a fact about nature, and nominal definitions are provisional, aiming at the real essence. Locke: making "gold" stand for an unknown real essence is an abuse of words (III.x.17-18). **The crux:** does a sort-name stand only for our idea, or aim through it at the constitution? The causal theory of natural-kind terms is a modern gloss on Leibniz's side ([philosophy-of-language-and-logic](../philosophy-of-language-and-logic/syllabus.md) 3.3). Live version: [metaphysics 2.4](../metaphysics/lessons/02-04-essence-and-grounding.md).

*Lessons:* [3.2](lessons/03-02-locke-qualities-substance-and-essence.md)

### Veil of ideas

The worry that if we perceive only our own ideas, we can never check them against things. A critics' label, not Locke's.

Locke's own question (IV.iv.3): "How shall the mind, when it perceives nothing but its own ideas, know that they agree with things themselves?" His answer (IV.iv.4): simple ideas, which the mind cannot make, conform to their causes as regular effects. "Veil of perception" is Jonathan Bennett's term (*Locke, Berkeley, Hume: Central Themes*, 1971). Whether Locke holds a picture-and-original theory at all is disputed (Woozley 1964): are ideas objects we look at, or acts by which we perceive things? The label presupposes the first, so it cannot settle the dispute. Pressed by Berkeley ([likeness principle](#likeness-principle)) and blamed on the [way of ideas](#way-of-ideas) by Reid.

*Lessons:* [3.2](lessons/03-02-locke-qualities-substance-and-essence.md)

### Identity relative to the idea

"Same?" has no answer until you say same *what*: as the idea, so the identity.

*Essay* II.xxvii.7 (chapter added in the second edition, 1694, at Molyneux's urging). A **mass** of matter is the same while it has the same particles (a sand heap that loses a grain is not). An **oak**, animal or **man** is the same while one continued life runs through changing particles (§§3-6). A **person** is the same by sameness of consciousness. Premise 1 of Locke's argument for the [consciousness criterion](#consciousness-criterion).

*Lessons:* [3.3](lessons/03-03-locke-personal-identity.md)

### Man, person, substance

"One thing to be the same substance, another the same man, and a third the same person" (II.xxvii.7): three ideas, three identity conditions, which can come apart.

| Idea | Same while | Cases |
|---|---|---|
| Substance | Same particles, or same soul | Nestor's soul in another body |
| Man | One continued life of one organized body | Prince and cobbler: the prince's consciousness in the cobbler's body is the same person, not the same man (§15) |
| Person | Same consciousness | Little finger: cut off, no longer part of me; if consciousness went with it, it would be the person (§17) |

**Person** (§9): "a thinking intelligent being, that has reason and reflection, and can consider itself as itself, the same thinking thing, in different times and places".

*Lessons:* [3.3](lessons/03-03-locke-personal-identity.md)

### Consciousness criterion

The same person reaches as far back as the same consciousness extends, whether in one substance or several.

II.xxvii.9-10. Outline:

1. Identity is relative to the idea (§7).
2. Mass: same particles; living thing: same continued life (§§3-6).
3. "Person" = a thinking being that can consider itself as itself at different times (§9).
4. It does so only by consciousness, which accompanies all thinking (§9; II.i.19).
5. So the same person extends as far as the same consciousness extends backwards (§§9-10).
6. We do not know whether consciousness is annexed to one substance or several, and our concern follows consciousness (§17).
7. "Person" is forensic (§§18, 26).

So personal identity consists in sameness of consciousness, not substance and not the man. **Analogy:** consciousness is to a person as life to an animal. "Matters not at all" is agnosticism about the soul, not denial; the "more probable opinion" is one immaterial substance (§25). Resurrection made intelligible: the same person in a body not of the same parts (§15). **Turns on premise 5's "same consciousness".** Locke saw that present consciousness of a past act might misrepresent it (§13, answered by God's goodness). Butler (1736): consciousness of a past act *presupposes* identity, so cannot constitute it. Reid (*Intellectual Powers*, 1785, III.6), the brave officer: memory is not transitive, identity is. Defender, via appropriation: Locke answers the forensic question of what is *mine to answer for*. Assessment: [metaphysics 6.5](../metaphysics/lessons/06-05-what-a-person-is.md), [6.6](../metaphysics/lessons/06-06-personal-identity-over-time.md).

*Lessons:* [3.3](lessons/03-03-locke-personal-identity.md)

### Consciousness and memory readings

Does Locke's "same consciousness" of a past act mean remembering it, or owning it?

**Memory reading** (Reid; the textbook one): a memory criterion; evidence: wholly losing the memory of parts of one's life (§20), "repeat the idea of any past action". **Appropriation reading** (Winkler 1991; Thiel, *The Early Modern Subject*, 2011): consciousness is present reflexive self-awareness that owns acts; memory is one way of appropriating; evidence: consciousness of actions "past or to come" (§10), and memory reaches no future. The words settle that Lockean consciousness is reflexive and appropriative, not whether anything but memory carries it backwards. "Psychological continuity theory" is a twentieth-century repair of Reid's objection; calling Locke one imports the repair into the text.

*Lessons:* [3.3](lessons/03-03-locke-personal-identity.md)

### Forensic person

"Person" is "a forensic term, appropriating actions and their merit" (II.xxvii.26): the courtroom concept, for pinning deeds on someone.

Reward and punishment follow the person (§18). Human law goes by the man, punishing the drunk or the sleepwalker for what he does not remember, because it cannot prove a lack of consciousness (§22); at the Great Day, when the secrets of all hearts are opened, no one answers for what he knows nothing of (§§22, 26). Day-man and night-man (§23): one man, two persons. The lesson states the drunkard passage without assessing its fit with the forensic thesis.

*Lessons:* [3.3](lessons/03-03-locke-personal-identity.md)

### Abstract ideas

Locke: the mind makes general ideas by abstraction. Berkeley: one cannot conceive apart what cannot exist apart; generality is a particular standing for others of its sort.

Locke's triangle (*Essay* IV.vii.9, as Berkeley quotes it, Intro §13): "neither oblique nor rectangle, neither equilateral, equicrural, nor scalenon, but all and none of these at once". Berkeley (*Principles* Introduction §§6-16): he can imagine a hand apart from a body, but not a motion neither swift nor slow (Intro §10); a geometer's one-inch line stands for all lines (Intro §12); selective attention is allowed (Intro §16). Weapon for [esse est percipi](#esse-est-percipi): separating existence from being perceived is "a subtle piece of abstraction" (§5). Hume endorses the attack (*Treatise* I.i.7). Resemblance nominalism: [metaphysics 1.4](../metaphysics/lessons/01-04-nominalism-and-tropes.md).

*Lessons:* [3.4](lessons/03-04-berkeley-immaterialism.md)

### Immaterialism

There is no material substance; only spirits (active, perceiving) and ideas (passive, perceived) exist, and God causes the ideas of sense.

Berkeley, *Principles* (1710), *Three Dialogues between Hylas and Philonous* (1713, "in opposition to sceptics and atheists"). Sensible things are not denied but analysed as ideas: he denies only "that which philosophers call matter" (§35); "we eat and drink ideas, and are clothed with ideas" (§38). Philonous's charge: the materialist is the sceptic, since a world behind our ideas can never be checked. Disowns Malebranche's view as built on abstract ideas and an absolute external world (*Dialogue* II). Kant calls it "dogmatical idealism" (Refutation of Idealism) and reads it as making objects products of the imagination, a reading Berkeley rejected; transcendental idealism is not this ([transcendental idealism](#transcendental-idealism)).

*Lessons:* [3.4](lessons/03-04-berkeley-immaterialism.md)

### Esse est percipi

Of sensible ("unthinking") things, "their esse is percipi": to be is to be perceived. Spirits' being is to perceive (§139).

**Core argument** (*Principles* §§3-4):

1. Sensible things are things we perceive by sense.
2. What we immediately perceive by sense are only our own ideas or sensations ([way of ideas](#way-of-ideas)).
3. An idea's existence consists in being perceived (§2).
4. So sensible things cannot exist unperceived.

**The unperceived table, two analyses joined by "or":** counterfactual ("if I was in my study I might perceive it"; anticipates phenomenalism) or actual ("some other spirit actually does perceive it"; the *Dialogues* lean on God). The text does not choose. The table *exists*: Berkeley analyses "exists". The Latin tag is a later compression. **Turns on premise 2 and "idea".** Russell (*Problems*, 1912, ch. IV): "confusing the thing apprehended with the act of apprehension". Defender: the identification is deliberate (1710 text of §5: "the object and the sensation are the same thing"; *Dialogue* I: intense heat is one sensation with the pain), and *ad hominem* against Locke, who asserts the premise. The crux: is the immediate object of perception an idea? Locke and Berkeley yes, Reid no. Ronald Knox's limerick is twentieth-century and gets the theology wrong.

*Lessons:* [3.4](lessons/03-04-berkeley-immaterialism.md)

### Way of ideas

The shared premise that the mind's immediate objects are its own ideas. Reid calls it the "ideal system".

Locke, *Essay* IV.i.1: the mind has no immediate object but its own ideas. Hume: "nothing is ever really present with the mind but its perceptions or impressions and ideas" (*Treatise* I.ii.6). Berkeley uses it *ad hominem* against Locke. Reid (*Inquiry* I.7) traces it from Descartes through Locke and Berkeley to Hume and argues the scepticism is "inlaid in it"; he rejects the premise itself ([direct realism](#direct-realism)). Yolton (1984) disputes Reid's history. Russell charges an act/object confusion in "idea".

*Lessons:* [3.4](lessons/03-04-berkeley-immaterialism.md), [4.5](lessons/04-05-reid-common-sense-against-the-way-of-ideas.md)

### Ideas and spirits

Ideas are inert and cause nothing; spirits are active, and known by a notion, not an idea.

*Principles* §§25-28, 139-140. Spirits: understanding and will. I can raise ideas of imagination at will (§28); ideas of sense come regardless of my will (§29). **From ideas to God** (§§25-33):

1. Ideas are passive: no idea can cause anything (§25).
2. Our ideas of sense have a cause (§26).
3. So that cause is a substance, and not a material one (§26).
4. It is not my will (§29).
5. So another spirit causes them; their regular order shows its wisdom and goodness (§§30-32).

Hume keeps premise 1 and finds no more power in the will than in bodies, and no self beyond perceptions, so the spirits go too.

*Lessons:* [3.4](lessons/03-04-berkeley-immaterialism.md)

### Real things and chimeras

Both are ideas; ideas of sense are stronger, more orderly and coherent, and not dependent on my will.

*Principles* §§29-34. Their regular order is what we call the laws of nature (§30). That difference keeps the world from being a dream. Continued existence: some other spirit perceives (§48; *Dialogue* III, "during the intervals"). Berkeley's God perceives everything always, not by sense, and is no occasional watcher.

*Lessons:* [3.4](lessons/03-04-berkeley-immaterialism.md)

**Arguments in Module 3**

### Likeness principle

"An idea can be like nothing but an idea; a colour or figure can be like nothing but another colour or figure" (*Principles* §8).

Berkeley's block on Locke's retreat to originals that ideas copy: either the originals are perceivable, so ideas, or not, so nothing a colour could resemble. Joined with the **inseparability claim** of §10 (no extension or motion conceivable without colour or some sensible quality) it turns Locke's II.viii against him: Locke grants colours as perceived are in the mind, so extension, inseparable from them, is too. The relativity cases of [primary and secondary qualities](#primary-and-secondary-qualities) are turned against primary qualities as well. The crux of §§8-10 is whether the immediate object of perception is an idea.

*Lessons:* [3.4](lessons/03-04-berkeley-immaterialism.md)

### Master argument

Conceive one sensible thing existing unconceived, and Berkeley concedes; but conceiving trees in an empty park is still conceiving them.

*Principles* §§22-23 (the label is later). §23: "imagine trees, for instance, in a park, or books existing in a closet, and nobody by to perceive them". **Standard objection** (e.g. Pitcher, *Berkeley*, 1977): conceiving *that* a tree is unconceived is not conceiving an unconceived tree. Berkeley's reply rests on his imagist theory of conception. A conceivability test of the kind in [philosophical-method 3.3](../philosophical-method/lessons/03-03-thought-experiments.md).

### Impressions and ideas

All perceptions are impressions (forceful: sensations, passions) or ideas (fainter copies), differing in force and vivacity.

*Treatise* I.i.1 (1739); *Enquiry* §II (1748). Touching a hot stove is an impression; remembering it is an idea. Impressions come from sensation and from reflection (passions, inner feelings). Hume's "idea" is **narrower than Locke's**: he restores "the word, idea, to its original sense, from which Mr Locke had perverted it" (I.i.1 note); Locke's idea of sensation is Hume's impression. **Weak point:** vivacity as the only difference. Hume grants that in sleep, fever or madness ideas approach impressions; Reid: perceiving and imagining differ in kind (belief in a present object). Humean reply: that "belief" is extra vivacity under another name ([belief as vivacity](#belief-as-vivacity)).

*Lessons:* [4.1](lessons/04-01-impressions-ideas-and-the-fork.md), [4.3](lessons/04-03-induction-and-the-sceptical-solution.md)

### Copy principle

Every simple idea, in its first appearance, is copied from a resembling simple impression; complex ideas are compounded from simple ones. Used as a test: "from what impression is that supposed idea derived?"

**Argument and test** (*Treatise* I.i.1; *Enquiry* §II):

1. All perceptions are impressions or ideas, differing in force and vivacity.
2. Every simple idea is derived from a resembling simple impression (evidence: analyse any idea; the man born blind has no idea of colour).
3. The imagination only compounds, transposes, enlarges or diminishes what experience supplies (the golden mountain).
4. So every idea is copied from impressions or built from copies (§13).
5. A term means something only if an idea is annexed to it.
6. So for any suspect term, ask from what impression its idea comes; if none, the term probably has no meaning (§17).

The verdict is modest: failure "will serve to confirm our suspicion", shifting a burden; the target is "a philosophical term". Exception conceded: the [missing shade of blue](#missing-shade-of-blue). **Runs on:** necessary connexion (§49, [4.2](lessons/04-02-causation-and-necessary-connection.md)) and the self (I.iv.6, [4.4](lessons/04-04-the-self-belief-and-the-passions.md)). **Kant** denies its universality for pure concepts like cause, granting it for empirical concepts ([metaphysical deduction](#metaphysical-deduction)). Locke's white paper made into a test.

*Lessons:* [4.1](lessons/04-01-impressions-ideas-and-the-fork.md), [4.2](lessons/04-02-causation-and-necessary-connection.md), [4.4](lessons/04-04-the-self-belief-and-the-passions.md), [5.3](lessons/05-03-concepts-and-the-categories.md)

### Missing shade of blue

Hume's own counterexample: a man who has seen every shade of blue but one can supply the missing shade from its neighbours.

*Enquiry* §16; *Treatise* I.i.1. So simple ideas are "not always, in every instance, derived" from impressions. Hume concedes it and keeps the general maxim: the case "does not merit that for it alone we should alter our general maxim" (*Treatise*: "scarce worth our observing").

*Lessons:* [4.1](lessons/04-01-impressions-ideas-and-the-fork.md)

### Association of ideas

Ideas follow three principles of connection: resemblance, contiguity in time or place, cause and effect.

*Enquiry* §III. A picture leads to the original (resemblance), one room to the next (contiguity), a wound to the pain (cause and effect). "A kind of attraction" in the mental world (*Treatise* I.i.4), a nod to Newton. A claim about how the mind moves, not about what justifies belief. ¶41 ties custom to these principles; memory (resemblance) and causation produce the fiction of a continuing self.

*Lessons:* [4.1](lessons/04-01-impressions-ideas-and-the-fork.md), [4.3](lessons/04-03-induction-and-the-sceptical-solution.md), [4.4](lessons/04-04-the-self-belief-and-the-passions.md)

### Humes fork

All objects of reason are relations of ideas (known by thought alone; denial contradictory) or matters of fact (denial conceivable; known by experience, via cause and effect). The label is later; "fork" appears nowhere in Hume.

*Enquiry* §IV Part I (¶¶20-22):

1. All objects of human reason are relations of ideas or matters of fact.
2. Relations of ideas (geometry, algebra, arithmetic) are discoverable by thought alone, and their denial implies a contradiction (¶20).
3. The contrary of every matter of fact is conceivable, so none can be demonstrated; beyond present sense and memory, matters of fact are reached only through cause and effect, known by experience (¶22).
4. So a claim resting on neither abstract reasoning about quantity and number nor experimental reasoning about fact has no rational support.

The *Enquiry*'s close (§XII Part III, ¶132): of a volume of "divinity or school metaphysics", "Commit it then to the flames: for it can contain nothing but sophistry and illusion"; the same section keeps theology founded on faith and revelation, and morals and criticism as matters of taste and sentiment. The *Treatise* version sorts seven philosophical relations (I.iii.1). Leibniz had divided truths of reason and of fact (*Monadology* §33); Hume's novelty is the use. **Turns on premise 1, exhaustiveness:** Kant keeps "known by thought alone vs by experience" but denies it coincides with "denial contradictory vs not", so there is a third tine, the [synthetic a priori](#synthetic-a-priori); Kant also charges that Hume treated mathematics as analytic (*Prolegomena* §4 in Carus). Not 1930s verificationism ([anachronism](#anachronism)). In 4.3 it splits demonstrative from probable reasoning.

*Lessons:* [4.1](lessons/04-01-impressions-ideas-and-the-fork.md), [4.3](lessons/04-03-induction-and-the-sceptical-solution.md), [5.1](lessons/05-01-the-critical-question.md)

### Constant conjunction

Like objects always followed by like objects: what repetition adds to contiguity and priority.

Changes nothing in the objects; it produces a habit in the observer (*Enquiry* ¶59). Basis of the first [definition of cause](#two-definitions-of-cause). Hume grants that contiguity, succession and regular conjunction hold independently of the understanding (*Treatise* I.iii.14).

*Lessons:* [4.2](lessons/04-02-causation-and-necessary-connection.md)

### Custom or habit

A propensity, produced by repetition, to renew an act without being impelled by any reasoning: "the great guide of human life".

*Enquiry* §V Part I ¶36: "wherever the repetition of any particular act or operation produces a propensity to renew the same act or operation, without being impelled by any reasoning or process of the understanding, we always say, that this propensity is the effect of *Custom*." Defined by the absence of reasoning, so not a covert argument; "ultimate reason" means ultimate *cause*. Works in infants and animals (the burned child, ¶33). Not social convention or laziness: a mechanism of the imagination tied to [association](#association-of-ideas) (¶41). **Two jobs from one mechanism:** it makes the inference from experience ([4.3](lessons/04-03-induction-and-the-sceptical-solution.md)) and is the impression of reflection from which the idea of necessity is copied ([4.2](lessons/04-02-causation-and-necessary-connection.md)). One-experiment cases are handled "in an oblique and artificial manner", via a habitual general principle that like causes produce like effects (*Treatise* I.iii.8). In 4.4: belief, a lively idea produced by custom, survives the despair of I.iv.7 because nature, not argument, sustains it.

*Lessons:* [4.2](lessons/04-02-causation-and-necessary-connection.md), [4.3](lessons/04-03-induction-and-the-sceptical-solution.md), [4.4](lessons/04-04-the-self-belief-and-the-passions.md)

### Necessary connection

The tie that would make an effect follow its cause. Hume finds no impression of it in bodies, the will or God; the idea is copied from the mind's felt customary transition and projected onto objects.

"An object may be contiguous and prior to another, without being considered as its cause. There is a NECESSARY CONNEXION to be taken into consideration" (*Treatise* I.iii.2). "They seem *conjoined*, but never *connected*" (*Enquiry* ¶58). Necessity "is something, that exists in the mind, not in objects" (*Treatise* I.iii.14); the mind's "great propensity to spread itself on external objects", as we locate sounds and smells in visible objects. Hume reasons causally throughout: he denies an *idea* of necessity as a quality of bodies, not that things cause things. **Kant** grants that no necessity appears in perceptions (*Prolegomena* §27) but denies it comes from habit: Hume "mistook the subjective necessity of habit for an objective necessity arising from insight" (*Prolegomena* preface, Carus). Search argument: [Hume's search for the impression](#humes-search-for-the-impression). Live regularity-vs-powers debate: [metaphysics 4.1](../metaphysics/lessons/04-01-humes-challenge-and-the-regularity-theory.md).

*Lessons:* [4.2](lessons/04-02-causation-and-necessary-connection.md), [5.5](lessons/05-05-the-second-analogy.md)

### Two definitions of cause

D1 (regularity): an object followed by another, where all objects similar to the first are followed by objects similar to the second. D2 (mind): an object followed by another, whose appearance always conveys the thought to that other.

*Enquiry* ¶60; *Treatise* I.iii.14 gives versions with contiguity and priority, presented as causation viewed as a *philosophical* relation (D1) and as a *natural* relation (D2), "the same object in two views". Whether they are equivalent, and which is prior, is disputed. Modern regularity theories descend from D1 alone; Hume's account always includes D2. "Humean supervenience" is David Lewis's term. Close reading is owned by [metaphysics 4.1](../metaphysics/lessons/04-01-humes-challenge-and-the-regularity-theory.md) (its Boss 4).

*Lessons:* [4.2](lessons/04-02-causation-and-necessary-connection.md)

### New Hume

Did Hume believe in real powers in objects that we cannot conceive, or is talk of necessity in objects empty?

| Reading | Who | Evidence |

| **New Hume / sceptical realist** | Wright, *The Sceptical Realism of David Hume* (1983); G. ### Uniformity principle

The supposition that the future will resemble the past, which every inference from experience presupposes.

*Enquiry* §IV (¶32). Neither demonstrable (its contrary, the sun not rising tomorrow, is conceivable) nor supportable by probable reasoning without circularity. Reid turns it into a first principle: the future will probably be like the past in similar circumstances ([first principles of common sense](#first-principles-of-common-sense)). Custom does its work *instead of* supplying it. "Induction" never occurs in the *Enquiry*, and only in passing in the *Treatise*; "the problem of induction" is a later label. Live problem and answers: [epistemology 4.2](../epistemology/lessons/04-02-humes-problem-of-induction.md), [4.3](../epistemology/lessons/04-03-answering-hume.md).

*Lessons:* [4.3](lessons/04-03-induction-and-the-sceptical-solution.md), [4.5](lessons/04-05-reid-common-sense-against-the-way-of-ideas.md)

### Belief as vivacity

Belief differs from fiction in manner, not content: "a more vivid, lively, forcible, firm, steady conception of an object, than what the imagination alone is ever able to attain" (*Enquiry* ¶40).

*Treatise* I.iii.7: a lively idea related to or associated with a present impression. The vivacity of the impression flows along the habitual path to the associated idea, and that borrowed vivacity *is* the believing (¶38). As unavoidable as love on receiving a benefit. **Reid's objection** (*Inquiry*, 1764, ch. II §5): the man who firmly believes there is *no* future state: if his idea of it is faint, firm belief goes with a faint idea; if lively, believing and disbelieving are one state. So belief is assent, not vivacity. Hume half-concedes in ¶40: the feeling can be described, not perfectly explained.

*Lessons:* [4.3](lessons/04-03-induction-and-the-sceptical-solution.md)

### Sceptical solution

*Enquiry* §V: custom, not reasoning, takes us from past to future; an explanation of the step plus a reason it cannot be argued away, not a justification.

**Positive argument** (¶¶33-40):

1. No argument takes the mind from a present impression to the idea of its usual attendant (§IV).
2. Yet the mind makes the step uniformly, in philosophers, peasants, infants and beasts (¶33).
3. If not argument, some other principle of equal weight and authority must induce it (¶34).
4. A propensity produced by repetition without reasoning is called custom (¶36).
5. The step appears only after repetition: a thousand instances yield what one, no different, does not (¶36).
6. So all inferences from experience are effects of custom (¶36).
7. Belief is a livelier, steadier conception ([belief as vivacity](#belief-as-vivacity), ¶40).
8. So belief in unobserved matters of fact is the joint effect of a present impression and a customary conjunction (¶38).

**Readings:** **sceptical** (Reid, Beattie: no rational ground; the title, ¶34); **naturalist** (Kemp Smith 1905/1941, Stroud 1977: clears ground for a science of human nature; belief among natural instincts, ¶38); **descriptivist** (Garrett 1997, Owen 1999: "reason" names inference through intermediate ideas, so §IV is about how belief is produced; §VI still ranks some inferences as *proofs*); **sceptical realist** (Wright 1983: unknown powers, and custom stands in for insight; ¶44's "pre-established harmony" between nature and our ideas). What the words settle: §V explains rather than justifies. What they leave open: whether ¶28's "*not* founded on reasoning" is a verdict on justification. **Pressure:** the theory of custom is itself believed by custom; defender: consistency, not regress. Modern gloss: custom as inductive bias ([statistical-learning 1.4](../statistical-learning/lessons/01-04-no-free-lunch-and-inductive-bias.md)).

*Lessons:* [4.3](lessons/04-03-induction-and-the-sceptical-solution.md)

### Bundle theory

The mind is "nothing but a bundle or collection of different perceptions, which succeed each other with an inconceivable rapidity" (*Treatise* I.iv.6); the identity we ascribe to it is a fiction of the imagination.

**Argument** (I.iv.6):

1. Every real idea is derived from some one impression.
2. The self, as philosophers describe it, is simple and the same through life.
3. So its impression would have to be constant and invariable.
4. No impression is constant and invariable.
5. So we have no idea of self in that sense.
6. Reflection finds only particular perceptions: "I always stumble on some particular perception or other" (the stumble passage).
7. Every perception is distinct and separable and may exist separately, needing no support.
8. So the mind is a bundle, and its identity is the imagination's work, along resemblance and causation.

The **theatre**, with its caveat: no stage, only the perceptions. Memory supplies resemblance; causation binds like the members of a **republic**, which survives changes of citizens and laws; hard cases are grammatical, not philosophical. Memory *discovers* rather than produces identity (against Locke). Hume denies an idea of a *simple, invariable* self, not the self we ascribe. The stumble passage's two claims differ in strength (never *without* a perception; never anything *but* the perception), and its close concedes another may perceive a simple self. Whether the view rests on the stumble experiment, the copy argument, or both is left to the boss problem. **Replies:** Reid (*Intellectual Powers* VI.5): thoughts have a thinker, a first principle; he mocks a succession of ideas that "eats and drinks, and is sometimes merry". Kant: the "I think" is a condition of having perceptions as one's own, not an object one stumbles on (B134, "as many-coloured and various a self"; Kant does not name the *Treatise*). Nietzsche continues the critique ([critique of the subject](#critique-of-the-subject)).

*Lessons:* [4.4](lessons/04-04-the-self-belief-and-the-passions.md), [5.4](lessons/05-04-the-transcendental-deduction.md)

### Appendix problem

In the 1740 Appendix Hume keeps the negative arguments but finds his account of what binds perceptions "very defective".

He cannot render consistent two principles: that all perceptions are distinct existences, and that the mind never perceives any real connexion among distinct existences (the second is [4.2](lessons/04-02-causation-and-necessary-connection.md)'s result). The labyrinth concerns the positive half, the "principle of connexion" uniting the bundle. Readers divide on what exactly fails (e.g. Garrett 1981).

*Lessons:* [4.4](lessons/04-04-the-self-belief-and-the-passions.md)

### Title Principle

"Where reason is lively, and mixes itself with some propensity, it ought to be assented to. Where it does not, it never can have any title to operate upon us" (*Treatise* I.iv.7). The name is Don Garrett's.

Follows the despair at the end of Book I: reason examining itself leaves Hume unable to prefer any opinion; reason cannot dispel the clouds, nature does; he dines, plays backgammon, talks with friends, and finds his speculations "cold" and "ridiculous". What survives is this rule for which reasonings deserve assent.

*Lessons:* [4.4](lessons/04-04-the-self-belief-and-the-passions.md)

### Mitigated scepticism

What is left of excessive doubt once common sense and reflection correct it: modesty in judgment, enquiry confined to common life.

*Enquiry* §12 Part 3. Excessive (Pyrrhonian) scepticism cannot be lived: "Nature is always too strong for principle" (Part 2). Not a half-measure between doubt and belief. Hume's alternative to the Cartesian "antecedent" doubt, which would be "entirely incurable" (§12 Part 1). Reid's answer to the total sceptic, that such distrust cannot be lived, is the same point.

*Lessons:* [4.4](lessons/04-04-the-self-belief-and-the-passions.md), [1.1](lessons/01-01-the-method-of-doubt.md)

### Reason and the passions

"Reason is, and ought only to be the slave of the passions, and can never pretend to any other office than to serve and obey them" (*Treatise* II.iii.3).

**Argument** (II.iii.3; III.i.1):

1. Reason discovers truth or falsehood, about relations of ideas or matters of fact.
2. Demonstrative reasoning alone never causes action; probable reasoning only directs an impulse from the prospect of pain or pleasure.
3. So reason alone is never a motive to the will.
4. Only a contrary impulse can oppose a passion.
5. So reason alone can never oppose a passion.
6. Moral distinctions excite passions and produce or prevent actions.
7. So moral distinctions are not conclusions of reason.

A passion is an original existence, neither true nor false; "unreasonable" only derivatively, when it rests on a false supposition of existence or chooses insufficient means, and then strictly the judgment is unreasonable. Reason keeps a real job: finding causes and means. **Targets of 1-2:** the rationalist moralists (Samuel Clarke's eternal fitnesses, Wollaston), for whom perceiving moral relations can move the will; Hume's reply is premise 4. "Humean theory of motivation" and "instrumental rationality" are modern labels. Promising as artificial obligation: [philosophy-of-debt 1.2](../philosophy-of-debt/lessons/01-02-hume-promises-as-artificial-obligation.md).

*Lessons:* [4.4](lessons/04-04-the-self-belief-and-the-passions.md)

### Is-ought passage

A reason "should be given, for what seems altogether inconceivable", how an *ought* is deduced from an *is* (*Treatise* III.i.1, closing paragraph).

Follows the inertness argument (reason inert, morals move us, so morals not conclusions of reason). A challenge, not a proof; "fact-value distinction" is later vocabulary. Close reading: [ethics 4.5](../ethics/lessons/04-05-the-new-natural-law-theory.md); metaethics: [ethics 6.1](../ethics/lessons/06-01-moral-realism.md).

*Lessons:* [4.4](lessons/04-04-the-self-belief-and-the-passions.md)

### Sensation and perception

A sensation has no object distinct from the act of feeling it (pain); a perception has an object distinct from the act, which may exist unperceived (the table's hardness).

Reid, *Inquiry* (1764) VI.20; the two are "extremely unlike in their natures". A pressure-feeling no more resembles cohesion than pain resembles the point of a sword; no sensation resembles any quality of body; the pillar example (V.2). The distinction the way of ideas blurred. Not naive realism: nothing in the rose resembles the smell.

*Lessons:* [4.5](lessons/04-05-reid-common-sense-against-the-way-of-ideas.md)

### Natural signs

By an original principle of our constitution, a sensation suggests a conception of a quality and a belief in its present existence, without resemblance or inference, as a word suggests its meaning to a fluent speaker.

*Inquiry* V.3. Not by resembling (Berkeley was right that a feeling can be like only a feeling) and not by inference (Berkeley and Hume were right that no argument runs from feelings to bodies). Reid also says natural causes are better called natural signs, keeping real efficiency for agents with active power (*Essays on the Active Powers*, 1788), which puts him near Malebranche on bodies.

*Lessons:* [4.5](lessons/04-05-reid-common-sense-against-the-way-of-ideas.md)

### Direct realism

Modern label for Reid's view that in perception the object is the quality in the body, not an idea. Reid's word is "immediate".

Perception has three parts (*Intellectual Powers*, 1785, II.5): a conception of the object, an irresistible belief in its present existence, and immediacy (the belief is not reached by reasoning). **Argument against the ideal system** (*Inquiry* V.2-7, VI.20; IP II.5):

1. A sensation has no object distinct from the act of feeling it.
2. No sensation resembles any quality of body.
3. We have clear conceptions of extension, figure, motion, hardness.
4. So these conceptions are neither sensations nor copies of sensations.
5. They are given by our constitution: a sensation suggests a conception and belief it does not resemble.
6. That belief arises without reasoning.
7. So the object of perception is the quality in the body; and since the belief never rested on inference, proofs that the inference fails do not touch it.

**Turns on premise 4**, Reid's *experimentum crucis* (V.7). Hume reported the opposite: the idea of extension copies coloured points in an arrangement (*Treatise* I.ii.3); two introspective reports, no neutral judge. Yolton (1984): for Arnauld, and arguably Descartes and Locke, "idea" often names the act of perceiving, so the "ideal system" may fit Malebranche, Berkeley and Hume better than its founders. Hamilton (1846 edition notes) called Reid's doctrine "vacillating"; direct and mediated readings divide. Not naive realism. Live arguments from illusion: [philosophy-of-mind](../philosophy-of-mind/syllabus.md) 4.4-4.5.

*Lessons:* [4.5](lessons/04-05-reid-common-sense-against-the-way-of-ideas.md)

### First principles of common sense

Judgments believed as soon as understood, neither needing nor admitting proof; "common sense" is the power that judges them.

*Intellectual Powers* VI.5: twelve first principles of **contingent** truths, among them that what I am conscious of exists, that what I distinctly remember happened, that my thoughts are those of a being I call myself, that what I distinctly perceive exists, that we have some power over our actions, that the natural faculties "are not fallacious", that testimony deserves some regard, and that in nature the future will probably be like the past in similar circumstances. VI.6: first principles of **necessary** truths: logical and mathematical axioms, and metaphysical ones such as that whatever begins to exist has a cause. "Not fallacious" is not "infallible": faculties are limited and can be disordered (the amputee's toes; the oar). **Kant** (*Prolegomena* preface) names "Reid, Oswald, Beattie, and lastly Priestley" as missing Hume's point and consulting common sense like an oracle. Testimony: [epistemology 4.4](../epistemology/lessons/04-04-testimony.md).

*Lessons:* [4.5](lessons/04-05-reid-common-sense-against-the-way-of-ideas.md)

### Humes search for the impression

Hume looks for the impression of necessary connexion in bodies, in the will, and in God, finds none, and finds it only in the mind's felt transition.

*Enquiry* §§49-61 (¶ numbers); *Treatise* I.iii.2, I.iii.14:

1. Every idea is copied from an impression (¶49).
2. We have an idea of necessary connexion (power, force, energy).
3. No single instance of body acting on body gives the impression; if it did we could predict the effect at first sight (¶50).
4. The will's command over the limbs gives none: the union is mysterious; we move the tongue not the heart; the newly paralysed man wills as hard; the will's immediate effect is in muscles and spirits we know nothing of (¶52).
5. The will's command over ideas gives none (¶53).
6. God gives none: our idea of God is built from reflection on our own faculties (¶57).
7. Many instances differ from one only in the habit they produce (¶59).
8. So the impression is the felt customary transition, and the idea is copied from it (¶¶59, 61).
9. So necessity belongs to the mind's determination, projected onto objects (I.iii.14).

**Source and crux:** the *Treatise* I.iii.14 footnote sends the reader to Malebranche; both assume a [true cause](#true-cause) would show a perceived necessary connexion; the crux is premise 6 (Malebranche perceives it in God's will, Hume nowhere). **Weak point, premise 8:** how can a *feeling* be the impression of *necessity*? (Stroud, *Hume*, 1977). Defender: Hume claims the only content "necessity" can have is that determination, not that the feeling represents a tie. **Reid** attacks premise 4 (a notion of active power from our own exertions); **Kant** attacks the outcome (habit gives only subjective necessity, [Second Analogy](#second-analogy)). Newton's *vis inertiae* and gravity are names for observed effects (¶57 note). Premise 5 is aimed at Berkeley's claim to raise ideas at will.

*Lessons:* [4.2](lessons/04-02-causation-and-necessary-connection.md)

### Parity argument

Reason and perception "came both out of the same shop, and were made by the same artist" (*Inquiry* VI.20), so reason has no credentials perception lacks.

1. The sceptic trusts reason and requires perception's verdicts to be certified by reason.
2. Reason, perception, memory and consciousness are natural faculties from the same source.
3. Any proof that a faculty is reliable must use some faculty, taking the faculty's testimony for its own truthfulness; Descartes's proof of a non-deceiving God begs the question this way (IP VI.5; compare the [circle](#cartesian-circle)).
4. So either distrust every faculty and stop arguing, or trust perception on the terms on which you trust reason.

IP VI.5 makes "the natural faculties ... are not fallacious" a first principle; VI.20 adds that it is not in his power to give the belief up. **Weak point, premise 1:** Hume is not that sceptic (*Treatise* I.iv.1, "Of scepticism with regard to reason"); against a sceptic who distrusts reason too, Reid answers only that such distrust cannot be lived, which Hume had said himself. The "same shop" line is the *Inquiry*'s, and it is not Plantinga's parity argument ([philosophy-of-religion 5.2](../philosophy-of-religion/lessons/05-02-reformed-epistemology.md)). The crux with Hume is reserved for the module boss problem.

### A priori and a posteriori

A priori: independent of *all* experience, marked by necessity and strict universality. A posteriori: possible only through experience, with only comparative universality.

Kant, B Introduction (B1-4). The man who undermines his house "knows a priori" it will fall only *relatively*, since he learned that bodies are heavy. **Not "innate":** all knowledge *begins* with experience without all *arising out of* it (B1); the a priori concerns how a judgment is justified, not when the knower acquired it. Kant rejects a "preformation-system" of implanted aptitudes because their necessity would be merely subjective, "just what the sceptic wants" (B167-168). Contrast Leibniz's [veined marble](#veined-marble). Live question (apriority vs necessity, Kripke): [epistemology 4.1](../epistemology/lessons/04-01-a-priori-knowledge.md).

*Lessons:* [5.1](lessons/05-01-the-critical-question.md)

### Analytic and synthetic

Analytic: the predicate is (covertly) contained in the subject concept, so analysis yields it. Synthetic: the predicate adds something the concept did not contain.

Introduction §IV. "All bodies are extended" (analytic, explicative); "all bodies are heavy" (synthetic, augmentative). Analytic judgments rest on the principle of contradiction. Stated for affirmative subject-predicate judgments. "A triangle has three angles" is analytic a priori; "gold is a yellow metal" is analytic, though "gold" is an empirical concept (*Prolegomena* §2b). Containment means what we *actually think* in the concept, though obscurely, not what we must join to it. **Not Hume's fork:** Hume has two bins sorted by how a claim is known; Kant has two distinctions that cut across each other. Reading "analytic" as "true by meaning" is a twentieth-century gloss; Quine ("Two Dogmas", 1951) called containment a metaphor. Leibniz's [predicate-in-subject](#predicate-in-subject-principle) survives here only as the mark of analytic truth.

| | A priori | A posteriori |
|---|---|---|
| **Analytic** | All bodies are extended | Empty: an analytic judgment needs nothing beyond its concept |
| **Synthetic** | 7 + 5 = 12; the angle sum; quantity of matter conserved | All bodies are heavy |

*Lessons:* [5.1](lessons/05-01-the-critical-question.md)

### Synthetic a priori

Necessary yet ampliative: the fourth cell, "How are synthetical judgements à priori possible?" (Introduction §VI).

What joins predicate to subject is "the unknown = X". Kant's occupants: arithmetic (**7 + 5 = 12**: the concept of the sum contains only the uniting of seven and five, not which number; add units in intuition, fingers or points), geometry (the angle sum; the straight line is the shortest; two sides of a triangle exceed the third), pure physics (quantity of matter conserved). Metaphysics *aspires* to it (the world must have a beginning). In the Aesthetic, geometry is the lead case, explained by space as the form of outer sense ([expositions of space](#the-expositions-of-space)). **Critics:** Frege (*Foundations of Arithmetic*, 1884): arithmetic analytic (provable from logic plus definitions), geometry synthetic a priori; a Kantian replies Frege changed the test from containment to provability. **Nietzsche** (*Beyond Good and Evil* §11): Kant's answer, "by means of a means (faculty)", is like the *virtus dormitiva*; replace the question with "Why is belief in such judgments necessary?": they preserve life and might be false ([perspectivism](#perspectivism)).

*Lessons:* [5.1](lessons/05-01-the-critical-question.md), [5.2](lessons/05-02-space-and-time-the-transcendental-aesthetic.md), [6.5](lessons/06-05-nietzsche-perspectivism-and-genealogy.md)

### Copernican revolution

Instead of assuming cognition conforms to objects, assume objects, as known, conform to our cognition, as an "experiment".

B preface, Bxvi (Meiklejohn): "Let us then make the experiment whether we may not be more successful in metaphysics, if we assume that the objects must conform to our cognition." The analogy is about **the observer, not centrality**: Copernicus explained apparent motions by the spectator's motion. Offered as a hypothesis to be judged by what it explains. Whether "objects" means things in themselves or objects of possible experience is [6.1](lessons/06-01-transcendental-idealism-and-the-thing-in-itself.md)'s question. Applied to objecthood in the deduction's definition of "object" (B137). In 6.5 it is the Kantian side of the crux: a condition necessary for any experience of ours is valid of every object of experience, since objects are appearances; Nietzsche denies that necessity-for-us implies truth.

*Lessons:* [5.1](lessons/05-01-the-critical-question.md), [5.4](lessons/05-04-the-transcendental-deduction.md), [6.5](lessons/06-05-nietzsche-perspectivism-and-genealogy.md)

### Transcendental method

Inquiry into the a priori conditions of the possibility of experience: "all knowledge which is not so much occupied with objects as with the mode of our cognition of these objects, so far as this mode of cognition is possible à priori" (Introduction §VII).

Argues backwards from a fact taken as given (geometry is synthetic a priori) to the conditions of its possibility; the last step of the transcendental exposition ("Obviously not otherwise") is an inference to the only explanation Kant sees, not a deduction. **Not "transcendent"** (overstepping experience); one result of the transcendental inquiry is that theoretical knowledge cannot be transcendent. **The critical question** (Introduction, prefaces), outline:

1. Metaphysics aims at a priori judgments that extend knowledge.
2. Analytic judgments only explicate.
3. Experience gives only comparative universality.
4. So metaphysics is possible only if synthetic a priori judgments are.
5. Hume showed, for cause, that neither analysis nor experience yields its necessity.
6. Pure mathematics is synthetic a priori too, so Hume's reasoning would destroy it.
7. Pure mathematics and natural science exist.
8. So synthetic a priori knowledge is actual; the question is how.
9. If objects as known conform to cognition, a priori knowledge is intelligible.
10. So test that hypothesis by asking what the a priori conditions of experience are.

**Weak points:** premise 7 is regressive (Kant: only the *Prolegomena* proceed from the given sciences; the *Critique* proceeds synthetically, *Prolegomena* §4); premise 6 (Frege). Transcendental arguments' gap between what we must think and how things are: Stroud 1968 ([transcendental deduction](#transcendental-deduction)).

*Lessons:* [5.1](lessons/05-01-the-critical-question.md), [5.2](lessons/05-02-space-and-time-the-transcendental-aesthetic.md)

### Intuition and concept

Intuition: singular and immediate, given by receptive sensibility. Concept: general and mediate, from the spontaneous understanding.

The graduated list (Dialectic Book I §1). Two stems, which "probably spring from a common, but to us unknown root" (Introduction). "Thoughts without content are void; intuitions without conceptions, blind" (A51/B75, Meiklejohn). Space is an intuition because "spaces" are *parts* in one space, not instances under a kind (metaphysical exposition, third argument). Space and time are forms of intuition, not categories; mixing them erases the two stems.

*Lessons:* [5.2](lessons/05-02-space-and-time-the-transcendental-aesthetic.md), [5.3](lessons/05-03-concepts-and-the-categories.md)

### Empirical reality and transcendental ideality

Everything we meet outwardly really is in space; abstracted from our sensibility, space is nothing.

Aesthetic §4 (Meiklejohn): "We maintain, therefore, the empirical reality of space in regard to all possible external experience, although we must admit its transcendental ideality"; the same for time (§§5-7). "Transcendental" concerns the conditions of knowing, not whether bodies exist: bodies exist as appearances. **Not Berkeley's illusion:** §8 argues that treating space as a thing in itself is what would excuse Berkeley for making bodies illusions. **Not Locke's secondary qualities:** colour is "quite insufficient" to illustrate it. Whether Kant's view finally differs from Berkeley's is [6.1](lessons/06-01-transcendental-idealism-and-the-thing-in-itself.md)'s question; the neglected-alternative objection continues there.

*Lessons:* [5.2](lessons/05-02-space-and-time-the-transcendental-aesthetic.md), [6.1](lessons/06-01-transcendental-idealism-and-the-thing-in-itself.md)

### Synthesis

"The process of joining different representations to each other and of comprehending their diversity in one cognition" (Meiklejohn).

An act of imagination, brought to unity by concepts of the understanding. Combination is never given by the senses; it is a spontaneous act of the understanding (B130), and it underlies both the identity of the I and the relation to an object. The melody of five notes: heard by five minds, no melody. Strawson (1966) called the doctrine unverifiable transcendental psychology. "The binding problem" is a modern gloss; Kant's synthesis is not a claim about brain processes.

*Lessons:* [5.3](lessons/05-03-concepts-and-the-categories.md), [5.4](lessons/05-04-the-transcendental-deduction.md)

### Table of judgments

The forms every judgment has once content is stripped away: four heads of three moments (A70/B95).

| Heading | Forms of judgment |
|---|---|
| Quantity | universal, particular, singular |
| Quality | affirmative, negative, infinite |
| Relation | categorical, hypothetical, disjunctive |
| Modality | problematical, assertorical, apodeictical |

Infinite judgment affirms a negative predicate ("non-mortal"). Spellings are Meiklejohn's. Its completeness is asserted, not proved ([metaphysical deduction](#metaphysical-deduction)). Term logic; quantifier logic does not sort judgments this way ([mathematical-logic 2.1](../mathematical-logic/lessons/02-01-quantifiers-syntax.md)).

*Lessons:* [5.3](lessons/05-03-concepts-and-the-categories.md)

### Categories

Twelve pure concepts of an object in general, one per form of judgment, valid only for objects of possible experience (A80/B106).

| Heading | Categories |
|---|---|
| Quantity | unity, plurality, totality |
| Quality | reality, negation, limitation |
| Relation | inherence and subsistence (substance and accident); causality and dependence (cause and effect); community (reciprocity of agent and patient) |
| Modality | possibility-impossibility; existence-non-existence; necessity-contingence |

In each triad the third combines the first two (totality is plurality taken as unity) yet needs its own act; readers dispute how the Quantity rows pair. **Not Aristotle's ten** ([ancient-medieval-philosophy 3.1](../ancient-medieval-philosophy/lessons/03-01-predication-categories-and-the-syllogism.md)): Aristotle's are highest kinds of predicate gathered without a principle, mixing in forms of sensibility (when, where, position) and an empirical concept (motion); Kant's are derived from judgment. Not learned early or innate (see [a priori](#a-priori-and-a-posteriori)). The antinomies and paralogisms follow the four headings.

*Lessons:* [5.3](lessons/05-03-concepts-and-the-categories.md), [5.4](lessons/05-04-the-transcendental-deduction.md)

### Schematism

A category cannot be seen, so a schema, a rule-governed determination of time, mediates between pure concept and intuition.

Causality is not "contained in the phenomenon". Schemata: **substance** = permanence of the real in time; **cause** = succession subject to a rule; **necessity** = existence at all times. Meiklejohn's "schema of reality" under Modality means *actuality* (German *Wirklichkeit*), not the Quality category. Without schemata the categories have no use except on appearances. The schema of cause is premise 6 of the [Second Analogy](#second-analogy).

*Lessons:* [5.3](lessons/05-03-concepts-and-the-categories.md), [5.5](lessons/05-05-the-second-analogy.md)

### Quid facti and quid juris

The jurists' question of fact (how you came to possess a concept) versus question of right (whether you hold title to use it). A "deduction" answers the right.

A84-85/B116-117. Locke's "physiological derivation" of ideas answers only the question of fact. Empirical concepts ("dog") need no deduction: experience shows instances. "Usurped" concepts (fortune, fate) lack one. Pure concepts claiming necessity (cause) need a *transcendental* deduction, since no history of acquisition shows title. Compare Hume's question of right and question of fact about induction ([sceptical solution](#sceptical-solution)).

*Lessons:* [5.4](lessons/05-04-the-transcendental-deduction.md)

### Unity of apperception

"The I think must be able to accompany all my representations" (B131-132): a representation I could never take as mine is, for me, nothing.

Meiklejohn's first sentence reads "must accompany", but the same paragraph restores "capable of accompanying" (German *muss ... begleiten können*). **Analytic unity** (the same I) presupposes **synthetic unity** (combination, an act, never given by sense; B133). **Transcendental apperception:** pure, identical, "a thought, not an intuition": "I am conscious of myself, not as I appear to myself, nor as I am in myself, but only that 'I am'" (B157). **Empirical apperception:** inner sense, "fragmentary and disunited". **Object** = "that, in the conception of which the manifold in a given intuition is united" (B137). Not Descartes's thinking substance: Kant keeps the certainty that I am and denies it is knowledge of what I am; in the paralogisms the I is a transcendental subject = x known only through its thoughts ([paralogisms](#paralogisms)). "Transcendental ego" is Husserl's later label ([phenomenology-and-personalism](../phenomenology-and-personalism/syllabus.md) 1.3). Against Hume's [bundle](#bundle-theory): B134's "as many-coloured and various a self"; the contrast is ours, Kant names no target.

*Lessons:* [5.4](lessons/05-04-the-transcendental-deduction.md), [6.2](lessons/06-02-the-soul-and-the-antinomies.md)

### Analogies of Experience

Experience is possible only through the representation of a necessary connection of perceptions; three analogies, one per mode of time.

| Analogy | Mode of time | Principle | MFNS (1786) pairing |
|---|---|---|---|
| First | Permanence | Substance persists through all change | Conservation of matter |
| Second | Succession | All alterations follow the law of cause and effect | Inertia |
| Third | Coexistence | Coexisting substances stand in reciprocity (community) | Action equals reaction |

**"Regulative" in the Analytic's sense:** the Analogies give a priori the *relation* to a fourth member, not the member itself, so they cannot construct existence; the mathematical principles (Axioms, Anticipations) are "constitutive". This is **not** the Dialectic's regulative use of ideas ([regulative and constitutive](#regulative-and-constitutive)). Friedman (*Kant and the Exact Sciences*, 1992) reads the Analogies through Newton.

*Lessons:* [5.5](lessons/05-05-the-second-analogy.md)

### Objective succession

An event: a succession of states in the object, as against the merely subjective succession of apprehension, which is always successive.

**House:** order of apprehension reversible (roof to foundation or back); parts coexist in space; no before and after. **Ship floating downstream:** order fixed; "by this order apprehension is regulated"; "I must ... deduce the subjective sequence of apprehension from the objective sequence of phenomena". Kant: only a rule can fix the event's order. Simultaneous cause and effect (leaden ball on a cushion; the fire and the warm room): the order of time holds without a lapse. Modern echo, not anticipation: relativity's invariant causal order ([relativity 1.4](../relativity/lessons/01-04-spacetime-interval-causality.md)).

*Lessons:* [5.5](lessons/05-05-the-second-analogy.md)

**Arguments in Module 5**

### The expositions of space

Space is not learned from experience, is necessary, is one whole not a kind, and is given as infinite; and geometry's synthetic a priori status shows it is the form of outer sense.

**Metaphysical exposition** (Aesthetic §2, B text): **M1** not empirical (to represent things as outside me and beside one another I already need space); **M2** necessary ("we never can imagine ... the non-existence of space, though we may easily enough think that no objects are found in it"); **M3** an intuition, not a concept (one space, "spaces" are its parts); **M4** an infinite given magnitude (infinitely many parts *in* it, not *under* it). **Transcendental exposition** (§3):

1. Geometry determines properties of space synthetically and a priori.
2. No proposition beyond a concept follows from the concept alone; so geometry rests on an intuition.
3. Geometry is necessary, which experience cannot teach; so the intuition is pure.
4. An outer intuition prior to objects that fixes in advance how they must be is possible only as the form of the subject's being affected.
5. So space is the form of outer sense, and geometry holds a priori of everything that can appear outwardly.

**Conclusions** (§4): space represents no property of things in themselves ([empirical reality and transcendental ideality](#empirical-reality-and-transcendental-ideality)). Time runs in parallel as the form of inner sense (§§5-7). **Incongruent counterparts** (*Prolegomena* §13; used for absolute space in 1768). **Weak point, the neglected alternative:** why not *also* a feature of things in themselves? (Pistorius, Trendelenburg; Fischer defended Kant). Kant (§4a): no determination of a thing in itself can be intuited before it is given; critic: that shows only we could not *know* it. **Non-Euclidean geometry** (Bolyai, Lobachevsky c. 1830; Riemann 1854; general relativity 1915): readings are strong (GR a counterexample), pure vs physical geometry, a thinner a priori (Helmholtz 1870; Reichenbach's relativized a priori, 1920), and conventionalism (Poincaré, 1902). The course does not adjudicate; M1-M4 never mention Euclid.

*Lessons:* [5.2](lessons/05-02-space-and-time-the-transcendental-aesthetic.md)

### Metaphysical deduction

The categories' a priori origin, shown by their agreement with the logical functions of judgment (name from B159).

1. The understanding uses concepts only to judge.
2. Every judgment is a function of unity.
3. The functions of unity in judgment fall under four heads of three, and the list is complete (A70/B95).
4. Cognizing an object requires synthesizing the manifold and bringing the synthesis to unity by concepts.
5. "The same function which gives unity to the different representation in a judgement, gives also unity to the mere synthesis of different representations in an intuition" (A79/B104-5).
6. So for each logical function there is a pure concept of an object in general: twelve categories.
7. So the list is complete and systematic, drawn from one principle.

Cause is the hypothetical form of judging turned on intuition, so not copied from any impression. **Weak points: premises 3 and 5.** Completeness asserted, not proved; Hegel (*Phenomenology*, Baillie): an outrage "to pick up the various categories again in any sort of way as a kind of happy find, hit upon, e.g. in the different judgments"; Körner (1955), Strawson (1966). Defenders: an inventory of acts of one capacity to judge (Reich 1932; Longuenesse, Eng. 1998). Why a logical function yields a concept *of objects* is earned, if anywhere, in the [transcendental deduction](#transcendental-deduction). Not the transcendental deduction, which concerns right, not origin.

*Lessons:* [5.3](lessons/05-03-concepts-and-the-categories.md)

### Transcendental deduction

Shows by what right the categories apply a priori to objects: they are conditions of the possibility of experience, because they are the rules by which one self combines its representations.

The problem it answers: appearances might lie "in such confusion" that "cause" is empty (A90/B123). **B deduction** (1787), two steps (Henrich 1969): §§15-20 for intuition in general, §26 for our space-time intuition.

1. The "I think" must be able to accompany all my representations (§16, B131-132).
2. I am conscious of myself as the same only if I combine them (B133).
3. Combination is never given by sense; it is an act of the understanding (§15, B130).
4. An object is that in whose concept a manifold is united (§17, B137).
5. The act that brings a manifold under one apperception is the logical function of judgment (§19; "the body is heavy" vs "when I carry a body I feel weight").
6. The categories are these functions, taken as determining intuition (§20).
7. So every manifold in one intuition, so far as it is mine, stands under the categories (§20, B143).
8. All our intuition is in space and time, whose unity is itself a synthesis; so all apprehension, even perception, is subject to the categories (§26, B160-161).
9. So the categories hold a priori of every object of possible experience, and of nothing else (§§26-27).

**A deduction** (1781): threefold synthesis; subjective vs objective deduction. Cost Kant "by far the greatest labour". **Weak points:** premises 5-6 (why *these* twelve? Kant, B145-146: as impossible to explain as "why we are endowed with precisely so many functions of judgement and no more") and premise 8. Strawson (*The Bounds of Sense*, 1966): keep an objectivity argument, drop the transcendental psychology and the list. Stroud ("Transcendental Arguments", 1968): at most what we must *think*, not how things *are*. Kantian reply via premise 9: conditions of experience are conditions of its objects (A158/B197), at the price of [transcendental idealism](#transcendental-idealism). "Deduction" is legal title, not logical derivation.

*Lessons:* [5.4](lessons/05-04-the-transcendental-deduction.md)

### Second Analogy

"All changes take place according to the law of the connection of Cause and Effect" (A189/B232, Meiklejohn, B text); A text, paraphrased: everything that happens presupposes something on which it follows by a rule.

**B proof:**

1. Apprehension of any manifold is always successive.
2. Time itself cannot be perceived.
3. So successive apprehension leaves undetermined whether the states succeed in the object.
4. To represent an event is to represent an order that binds apprehension ([objective succession](#objective-succession)).
5. For appearances, such an order can come only from a rule: something in the preceding state on which the later follows necessarily.
6. Such a rule is the concept of cause, schematized as succession subject to a rule ([schematism](#schematism)).
7. So experience of any event is possible only if all alterations of appearances stand under cause and effect.

**Reply to Hume:** grants that no necessity appears in perceptions (*Prolegomena* §27), denies it comes from habit, since perceiving a succession in objects already uses the concept; categories are vindicated "not by deriving them from experience, but by deriving experience from them" (§30). **What it does not show:** particular laws ("experience must be superadded", B165); anything about things in themselves; whether it touches [induction](#uniformity-principle) is left to the boss problem. **Weak point, 4 to 5:** Lovejoy (1906); Strawson (1966): a slide from the conditional necessity of my perceptions (given that the ship moved) to causal necessity. Schopenhauer (*Fourfold Root* §23, 1847): things follow *upon* without following *from* (the falling tile, day and night); Kantian reply: the rule concerns something in the preceding state, not the earlier perceived item, at the cost of saying little in any case. **Defenders:** Allison (1983: what it takes to *judge* a succession objective); Guyer (1987: knowing an objective succession requires knowing a law). Necessity is not "in the mind" in Hume's sense: a rule of the understanding, not a feeling bred by repetition.

*Lessons:* [5.5](lessons/05-05-the-second-analogy.md)

### Transcendental idealism

Everything we can know is appearance, empirically real and transcendentally ideal; things in themselves are thinkable, not knowable.

Definition, Antinomy Section VI (A490-494/B518-522, Meiklejohn): objects of possible experience are "nothing but phenomena, that is, mere representations" with "no self-subsistent existence apart from human thought"; their "non-sensuous cause" is unknown. Kant's note calls it **formal** (vs material) idealism; the *Prolegomena*, "critical" idealism. "Transcendental" concerns the conditions of knowing, not the existence of bodies. **Assembled argument:**

1. Space and time are forms of our intuition, not properties of things in themselves.
2. Cognition needs intuition and concept.
3. The categories have cognitive use only on what is given in space and time.
4. So we cognize objects only as appearances.
5. What is given under our forms is not, as such, how anything is apart from them.
6. So we have no cognition of things in themselves.
7. Yet an appearance must be an appearance of something (Bxxvi).
8. So things in themselves must be thought, though not cognized.

**Second route:** the indirect proof from the mathematical [antinomies](#antinomies) (the world is neither finite nor infinite, so not a whole in itself). **Not Berkeley's:** bodies exist outside us in space as appearances of something unknown; space is an a priori form, not an idea of sense; Aesthetic §8 blames Berkeley's idealism on treating space as a thing in itself. Kant calls Berkeley's view "dogmatical idealism". No veil: nothing is claimed to *resemble* the thing in itself.

*Lessons:* [6.1](lessons/06-01-transcendental-idealism-and-the-thing-in-itself.md), [6.2](lessons/06-02-the-soul-and-the-antinomies.md)

### Thing in itself

Whatever appears, taken apart from the conditions under which it can appear to us: cannot be cognized, but must be thought.

Otherwise there would be "an appearance, without something that appears", which Kant calls absurd (Bxxvi). Empirical vs transcendental "in itself": the rainbow is appearance relative to the rain, which is "thing in itself" only empirically; both are appearances transcendentally (Aesthetic §8). **Hegel's target:** the instrument/medium picture of knowledge and the claim that a limit known as a limit is already passed ([determinate negation](#determinate-negation), [the limit argument](#the-limit-argument)). **Nietzsche's target:** Plato's true world grown "pale, northern, Königsbergian" ([true world fable](#true-world-fable)); GM I §13 lists it among the changelings of "the subject". Fichte (1794) and Schelling (1800) dropped it.

*Lessons:* [6.1](lessons/06-01-transcendental-idealism-and-the-thing-in-itself.md), [6.4](lessons/06-04-hegel-dialectic-and-spirit.md), [6.5](lessons/06-05-nietzsche-perspectivism-and-genealogy.md)

### Noumenon negative and positive

Negative: a thing so far as it is not an object of our sensuous intuition, a limiting concept. Positive: an object of an intellectual intuition we lack, and inadmissible.

B307 (Meiklejohn): "If, by the term noumenon, we understand a thing so far as it is not an object of our sensuous intuition, thus making abstraction of our mode of intuiting it, this is a noumenon in the negative sense". It is "merely a limitative conception and therefore only of negative use". A division into a sensible and an intelligible world is inadmissible in a positive sense. **Kantian reply to Hegel's limit objection:** to know our intuition is sensible needs only the thought that some other kind is possible, not knowledge of what lies beyond; Kant claims to know the *character* of our knowing, not what lies past it. Hegel's side: even that credits knowledge with a truth about the unknowable.

*Lessons:* [6.1](lessons/06-01-transcendental-idealism-and-the-thing-in-itself.md), [6.4](lessons/06-04-hegel-dialectic-and-spirit.md)

### Two-world and two-aspect readings

Are appearances and things in themselves two sets of things, or one set considered two ways?

| Reading | Claim | Texts | Who | Main problem |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Two-world** | Numerically distinct: appearances are representations (or law-governed systems of them); things in themselves their unknown non-spatial grounds | Section VI's "mere representations"; phenomena not given in perception do not exist at all | Strawson, *The Bounds of Sense* (1966), roughly, rejecting the doctrine so read | Must call things in themselves *causes* and count them, which the restriction of the categories forbids |
| **Two-aspect** | One set of things considered as they appear and as they are in abstraction from our forms | B preface: one object "in two senses"; Bxviii-xix note, "double point of view" | Allison, *Kant's Transcendental Idealism* (1983), after Bird (1962), Prauss (1974) | Kant says space is *nothing* in itself, a claim about the things; and the doctrine risks becoming a truism |
| **Intrinsic/relational** | Same things; we know relational properties, not intrinsic ones | | Langton, *Kantian Humility* (1998) | |

The crux: the numerical identity of appearance and thing in itself. The Source sentence feeds both ("mere representations" vs "as presented to us"). Langton's reading is a cousin of epistemic structural realism ([philosophy-of-science](../philosophy-of-science/syllabus.md) 5.3).

### Affection problem

Sensibility is receptive, so something affects it; Kant says things in themselves do, but cause is a category valid only for appearances.

Texts: "I grant by all means that there are bodies without us ... we yet know by the representations which their influence on our sensibility procures us" (*Prolegomena* §13, Remark II, Carus); Section VI's "non-sensuous cause". **Jacobi** (appendix to *David Hume on Faith*, 1787; paraphrased): without the thing in itself one cannot enter the system, with it one cannot stay inside. Schulze, *Aenesidemus* (1792); Adickes's double affection (1929). **Reply:** the categories may be used to *think* without contradiction, though not to cognize, so "cause" of the thing in itself is a thought, not knowledge. **Rejoinder:** "there are bodies without us" asserts existence. Fichte (1794) and Schelling (1800) dropped the thing in itself.

*Lessons:* [6.1](lessons/06-01-transcendental-idealism-and-the-thing-in-itself.md)

### Transcendental illusion

Reason takes its subjective demand (if the conditioned is given, the whole series of its conditions is given) for an objective principle; natural, unavoidable, and persisting after exposure.

Like the astronomer who still sees "the moon larger at its rising" (Meiklejohn), it does not vanish when exposed, unlike a fallacy. Yields three ideas: the soul (unconditioned subject), the world (unconditioned totality of appearances), God (unconditioned condition of all things). Kant's 1798 letter to Garve (paraphrased): the antinomy, not God or immortality, first woke him.

*Lessons:* [6.2](lessons/06-02-the-soul-and-the-antinomies.md)

### Regulative and constitutive

In the Dialectic: an idea used **constitutively** claims an object answers to it (illegitimate, transcendental illusion); used **regulatively** it is a rule to seek ever further conditions and treat no empirical limit as absolute.

Regress *in indefinitum* (ancestors of the living) vs *in infinitum* (division of a given body). For God: philosophize "as if there existed a necessary primal basis of all existing things, solely for the purpose of introducing systematic unity into your knowledge" (Ideal s.V); "a mere ideal, though a faultless one" (s.VII). **A different doctrine in the Analytic:** the [Analogies of Experience](#analogies-of-experience) are "regulative" as against the "constitutive" mathematical principles (Axioms, Anticipations), meaning they fix relations of existence a priori but cannot construct the related term.

*Lessons:* [6.2](lessons/06-02-the-soul-and-the-antinomies.md), [6.3](lessons/06-03-the-critique-of-the-proofs-of-god.md), [5.5](lessons/05-05-the-second-analogy.md)

### Transcendental ideal

Complete determination of any thing presupposes the idea of a sum of all reality; reason objectifies, hypostatizes and personifies it as the *ens realissimum*, "God in its transcendental sense".

Ideal of Pure Reason s.II. Negations are limitations of reality (a blind man has no notion of darkness without light). The step from idea to existing individual is unwarranted. A second pressure joins it: from "something exists" reason infers that something exists *necessarily*, and seeks a concept fit to bear necessity; the most real being looks like the best candidate. Kept with a regulative use. Kant's 1763 *Only Possible Argument* already held existence is not a predicate and offered an a priori proof from possibility, which the *Critique* also rejects.

*Lessons:* [6.3](lessons/06-03-the-critique-of-the-proofs-of-god.md)

### Three speculative proofs

From determinate experience (physico-theological), indeterminate experience (cosmological), or no experience (ontological): "more there cannot be".

Ideal s.III. Kant examines them in reverse order, because the idea of reason, not experience, steers all three. Claim: the physico-theological rests on the cosmological, and the cosmological on the ontological ([conversion objection](#conversion-objection), [architect not creator](#architect-not-creator)). What remains: speculative reason can neither prove nor disprove a supreme being; the moral argument (*Critique of Practical Reason*, 1788) is [philosophy-of-religion 3.3](../philosophy-of-religion/lessons/03-03-moral-arguments.md). Not an argument for atheism.

*Lessons:* [6.3](lessons/06-03-the-critique-of-the-proofs-of-god.md)

### Paralogisms

Rational psychology's four claims about the soul from the bare "I think", one per category heading; Kant grants each as analytic of the I in thought and denies it as synthetic of the thing that thinks.

| Claims | Kant grants (about thought) | Kant denies (about the object) |
|---|---|---|
| Substance | In judging, the I is always subject, never predicate | That I am a self-subsistent thing |
| Simple | The I of apperception is logically simple | That the thinking I is a simple substance |
| Identical over time | One and the same I in all my representations | Identity of a persisting substance (personality) |
| Distinct from bodies | I distinguish myself from things outside me, my body included | That I could exist as a thinking being without a body |

**First paralogism** (B text): "That which cannot be cogitated otherwise than as subject, does not exist otherwise than as subject, and is therefore substance. A thinking being, considered merely as such, cannot be cogitated otherwise than as subject. Therefore it exists also as such, that is, as substance." Valid form; the middle term shifts between beings as given in intuition (major) and the I only in thought (minor): *sophisma figurae dictionis*. Substance yields knowledge only on something permanent in intuition, and inner sense gives nothing permanent; the I is a transcendental subject = x. Materialism is equally insufficient. Mendelssohn's *Phaedon* (1767) refuted by intensive magnitude (the soul's powers could fade by "elanguescence"). Targets: Descartes's [thinking thing](#thinking-thing) and [divisibility argument](#divisibility-argument) (second paralogism); Locke's person (third). Kant's footnote: "I exist" cannot be inferred from "I think" "as Descartes maintained", since they are identical; that is Kant's reading, not a verdict on the cogito.

*Lessons:* [6.2](lessons/06-02-the-soul-and-the-antinomies.md)

### Antinomies

Four conflicts of reason over the world as a whole, one per category heading; each side proved indirectly, and both assume the world is a whole given in itself.

| | Thesis | Antithesis |
|---|---|---|
| 1 (quantity) | The world has a beginning in time and limits in space | Neither; infinite in both |
| 2 (quality) | Every composite consists of simple parts | Nothing in the world is simple |
| 3 (relation) | Besides natural causality there is a causality of freedom | Everything happens by laws of nature alone |
| 4 (modality) | An absolutely necessary being exists | None exists |

**Diagnosis:** (1) both sides assume transcendental realism; (2) on it the pairs are contradictories and each proof valid; (3) drop it, and the pairs relate by kind of synthesis. **Mathematical (1-2):** both false; dialectical, not analytic, opposition ("every body smells good" / "every body smells bad", both false of a body with no smell). **Dynamical (3-4):** both may be true (a heterogeneous, intelligible condition). Kant calls the second thesis principle "the dialectical principle of Monadology". **Indirect proof of [transcendental idealism](#transcendental-idealism)** from 1-2. **Weak point:** it needs every proof valid. Schopenhauer: the thesis proofs are sophisms; Russell (*Problems*, 1912, ch. XIV): after Cantor, infinite collections are "not in fact self-contradictory". Kantian reply: the thesis says an infinite series cannot be *completed by successive synthesis*, which concerns how a world could be given, not sets. Not a contradiction in things: reason's conflict with itself. Ancestor of the kalam traversal argument ([philosophy-of-religion 2.5](../philosophy-of-religion/lessons/02-05-cosmological-arguments-the-kalam.md)). Hegel takes such contradictions as thought's engine.

*Lessons:* [6.2](lessons/06-02-the-soul-and-the-antinomies.md)

### Third antinomy

Thesis: a causality of freedom besides natural causality. Antithesis: "There is no such thing as freedom, but everything in the world happens solely according to the laws of nature."

**Resolution as the lesson gives it:** every event in nature has a preceding cause by a rule (the [Second Analogy](#second-analogy)), so the antithesis holds without exception of appearances; the same agent may also be thought under an **intelligible character**, as thing in itself not in time, so not subject to the law that every beginning has a prior cause; the act is necessary as appearance and may be free in relation to its intelligible ground. "If phenomena are things in themselves, freedom is impossible." Kant claims to show neither the reality nor even the possibility of freedom, only its consistency with nature. Reopens Spinoza's question ([2.2](lessons/02-02-spinoza-mind-body-and-necessity.md)). Ethics: [ethics Module 2](../ethics/lessons/02-01-the-good-will-and-acting-from-duty.md); determinism live: [metaphysics 6.1](../metaphysics/lessons/06-01-determinism-and-the-consequence-argument.md). Nietzsche's genealogy of the free noumenal self: GM I §13.

*Lessons:* [6.2](lessons/06-02-the-soul-and-the-antinomies.md)

### Determinate negation

A refutation is not "pure nothingness" (the sceptic's result) but the nothing "of what it comes from", so it has a content, and a new shape of consciousness arises from it.

Hegel, *Phenomenology* Introduction (Baillie). Consciousness tests its knowledge by its own standard: it distinguishes what it *knows* of an object from what the object *is in itself*, both within consciousness. When they fail to match, knowledge and object both change; this movement is "experience". A road of despair for natural consciousness: unlike Descartes's doubt, which suspends beliefs to recover them, it never returns to its start. Example: [sense-certainty](#sense-certainty).

*Lessons:* [6.4](lessons/06-04-hegel-dialectic-and-spirit.md)

### Aufhebung

*Aufheben*: everyday senses "abolish" and "keep" (literally "pick up"); Baillie: "to supersede (*aufheben*) is at once to negate and to preserve" (Perception chapter).

Elsewhere translated "sublate". What happens to a failed shape of consciousness: negated and kept. **Not compromise**, and not combination with an opposite brought in from outside. Contrast **abstract negation** (death in the life-and-death struggle), which cancels without preserving. "Raise" is a literal sense commentators add, not a third sense in Hegel's gloss.

*Lessons:* [6.4](lessons/06-04-hegel-dialectic-and-spirit.md)

### Geist

Spirit (Baillie: "Mind"): reality as an activity that develops and comes to know itself; after self-consciousness learns its dependence on others, an "Ego that is 'we,' a plurality of Egos, and 'we' that is a single Ego."

Preface: grasp the truth "not as Substance but as Subject as well" (against Spinoza's inert one substance; Baillie's note); "The truth is the whole" (a result is true only with the path that produced it, as the fruit needs the blossom). **Readings:** cosmic subject coming to know itself (Charles Taylor, *Hegel*, 1975; the Preface's talk of the life of God) vs the self-correcting social practice of giving reasons (Pippin, *Hegel's Idealism*, 1989; Pinkard, *Hegel's Phenomenology: The Sociality of Reason*, 1994; the "Ego that is 'we'"). The words do not settle it. Hegel's "phenomenology" (knowledge as it appears) is not Husserl's descriptive method.

*Lessons:* [6.4](lessons/06-04-hegel-dialectic-and-spirit.md)

### Sense-certainty

The *Phenomenology*'s first shape: it claims immediate knowledge of the singular *this*, *here*, *now*, and finds by its own test that what it knows is a universal.

Write down "the Now is night-time"; at noon the truth "has turned stale". The Now persists as neither night nor day: a universal. Pointing yields "a Now which is any number of Nows". By [determinate negation](#determinate-negation), the truth of sense-certainty is the universal it can only *say*, not the singular it *meant*; next shape: perception (things as bearers of universal properties).

*Lessons:* [6.4](lessons/06-04-hegel-dialectic-and-spirit.md)

### Lordship and bondage

Self-consciousness needs recognition by another; a life-and-death struggle ends with one side preferring life (bondsman) and the other master; then the reversal.

*Phenomenology*, Self-consciousness A. If one dies, nothing is proved: death is abstract negation. **Reversal:** the master's recognition comes from a consciousness reduced to a thing, so is worthless to him, and his enjoyment passes; the bondsman, through fear, service and labour ("desire restrained and checked"), finds himself in what he shapes. Next shapes: Stoicism, scepticism, the unhappy consciousness. Strain: which side yields looks contingent. **Readings:** Kojève's anthropological/historical (Paris lectures 1933-39, pub. 1947: desire for recognition drives history) vs a stage in self-consciousness's education with no historical thesis. The text supports the reversal's structure, not whether it is history, logic or both. Hegel's political philosophy: [history-of-political-thought 6.1](../history-of-political-thought/lessons/06-01-hegel-freedom-realized-in-the-state.md).

*Lessons:* [6.4](lessons/06-04-hegel-dialectic-and-spirit.md)

### Thesis antithesis synthesis

Not Hegel's formula for his method.

Kant's antinomies pair thesis and antithesis; the triadic vocabulary is Fichte's; Heinrich Moritz Chalybäus popularized it as a description of Hegel; Gustav Mueller's 1958 article calls the attribution a legend. Hegel's Preface praises Kant's "triplicity" (the third-from-first-two pattern of the categories) as rediscovered by instinct but attacks its use as "a lifeless schema". A shape breaks down on its own terms; it is not combined with an opposite from outside, and *Aufhebung* is not compromise.

*Lessons:* [6.4](lessons/06-04-hegel-dialectic-and-spirit.md)

### True world fable

*Twilight of the Idols*, "How the 'True World' ultimately became a Fable: the history of an error" (Ludovici 1911): six stages from Plato's true world to its abolition, with the apparent world.

1. Plato: the sage lives in it. 2. Christianity: promised to the penitent. 3. Kant: unattainable, unprovable, "yet even as a thought, alone, it is a comfort, an obligation, a command"; "pale, northern, Königsbergian" (the Dialectic plus the practical postulates). 4. Positivism: unknown, so it binds no one. 5. Free spirits abolish it. 6. Noon: "In abolishing the true world we have also abolished the world of appearance!" Stage 6 is not phenomenalism: "appearance" stops meaning less than real. Clark (1990) reads stage 6 as Nietzsche's own retraction of the claim that our knowledge falsifies. "Kant (after all a Christian in disguise)" is polemic.

*Lessons:* [6.5](lessons/06-05-nietzsche-perspectivism-and-genealogy.md)

### Critique of the subject

"I think" is no immediate certainty but an interpretation that grammar adds to a process.

*BGE* §16: "I think" packs in that it is *I* who think, that something must think, that thinking is an agent's activity, that I know what thinking is. §17: "a thought comes when 'it' wishes"; the agent is supplied by the grammatical formula that every activity requires an agent. *TI*: "I fear we shall never be rid of God, so long as we still believe in grammar." GM I §13: lightning and its flash; the neutral free subject behind the strong is a belief the weak need. Extends Lichtenberg's "it thinks" against the [cogito](#cogito); a cousin of Hume's [bundle](#bundle-theory).

*Lessons:* [6.5](lessons/06-05-nietzsche-perspectivism-and-genealogy.md)

### Perspectivism

"There is only a seeing from a perspective, only a 'knowing' from a perspective" (*GM* III §12, Samuel): more eyes on "the same thing" make a more complete "objectivity".

Rejects the will-less, painless, timeless knowing subject: an eye looking in no direction. **Argument against the Kantian settlement** (*BGE* §§11, 16-17; *TI*; *GM* III §12; the numbering is the lesson's):

1. Kant answers "how is synthetic a priori knowledge possible?" with a faculty.
2. Naming a faculty restates the question (*virtus dormitiva*).
3. What can be explained is why creatures like us must believe such judgments: they preserve life.
4. Being necessary for us does not make a belief true ("might naturally be false").
5. "I think" is an interpretation grammar adds.
6. The true world is unknown, and an unknown standard does no work.
7. So all knowing is perspectival, and objectivity is the range of perspectives brought to bear.

**Crux with Kant: 3 to 4** (Kant: necessary-for-experience is valid of every object of experience, since objects are appearances; [Copernican revolution](#copernican-revolution)). **Weak point:** premise 4 sits badly with 6: "false" needs something to be false *of*, the very standard 6 discards (Clark, *Nietzsche on Truth and Philosophy*, 1990). Premise 1 contested: Kant gives a transcendental argument, not a bare faculty; Nietzsche's defender: §11 aims at Kant's faculty-hunting successors. **Readings:** about how knowing is conditioned (Clark, Leiter) vs denial of truth. "No facts, only interpretations" is an unpublished notebook line; "anything goes" a later relativism. Self-reference objection.

*Lessons:* [6.5](lessons/06-05-nietzsche-perspectivism-and-genealogy.md)

### Death of God

A cultural diagnosis: the Christian God has become unbelievable, and what rested on that belief will collapse.

*Gay Science* §125 (1882; Common, *Joyful Wisdom*, 1910): a madman with a lantern tells laughing unbelievers "we have killed him", and that the deed has not yet reached them. §343 (Book V, added 1887): the God has become unbelievable. Nietzsche's atheism is not in dispute; the dispute is whether §125 *argues* for atheism (its text offers no premises about God's existence) or reports an event and asks what survives it. Sociology of secularization: [social-theory 7.1](../social-theory/lessons/07-01-classical-secularization-theory.md).

### Genealogy

A critique of values needs knowledge of the conditions under which they grew: "the value of these values is for the first time to be called into question" (*GM* Preface §6).

**Steps:** (i) a value treated as unconditioned; (ii) its descent; (iii) its function for the type of life that held it; (iv) the question of its value now. First Essay: noble "good" begins as self-affirmation, "bad" an afterthought; slave "good" is born when resentment becomes creative and first says no to the strong as "evil" (I §10). **Origin is not validity:** treating it so is the genetic fallacy; history is a tool for the question of value, not a refutation. Genealogies can vindicate (Williams, *Truth and Truthfulness*, 2002). A genealogy of the subject (I §13) explains a belief's appeal; to show it false needs the grammar argument of *BGE* §17. The Second Essay's *Schuld* (guilt as debt) belongs to [philosophy-of-debt 4.1](../philosophy-of-debt/lessons/04-01-the-animal-with-the-right-to-make-promises.md); evolutionary debunking: [ethics 6.4](../ethics/lessons/06-04-constructivism-and-debunking.md).

### Being is not a real predicate

"Being is evidently not a real predicate, that is, a conception of something which is added to the conception of some other thing" (A598/B626); it is "merely the positing of a thing".

**Kant's replies to the ontological proof** (the defender: the *ens realissimum* has all reality, existence is a reality, so denying it contradicts the concept):

1. Rejecting subject and predicates together yields no contradiction: "Annihilate its existence in thought, and you annihilate the thing itself with all its predicates" (Descartes's triangle turned around: geometric necessity binds a predicate only once the subject is posited).
2. "This thing exists" is analytic (an empty tautology, existence put in at the start) or synthetic (deniable without contradiction).
3. Being is a logical predicate only, not a real one (a determination that enlarges a concept): a hundred real "dollars" (Meiklejohn for *Taler*) contain no more than a hundred possible ones.
4. So no concept contains its object's existence; knowledge of existence comes only through connection with perception.

Footnote aimed at Leibniz: logical possibility (no contradiction) is not real possibility. **Misquotation:** not "existence is not a predicate" simpliciter. Kant's necessity is not "true in all possible worlds" (modern gloss). Target: [Cartesian ontological argument](#cartesian-ontological-argument), "the celebrated ontological or Cartesian argument". Whether it defeats Anselm: [philosophy-of-religion 2.1](../philosophy-of-religion/lessons/02-01-anselms-ontological-argument.md); the Kant-Frege line vs the Thomist real distinction: [metaphysics 2.5](../metaphysics/lessons/02-05-essence-and-existence.md).

*Lessons:* [6.3](lessons/06-03-the-critique-of-the-proofs-of-god.md)

### Conversion objection

The cosmological proof's *nervus probandi*, "every absolutely necessary being is an *ens realissimum*", converts into a claim settled by concepts alone, which is the ontological argument's premise.

**The proof** (Leibniz, from contingency):

1. If something exists, an absolutely necessary being exists.
2. I, at least, exist.
3. So an absolutely necessary being exists.
4. A necessary being must be completely determined by its concept alone.
5. Only the concept of the *ens realissimum* determines a thing completely a priori.
6. So every absolutely necessary being is an *ens realissimum*.

6 converts (per accidens): some *ens realissimum* is necessary; since no *ens realissimum* differs from another, every one is: "exactly what was maintained in the ontological argument". Experience took the argument only to *some* necessary being. Further fault: "everything contingent has a cause" has meaning only within sensible experience. Kant concedes that "we are free to consider all limited beings as likewise unconditionally necessary". **Where it turns:** the dependence claim. Defender: the cosmological proof gets existence from experience (2) and only *shares a premise* with the ontological, not its inference; Kant: the shared premise inherits the failure. Second defence: necessity as having "the reason of its existence in itself" (*Monadology* §45), not conceptual necessity; Kant's charge holds only if that collapses into his sense. *Monadology* §40's step to a being "illimitable" with "as much reality as is possible" rests on the [PSR](#principle-of-sufficient-reason). The gap problem: [philosophy-of-religion 2.4](../philosophy-of-religion/lessons/02-04-cosmological-arguments-sufficient-reason.md).

*Lessons:* [6.3](lessons/06-03-the-critique-of-the-proofs-of-god.md)

### Architect not creator

The design analogy with "a house, a ship, or a watch" shows at most a contingent *form*, so at most an architect limited by his material, not a creator.

Ideal s.VI, on the physico-theological proof. "Very great" or "immeasurable" power and wisdom fix no determinate concept; only all reality does. To get there the physico-theologian leaps to the world's contingency and falls back on the cosmological proof, and behind it the ontological. Kant respects it as the oldest, clearest proof and rejects only its claim to demonstrate. Paley's *Natural Theology* (1802) is later; Kant's watch is eighteenth-century physico-theology. Design after Darwin: [philosophy-of-religion 3.1](../philosophy-of-religion/lessons/03-01-from-design-to-fine-tuning.md).

### The limit argument

Hegel: to know a limit as a limit one must already have in view what lies past it, so Kant's limit is either already passed or ungrounded.

*Phenomenology* Introduction; *Encyclopaedia* §60 (paraphrased from Wallace: no one knows or feels something is a limit or defect unless already beyond it).

1. Kant: knowledge reaches only appearances; the [thing in itself](#thing-in-itself) is thought, not known.
2. To assert this is to claim to know how our knowing stands to what lies beyond it.
3. To know a limit as a limit, one must already have in view what lies past it.
4. So either the limit is known, and knowledge has passed it, or it is not known, and the claim is ungrounded.

**The alternative** (5-8): consciousness distinguishes its knowledge from its object's being in itself, both within it; mismatch alters both; failure is determinate; so a series of shapes ends only at absolute knowing. **The Source:** fear of error presupposes knowledge as instrument or medium, cut off from the Absolute yet true of its limits: "fear of error ... makes itself known rather as fear of the truth"; Kant is not named, and the fit to Kant is disputed. **Weak points:** premise 3 (Kantian reply via the [negative noumenon](#noumenon-negative-and-positive)); 6 to 8 (Trendelenburg, *Logical Investigations*, 1840: necessary transitions or rhetorical bridges? Defender: each step needs only the failed shape's own standard).

## Pitfalls

### Anachronism and textbook labels

- "Rationalist" and "empiricist" name camps that did not exist: Locke grants innate faculties and grounds certainty in intuition, Leibniz needs experience to occasion every thought, Berkeley proves his case "a priori". Name the premise in dispute instead. *([2.3](lessons/02-03-leibniz-sufficient-reason-and-monads.md), [3.1](lessons/03-01-locke-against-innate-ideas.md), [3.4](lessons/03-04-berkeley-immaterialism.md))*
- "Trademark argument", "Cartesian circle", "interaction problem", "parallelism", "master argument", "Hume's fork", "Title Principle", "direct realism" are later labels. Use them, but do not put them in the author's mouth. *([1.3](lessons/01-03-god-clear-and-distinct-ideas-and-the-circle.md), [1.4](lessons/01-04-the-real-distinction-and-the-interaction-problem.md), [2.4](lessons/02-04-interaction-and-causation.md), [3.4](lessons/03-04-berkeley-immaterialism.md), [4.1](lessons/04-01-impressions-ideas-and-the-fork.md), [4.4](lessons/04-04-the-self-belief-and-the-passions.md), [4.5](lessons/04-05-reid-common-sense-against-the-way-of-ideas.md))*
- The demon is not a brain in a vat: the vat fits the demon's list but not the deceiving God, since a vat-brain's sums still come out right. *([1.1](lessons/01-01-the-method-of-doubt.md))*
- "Objective reality" in Meditation III is the reverse of modern "objective": being *as represented* in an idea. *([1.3](lessons/01-03-god-clear-and-distinct-ideas-and-the-circle.md))*
- Elisabeth is not raising conservation of energy (a nineteenth-century concept) or the hard problem; she asks how motion is determined inside contact mechanics. *([1.4](lessons/01-04-the-real-distinction-and-the-interaction-problem.md))*
- Spinoza is not a mind-brain identity theorist, "dual-aspect monist" or (in so many words) panpsychist; "pantheist" depends on the reading of "in God". *([2.1](lessons/02-01-spinoza-one-substance.md), [2.2](lessons/02-02-spinoza-mind-body-and-necessity.md))*
- Hume's regularity theory, "Humean supervenience" (Lewis), the "Humean theory of motivation", "fact-value distinction" and "problem of induction" are twentieth-century vocabulary. *([4.2](lessons/04-02-causation-and-necessary-connection.md), [4.3](lessons/04-03-induction-and-the-sceptical-solution.md), [4.4](lessons/04-04-the-self-belief-and-the-passions.md))*
- Locke is not a "psychological continuity theorist": that theory repairs Reid's objection, and Locke's criterion is direct consciousness of the act. *([3.3](lessons/03-03-locke-personal-identity.md))*
- Kant's categories are not "hard-wired"; his synthesis is not a solution to "the binding problem"; "transcendental ego" is Husserl's label. *([5.2](lessons/05-02-space-and-time-the-transcendental-aesthetic.md), [5.3](lessons/05-03-concepts-and-the-categories.md), [5.4](lessons/05-04-the-transcendental-deduction.md))*

### Famous misquotations

- "Cogito ergo sum" is not in the *Meditations*: Meditation II has "I am, I exist"; "I think, hence I am" is the *Discourse* (1637); the Latin *ergo* is *Principles* I.7 and the Second Replies. *([1.2](lessons/01-02-the-cogito-and-the-wax.md))*
- Descartes did not hold the mind is in the body "like a pilot in a ship": that is the picture he calls insufficient. *([1.4](lessons/01-04-the-real-distinction-and-the-interaction-problem.md))*
- "God or Nature" is not in *Ethics* Part I; it first appears in the Part IV preface. *([2.1](lessons/02-01-spinoza-one-substance.md))*
- "Tabula rasa" is Leibniz's word for Locke's view; Locke says "white paper" and "empty cabinet". "Tertiary qualities" is not Locke's. *([3.1](lessons/03-01-locke-against-innate-ideas.md), [3.2](lessons/03-02-locke-qualities-substance-and-essence.md))*
- "Esse est percipi" compresses "their esse is percipi" and covers only unthinking things; the God-in-the-Quad limerick is Ronald Knox's. *([3.4](lessons/03-04-berkeley-immaterialism.md))*
- The "same shop" line is Reid's *Inquiry* (VI.20), not the *Intellectual Powers*, and is not Plantinga's parity argument. *([4.5](lessons/04-05-reid-common-sense-against-the-way-of-ideas.md))*
- Kant says being is not a *real* predicate, not that existence is no predicate at all; Meiklejohn's "dollars" are Kant's *Taler*. *([6.3](lessons/06-03-the-critique-of-the-proofs-of-god.md))*
- "No facts, only interpretations" is an unpublished notebook line, not the published perspectivism. *([6.5](lessons/06-05-nietzsche-perspectivism-and-genealogy.md))*

### A priori is not innate

- Kant's a priori concerns how a judgment is justified, not when it was acquired: all knowledge *begins* with experience (B1), and Kant rejects implanted "preformation" (B167-168). *([5.1](lessons/05-01-the-critical-question.md), [5.3](lessons/05-03-concepts-and-the-categories.md))*
- Locke's target is innate *propositions* and *ideas*, not innate faculties; Leibniz's innate truths are dispositions, not Plato's recollection. *([3.1](lessons/03-01-locke-against-innate-ideas.md))*
- "Transcendental" (conditions of possible experience) is not "transcendent" (beyond experience). *([5.1](lessons/05-01-the-critical-question.md))*

### Fork versus grid

- Hume sorts claims into two bins by how they are known; Kant crosses two distinctions (a priori/a posteriori, analytic/synthetic) and puts mathematics in the synthetic a priori cell that Hume's bins leave out. *([4.1](lessons/04-01-impressions-ideas-and-the-fork.md), [5.1](lessons/05-01-the-critical-question.md))*
- Kant's "analytic" is containment in a subject concept, not "true by meaning". *([5.1](lessons/05-01-the-critical-question.md))*
- Hume's "idea" is narrower than Locke's: Locke's idea of sensation is Hume's impression. *([4.1](lessons/04-01-impressions-ideas-and-the-fork.md))*
- The copy test does not prove a term meaningless; it shifts a burden ("will serve to confirm our suspicion"). The flames paragraph sorts kinds of reasoning, not meaningful sentences. *([4.1](lessons/04-01-impressions-ideas-and-the-fork.md))*

### Sceptical solution is not justification

- §V explains how we come to infer and why the doubt cannot stop us; it concedes the doubt and supplies no missing premise. *([4.3](lessons/04-03-induction-and-the-sceptical-solution.md))*
- Custom is a mechanism of the imagination built by repetition, not social convention or laziness. *([4.3](lessons/04-03-induction-and-the-sceptical-solution.md))*
- Hume does not deny that things cause things; he denies we have an idea of necessity as a quality of bodies. *([4.2](lessons/04-02-causation-and-necessary-connection.md))*
- Mitigated scepticism is not a half-measure: excessive doubt cannot be lived at all. *([4.4](lessons/04-04-the-self-belief-and-the-passions.md))*
- Kant does not agree that necessity is "in the mind" in Hume's sense: for Kant it is a rule of the understanding, not a habit-bred feeling; and the Second Analogy proves the principle, not particular laws. *([5.5](lessons/05-05-the-second-analogy.md))*

### Hegel method and vocabulary

- "Thesis, antithesis, synthesis" is not Hegel's formula: the pair is Kant's antinomies, the triad Fichte's, the attribution Chalybäus's. *([6.4](lessons/06-04-hegel-dialectic-and-spirit.md))*
- *Aufhebung* is not compromise: a shape breaks down by its own standard and is negated and preserved. *([6.4](lessons/06-04-hegel-dialectic-and-spirit.md))*
- *Geist* as a world-mind floating above people is one reading (Taylor), not the text's verdict; the social-practice reading (Pippin, Pinkard) has text too. *([6.4](lessons/06-04-hegel-dialectic-and-spirit.md))*
- Hegel's "phenomenology" is knowledge as it appears, not Husserl's method. *([6.4](lessons/06-04-hegel-dialectic-and-spirit.md))*

### Readings, not verdicts

- Where scholars divide, give the readings and their texts, not a winner: the cogito (inference, intuition, performance); the circle (memory, conviction, general rule); "in God" (inherence, causal); necessitarianism (strict, moderate); II.viii (powers, in the mind); Lockean consciousness (memory, appropriation); the unperceived table (counterfactual, actual perceiver); New Hume vs traditional; §V (sceptical, naturalist, descriptivist, sceptical realist); transcendental idealism (two-world, two-aspect, intrinsic/relational); *Geist*; lordship and bondage; perspectivism. *([1.2](lessons/01-02-the-cogito-and-the-wax.md), [1.3](lessons/01-03-god-clear-and-distinct-ideas-and-the-circle.md), [2.1](lessons/02-01-spinoza-one-substance.md), [2.2](lessons/02-02-spinoza-mind-body-and-necessity.md), [3.2](lessons/03-02-locke-qualities-substance-and-essence.md), [3.3](lessons/03-03-locke-personal-identity.md), [3.4](lessons/03-04-berkeley-immaterialism.md), [4.2](lessons/04-02-causation-and-necessary-connection.md), [4.3](lessons/04-03-induction-and-the-sceptical-solution.md), [6.1](lessons/06-01-transcendental-idealism-and-the-thing-in-itself.md), [6.4](lessons/06-04-hegel-dialectic-and-spirit.md), [6.5](lessons/06-05-nietzsche-perspectivism-and-genealogy.md))*
- Kant's footnote that Descartes inferred "I exist" is Kant's reading of the cogito, not a settlement of it. *([6.2](lessons/06-02-the-soul-and-the-antinomies.md))*

### Misreading the doctrine

- Descartes does not believe the world probably unreal, or believe in the demon; he concludes only that nothing is certain. *([1.1](lessons/01-01-the-method-of-doubt.md))*
- Meditation II does not prove mind distinct from body; that waits for Meditation VI and needs God. *([1.2](lessons/01-02-the-cogito-and-the-wax.md))*
- "Really distinct" means *able* to exist apart, not actually apart. *([1.4](lessons/01-04-the-real-distinction-and-the-interaction-problem.md))*
- Descartes did not miss the circle: he printed both objections and answered them. *([1.3](lessons/01-03-god-clear-and-distinct-ideas-and-the-circle.md))*
- The geometrical method guarantees only that results follow from the definitions, not that the definitions are true. *([2.1](lessons/02-01-spinoza-one-substance.md))*
- Necessitarianism is not fatalism: our efforts are among the causes. *([2.2](lessons/02-02-spinoza-mind-body-and-necessity.md))*
- Windowless monads lack influence, not representation; the identity of indiscernibles is not Leibniz's law (its converse is). *([2.3](lessons/02-03-leibniz-sufficient-reason-and-monads.md))*
- Occasionalism keeps nature lawful (God acts by general laws); "perpetual miracle" is Leibniz's charge. The two clocks are older than Leibniz. *([2.4](lessons/02-04-interaction-and-causation.md))*
- Locke denies a clear *idea* of substance, not substance; and he does not define the person by memory, nor deny the soul. *([3.2](lessons/03-02-locke-qualities-substance-and-essence.md), [3.3](lessons/03-03-locke-personal-identity.md))*
- Berkeley does not deny tables and bread exist; he analyses "exists" and denies only philosophers' matter. *([3.4](lessons/03-04-berkeley-immaterialism.md))*
- Hume denies an idea of a *simple, invariable* self, not the self we ascribe. *([4.4](lessons/04-04-the-self-belief-and-the-passions.md))*
- Reid's "common sense" is a power of judging first principles, not majority opinion; "not fallacious" is not "infallible"; he is not a naive realist. *([4.5](lessons/04-05-reid-common-sense-against-the-way-of-ideas.md))*
- Space and time are forms of intuition, not categories; transcendental ideality does not make bodies illusions. *([5.2](lessons/05-02-space-and-time-the-transcendental-aesthetic.md))*
- Kant's categories are not Aristotle's reorganized; Meiklejohn's "is not mortal" hides the infinite judgment and his "schema of reality" means actuality. *([5.3](lessons/05-03-concepts-and-the-categories.md))*
- Kant's "I think" is not Descartes's thinking thing; "deduction" is a legal title, not a logical derivation; Kant names no Humean target for §16. *([5.4](lessons/05-04-the-transcendental-deduction.md))*
- The Second Analogy holds of appearances only; the Analogies' "regulative" is not the Dialectic's. *([5.5](lessons/05-05-the-second-analogy.md))*
- Transcendental idealism is not Berkeley's; "noumenon" in the licensed sense names no hidden world; appearances are not copies behind a veil. *([6.1](lessons/06-01-transcendental-idealism-and-the-thing-in-itself.md))*
- An antinomy is reason's conflict with itself, not a contradiction in things: mathematical pairs are both false, dynamical pairs may both be true. *([6.2](lessons/06-02-the-soul-and-the-antinomies.md))*
- The critique of the proofs is not an argument for atheism: speculative reason can neither prove nor disprove God. *([6.3](lessons/06-03-the-critique-of-the-proofs-of-god.md))*
- Stage 6 of the fable is not phenomenalism: the apparent world goes with the true one. The death of God is a diagnosis, not a proof of atheism. *([6.5](lessons/06-05-nietzsche-perspectivism-and-genealogy.md))*
