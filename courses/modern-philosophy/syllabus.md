# Modern Philosophy — Syllabus

> Philosophy · Tier 1 · ~27 lessons · Prereqs: [`philosophical-method`](../philosophical-method/syllabus.md) · Roadmap id: `modern-philosophy`

## Goal

Read the arguments that ran from Descartes to Kant as one long exchange. Descartes rebuilt knowledge on the thinking self and left two problems: how mind and body interact, and whether anything but God is really a cause. Spinoza, Malebranche and Leibniz built whole systems around those problems. Locke, Berkeley and Hume asked what experience alone can deliver, and Hume's answer put causation, induction and the self in doubt. Reid replied from common sense, and Kant asked how synthetic knowledge prior to experience is possible at all, and where reason oversteps. A short final stretch shows two ways past Kant: Hegel's and Nietzsche's. You will finish able to close-read a passage from the *Meditations*, the *Essay*, the *Treatise* or the first *Critique* and say which reading its words support, reconstruct each thinker's central arguments in their own terms, and name the premise on which a rationalist and an empiricist actually part ways. The course reads these problems **historically**: what each thinker argued, why, and whether it works on its own premises. Whether induction can be justified, what a person is, or whether dualism is true are argued as live questions elsewhere. It deliberately skips the ethics and political thought of these figures (owned elsewhere, see below), the scientific context beyond what the arguments need, the minor Cartesians and the German idealists between Kant and Hegel (Fichte and Schelling get one mention), Kant's third *Critique*, Schopenhauer, Kierkegaard and the British idealists.

## How practice works in this course

Problems follow [HUMANITIES-BUILD-BRIEF.md](../../HUMANITIES-BUILD-BRIEF.md), verdict-neutral: Descartes and his objectors, Locke and Leibniz, Hume and Reid, Kant and Hegel are each presented at the strength their best readers would recognize, and no rubric rewards siding with any of them. Three archetypes carry the course:

- **Close reading** is the workhorse: a public-domain passage of 150 words or fewer, and the question of which interpretation its words support (does Meditation II infer "I exist" from "I think"? are Locke's secondary qualities in the mind or powers in the thing?). Graded strictly as exegesis.
- **Reconstruct**: an argument in numbered premises, in the thinker's own terms (a Cartesian "idea" is not a Humean "idea"; Kant's "a priori" is not "innate"), with the weakest premise flagged.
- **Find the crux**: the rationalist–empiricist exchange is the course's spine, and most modules end by naming the single premise on which two thinkers split (Locke and Leibniz on innate ideas, Malebranche and Leibniz on what a real cause must be, Hume and Kant on where necessary connection comes from).

Counterexample, apply-to-a-hard-case and steelman-and-reply appear in most modules; every module uses at least three archetypes. Evaluative parts ask whether an argument works **on its author's premises** or against a named contemporary's reply; they do not ask for a verdict on the live problem, which belongs to [`epistemology`](../epistemology/syllabus.md), [`metaphysics`](../metaphysics/syllabus.md) and `philosophy-of-mind`. A recurring exegetical discipline is **anachronism**: "rationalist" and "empiricist" are later textbook labels that fit some figures badly (Leibniz did empirical science; Locke held that some knowledge is intuitive), and a modern term silently imported into an old text is graded as a misreading.

**Texts.** All close-reading passages come from public-domain translations or English originals: Descartes, *Meditations*, *Discourse* and *Principles* (Veitch, or Haldane and Ross); Malebranche, *The Search after Truth* (a seventeenth-century English translation); Spinoza, *Ethics* (Elwes); Leibniz, *Discourse on Metaphysics*, *Monadology* and *New System* (Latta, Montgomery); Locke, *An Essay Concerning Human Understanding*; Berkeley, *Principles* and *Three Dialogues*; Hume, *A Treatise of Human Nature* and *An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding*; Butler's dissertation "Of Personal Identity"; Reid, *Inquiry* and *Essays on the Intellectual Powers*; Kant, *Critique of Pure Reason* (Meiklejohn or Müller) and *Prolegomena* (Carus); Hegel, *Phenomenology of Mind* (Baillie); Nietzsche (Zimmern, Common, Samuel, Ludovici). Princess Elisabeth's letters and all modern commentators (Kemp Smith's and Guyer–Wood's Kant translations included) are paraphrased and cited, never quoted at length.

## Dangerous Checklist

When you finish, you can:

- [ ] Reconstruct Descartes's route from universal doubt through the cogito and God to the real distinction of mind and body, and state the Cartesian circle and the interaction objection at full strength
- [ ] Explain Spinoza's argument that there is only one substance, and what follows for mind, body, freedom and contingency
- [ ] State the principle of sufficient reason and use it to derive Leibniz's monads, the identity of indiscernibles and pre-established harmony
- [ ] Lay out interactionism, occasionalism, parallelism and pre-established harmony on the same case, and say what each gives up to avoid the others' problems
- [ ] Explain Locke's case against innate ideas, his primary/secondary quality distinction, and his consciousness account of the person as a forensic notion
- [ ] Reconstruct Berkeley's argument from the likeness principle and the attack on abstract ideas to immaterialism, and say which Lockean premise it exploits
- [ ] Use Hume's copy principle and fork to reconstruct his arguments about necessary connection, induction and the self, and state his "sceptical solution" without turning it into the problem it answers
- [ ] Say what Reid's common-sense philosophy denies about the "way of ideas," and whether his parity argument answers Hume or changes the question
- [ ] Classify a judgment as analytic or synthetic, a priori or a posteriori in Kant's sense, and explain why the synthetic a priori is the question the first *Critique* exists to answer
- [ ] Explain what Kant means by forms of intuition, categories and the unity of apperception, and reconstruct the Second Analogy as a reply to Hume
- [ ] State transcendental idealism, the two main readings of the thing in itself, the four antinomies with Kant's resolution, and Kant's case that the ontological, cosmological and design arguments all fail as proofs
- [ ] Say what Hegel's dialectic and Nietzsche's perspectivism each reject in Kant, and name the Kantian premise each one denies

## Modules

### Module 1: Descartes

A foundation for knowledge built from the inside out: doubt everything doubtable, find what survives, and rebuild the world from the thinking self and God. Then the problems the foundation leaves.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1.1 | The method of doubt | Reconstruct the three waves of doubt in Meditation I and say what each one removes | the scholastic starting point in the senses, foundationalism, the senses, dreaming, the deceiving God and the evil demon, doubt as method not belief, **close reading** |
| 1.2 | The cogito and the wax | Decide whether "I am, I exist" is an inference, and say what Descartes thinks the self is | Meditation II, the *Discourse* and *Principles* formulations, inference vs intuition, the thinking thing, the wax passage and knowledge by the intellect |
| 1.3 | God, clear and distinct ideas, and the circle | Reconstruct the trademark and ontological arguments as they function in the system, and state the circle objection | formal vs objective reality, the causal adequacy principle (Meditation III), the Meditation V argument, the truth rule, the Cartesian circle (Mersenne and Arnauld in the *Objections*), evaluation of the proofs ceded to [`philosophy-of-religion`](../philosophy-of-religion/syllabus.md) |
| 1.4 | The real distinction and the interaction problem | Reconstruct the Meditation VI arguments for dualism and state Elisabeth's objection | conceivability and divine power, the divisibility argument, the union of mind and body, Elisabeth's 1643 objection and Descartes's reply, Hobbes and Gassendi as objectors |

**Boss problem 1:** Take Meditations II and VI (Veitch or Haldane–Ross, public domain). (a) Close-read the passage in Meditation II where the meditator first concludes that "I am, I exist" is true whenever it is conceived. Does the text present the conclusion as inferred from a premise about thinking, or as known in the act of thinking it? Point to the words that decide it, and say how the *Principles* formulation differs. (b) Reconstruct the Meditation VI conceivability argument for the real distinction of mind and body in standard form, and mark the premise where God's power or veracity does the work. (c) Elisabeth's objection, paraphrased in the problem, is that an unextended mind cannot move a body, since moving requires contact or extension. Find the crux between her and Descartes: which premise about causation must one of them deny? (d) Assess whether Descartes's reply, that the union of mind and body is its own primitive notion known through the senses, answers the objection or relocates it, in 300–500 words. Parts (a)–(c) are exegetical; (d) is evaluative.

### Module 2: The rationalist systems

Three thinkers take Descartes's key terms, substance, cause and God, more strictly than he did, and get three very different worlds. The module ends on the problem all of them were solving.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2.1 | Spinoza I: one substance | Reconstruct the argument of *Ethics* Part I that God is the only substance | the geometrical method, definitions of substance, attribute and mode, why two substances cannot share an attribute (I p5), God or Nature, Descartes's own definition of substance (*Principles* I.51) as the premise Spinoza radicalizes |
| 2.2 | Spinoza II: mind, body and necessity | Explain parallelism, necessitarianism and Spinoza's freedom, and why they follow from Part I | "the order and connection of ideas" (II p7 and scholium), the mind as the idea of the body, no contingency (I p29, p33), *conatus*, freedom as understanding necessity, the ethics of Parts IV–V as a sketch only |
| 2.3 | Leibniz: sufficient reason and monads | Derive the monads, the complete individual concept and the identity of indiscernibles from Leibniz's two great principles | contradiction and sufficient reason, predicate-in-subject, the complete concept and Arnauld's worry about contingency, infinite analysis, simple substances and perception, the best possible world (its theodicy ceded), the PSR's live defence ceded to [`metaphysics`](../metaphysics/syllabus.md) 3.3, Leibniz's cosmological argument ceded to [`philosophy-of-religion`](../philosophy-of-religion/syllabus.md) 2.4, Leibniz–Clarke on space as a named bridge |
| 2.4 | The problem they left: interaction and causation | Put the four seventeenth-century answers to mind–body causation on one case and say what each costs | Malebranche's argument that no finite thing has necessary connection with its effect (*Search* Book VI), occasionalism, Spinoza's parallelism revisited, pre-established harmony and the two clocks, Leibniz's "perpetual miracle" charge, Malebranche's argument as the source of Hume's |

**Boss problem 2:** The case: I will to raise my arm, and it rises. (a) For Descartes, Malebranche, Spinoza and Leibniz, say what each holds is the cause of the arm's rising and what, if anything, the volition contributes. (b) Close-read *Ethics* II p7 with its scholium (Elwes). Is Spinoza describing two series that run in step, or one thing described twice? Point to the words that decide it. (c) Find the crux between Malebranche and Leibniz: Leibniz charges that occasionalism makes nature a perpetual miracle, and Malebranche holds that a true cause must be one whose connection with its effect the mind perceives as necessary. Name the premise about what a genuine cause is that one of them must deny. (d) Decide which of the four views pays the least to solve the interaction problem, naming the price each pays (finite causation, contingency, freedom, intelligibility of the union), and reply to the strongest objection to your choice, in 300–500 words. Parts (a)–(c) are exegetical; (d) is evaluative.

### Module 3: Locke and Berkeley

The empiricist programme: all ideas come from experience. Locke builds a theory of mind, matter and persons on it. Berkeley argues that, followed consistently, it leaves no room for matter at all.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3.1 | Locke: against innate ideas | Reconstruct Book I's case against innate principles and see how Leibniz answered it | universal assent, the children-and-idiots argument, the mind as white paper (II.i.2), sensation and reflection, simple and complex ideas, Leibniz's *New Essays* and the veined marble, **find the crux** between the two |
| 3.2 | Locke: qualities, substance and essence | Explain the primary/secondary quality distinction and Locke's agnosticism about substance and real essence | the corpuscular hypothesis (Boyle), resemblance, secondary qualities as powers (II.viii), the porphyry and the hot-and-cold water examples, substance as an unknown support (II.xxiii), real vs nominal essence (III.iii, III.vi), the "veil of ideas" worry |
| 3.3 | Locke: personal identity | Apply Locke's consciousness criterion of personal identity to a case and state the two classic objections | man vs person vs substance (II.xxvii), person as a "forensic" term, the prince and the cobbler, drunkenness and punishment, Locke's own worries about the resurrection and the soul, Butler's and Reid's objections named (argued in [`metaphysics`](../metaphysics/syllabus.md) 6.6) |
| 3.4 | Berkeley: immaterialism | Reconstruct Berkeley's arguments that to be is to be perceived, and see which Lockean premises they turn against Locke | the attack on abstract ideas (*Principles* Introduction), "an idea can be like nothing but an idea" (§8), the inseparability of primary from secondary qualities, the "master argument" (§§22–23), passive ideas and active spirits, God and the continued existence of things, the *Dialogues* as defence against the charge of skepticism |

**Boss problem 3:** Take Locke's *Essay* II.viii on primary and secondary qualities, and Berkeley's *Principles* §§8–10, both public domain. (a) Close-read the Locke passages: does Locke say colours and tastes are in the mind, or that they are powers in bodies? Point to the words that support each reading, and say which one the text better supports. (b) Reconstruct Berkeley's argument from §§8–10 in standard form: the likeness principle, and the claim that extension cannot be conceived apart from colour. Mark which premise Locke is already committed to. (c) Name the crux: which single premise must a defender of Locke's distinction deny to stop Berkeley's argument? (d) Apply Locke's account of personal identity (II.xxvii) to an invented case: a man who committed a crime while so drunk he has no memory of it at all. Say what Locke's criterion implies and what Locke himself says about punishing the drunkard. Then assess whether that answer is consistent with calling "person" a forensic term, in 150 words or fewer. Parts (a)–(c) and the first half of (d) are exegetical; the second half of (d) is evaluative.

### Module 4: Hume, and Reid's reply

Empiricism made rigorous and turned on its own foundations: if every idea is copied from an impression, what becomes of necessary connection, inference from the past, and the self? Then the Scot who said the fault lay in the theory of ideas itself.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4.1 | Impressions, ideas and the fork | Use the copy principle and Hume's fork as tests, and say what they rule out | impressions vs ideas, the copy principle and the missing shade of blue, association (resemblance, contiguity, causation), relations of ideas vs matters of fact, the closing "commit it to the flames" paragraph of the *Enquiry* |
| 4.2 | Causation and necessary connection | Reconstruct the search for the impression of necessary connection and state Hume's two definitions of cause | priority, contiguity, constant conjunction, no impression from single cases or from the will, the idea as copied from a felt determination of the mind (*Enquiry* §7), the two definitions (their close reading and the regularity theory they inspired owned by [`metaphysics`](../metaphysics/syllabus.md) 4.1), the "New Hume" dispute named; Malebranche (2.4) as the source |
| 4.3 | Induction and the sceptical solution | Reconstruct the argument that inference from experience rests on no reasoning, and say what custom explains | the uniformity principle, the dilemma through the fork (*Enquiry* §4), circularity, custom or habit (§5), belief as a lively idea, what the sceptical solution is and is not; the answers to Hume ceded to [`epistemology`](../epistemology/syllabus.md) 4.3; bridge: [`statistical-learning`](../statistical-learning/syllabus.md) 1.4 on no free lunch |
| 4.4 | The self, belief and the passions | Explain the bundle theory, Hume's mitigated skepticism, and why reason alone cannot move to action or yield an "ought" | "Of personal identity" (*Treatise* I.iv.6) and the second thoughts of the Appendix, nature over reason and the backgammon passage (I.iv.7), mitigated skepticism (*Enquiry* §12), reason and the passions (II.iii.3), the is–ought paragraph (III.i.1) read as a consequence, its metaethics ceded to [`ethics`](../ethics/syllabus.md) 6.1 |
| 4.5 | Reid: common sense against the way of ideas | State Reid's diagnosis of the theory of ideas and reconstruct his parity argument | the "ideal system" from Descartes to Hume, sensation vs perception, direct realism, first principles of contingent truths (*Intellectual Powers* VI.5), the parity of reason and perception, the brave officer against Locke (III.6) named (argued in [`metaphysics`](../metaphysics/syllabus.md) 6.6), active power named; Reid on testimony ceded to [`epistemology`](../epistemology/syllabus.md) 4.4 |

**Boss problem 4:** (a) Reconstruct the argument of *Enquiry* §7 that we get no impression of necessary connection from single instances, whether of bodies acting on bodies or of the will moving the limbs, in standard form, and say where the copy principle (4.1) does the work. (The *Enquiry* §4 dilemma is reconstructed in [`epistemology`](../epistemology/syllabus.md) Boss 4 and not repeated here.) (b) Close-read the passage of *Treatise* I.iv.6 where Hume reports that, entering most intimately into what he calls himself, he always stumbles on some particular perception. Does the text present the bundle view as a report of introspection, a consequence of the copy principle, or both? Point to the words that decide it, and say what the Appendix later admits he cannot explain. (The two definitions of cause are close-read in [`metaphysics`](../metaphysics/syllabus.md) Boss 4 and not repeated here.) (c) Reid holds that the faculties of perception and memory have the same claim to trust as reason, since all come from the same source. Find the crux between Reid and Hume's sceptic: which premise about what a belief needs to be justified must one of them deny? (d) Does Reid answer Hume, or does he change the question Hume was asking? Take one side, state the strongest case for the other, including Hume's own claim that nature makes the scepticism idle in practice (4.4), in 300–500 words. Parts (a)–(c) are exegetical; (d) is evaluative.

### Module 5: Kant I: the conditions of experience

Kant's question after Hume: how can we know anything necessary about the world before experiencing it? His answer: because the mind supplies the forms experience must take. This module is the setup `phenomenology-and-personalism` builds on.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5.1 | The critical question | Classify judgments in Kant's two distinctions and say why synthetic a priori knowledge is the problem | Hume and the "dogmatic slumber" (*Prolegomena* preface), analytic vs synthetic, a priori vs a posteriori, 7 + 5 = 12, "all bodies are extended" vs "all bodies are heavy", the Copernican revolution (B preface), the transcendental method: conditions of possibility |
| 5.2 | Space and time: the Transcendental Aesthetic | Reconstruct the arguments that space and time are forms of intuition, not things or relations among things | sensibility vs understanding, intuition vs concept, the metaphysical and transcendental expositions, geometry as synthetic a priori, empirical reality and transcendental ideality; bridge: non-Euclidean geometry and [`relativity`](../relativity/syllabus.md) as the standing challenge |
| 5.3 | Concepts and the categories | Explain why intuition needs concepts and derive the twelve categories from the forms of judgment | "thoughts without content are empty, intuitions without concepts are blind" (A51/B75), synthesis, the table of judgments and the table of categories, the metaphysical deduction, the schematism named |
| 5.4 | The transcendental deduction and the "I think" | Reconstruct the deduction's aim and its central claim about the unity of apperception | *quid facti* vs *quid juris*, the "I think" that must be able to accompany all my representations (B131), transcendental vs empirical apperception, objectivity as rule-governed synthesis, the A (1781) and B (1787) versions named; the transcendental ego that Husserl inherits |
| 5.5 | The Second Analogy: Kant's reply to Hume | Reconstruct the argument that the concept of cause is a condition of experiencing objective succession | subjective vs objective succession, the house and the ship, the rule that fixes an order in time, why this is a reply to Hume on necessary connection (4.2), what it does and does not show about particular causal laws |

**Boss problem 5:** (a) Classify each in Kant's two distinctions, with his reason: 7 + 5 = 12; all bodies are extended; all bodies are heavy; every event has a cause; the shortest line between two points is straight. (b) Reconstruct the Second Analogy's argument from the house and the ship (Meiklejohn or Müller) in standard form. (c) Find the crux between Hume (*Enquiry* §7) and Kant on where the necessity in "cause" comes from, naming the premise each denies. (d) A critic says the Second Analogy, even if sound, proves only that every event has *some* cause, and so leaves Hume's problem of induction (4.3) exactly where it was. State the critic's point precisely, give the strongest Kantian reply, and assess it, in 300–500 words. Parts (a)–(c) are exegetical; (d) is evaluative.

### Module 6: The limits of reason, and two ways past Kant

Kant draws a boundary: knowledge reaches as far as possible experience and no further, and reason that crosses it breeds illusion. Hegel denies the boundary can be drawn from one side only; Nietzsche denies there is a "true world" behind it at all.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 6.1 | Transcendental idealism and the thing in itself | State transcendental idealism and the two main readings of the phenomena/noumena distinction | appearances vs things in themselves, noumenon in the negative sense, the two-world vs two-aspect readings, the affection problem (Jacobi's objection named), Fichte and Schelling named as the first responses |
| 6.2 | The Dialectic: the soul and the antinomies | Explain transcendental illusion, the paralogisms against rational psychology, and the four antinomies with their resolution | reason and the unconditioned, the paralogisms as a reply to the Cartesian soul (1.2), the four antinomies, mathematical vs dynamical resolution, the third antinomy and freedom (its ethics ceded to [`ethics`](../ethics/syllabus.md) Module 2) |
| 6.3 | The critique of the proofs of God | Reconstruct Kant's objections to the ontological, cosmological and physico-theological arguments | "being is not a real predicate" (A598/B626), the hundred thalers, the claim that the cosmological argument depends on the ontological, the design argument's limit, the transcendental ideal and the regulative use of the idea of God, the moral argument named; the assessment of the arguments ceded to [`philosophy-of-religion`](../philosophy-of-religion/syllabus.md) 2.1–2.4, 3.1, 3.3 |
| 6.4 | Hegel: dialectic and spirit | Say what Hegel rejects in Kant's limits and how his dialectic is meant to work | the critique of the thing in itself (a limit known as a limit is already passed), determinate negation and *Aufhebung*, why "thesis–antithesis–synthesis" is not Hegel's formula, the *Phenomenology*'s path of consciousness, lordship and bondage, *Geist*; *Philosophy of Right* ceded to [`history-of-political-thought`](../history-of-political-thought/syllabus.md) 6.1 |
| 6.5 | Nietzsche: perspectivism and genealogy | Say what Nietzsche rejects in Kant and in the whole modern project, and how genealogy works as a method | "How the 'True World' Finally Became a Fable" (*Twilight of the Idols*), perspectivism, the critique of the subject and the "I think" (*Beyond Good and Evil*), the death of God (*The Gay Science* §125), genealogy as method; *Schuld* ceded to `philosophy-of-debt` |

**Boss problem 6:** (a) Close-read Kant's passage that being is not a real predicate, with the hundred thalers (Meiklejohn or Müller). What is a "real" predicate, and why does "exists" not qualify? (b) Reconstruct Descartes's Meditation V argument (1.3) in standard form and mark the exact premise Kant's thesis denies. (c) State the thesis and antithesis of the third antinomy, and explain how transcendental idealism lets both be true. (d) Steelman the post-Kantian objection, pressed by Hegel among others, that Kant cannot know there is a limit to knowledge without already knowing something beyond it. Then reply as a Kantian would, drawing on the negative sense of "noumenon" (6.1). 150 words or fewer for each. Parts (a)–(c) are exegetical; (d) is evaluative.

## Notes

- **The setup for `phenomenology-and-personalism`,** which lists this course as its only prerequisite. It can assume: Cartesian doubt and the cogito (1.1–1.2), which Husserl's *Cartesian Meditations* restage; the empiricist theory of ideas and Hume's bundle self (3.1, 4.1, 4.4), which phenomenology rejects; Kant's intuition/concept distinction, synthesis, the transcendental method and the transcendental "I think" (5.1, 5.3–5.4); the thing in itself and its two readings (6.1), against which Husserl's slogan "to the things themselves" is often read; Hegel's *Phenomenology* as the older use of the word (6.4); and Nietzsche's genealogy (6.5), which Scheler's work on *ressentiment* answers. Brentano and the neo-Kantians belong to that course.
- **Historical here, live there.** The same problems return as arguments in their own right: induction, skepticism and foundationalism in [`epistemology`](../epistemology/syllabus.md), which already cites this course for the historical figures; causation, personal identity, substance and free will in [`metaphysics`](../metaphysics/syllabus.md), which already cites this course for the systems; dualism and the interaction problem in `philosophy-of-mind`; the ontological and cosmological arguments in [`philosophy-of-religion`](../philosophy-of-religion/syllabus.md), which already uses Kant's predicate objection in its 2.1. Those courses should cite 1.1–1.4, 2.4, 3.3, 4.2–4.5 and 6.3 for the historical sources rather than re-teach them.
- **Cross-subject bridges.** Hume on induction (4.3) ↔ the no-free-lunch theorem ([`statistical-learning`](../statistical-learning/syllabus.md) 1.4, which already names the connection). Kant on geometry as synthetic a priori (5.2) ↔ non-Euclidean geometry and curved spacetime ([`relativity`](../relativity/syllabus.md), [`differential-geometry`](../differential-geometry/syllabus.md)). Leibniz–Clarke on space (2.3) ↔ `philosophy-of-physics`.
- **Lesson count.** The roadmap estimated ~22; this runs 27 (+23 percent, inside the ±25 percent band). The extra lessons come from two decisions. Kant gets eight lessons across two modules, because he is the gateway to `phenomenology-and-personalism` and the deduction, the Second Analogy and the Dialectic each need their own ten minutes. And the mind–body and causation problems get a comparison lesson of their own (2.4), which is where Malebranche's occasionalism lives and where the road to Hume's causation begins. Hegel and Nietzsche stay at one lesson each, as the roadmap's "a look at" implies. Nothing is crammed: Hume's is–ought passage is folded into 4.4 only because [`ethics`](../ethics/syllabus.md) owns its analysis.
- **Deliberate omissions** are listed in the Goal. If a later course needs one of them (Kant's *Critique of Judgment*, Schopenhauer, Kierkegaard), it should own it rather than this course growing a lesson.
