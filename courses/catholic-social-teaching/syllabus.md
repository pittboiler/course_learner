# Catholic Social Teaching — Syllabus

> Catholic Theology · Tier 2 · ~27 lessons · Prereqs: [`moral-theology`](../moral-theology/syllabus.md) · Roadmap id: `catholic-social-teaching`

## Goal

This course teaches the Church's social doctrine from within the tradition: what the Magisterium has taught about work, property, the economy, the state, war, migration and the created order, on what grounds, and at what weight. Its central skill is a distinction the corpus itself insists on and almost every popular use of it blurs — the difference between the **permanent principles** of the social doctrine and the **contingent prudential judgments** by which those principles are applied to a particular time, economy or bill. You will finish able to read any social encyclical and say which of its claims bind a Catholic's assent and which invite it, to state human dignity, the common good, subsidiarity, solidarity, the universal destination of goods and the preferential option for the poor precisely enough to run on a real case, to trace the corpus from *Rerum Novarum* (1891) to *Fratelli Tutti* (2020) and say what each document added, to give the Church's critique of socialism and of liberal capitalism at the strength each critique actually has, to state what *Dignitatis Humanae* teaches and what it deliberately leaves untouched, and to put the distributist, Catholic liberal, integralist and progressive readings of this tradition on one map with each at full strength. It also teaches the doctrine's own account of its limits: the Church claims competence in the moral order, disclaims technical solutions, and proposes no economic or political system of its own.

It deliberately skips the analysis of moral acts, conscience and the life issues (owned by [`moral-theology`](../moral-theology/syllabus.md)), the historical events surrounding the documents ([`church-history`](../church-history/syllabus.md)), Augustine and Aquinas as political thinkers ([`history-of-political-thought`](../history-of-political-thought/syllabus.md)), the secular theories of justice, liberty and legitimacy against which these positions are usually set ([`political-philosophy`](../political-philosophy/syllabus.md)), Wojtyła's philosophical personalism ([`phenomenology-and-personalism`](../phenomenology-and-personalism/syllabus.md)), the economics of public provision and of debt ([`public-economics`](../public-economics/syllabus.md), [`economics-of-debt`](../economics-of-debt/syllabus.md)), the Church's teaching on debt, usury and finance ([`theology-of-debt`](../theology-of-debt/syllabus.md)), and religious liberty as United States constitutional doctrine ([`constitutional-law`](../constitutional-law/syllabus.md) Module 7).

## How practice works in this course

Problems follow [HUMANITIES-BUILD-BRIEF.md](../../HUMANITIES-BUILD-BRIEF.md), with the Catholic Theology stance: taught from within the tradition, with the level of authority marked on every claim whose weight matters, using the ladder in the Notes of [`fundamental-theology`](../fundamental-theology/syllabus.md).

- **The course's signature exegetical error runs in two directions, and both are graded strictly.** Presenting a contingent prudential application as if it bound like a principle ("the Church teaches that this bill must pass") is an error. So is demoting a permanent principle to a policy preference ("subsidiarity is just one opinion about federalism"). Every problem that touches a document asks which the claim is, and at what level.
- **Exegetical parts are strict.** What *Rerum Novarum* actually defends about private property and against whom; what *Laborem Exercens* means by the objective and subjective dimensions of work; what *Dignitatis Humanae* declares and what its opening section says it leaves untouched; what the 1984 and 1986 instructions affirmed and what they criticized in liberation theology; which paragraph of *Centesimus Annus* answers the capitalism question and what its conditions are.
- **Evaluative parts, verdict-neutral, are reserved for questions genuinely open to Catholics.** Whether *Dignitatis Humanae* develops or reverses the nineteenth-century teaching; whether *Centesimus Annus* endorsed or merely conditionally accepted the market economy; whether distributism is the teaching's natural implementation or one contingent proposal among many; whether the traditional just-war criteria can still be satisfied; how far an economy of the present kind is compatible with the universal destination of goods; and every question that is by its nature prudential — a wage floor, an immigration quota, a climate policy. The rubric never rewards the grader's side. Module 6 is built around this, with the Catholic liberal, integralist and progressive readings each stated at full strength.
- **Apologetic parts** ask for the Catholic position against a named alternative stated as its own best defenders state it: a libertarian on property and taxation, a democratic socialist on the wage, a secular liberal on religious liberty and public reason, a realist on war. Fidelity to the other side is graded first.
- **Archetypes.** Apply-to-a-hard-case is the workhorse, because the principles exist to be run on cases. Diagnose (invented op-eds, homilies, party leaflets and bishops' letters, never attributed to real people) is the standing test for principle-versus-application. Every module also uses at least two of close reading, reconstruct, find the crux and steelman-and-reply. Formal work is light: one lesson uses simple arithmetic on a wage and cost-of-living table (4.2).
- **Texts.** Close reading uses public-domain texts only: *Rerum Novarum* (1891) in the Vatican English translation; Leo XIII's *Immortale Dei* (1885) and *Libertas* (1888) and Pius IX's *Quanta Cura* (1864) in their nineteenth-century translations; Aquinas, *Summa Theologiae* (English Dominican Province); scripture in the Douay–Rheims (Challoner); the Fathers in Schaff's *Ante-Nicene* and *Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers*; Hilaire Belloc, *The Servile State* (1912) and G. K. Chesterton, *The Outline of Sanity* (1926). **Every document from 1931 onward is paraphrased and cited by name and section, never quoted from a published modern translation** — including *Quadragesimo Anno*, whose English translation this course does not assume to be public domain.

## Dangerous Checklist

When you finish, you can:

- [ ] Place any social document in the corpus by genre, year and weight, and state what the Church's social doctrine claims for itself and what it expressly disclaims
- [ ] **Assign an authority level to a social teaching, and separate a permanent principle from the contingent prudential judgment that applies it — saying what assent each asks for**
- [ ] Run see-judge-act on a current question, naming where the empirical premises come from, where the moral principle enters, and what follows for how revisable the conclusion is
- [ ] Close-read *Rerum Novarum* and state exactly what it defends about property, wages, the state and workers' associations, and against which two opponents
- [ ] State human dignity, the common good, subsidiarity and solidarity precisely enough to apply, diagnose the standard misreading of each, and show how the universal destination of goods and the right to private property are ordered to one another
- [ ] State the preferential option for the poor as the Magisterium states it, and say exactly what the 1984 and 1986 instructions affirmed and what they criticized in liberation theology
- [ ] Reconstruct *Laborem Exercens*'s argument for the priority of labour over capital, and derive the just-wage teaching from it well enough to apply it to a real payroll
- [ ] Give the Church's critique of socialism and of liberal capitalism at full strength, and say what *Centesimus Annus* did and did not concede about "capitalism"
- [ ] State what *Dignitatis Humanae* teaches, what its opening section leaves untouched, and both readings of whether it develops or reverses the nineteenth-century teaching
- [ ] Apply the Church's teaching on war to a case, and state the nuclear-deterrence position and the recent challenge to just war with each side at full strength
- [ ] Apply the teaching on migration and on integral ecology to a concrete policy, marking in each sentence what binds and what is left to prudential judgment
- [ ] Place the distributist, Catholic liberal, integralist and progressive readings of this tradition on one map, and name the premise each pair actually disagrees about

## Modules

### Module 1: What the Church's social doctrine is

Before any principle: what kind of thing this corpus is, what weight its claims carry, how it reasons, and where it came from.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1.1 | The social doctrine: sources, genre and claim | Say what the corpus is, what it claims competence over, and what it disclaims | the body of teaching from *Rerum Novarum* to the present; the *Compendium of the Social Doctrine of the Church* (Pontifical Council for Justice and Peace, 2004) and its architecture; social doctrine as moral theology applied, and as part of the Church's evangelizing mission; the disclaimers the documents make of themselves — no technical solutions, no economic or political model, no "third way", competence in the moral order; social doctrine and the layperson's proper role |
| 1.2 | Permanent principles and contingent applications | Assign a social claim its level, and say whether it is principle or prudential judgment | the ladder from [`fundamental-theology`](../fundamental-theology/syllabus.md) 4.4-4.5 applied to social documents; *Gaudium et Spes* on not claiming the Church's authority for one's own opinion, and on the Church being bound to no political or economic system; *Octogesima Adveniens* on the difficulty of one universal message and the local community's task; religious submission owed to an authentically taught principle vs the respect owed to a prudential application; a bishops' conference document that marks its own levels (*The Challenge of Peace*, 1983; *Economic Justice for All*, 1986); the two symmetrical errors |
| 1.3 | See, judge, act | Run the method, and say where the empirical premises enter and what they make revisable | the method's origin in Catholic Action (Cardijn) and its adoption in *Mater et Magistra*; "the signs of the times" (*Gaudium et Spes*); induction from a situation, judgment by principle, action in a concrete setting; the place of the social sciences and of contested empirical claims; natural law as the claimed common language with non-believers (theory assumed from [`ethics`](../ethics/syllabus.md) 4.4-4.5); the objection that inductive method makes the teaching unstable, and the reply |
| 1.4 | Before 1891: the roots and the social question | Name what the tradition brought to the social question and what was genuinely new in 1891 | scripture on the land, the poor and the stranger (cited to [`old-testament`](../old-testament/syllabus.md), [`new-testament`](../new-testament/syllabus.md)) and the Fathers on wealth and almsgiving (cited to [`patristics`](../patristics/syllabus.md)); Aquinas on property and on the man in necessity (ST II-II q.66 a.2, a.7 — **close reading**; his politics cited to [`history-of-political-thought`](../history-of-political-thought/syllabus.md) 2.3-2.4); the just price and usury handed to [`theology-of-debt`](../theology-of-debt/syllabus.md); industrialization and the social question; the nineteenth-century social Catholics (Ketteler, Ozanam, La Tour du Pin, the Fribourg Union); the two rival answers Leo XIII faced, liberal political economy and socialism |

**Boss problem 1:** (a) Assign each of the following a level on the ladder and say whether it is a permanent principle or a contingent application, and what assent it asks: (i) every person possesses an inviolable dignity; (ii) workers have a right to form associations; (iii) goods are destined for all; (iv) the principle of subsidiarity; (v) a named encyclical's judgment that a particular international institution should be reformed in a particular way; (vi) a bishops' conference's judgment that a specific minimum-wage figure is just; (vii) a papal remark in an in-flight press conference; (viii) the claim that the arms race is in itself a grave injustice. Overstating or understating any level is an error. *(Exegetical.)* (b) Close-read ST II-II q.66 a.7 (English Dominican Province): state exactly what Aquinas says a person in extreme necessity may take, on what ground, and what the article does **not** license. Name the words that decide each. *(Exegetical.)* (c) Diagnose an invented parish-bulletin paragraph that moves from "the Church teaches the preferential option for the poor" to "Catholics are therefore obliged to support this bill." Name every step where principle silently became application, and correct each with the right level. *(Exegetical.)* (d) In 300 words: does the inductive, situation-first method of 1.3 make social teaching more revisable than the rest of moral theology, or only more obviously so? Any verdict passes if the principle/application distinction from 1.2 is used and the strongest case for the other side is stated. *(Evaluative.)*

### Module 2: The tradition takes shape, 1891-1971

The founding documents, read for what each one added and what fight each one entered. Later documents are read in the thematic modules that own them.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2.1 | *Rerum Novarum* (1891) | Close-read the charter and state what it defends, against whom, and with what argument | the condition of the workers; the refusal of the socialist remedy and the argument from the worker's own interest in owning; private property defended as a natural right founded on labour and on providing for a family; the reciprocal duties of employers and workers; the passage on wages and the sufficiency of a frugal worker's living; the state's duty to protect the weak and its limits; the right of association and the Catholic workers' societies; the Church's own claim to be heard on the question; "rapacious usury under a different guise" handed to [`theology-of-debt`](../theology-of-debt/syllabus.md) 6.1; the encyclical as an event cited to [`church-history`](../church-history/syllabus.md) 8.3 |
| 2.2 | *Quadragesimo Anno* (1931) and the interwar proposals | Say what Pius XI added forty years on, and separate the principle from the proposal of its moment | the twofold character of ownership, individual and social; the just wage restated as a family wage, with the firm's viability and the common good as further measures; "social justice" as a term of art entering magisterial usage; the formulation of subsidiarity (taught in 3.3); the critique of unbridled free competition and of the concentration of economic power; the judgment on socialism, moderate and full; the proposal of vocational orders, and the honest problem of its resemblance to interwar corporatism (the politics cited to [`church-history`](../church-history/syllabus.md) 9.1) — a worked example of a permanent principle carried by a contingent institutional proposal; Pius XII's radio messages and the post-war restatement |
| 2.3 | John XXIII: *Mater et Magistra* and *Pacem in Terris* | State the widening of the agenda and the move into the language of rights | *Mater et Magistra* (1961): socialization and its ambivalence, agriculture and the rural poor, aid to developing nations, the adoption of see-judge-act; *Pacem in Terris* (1963): order in the person, between persons and public authority, among states, and in the world community; the enumerated rights and their paired duties; the encyclical addressed to all people of good will; the distinction between error and the person who errs, and between a false philosophy and the historical movements it inspires — the hinge that made later engagement possible; the universal common good and the question of an authority competent for it; Maritain's Christian democracy as the philosophical neighbour |
| 2.4 | The Council and after: *Gaudium et Spes* to *Justice in the World* | Say what the Council did to the social doctrine and what the 1967-71 documents added | *Gaudium et Spes* (1965) as a pastoral constitution, its two parts and the differing weight of its sections; the person as origin, subject and end of every social institution; the rightful autonomy of earthly affairs; the common good and political community; *Populorum Progressio* (1967): integral development of the whole person and of every person, development as the new name for peace, the international dimension; *Octogesima Adveniens* (1971) handing discernment to local communities, and its treatment of socialism and of liberal ideology; the 1971 Synod's *Justice in the World* and its disputed phrase about action for justice as a constitutive dimension of preaching the Gospel; what "constitutive" was and was not taken to settle |

**Boss problem 2:** (a) Close reading from *Rerum Novarum* (1891 Vatican English translation, public domain): the passage defending private property and the passage on wages. State the argument of each in numbered premises, mark which premise carries the appeal to nature and which the appeal to the family, and say which words in the wage passage make the worker's sufficient living a matter of justice rather than of charity. *(Exegetical.)* (b) Reconstruct *Rerum Novarum*'s case against the socialist remedy, then state that remedy as a democratic socialist of the 1890s would have stated it, and reply in 150 words. The statement of the opposing view is graded first. *(Apologetic.)* (c) Trace one theme — the state's proper role in the economy — through *Rerum Novarum*, *Quadragesimo Anno*, *Mater et Magistra* and *Gaudium et Spes* (the last three paraphrased). For each, say what is claimed and at what level, and identify the one place where the change is plainly a change of application rather than of principle. *(Exegetical.)* (d) In 400-500 words: is the corpus from 1891 to 1971 a single developing teaching, or a series of responses whose continuity is mostly verbal? Argue either side, tag each premise with its document and level, and use at least two of Newman's notes from [`fundamental-theology`](../fundamental-theology/syllabus.md) 5.2. Concede the strongest point against your verdict. *(Evaluative.)*

### Module 3: The principles

The six load-bearing principles, each stated precisely enough to run on a case, each with the misreading it invites.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3.1 | Human dignity | State the ground of dignity in this tradition and what it rules out | the image of God (Genesis 1:26-27) and the person as body-soul unity; the person as origin, subject and end of institutions (*Gaudium et Spes*); dignity as inherent and not conferred, and not forfeited by condition or capacity; the personalistic norm assumed from [`phenomenology-and-personalism`](../phenomenology-and-personalism/syllabus.md) 5.2; rights language grounded on dignity (*Pacem in Terris*); *Dignitas Infinita* (Dicastery for the Doctrine of the Faith, 2024) and its distinction of senses of dignity; the contrast with dignity grounded on rational capacity, on interests, or on recognition (cited to [`ethics`](../ethics/syllabus.md) and [`political-philosophy`](../political-philosophy/syllabus.md), not argued) |
| 3.2 | The common good | Define the common good as the tradition defines it, and tell it from three near neighbours | the classical definition (the sum of conditions of social life allowing groups and individuals to reach fulfilment more fully and easily, *Gaudium et Spes*); its elements in the Catechism (respect for the person, social well-being and development, peace); why it is not the sum of individual goods, not the majority's good, and not the good of the collective over persons; political authority as ordered to it and legitimated by it; the universal common good and the international order; the contrast with utilitarian aggregation ([`political-philosophy`](../political-philosophy/syllabus.md) 2.1) and with state neutrality (its 4.1-4.3), reported and cited, not argued |
| 3.3 | Subsidiarity | State subsidiarity in both its halves and diagnose its two standard misuses | the formulation in *Quadragesimo Anno* (paraphrased): what a lesser and subordinate body can do should not be assigned to a greater and higher one; *subsidium* as help, so the principle both restrains and obliges the higher body; the family and intermediate associations, and civil society; what it does not say — it is not a presumption for small government, nor a rule about federal structure as such, nor a licence to withdraw help; its level, named by [`fundamental-theology`](../fundamental-theology/syllabus.md)'s own table as authentic ordinary magisterium; its economic cousin, fiscal federalism (cited to [`public-economics`](../public-economics/syllabus.md) 8.1-8.2) and public-goods assignment (its 1.1-1.3) |
| 3.4 | Solidarity | State solidarity as a virtue rather than a sentiment, and apply it to an institution | the older vocabulary of social charity and social friendship; interdependence as a fact that becomes a moral category; *Sollicitudo Rei Socialis*'s definition of solidarity as a firm and persevering determination to commit oneself to the common good; solidarity as neither collectivism nor philanthropy; structures of sin applied to economies and institutions (the category assumed from [`moral-theology`](../moral-theology/syllabus.md) 3.5); participation as the philosophical root (cited to [`phenomenology-and-personalism`](../phenomenology-and-personalism/syllabus.md) 5.5); fraternity and social friendship as the successor vocabulary in *Fratelli Tutti* (2020); how subsidiarity and solidarity are meant to correct rather than oppose each other |
| 3.5 | The universal destination of goods and private property | Order the two rightly, and run the ordering on a case | goods created for all as the first principle, private property as the ordinary and legitimate means of securing it, not its rival; the argument of *Rerum Novarum* and the twofold character of ownership in *Quadragesimo Anno*; Aquinas on the power to procure and dispense against the use of goods (ST II-II q.66 a.2) and necessity (a.7, from 1.4); the social mortgage on all private property (*Sollicitudo Rei Socialis*); what the ordering forbids to socialism and what it forbids to unlimited property rights alike; goods that fit property badly — land, water, common resources, knowledge (taken up in 6.1); what this course supplies to [`political-philosophy`](../political-philosophy/syllabus.md) 6.4 and [`philosophy-of-economics`](../philosophy-of-economics/syllabus.md) 5.4 |
| 3.6 | The preferential option for the poor | State the option as the Magisterium states it, and say exactly what was affirmed and criticized in liberation theology | the biblical ground (the poor, the widow, the orphan, Matthew 25) cited to [`old-testament`](../old-testament/syllabus.md) and [`new-testament`](../new-testament/syllabus.md); Medellín (1968) and Puebla (1979) and the phrase's entry into magisterial usage; adoption by Paul VI and John Paul II and its restatement in *Sollicitudo Rei Socialis* and *Centesimus Annus*; the CDF instructions *Libertatis Nuntius* (1984) and *Libertatis Conscientia* (1986) — what each affirms about injustice, structures and the Church's duty, and what each criticizes in the use of Marxist analysis, in a reduction of salvation to liberation, and in class struggle as a hermeneutic; "preferential" as not exclusive and not ideological; Francis's usage |

**Boss problem 3:** A case written for the problem: a national government proposes to replace a patchwork of parish, diocesan and municipal food assistance with a single centrally administered benefit, financed by a new tax, arguing that coverage will be more uniform. (a) Run each of the four principles of 3.1-3.4 on the proposal, stating for each what it requires, permits or forbids here, and where it stops giving a verdict. *(Exegetical for the statements; evaluative for the verdicts.)* (b) The subsidiarity objection and the solidarity argument here point in opposite directions. Name the crux — the single claim about the lesser bodies' actual capacity on which the disagreement turns — and say what evidence would settle it and who is competent to weigh it. *(Exegetical.)* (c) Close-read the passage in *Rerum Novarum* on the state's duty toward the weak, and say whether the proposal is the kind of intervention it contemplates. *(Exegetical.)* (d) A critic writes that the preferential option for the poor is either an empty piety or a Marxist analysis in vestments. State the objection at its strongest, then reply in 200 words using *Libertatis Nuntius* and *Libertatis Conscientia* (paraphrased), conceding what the 1984 instruction itself conceded about the injustices that provoked liberation theology. *(Apologetic.)*

### Module 4: Work, the economy and the two critiques

The doctrine's densest region: what work is, what a wage owes, and why the Church has judged both socialism and liberal capitalism and adopted neither.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |

## Notes

- **The course's central discipline.** Every lesson marks, for every claim it teaches, whether it is a permanent principle or a contingent application, and at what level. Lessons must not hide the hard edges of that discipline: (1) the *principle* of subsidiarity is authentic ordinary magisterium, and [`fundamental-theology`](../fundamental-theology/syllabus.md)'s own levels table uses it as the example of that rung; (2) the *proposal* of vocational orders in *Quadragesimo Anno*, the *proposal* of an international authority in *Pacem in Terris* and *Caritas in Veritate*, and every figure in every policy recommendation are applications; (3) how far a given judgment of Francis on nuclear weapons or on migration is a development of principle or a reading of present conditions is itself argued among Catholic theologians, and lessons present that argument rather than settle it in the lesson's voice; (4) the doctrine's own disclaimers (no technical solutions, no model, no party) are part of the teaching, not modesty about it.
- **Authority discipline for the hardest claims.** *Dignitatis Humanae* is a conciliar declaration whose core teaching the Council proposed authoritatively; it is not presented as a solemn definition, and neither are the nineteenth-century statements it must be reconciled with. Lessons state the levels exactly (5.2) and let the development question stay open, in step with [`fundamental-theology`](../fundamental-theology/syllabus.md) Boss 5, which sets this as its development case and cedes the substance here. *Centesimus Annus*'s answer on the market economy is conditional and lessons never quote it without its conditions. The just-war conditions as the Catechism presents them are magisterial; the judgment whether a particular war meets them is assigned by the Catechism itself to those responsible for the common good, and lessons preserve that assignment. Nothing in Module 6 licenses a reading that contradicts what is definitively taught.
- **Accuracy: verify before writing each lesson; assert nothing from memory.** Every section number of every document named in this syllabus is deliberately omitted and must be checked against the text before it is cited, including the subsidiarity paragraphs of *Quadragesimo Anno*, the solidarity and social-mortgage paragraphs of *Sollicitudo Rei Socialis*, the capitalism paragraph and the democracy paragraph of *Centesimus Annus*, the common-good definition in *Gaudium et Spes*, the clause in the opening section of *Dignitatis Humanae*, the just-war and migration paragraphs of the Catechism, and the "constitutive dimension" sentence in *Justice in the World*. Prefer naming a document without a number to naming it with a wrong one. No DH numbers are used unless checked against Denzinger–Hünermann. The dates of the CELAM conferences, of *Exsul Familia*, of the 1982 UN message and of the US bishops' letters are checked before use. **Copyright:** every document from 1931 onward, including *Quadragesimo Anno*, is paraphrased only and never quoted from a published modern translation; direct quotation is confined to the public-domain texts listed in "How practice works in this course".
- **Ownership collisions found and how they were resolved.** A grep of all built lessons found none teaching any topic of this course, so every collision was with an on-disk syllabus, and every one of them already points here. [`moral-theology`](../moral-theology/syllabus.md) cedes the economy, property, labor, the state, war and religious liberty (its Scope table and its 3.3, 3.5, 4.2), and this syllabus takes them without redefining social sin or cooperation. [`theology-of-debt`](../theology-of-debt/syllabus.md) is the one place where the steering and an on-disk syllabus could have clashed: its Scope table claims usury, finance, **international debt** and personal debt, and cedes "Catholic social teaching generally" here. That course wins; 6.2 teaches development and the international order and hands the debt question over by name. [`fundamental-theology`](../fundamental-theology/syllabus.md) cedes the substance of religious liberty (5.2) while keeping the ladder and the development method. [`constitutional-law`](../constitutional-law/syllabus.md) Module 7 keeps the US doctrine and asks for the comparison in 5.2. [`political-philosophy`](../political-philosophy/syllabus.md) names four places that cite this course (4.3 the common good, 6.2 immigration, 6.3 religious liberty and exemptions, 6.4 subsidiarity and the universal destination of goods); 3.2, 3.3, 3.5, 5.2 and 5.5 supply exactly those, and nothing here contradicts its Scope table or its verdict-neutral stance.
