# Christology · Lesson 1.2: True God and true man

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 1: The Word made flesh · Builds on: [1.1 What Christology asks](01-01-what-christology-asks.md) · Unlocks: [1.3 Why the Incarnation?](01-03-why-the-incarnation.md), [1.4 Arius and the divinity of the Son](01-04-arius-and-the-divinity-of-the-son.md), and every controversy in Module 2

## Why this matters

Everything in this course is this lesson's content or an argument about it. Module 2 runs the controversies that produced it; Module 3 asks what its words mean; Modules 4 and 5 test it against Gethsemane and the cross. This is the one lesson that states the result before the history — [the dogmatic core](../reference.md#the-dogmatic-core), in the words the Church defined it in — and gives you the tool the rest of the course leans on: every clause is the negative image of a particular mistake.

## The idea

[1.1](01-01-what-christology-asks.md) split every sentence about Christ into two questions: **who** is he, and **what** is he? The Church's answer fits on a card. *Who:* one subject, the eternal Word, the second person of the Trinity. *What:* two natures, the divine and the human, each complete, neither diminished by the other.

Now notice how few ways there are to get that wrong. You can subtract from the divine nature: the Son is the first and greatest creature, not God as the Father is God. You can subtract from the human nature, either the body (it only *seemed* to be flesh) or the mind (the Word took flesh but did the thinking himself). You can divide the *who*: a divine Son and a human son, closely joined. Or you can fuse the *what*: the humanity dissolved into the divinity, leaving one nature after the union. Five moves, five names — **Arianism, Docetism, Apollinarianism, Nestorianism, Eutychianism** — and one sentence written to block all five at once.

That is how to read a dogma: less a description of the mystery than a fence around it, each clause marking a place where someone went over. Leo put the whole fence in one line in the letter Chalcedon adopted, that we must believe "neither manhood to exist without true Godhead, nor Godhead without true manhood" (Tome, Percival's translation). The rest of this lesson matches clauses to posts.

## Source

The Definition of Chalcedon (451), in Percival's translation (*The Seven Ecumenical Councils*, NPNF 2nd series, vol. 14). First, from its preamble, the council's own description of what Leo's letter was written against; then the opening of the confession itself. Stop before "in two natures" — the four adverbs that follow are [2.3](02-03-eutyches-leo-and-chalcedon.md)'s.

> For it opposes those who would rend the mystery of the dispensation into a Duad of Sons; ... it resists those who imagine a mixture or confusion of the two natures of Christ; it drives away those who fancy his form of a servant is of an heavenly or some substance other than that which was taken of us ...
>
> Following the holy Fathers we teach with one voice that the Son [of God] and our Lord Jesus Christ is to be confessed as one and the same [Person], that he is perfect in Godhead and perfect in manhood, very God and very man, of a reasonable soul and [human] body consisting, consubstantial with the Father as touching his Godhead, and consubstantial with us as touching his manhood; made in all things like unto us, sin only excepted ...

First, **the council names its targets.** The "Duad of Sons" is the two-subject Christ; the "mixture or confusion" is the fused one; the "heavenly" flesh is a body not taken from us. You do not have to guess the negative image — the Definition prints it.

Second, **"the same" keeps coming back.** In the Greek, *ton auton* ("the same one") recurs through the sentence, and Schaff's translation keeps it before each pair. Every pair of predicates — Godhead and manhood, Father and us — hangs on one subject. The grammar is the doctrine.

## The argument

The dogmatic core, clause by clause, with the defining act that fixed each one.

1. **Truly God, consubstantial with the Father.** Nicaea (325) put *homoousios*, "of one substance", into its creed, and the creed read at Chalcedon says of the Son "very God of very God, begotten not made, being of one substance with the Father" (Percival). Nicaea's anathema condemns saying he was made from nothing or is a creature. *In words:* whatever the Father is as God, the Son is. The argument that won this is [1.4](01-04-arius-and-the-divinity-of-the-son.md)'s; its conciliar history is [`trinity-and-god` 3.1](../../trinity-and-god/lessons/03-01-nicaea.md).
2. **Truly man, with a true body.** The same creed: "was incarnate by the Holy Ghost and the Virgin Mary, and was made man." Chalcedon adds "consubstantial with us as touching his manhood" and "in all things like unto us, sin only excepted." *In words:* whatever we are as human beings, he is — except sin, which is a wound in human nature, not part of it.
3. **With a rational soul.** Chalcedon's "of a reasonable soul and body", after Constantinople I (381) had anathematized the Apollinarians by name in its first canon. *In words:* the humanity includes a human mind; the Word did not stand in for it.
4. **One and the same.** Ephesus (431) judged Cyril's second letter to Nestorius consonant with Nicaea and deposed Nestorius; that letter says the holy Virgin is Mother of God because the body born of her, with a rational soul, is the Word's own. Chalcedon repeats the [*Theotokos*](../reference.md#theotokos) and the "one and the same." *In words:* there is exactly one *who* in Christ, and it is the Word; everything born, suffering or dying is his.
5. **Perfect in manhood, even after the union.** Chalcedon, against Eutyches, who confessed two natures before the union and one after it (as Leo's Tome reports him). *In words:* the humanity is not absorbed; it remains a complete human nature for ever.
6. **Two natural wills and operations.** Constantinople III (681) drew out what "perfect in manhood" implies: two natural wills and two natural operations, the human will following the divine, not resisting it. *In words:* a complete human nature includes a human willing, or it is not complete. That is [2.5](02-05-monothelitism-and-constantinople-iii.md)'s.

The Catechism gathers the same core under two headings — "True God and true man" (CCC 464–469), which walks the councils from Nicaea to Constantinople II, and "How is the Son of God man?" (CCC 470–478), which spells out the human soul with real knowledge, the human will, the true body, and the human heart. Its opening move is the one to remember: the Incarnation does not make Christ part God and part man, nor a confused mixture of the two (CCC 464, paraphrased).

**Mark the levels.** Every clause above is **rung 1, defined dogma** ([`fundamental-theology` 4.4](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-04-the-ladder-of-doctrinal-authority.md)): Nicaea for the divinity, the creed of 381 and Chalcedon for the true body and rational soul (with Constantinople I's canon against the Apollinarians), Ephesus and Chalcedon for the single subject and *Theotokos*, Chalcedon for the integrity of both natures, Constantinople III for the two wills. The true body was the apostolic faith before any council met (1 John 4:2–3), and the creed's "made man" and Chalcedon's "consubstantial with us" define it all the same. Understating any of these as "one theological model" is an error; so is inflating to the same level the *explanations* of how the union works, which are Module 3's business and mostly lower on the ladder ([the levels in Christology](../reference.md#the-levels-in-christology)).

## What each clause excludes

| Clause | Excludes | The error in one line | Map square ([1.1](01-01-what-christology-asks.md)) |
|---|---|---|---|
| "very God", "consubstantial with the Father", "perfect in Godhead" | **Arianism** | the Son is the highest creature, not God as the Father is | subtract from the divine |
| "very man", "consubstantial with us", "of a ... body", flesh not "heavenly" | **Docetism** | the body was an appearance; he only seemed to suffer | subtract from the human |
| "of a reasonable soul" | **Apollinarianism** | the Word took flesh and did the thinking in place of a human mind | subtract from the human |
| "one and the same", "Mother of God" | **Nestorianism** | a divine Son and a human son, united in will and honour — a "Duad of Sons" | divide the *who* |
| "perfect in manhood", "consubstantial with us", no "mixture or confusion" | **Eutychianism** | one nature after the union, the humanity absorbed | fuse the *what* |

Two cautions. The names are textbook labels; the real positions were subtler, and Module 2 states each at its strongest before critiquing it — Apollinaris was defending the unity of Christ, Nestorius the integrity of the humanity, and the patristic docetists are known mostly through their opponents ([`patristics` 1.3](../../patristics/lessons/01-03-ignatius-of-antioch.md)). And the columns are not one-to-one: "consubstantial with us" blocks both Docetism and Eutychianism, because both leave Christ's humanity something other than ours. 
Why these five and not others? Because behind every clause stands the principle [2.1](02-01-apollinaris-a-complete-humanity.md) reconstructs: [what is not assumed is not healed](../reference.md#the-soteriological-rule). Subtract the mind, and the mind is not saved.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 — the clean case.** An invented Christmas card: *"God came among us in human disguise."*

Run the table. A disguise is an appearance that is not what it seems — the costume is not the wearer's own nature. So "human disguise" says the humanity is an appearance: **Docetism**, the subtract-from-the-human square. The clause that excludes it is "very man ... consubstantial with us as touching his manhood": the humanity is as real as ours, not a covering over something else. What the writer was reaching for is true: the divinity *was* hidden, and people looked at Jesus and saw a carpenter. But the hiddenness is of the divinity *in* a real humanity, not of the divinity *behind* a fake one. The honest rewording is "God came among us as one of us" — [3.3](03-03-kenosis.md) will say exactly what was hidden and what was not.

**Example 2 — where the machinery strains.** The catechetical slogan *"Jesus is 100% God and 100% man."*

It sounds like arithmetic heresy, but the slogan is **true only with a distinction**, and the distinction is the who/what split. Read as a claim about *natures*, it is exactly Chalcedon: "perfect in Godhead and perfect in manhood" — neither nature is a fraction, which is what the Catechism's "not part God and part man" is ruling out. Read as a claim about *subjects*, it goes wrong: 100 percent of one somebody plus 100 percent of another is two somebodies, the Nestorian Duad. The percentages quantify over natures, never over persons. There is a second trap: "100% man" could be heard as "a human person", and the tradition says the *who* of the humanity is the Word. That is [3.1](03-01-person-and-nature-the-hypostatic-union.md)'s question, and it is where the arithmetic image stops helping, because no ratio shows a complete human nature with no human *who* of its own.

Verdict: keep the slogan for the natures, and add in the same breath "one person, the Son" — which is what the Definition does with "the same."

## Watch out

- **You might think "part God, part man" is a harmless simplification.** It is the error the Catechism names first (CCC 464). Halve the divinity and you are in the **Arian** square; halve the humanity and you are **Apollinarian** — Apollinaris's Christ was precisely human body plus divine mind.
- **You might think "the divinity took over the humanity" is just a strong way of saying Christ was divine.** An absorbed humanity is no longer consubstantial with us: that is **Eutychianism**, and Chalcedon's "perfect in manhood" was written against it.
- **You might think the core is only as old as Chalcedon, so it is "Greek philosophy imposed on the Gospel."** The councils added vocabulary (*homoousios*, *hypostasis*) to exclude readings, not new content. Whether the vocabulary was the right tool is argued in [6.4](06-04-christology-today.md).
- **You might overstate in the other direction** and treat every standard explanation — how the natures are united, whether Christ has one *esse* — as defined. Only the clauses in the argument are rung 1.

## One-liner

> One *who*, two complete *whats*: every clause of the Definition is a fence post where someone divided the one or subtracted from the two.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢)** *(Exegetical)* The Athanasian Creed (Latin, of uncertain authorship; long used in the Western liturgy), verses 34–37, as Schaff prints them (*Creeds of Christendom* vol. 2; his brackets):

> 34. Who although he be [is] God and Man; yet he is not two, but one Christ.
> 35. One; not by conversion of the Godhead into flesh: but by taking [assumption] of the Manhood into God.
> 36. One altogether; not by confusion of Substance [Essence]: but by unity of Person.
> 37. For as the reasonable soul and flesh is one man: so God and Man is one Christ;

(a) Verses 35 and 36 each deny one account of *how* Christ is one. Name the account each denies, and say which square of the who/what map it lands in. (b) Verse 37 compares Christ to a human being. In two sentences: what does the comparison establish, and where must it not be pressed?

**P2 (🟡)** *(Exegetical)* An invented handout for a parish adult-formation evening:

> "(i) At the Annunciation, God's Son joined himself to a holy baby in Mary's womb, and from then on the two of them shared one life and one purpose. (ii) The Son of God did not need a human mind; the divine Word did all his thinking. (iii) After the resurrection his humanity was swallowed up in his divinity, like a drop of water in the ocean."

For each sentence: name the error it lands in, and quote the words of the Chalcedonian opening (the Source) that exclude it. **One sentence per item.**

**P3 (🔴)** *(Exegetical)* Mark each sentence **true**, **false**, or **true only with a distinction**. For each true or distinguished one, name the conciliar act that fixes it and its rung; for each false one, name the clause that excludes it. **120 words total.**

(i) Christ's body was not taken from Mary but formed in heaven.
(ii) The Son is less than the Father.
(iii) Christ has a human will as well as a divine one.
(iv) Mary is the mother of Christ's humanity, not the mother of God.

<details>
<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical (a) · Exegetical (b))*

**Must hit, strict (a):** v.35 denies that Christ is one because **the Godhead was changed into flesh** — a divine nature that turned into something else. That subtracts from the divine (a changeable Godhead is exactly what Nicaea's anathema on "subject to change or conversion" excluded) and fuses the natures by conversion; credit either square if the change of the divine nature is named. Its positive half puts the union in an *assuming*: the humanity is taken up, nothing divine is lost. v.36 denies that Christ is one by **confusion of substance** — a mixture producing one nature: **Eutychianism**, fuse the *what*. Its positive half puts the unity where Chalcedon does, in the **person**.

**Must hit, strict (b):** the comparison establishes **one subject out of two really distinct components** — soul and flesh do not become each other, yet make one man. It must not be pressed to the point of **one nature**: soul and flesh compose a single human nature, while God and man do not compose a third nature — that reading is the Eutychian (or Apollinarian) mistake the Creed just excluded in v.36.

**Wrong turns:** calling v.36 anti-Nestorian because it mentions the person — the *denial* is of confusion; "unity of Person" is the positive answer. Reading v.37 as saying the Word is Christ's soul — that is Apollinaris exactly.

**Model answer (b):** The analogy shows that two really different things can make one individual without either turning into the other. It fails if pressed to one nature, because soul and body make one human nature, whereas the Word and his humanity remain two natures in one person.

**P2** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict:** (i) **Nestorianism** — two subjects, the Son and "a holy baby", joined by shared life and purpose; excluded by "**one and the same** [Person]" (credit "Mother of God" also, from the Definition's continuation). (ii) **Apollinarianism** — the Word replaces the human mind; excluded by "**of a reasonable soul** and [human] body consisting". (iii) **Eutychianism** — the humanity absorbed; excluded by "**perfect in manhood**" and "**consubstantial with us** as touching his manhood" (either suffices; credit citing the preamble's "mixture or confusion").

**Wrong turns:** calling (i) adoptionism without naming the two-subject structure — adoptionism is a close relative, but the sentence's error is that there are two *who*s from conception; calling (iii) Docetism, which denies the humanity was ever real, not that it later ceased; thinking the resurrection changes the rule — the risen Christ is still consubstantial with us.

**P3** *(Exegetical — strict on levels)*

**Must hit, strict:** (i) **False** — the heavenly-flesh view the preamble "drives away"; excluded by "consubstantial with us as touching his manhood". (ii) **True only with a distinction** — as man, yes (the Athanasian Creed: "inferior to the Father as touching his Manhood"); as God, no — "consubstantial with the Father", **Nicaea**, rung 1 ([`trinity-and-god` 2.2](../../trinity-and-god/lessons/02-02-the-father-and-the-son-in-the-new-testament.md) reads John 14:28 this way). (iii) **True** — **Constantinople III (681)**, two natural wills, rung 1. (iv) **False** — Mary is mother of a *who*, not of a nature, and the one born of her is the Word; excluded by "Mother of God", **Ephesus** (Cyril's second letter approved) and **Chalcedon**.

**Wrong turns:** marking (ii) simply false (loses the manhood half) or simply true (Arian); assigning (iii) to Chalcedon alone, or putting it below rung 1 because it came "late"; defending (iv) as a careful distinction — it separates what the Definition joins in "the same".

**Model answer:** (i) False: the humanity is consubstantial with us (Chalcedon). (ii) Distinguish: less as man, equal as God — consubstantial with the Father, Nicaea, rung 1. (iii) True: Constantinople III, rung 1. (iv) False: one subject is born, and he is God — *Theotokos*, Ephesus and Chalcedon, rung 1.
