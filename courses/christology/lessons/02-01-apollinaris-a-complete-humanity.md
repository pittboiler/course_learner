# Christology · Lesson 2.1: Apollinaris: a complete humanity

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 2: From Apollinaris to the two wills · Builds on: [1.2 True God and true man](01-02-true-god-and-true-man.md), [1.4 Arius and the divinity of the Son](01-04-arius-and-the-divinity-of-the-son.md) · Unlocks: [2.2 Nestorius, Cyril, and Ephesus (431)](02-02-nestorius-cyril-and-ephesus.md)

## Why this matters

Nicaea settled that the one who became man is God without qualification ([1.4](01-04-arius-and-the-divinity-of-the-son.md)). That answer created the next problem at once: if a whole God and a whole man are in Christ, why are there not two of him? The first serious answer came from a Nicene bishop: elegant, scriptural and wrong. The reply produced the sentence that drives the rest of this module: **what the Word did not assume, he did not heal.** Every controversy from here to 681 is that sentence applied to one more part of the human being.

## The idea

Apollinaris, bishop of Laodicea in Syria, was no Arian. He was a Nicene of the first generation; Sozomen reports that his non-Nicene bishop excommunicated him for receiving Athanasius (Socrates gives another reason). His worry was the opposite of Arius's. He took the Son's full divinity as settled, and asked how a fully divine Word and a fully human man could be one Christ rather than two.

His answer used an anthropology common at the time, the trichotomy of 1 Thessalonians 5:23: a human being is **body**, **animal soul** (the life-principle we share with beasts), and **[*nous*](../reference.md#nous)**, the rational mind, which is the self that knows, chooses and steers. In Christ, Apollinaris said, the Word takes the place of the *nous*. Christ has a real body and a real animating soul. The Word is his mind.

This bought three things, and each is worth taking seriously.

1. **Unity.** A complete man already has a steering mind. Add the Word and you have two steering selves, which is two sons. Gregory reports the objection in its shortest form: he "could not contain Two perfect Natures." Remove one mind and exactly one subject is left, and it is the Word.
2. **Sinlessness.** A human mind is changeable and self-determining, and that is where sin happens. Put the unchangeable Word in its place and Christ's sinlessness is guaranteed by construction, not by a struggle whose outcome might have gone the other way.
3. **Scripture.** "The Word was made *flesh*" (John 1:14), not "the Word was made mind."

That is [Apollinarianism](../reference.md#apollinarianism): one subject, sinlessness secured, and a humanity missing its top floor.

## Source

Gregory of Nazianzus, Letter 101, to Cledonius (NPNF 2.7, trans. Browne and Swallow). [`patristics` 3.3](../../patristics/lessons/03-03-the-two-gregories.md) reads the letter's famous sentence. Here are the lines on either side of it, where the argument is:

> If only half Adam fell, then that which Christ assumes and saves may be half also; but if the whole of his nature fell, it must be united to the whole nature of Him that was begotten, and so be saved as a whole. ...
>
> But, it may be said, our mind is subject to condemnation. What then of our flesh? Is that not subject to condemnation? You must therefore either set aside the latter on account of sin, or admit the former on account of salvation. ... If the mind was utterly rejected, as prone to sin and subject to damnation, and for this reason He assumed a body but left out the mind, then there is an excuse for them who sin with the mind; for the witness of God--according to you--has shewn the impossibility of healing it.

**"Half Adam"** turns a question about Christ into a question about the fall: the extent of the assumption must match the extent of the damage. **"What then of our flesh?"** meets Apollinaris's second purchase head-on. If the mind is left out because it is where sin lives, the flesh should be left out too, since it is also condemned. The objection proves too much. **"An excuse for them who sin with the mind"** is the sting: a Christ without a mind does not merely lack something. He announces that the mind cannot be saved.

## The argument

The [soteriological rule](../reference.md#the-soteriological-rule), reconstructed:

1. **The Word heals human nature by uniting it to himself.** *In words:* salvation happens by contact, not by decree. This is Irenaeus's recapitulation, in which Christ goes over Adam's ground again and reverses it ([`patristics` 2.2](../../patristics/lessons/02-02-irenaeus-recapitulation.md)), and Athanasius's God made man that man might be made God ([`patristics` 3.1](../../patristics/lessons/03-01-athanasius.md)).
2. **What is not united to him is not healed by him.** *In words:* assumption is not just one way of healing; it is the way. This is the premise that does the work, and it is the one to test.
3. **The whole of human nature fell, the mind included.** Apollinaris concedes it: the mind's liability to sin is his reason for leaving it out. Adam's transgression was a choice before it was a symptom.
4. **So the whole of human nature, mind included, must be assumed.**
5. **Apollinaris's Christ has no human mind.**
6. **So Apollinaris's Christ leaves the human mind unhealed**, which is the one part that most needed healing.

*In words:* you cannot save a mind by removing it.

Gregory answers the other two purchases separately. To **unity**, he says the "two perfects" objection counts minds as if they were bodies. A bushel cannot hold two bushels. But incorporeal things do not crowd each other out: a little torch brought near a great blaze is not destroyed; it becomes part of one blaze. To **sinlessness**, he gives the flesh argument from the Source. A mind that is assumed is not left as it was. It is sanctified by its union with God.

Premise 2 is the weak point, and it is the right place to push. Why could God not heal a mind he did not take? The Fathers' answer is not a proof. It is their whole account of salvation as divinization: what is healed is what is joined to God, as iron is made hot by being put in the fire. Think of salvation purely as a legal verdict and premise 2 loses much of its force, one reason some later Western accounts of redemption lean on it less (Module 5).

**Mark the levels.** *That Christ has a rational human soul is defined dogma.* Chalcedon confesses him "perfect in manhood ... of a reasonable soul and [human] body consisting" (Percival), and the Chalcedonian Definition is a solemn conciliar judgment (rung 1 in [`fundamental-theology` 4.4](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-04-the-ladder-of-doctrinal-authority.md)). The earlier acts point the same way. **Constantinople I** (381), in the list of heresies anathematized in its first canon (Percival, Canon I), names "the Apollinarians." A **Roman synod under Damasus** condemned the teaching in the later 370s. Gregory's letter mentions "the Western Synod, by which they were formerly condemned," and the NPNF editors date it 377; the exact date is uncertain. *The rule itself was never defined.* It is the common axiom of the Fathers (rung 4), and the dogma does not depend on accepting every use made of it. The trichotomy is an anthropology, not a doctrine at any level. See the full [levels in Christology](../reference.md#the-levels-in-christology).

## The rule, run forward

Each controversy in this module subtracts or fuses something, and each gets the same answer.

| Lesson | What goes missing | Who | Rule's verdict | Clause that fixed it |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2.1 | the human **mind** | Apollinaris | the mind unhealed | "reasonable soul"; perfect in manhood |
| [2.2](02-02-nestorius-cyril-and-ephesus.md) | **union**: the man is joined to the Word, not his own | Nestorius | a humanity never assumed, only accompanied | *Theotokos*; one subject |
| [2.3](02-03-eutyches-leo-and-chalcedon.md) | the human **nature** as distinct | Eutyches | a humanity absorbed, not healed | "unconfusedly, immutably" |
| [2.5](02-05-monothelitism-and-constantinople-iii.md) | the human **will** | monothelites | human willing unhealed | two natural wills |

Note the second row: Nestorius subtracts no part but withholds the union, and a complete man merely accompanied by the Word has not been assumed, so not healed. The shapes differ: Apollinaris and the monothelites subtract (the *what* made incomplete); Eutyches fuses (the *what* confused); Nestorius divides (the *who* doubled).

**Two grammars.** Aloys Grillmeier (*Christ in Christian Tradition*, vol. 1; paraphrased) sorted fourth-century Christology into a **[*Logos-sarx* and a *Logos-anthropos*](../reference.md#logos-sarx-christology)** framework. *Logos-sarx* (Word-flesh) is broadly Alexandrian: the Word is the acting subject, and the flesh is his instrument. It secures unity, and at its extreme is Apollinaris. *Logos-anthropos* (Word-man) is broadly Antiochene (Diodore, Theodore): the Word unites a complete man to himself. It secures completeness, and its danger is two subjects. The next century argues in these two grammars; Chalcedon tries to keep the strength of each.

The schema is useful and too neat, much as the de Régnon paradigm is for the Trinity ([`trinity-and-god` 1.1](../../trinity-and-god/lessons/01-01-the-treatise-and-its-order.md)). Grillmeier put Athanasius on the *Logos-sarx* side, because Christ's soul plays no visible part in his arguments. Critics answer that silence is not denial. Athanasius's own *Tome to the Antiochenes* (362, §7) records agreement that the Saviour had "not a body without a soul, nor without sense or intelligence." Whether that phrase already meant a rational soul in the anti-Apollinarian sense is itself disputed. Use the frameworks as tendencies, not as teams.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: the rule on a clean case: flesh from heaven.** Gregory reports (Letter 202, to Nectarius) that Apollinaris made Christ's flesh eternal: it was not taken from Mary but "in the Son from the beginning," on the strength of 1 Corinthians 15:47, "The Second Man is the Lord from Heaven." Run the rule. Our flesh descends from Adam. A flesh that did not come from Adam is not ours, so ours was not assumed, and so ours is not healed. Hence Gregory's anathema in Letter 101 on anyone who says "His flesh came down from heaven, and is not from hence, nor of us." Chalcedon's own preamble says the Definition drives away those who imagine the form of a servant to be "of an heavenly or some substance other than that which was taken of us." Fairness note: the NPNF editors record the view that Gregory pushed Apollinaris's statements to conclusions he would have repudiated; the heavenly flesh may be his disciples' more than his.

**Example 2: where the rule strains: sin.** If whatever is not assumed is not healed, and Christ assumed no sin ("in all things like unto us, sin only excepted"), is sin left unhealed? The Apollinarian presses: if sin lives in the mind, a sinless mind is not *our* mind. Two replies, which need each other. First, the rule's object is **nature**, not defect. Sin is not a part of human nature, as the mind is; it is a wound in it. What must be assumed is everything that belongs to being human, and a sinless mind is a whole mind. Second, the healing of sin is not assumption but **removal**: the nature is assumed so that the wound can be closed. The strain is still real. Whether the Word assumed a *fallen* nature, one that bears the effects of sin without the sin, is a question the rule raises and does not settle. It reappears with Christ's sinlessness in [3.4](03-04-the-grace-of-the-head.md).

## Watch out

- **You might think Apollinaris denied Christ's humanity.** He gave Christ a body and an animating soul. What he denied was a human *mind*. Calling him a Docetist erases the precise subtraction the rule condemns, and hides why he was attractive.
- **You might think "a human soul" settles it.** "Jesus had a soul, but God did his thinking" is Apollinarianism word for word. The dogma is a *reasonable* (rational) soul, and the qualifier is the whole point.
- **You might think the fix is two minds and therefore two selves.** That is **Nestorianism**. Two minds belong to two natures, not to two subjects. How one subject can have two complete natures is the next lesson's question, not this one's answer.
- **You might think the rule is defined dogma.** The conclusion it served is dogma; the sentence itself is a patristic argument (rung 4).

## One-liner

> Apollinaris took out Christ's mind to make him one and sinless; Gregory answered that a mind left out is a mind left unsaved.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢)** *(Exegetical)* Mark each sentence true, false, or true only with a stated distinction, and name the rule or conciliar text that decides it. One line each.

(i) "Christ had a human soul, but the Word supplied its reason."
(ii) "In Christ, the Word thought with a human mind."
(iii) "Since Christ could not sin, he had no need of a human mind."
(iv) "Christ did not assume sin, so our sin is left unhealed."
(v) "Christ's human mind was healed by being assumed."

**P2 (🟡)** *(Exegetical)* An **invented** carol verse, written for this problem and not the words of any real person:

> *No wandering thought his heart could stir,*
> *No human doubt, no human fear:*
> *God's own mind in the manger lay,*
> *Wrapped in our flesh, to guide our clay.*

(a) Name the error in the verse and the line that commits it most exactly. (b) Give the rule-based objection in two sentences, and name the conciliar text that excludes the error and its level. (c) In one sentence, say what true thing the author was reaching for. 120 words total.

**P3 (🔴)** *(Exegetical)* Read this passage from Letter 101 (NPNF 2.7):

> But, he asserts, He could not contain Two perfect Natures. Not if you only look at Him in a bodily fashion. For a bushel measure will not hold two bushels, nor will the space of one body hold two or more bodies. But if you will look at what is mental and incorporeal, remember that I in my one personality can contain soul and reason and mind and the Holy Spirit ... For such is the nature of intellectual Existences, that they can mingle with one another and with bodies, incorporeally and invisibly.

(a) State the premise of Apollinaris's objection that Gregory denies, and name the words that deny it. (b) Say what the reply establishes, and what it does *not* establish that the next two controversies will need. Name one word in the passage a later party could exploit. 120 words total.

<details>
<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict:**
- (i) **False.** This is Apollinarianism exactly: soul as life-principle, with the Word as *nous*. Decided by Chalcedon's "of a reasonable soul," and by the rule, since a mind that is not assumed is not healed.
- (ii) **True.** The one subject of Christ's human acts is the Word, and the human mind is the Word's own. The sentence names the person (the Word) and the nature he acts through (a human mind). It would be false only if "the Word" were read as "the divine nature," but the sentence does not say that.
- (iii) **False.** This is Apollinaris's second purchase. Sinlessness does not require removing the mind: an assumed mind is sanctified by the union. Gregory's flesh argument applies too: flesh is also condemned, yet it is assumed.
- (iv) **False.** The rule's object is nature, not defect. Sin is a wound in the nature, not a part of it. The whole nature is assumed so that the wound can be removed.
- (v) **True only with a distinction.** Christ's *own* mind was never sick, since he was sinless from the first moment. Nothing in it was healed from a prior disorder. What is true is that the human mind *as a nature* is healed in him: joined to God, it is sanctified, and that healing reaches ours.

**Wrong turns:** marking (ii) false because "God cannot think humanly." That confuses the person with the nature, and it is the Nestorian reflex. Marking (v) simply true, which implies that Christ's mind was disordered before the union. Deciding (iii) by appeal to impeccability alone, without seeing that it is Apollinaris's argument.

**Model answer:** (i) F: Apollinarian; Chalcedon's "reasonable soul." (ii) T: one subject, human mind. (iii) F: an assumed mind is sanctified, not removed. (iv) F: the rule concerns nature, not defect; sin is removed, not assumed. (v) T with a distinction: healed as nature, never sick as his.

**P2** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** **Apollinarianism.** The deciding line is "God's own mind in the manger lay," with "wrapped in our flesh" beside it: the divine mind is in the place of a human one, clothed in flesh. Lines 1-2 alone could be read as sinlessness plus hyperbole. Line 3 makes it a substitution.

**Must hit, strict (b):** The whole of human nature fell, the mind first. What is not assumed is not healed, so a Christ without a human mind leaves our minds unsaved. Chalcedon ("perfect in manhood ... of a reasonable soul") excludes it: **defined dogma, rung 1.** Credit for adding Constantinople I, canon 1, naming the Apollinarians.

**Must hit, strict (c):** Christ's sinlessness and the unity of his person: there is one "he" in the manger, and it is the Word.

**Wrong turns:** calling it Docetism, when the flesh is real in the verse. Calling it Arianism, when the verse makes the mind *more* divine, not less. Giving the rule's level as the dogma's. Condemning line 2 ("no human fear"), which is a separate error about Christ's passions (see [4.1](04-01-christs-human-will-in-act.md)) but not the Apollinarian one.

**Model answer:** (a) Apollinarianism, in line 3: God's mind stands where a human mind should be, wrapped in flesh. (b) Since the whole of human nature fell, mind first, and only what is assumed is healed, a Christ with no human mind leaves the mind unsaved. Chalcedon's "reasonable soul" excludes it, and that is defined dogma. (c) The author means that Christ is sinless and that the one in the manger is the Word himself.

**P3** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** The premise denied is that two complete natures must *compete for the same space*: two perfect things cannot be one, because each fills what the other would occupy. The denying words are "in a bodily fashion" (the objection treats minds like bodies) together with "mental and incorporeal" and "can mingle ... incorporeally."

**Must hit, strict (b):** The reply establishes **compatibility**: a complete human mind and the divine Word do not crowd each other out, so completeness does not require subtraction. It does **not** establish **unity of subject**. His own analogy (one person containing soul, mind and the Holy Spirit) shows only that several things can be *in* one subject, not *whose* the human mind is. Nestorius (2.2) will grant compatibility and still posit a conjunction. The exploitable word is **"mingle"** (and the torch-and-blaze image just after it), which a Eutychian reading (2.3) can take as fusion.

**Wrong turns:** saying Gregory denies that Christ has two natures, when he affirms "two perfect Natures." Reading "one personality" as a technical claim about *hypostasis*, when the vocabulary is not yet fixed in 382.

**Model answer:** (a) Apollinaris assumes that two perfect natures must occupy the same room, so one must go; Gregory denies it with "in a bodily fashion," because minds are "incorporeal" and "mingle" without displacement. (b) That shows a human mind and the Word can coexist in Christ. It does not show that they are one subject, which is Cyril's quarrel with Nestorius. And "mingle" invites the fusion Chalcedon had to forbid.

## Flashback

**From Lesson [1.3](01-03-why-the-incarnation.md) (Why the Incarnation?):** *(Exegetical.)* Diagnose. An **invented** parish-bulletin paragraph, written for this problem and not the words of any real person:

> "Why did the Son of God come among us? Above all to show us how to live: he set the example, and now it is up to us to follow it. His coming also means that one day we will be divine exactly as he is divine. And Aquinas showed that the Incarnation follows from God's goodness, so a clear enough thinker could have worked it out before Bethlehem."

The paragraph makes three errors, one per sentence after the question. For each, say what it gets wrong, name the error it lands in where there is one, and give the correction. **One or two sentences each.**

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict:**
- **Example only.** It reduces the purposes of the Incarnation to one. The Catechism's list (CCC 456–460) puts the model of holiness beside reconciliation with God, knowing God's love, and sharing the divine nature. A Christ who only shows the way leaves us to walk it by our own power, which is the shape of **Pelagianism**. The example is real, but it serves the rescue and the lifting.
- **"Exactly as he is divine."** Deification by nature collapses creature into God: **pantheism**, which Vatican I condemns (*Dei Filius*, canons on God the Creator). The correction: we become "partakers of the divine nature" (2 Peter 1:4) **by grace**, sharing by gift what the Son is by nature.
- **"Worked it out before Bethlehem."** This reverses the **direction of fittingness**. ST III q.1 a.1 starts from the revealed fact and shows why it befits God's goodness. It concludes that the Incarnation is *fitting*, which is not a proof, and no one could have deduced it in advance.

**Wrong turns:** correcting the first sentence by denying that Christ is our example. Calling the second sentence Arian or Nestorian: it errs about *us*, not about Christ. Correcting the third by saying the Incarnation has no reasons at all, which swaps compulsion for arbitrariness. Answering the third only with "God did not *have* to," which is a different error (P2 of 1.3); the fault here is running from the reasons to the fact.

**Model answer:** (1) It shrinks four purposes to one. An example we must follow by our own strength is the Pelagian shape, while Christ came to reconcile and deify as well as to model. (2) "Exactly as he is divine" is pantheism; we share by grace what the Son is by nature. (3) Fittingness runs from the revealed fact to its reasons: Aquinas shows the Incarnation befits God's goodness once it has happened, not that anyone could have deduced it.
