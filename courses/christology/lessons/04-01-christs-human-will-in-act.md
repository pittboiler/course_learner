# Christology · Lesson 4.1: Christ's human will in act

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 4: The human mind of Christ · Builds on: [2.5 Monothelitism and Constantinople III](02-05-monothelitism-and-constantinople-iii.md), [3.4 The grace of the Head](03-04-the-grace-of-the-head.md) · Unlocks: [4.2 Christ's human knowledge: the classical account](04-02-christs-human-knowledge-the-classical-account.md), [5.1 The Paschal Mystery and the threefold office](05-01-the-paschal-mystery-and-the-threefold-office.md)

## Why this matters

[2.5](02-05-monothelitism-and-constantinople-iii.md) proved that Christ has a human will. This lesson asks what it did. The test case is a garden: a man prays not to die, then prays that another will be done. Read Gethsemane badly and the recoil from death is either play-acting or a second agent bargaining with God. Read it well and you have the tradition's answer to a harder question: how can a will that cannot sin be free?

## The idea

Recall the settled point in one sentence: willing belongs to a nature, not to a person, so a complete human nature brings a complete human will ([2.5](02-05-monothelitism-and-constantinople-iii.md); the text is read in [`patristics` 5.4](../../patristics/lessons/05-04-maximus-and-john-of-damascus.md)). That settles the *what*: two wills. The *who* is untouched. There is one willer, the Word, who wills divinely with the divine will and humanly with the human will. Two wills, one agent.

Now look inside the human will. Aquinas separates three movements in it:

- **Sensuality**, the sensitive appetite. It recoils from pain the way a hand pulls back from a flame.
- **Will as nature**: the rational will drawn toward what is good in itself (life, health) and away from what is evil in itself (death).
- **Will as reason**: the rational will weighing a means against an end, the way a patient chooses surgery he does not want for the sake of health he does.

In a good person the first two can say no to what the third says yes to, without sin: a man who chooses the knife while flinching from it is not divided against himself. Gethsemane is that structure at its limit: "remove this chalice" is nature, "not my will, but thine" is reason, and reason's choice is one with the divine will, which is also the Word's own.

## Source

The definition of Constantinople III (680–681), read at Session XVIII (Percival, *NPNF* 2.14):

> we likewise declare that in him are two natural wills and two natural operations indivisibly, inconvertibly, inseparably, inconfusedly, according to the teaching of the holy Fathers. And these two natural wills are not contrary the one to the other (God forbid!) as the impious heretics assert, but his human will follows and that not as resisting and reluctant, but rather as subject to his divine and omnipotent will. ... For as his flesh is called and is the flesh of God the Word, so also the natural will of his flesh is called and is the proper will of God the Word ... so also his human will, although deified, was not suppressed, but was rather preserved

The four adverbs are Chalcedon's, carried from natures to wills: the same doctrine, not a new one. Three phrases decide how the will acts:

- **"Follows ... as subject."** Only something with its own motion can be *subject*; the human will yields, it is not an empty channel.
- **"Not as resisting and reluctant."** This rules out a human will dragged, protesting, into line.
- **"The proper will of God the Word."** This is the *who* clause. The human will belongs to the Word as his own, exactly as the flesh does. So it is never a second person's will. "Deified ... not suppressed" is the phrase the whole lesson turns on: union with God perfects the human will and does not replace it.

## The argument

1. **Two wills, not one** (ST III q.18 a.1). The will is a natural power of a complete human nature, and the Word assumed a complete nature, lacking nothing. *In words:* take away the human will and you have Apollinaris's Christ without a mind, moved from outside.
2. **Within the human will, one power and several acts** (aa.2–3). Sensuality is "will by participation," because it can obey reason. The rational will has two acts: will as nature (*thelēsis*, in John of Damascus's term) and will as reason (*boulēsis*). These are acts, not extra faculties. *In words:* still one human will, and it can move in two directions at once without splitting.
3. **Christ had free choice** (a.4). Choice is the act of will as reason, and he had will as reason. The objections were that choice needs doubt and that a will fixed on the good cannot choose. Aquinas's replies: doubt is incidental to choice, found only "in an ignorant nature" (ad 1–2), and Christ's will is "determined to good" but "not ... to this or that good," so he chooses as the blessed do, "with a free-will confirmed in good" (ad 3). *In words:* freedom is not the power to sin. It is the power to set yourself toward the good you understand, and sin is a failure of that power, not a use of it.
4. **By his sensuality and his will as nature, Christ could will what God did not will. By his will as reason, never** (a.5). God did not will the passion for its own sake but for salvation. Nature and sense shrink from death as such; reason chooses it as the means. *In words:* "remove this chalice" and "thy will be done" come from different acts of the same human will.
5. **So the wills are not contrary** (a.6). Contrariety needs opposition about the same thing, in the same will, and neither holds: the lower movements never rejected the reason why the passion was willed (salvation), and they never held back reason or the divine will. And it pleased Christ, by his divine will and his will as reason, to let his nature and sense move "according to the order of their nature." *In words:* the fear was permitted, not suppressed — and the permitting was itself willed.
6. **The affections were real** (q.15 aa.4, 6–7). Christ felt true pain, true sorrow and true fear of the evil to come. He did not feel the fear that comes from uncertainty. Borrowing Jerome's term, Aquinas calls these movements [propassions](../reference.md#propassion): they begin in the sensitive appetite and do not overturn reason. *In words:* "propassion" makes it no less real; it says only that it did not rule him.
7. **The whole life has the form of obedience.** He "learned obedience by the things which he suffered" (Hebrews 5:8, DR), and he became obedient unto death (Philippians 2:8), the text Aquinas uses to show that Christ died out of obedience (q.47 a.2). Obedience makes the thing commanded necessary. It leaves the carrying-out free (a.2 ad 2).

**Mark the levels.** [Two natural wills and two natural operations](../reference.md#two-natural-wills-and-operations), not contrary, the human will subject to the divine, is **defined dogma** (Constantinople III). So is Christ's sinlessness ("sin only excepted," repeated in the same definition). That his sufferings and his soul's anguish were real follows from the defined complete humanity and from the Gospels' narrative; to make them feigned is Docetism. That his human obedience was free is presupposed by Trent, which teaches that he *merited* our justification by his passion (Session VI, chapter 7). The Thomist machinery — sensuality, [will as nature and will as reason](../reference.md#will-as-nature-and-will-as-reason), propassion, [freedom confirmed in good](../reference.md#freedom-confirmed-in-good) — is **theologically certain / common teaching** in its main lines, and it is school theology, not definition. Calling "will as nature" a dogma overstates the level. How Christ's freedom fits with a command to die and a will that cannot sin was a long post-Tridentine school dispute, and it is **freely disputed** ([the levels in christology](../reference.md#the-levels-in-christology); [`fundamental-theology` 4.4](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-04-the-ladder-of-doctrinal-authority.md)).

## The case: Gethsemane, layer by layer

| Movement | Toward the passion | Text | Sin? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sensuality | shrinks from pain | "my soul is sorrowful even unto death" (Mark 14:34) | no — a propassion |
| Will as nature | shrinks from death as evil in itself | "remove this chalice from me" | no — the will of an unfallen nature |
| Will as reason | chooses death for the sake of salvation | "not my will, but thine be done" | — the act that merits |
| Divine will | wills the passion for salvation | John 10:18: the command received from the Father | — |

The prayer is a single sentence (Luke 22:42, DR):

> Father, if thou wilt, remove this chalice from me: but yet not my will, but thine be done.

"My will" is the first two rows, and "thine" is the fourth. The man saying "but yet" is the third row, choosing. The one saying all of it is the Word. Maximus's own reading, in his Gethsemane treatise (*Opusculum* 6 in the usual numbering), makes the prayer the human will's consent, not its defeat. Benedict XVI's general audience on Maximus (25 June 2008) puts the same point in modern terms: Adam took saying no to be the peak of freedom, and Gethsemane shows that the peak is saying yes.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 — clean: "he began to fear and to be heavy" (Mark 14:33).** *Who* fears? The Word, in his human nature. *What* is feared? The coming death, a future evil. Fear from uncertainty? No; he knows what is coming (q.15 a.7). A passion that overturns reason? No, a propassion: "began to" marks where it starts, in the sensitive appetite, and it goes no further. Verdict: real, his, sinless. Drop any one and the text breaks — unreal makes it theatre, not the Word's makes a second subject, sinful contradicts the definition.

**Example 2 — where it strains: freedom under a command.** Christ says he received a commandment from the Father to lay down his life (John 10:18). His will cannot sin (common teaching; see [3.4](03-04-the-grace-of-the-head.md)). Then he *could not* refuse, and a death he could not refuse looks neither free nor meritorious. Trent says it was meritorious.

Aquinas's answer runs through premise 3. What freedom needs is not the ability to sin but self-determination: the act comes from the agent's own judgment of the good, "confirmed in good" as the blessed are, and not from outside compulsion. That is a *source* view of freedom, not a *leeway* view, in the terms of [`metaphysics` 6.3](../../metaphysics/lessons/06-03-frankfurt-cases-and-alternate-possibilities.md). His obedience is necessary as to the thing commanded and free as to the carrying-out (q.47 a.2 ad 2). The strain is real: a defender of freedom of indifference, for whom freedom *means* being able to do otherwise at the moment of choice, will say this concedes the objection. Post-Tridentine theologians argued at length over how the command was framed (strict precept or counsel? death itself, or only its circumstances?) to leave room for alternatives. That is freely disputed; the conclusion every side had to reach — he was free, and he merited — is not.

## Watch out

- **You might read "not my will but thine" as the Son yielding to the Father at the level of the Godhead.** Father and Son have one divine will, because will follows nature ([2.5](02-05-monothelitism-and-constantinople-iii.md)). A divine will submitting to another is **Arianism**. "My will" is the human will (q.18 a.1, following Ambrose).
- **You might think the fear was staged "for our instruction" and not felt.** That is **Docetism** about the soul. Aquinas reads even Hilary's harsh lines on this, which seem to deny the pain, as denying only its *necessity* and not its reality (q.15 a.5 ad 1, a.7 ad 2). Whatever Hilary meant, the reading that makes it theatre is excluded.
- **You might picture two agents negotiating in the garden.** Two wills are not two willers. A human "someone" who agrees with the Word is **Nestorianism**. The Source's "proper will of God the Word" is the guard against it.
- **You might say only his flesh shrank and the choosing was divine.** Then the choice and the merit are not human, and human willing was never healed: **monothelitism**, in Apollinaris's shape.
- **Overstating the level.** The Thomist layering and *propassio* are school theology. Only the two wills, their non-contrariety and sinlessness are defined.

## One-liner

> In the garden, the Word's human nature said no to death and his human reason said yes to the Father: two wills, one willer, no contradiction, and his freedom reached its height in the yes.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢)** *(Exegetical)* Mark each sentence **true**, **false**, or **true only with a distinction**, and give the rule or article that decides it (one line each):
(i) "At Gethsemane the Son's divine will gave way to the Father's divine will."
(ii) "In Christ there was something willed by a human will that God did not will."
(iii) "Christ could not have sinned, so he did not choose."
(iv) "The human will of Christ is the will of God the Word."
(v) "Christ's fear in the garden was a passion that overcame his reason, but without sin."

**P2 (🟡)** *(Exegetical)* Read ST III q.18 a.6, reply to objection 3 (English Dominican Province). The objection had argued from Luke 22:43, "being in an agony," that a struggle means contrary wills:

> The agony in Christ was not in the rational soul, in as far as it implies a struggle in the will arising from a diversity of motives, as when anyone, on his reason considering one, wishes one thing, and on its considering another, wishes the contrary. For this springs from the weakness of the reason, which is unable to judge which is the best simply. Now this did not occur in Christ, since by His reason He judged it best that the Divine will regarding the salvation of the human race should be fulfilled by His passion. Nevertheless, there was an agony in Christ as regards the sensitive part, inasmuch as it implied a dread of coming trial.

(a) Name the kind of struggle the reply denies to Christ, and link it to Maximus's *gnomic* will ([natural will and gnomic will](../reference.md#natural-will-and-gnomic-will)). (b) Say where the reply puts the agony, and why that keeps the agony real rather than feigned. **Two sentences per part.**

**P3 (🔴)** *(Exegetical)* An invented Holy Thursday homily contains this paragraph:

> "In the garden Jesus had to fight himself. His human side wanted to run, his divine side wanted the cross, and for one terrible hour the two were at war inside him. That is why he sweated blood. But of course he wasn't really afraid — he knew he would rise — and he only let the disciples see him troubled so they would know how to pray when they were afraid."

(a) Name the two separate errors in the paragraph and the heresy each lands in. (b) Cite the phrase of Constantinople III's definition that corrects the first error, and state its level. (c) In one sentence, say what true thing the preacher was reaching for. **120 words total.**

<details>
<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict:**
(i) **False.** The Father and the Son have one divine will, so nothing divine can give way to the Father. "My will" is the human will (q.18 a.1; Ambrose). Reading it the other way is Arian.
(ii) **True only with a distinction.** True of the will of sensuality and the will as nature, which shrank from the death God willed. False of the will as reason, which always willed what God willed (q.18 a.5).
(iii) **False.** His will is determined to good but not to this or that good, and doubt is incidental to choice, so he chooses with a free will confirmed in good (q.18 a.4 ad 1–3).
(iv) **True.** The definition says the natural will of his flesh "is called and is the proper will of God the Word." This is the *who*: the human will belongs to the one person. It is not the same as the divine will, which would be the *what* fused.
(v) **False.** Real fear, but a *propassion*: it began in the sensitive appetite and did not overturn reason (q.15 a.7 ad 1). A passion that overcame reason is exactly what Aquinas denies.

**Wrong turns:** marking (iv) false because "the will of God" sounds like the divine will. Under the communication of idioms ([3.2](03-02-the-communication-of-idioms.md)) the concrete term "God the Word" names the person, and the human will is his. Marking (ii) simply false on the strength of the definition's "not contrary." Different objects, different acts, no contrariety (a.6). Marking (v) true because the fear was sinless: sinless, yes, but the sentence's claim that it "overcame his reason" is the error.

**Model answer:** (i) F, one divine will. (ii) Distinction: yes by nature and sense, never by reason, a.5. (iii) F, freedom confirmed in good, a.4 ad 3. (iv) T, the definition's "proper will of God the Word." (v) F, propassion, q.15 a.7 ad 1.

**P2** *(Exegetical (a) · Exegetical (b))*

**Must hit, strict (a):** it denies the deliberative struggle of a reason torn between motives, which "springs from the weakness of the reason" that cannot judge what is best. This is the mode of willing Maximus calls *gnomic*: the hesitating, deliberating will of a creature that does not know what is best. It is denied to Christ because his reason judged what was best without that weakness, not because he lacked a human will.

**Must hit, strict (b):** the agony is placed in the *sensitive part*, as dread of the coming trial. That is a real movement of a real human appetite, so the agony is genuine. Keeping it out of the rational will is what stops it from becoming contrariety of wills, and Luke's "agony" stays literally true.

**Wrong turns:** concluding that the reply denies Christ any real struggle. It denies only one *kind*. Equating the gnomic will with the natural will, or with free choice: Aquinas keeps choice in Christ (a.4) while denying deliberation born of ignorance, which is what the gnomic will is. Saying the agony was in the divine nature.

**Model answer:** (a) The reply denies the struggle of a reason divided between motives because it cannot see what is best. That is the gnomic, deliberating will Maximus denies to Christ, whose reason judged at once that the passion should be fulfilled. (b) It places the agony in the sensitive appetite, as dread of what was coming. That is a true human passion, so the agony is real, but it never divided his rational will against the divine will.

**P3** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** *Error 1:* "his divine side wanted the cross … at war inside him" makes the wills **contrary**, a real conflict between the human and the divine. That is the error the definition rejects by name ("as the impious heretics assert"). Picturing two "sides" at war, each with its own wants, drifts toward two subjects, which is **Nestorian**. Credit either diagnosis if it names contrariety. *Error 2:* "wasn't really afraid … only let them see him troubled" makes the passions feigned. That is **Docetism** about Christ's soul (q.15 a.7: fear of future evil was truly in him).
**Must hit, strict (b):** "not contrary the one to the other … but his human will follows … not as resisting and reluctant, but rather as subject to his divine and omnipotent will." **Defined dogma**, Constantinople III.
**Must hit, strict (c):** there really is a pull in two directions: nature's recoil from death and reason's choice of the Father's will. The sweat of blood shows how costly the yes was.

**Wrong turns:** calling Error 1 monothelitism: the homily has two wills set at war, and monothelitism has one. Defending "he knew he would rise" as the reason fear was impossible: q.15 a.7 allows fear of a known future evil, and denies only the fear that comes from uncertainty. Marking (b) as common teaching, as though the Thomist layering were what is at stake; non-contrariety is in the definition itself.

**Model answer:** (a) The "war" makes the two wills contrary and gives each "side" its own wants, which heads toward two subjects (Nestorian). The staged fear is Docetism. (b) Constantinople III: the wills are "not contrary," the human will "subject to his divine and omnipotent will" — defined dogma. (c) He really did feel nature's recoil from death and chose the Father's will through it.

## Flashback

**From Lesson [3.3](03-03-kenosis.md) (Kenosis):** *(Exegetical.)* An **invented** youth-retreat talk, not the words of any real person:

> "At Bethlehem the Son didn't stop being God. He kept every divine power; he just chose not to use them. Think of a chess grandmaster playing a child with one hand tied behind his back: his divine knowledge was switched off for thirty years, so that he could really learn carpentry from Joseph. On Mount Tabor he let the switch flicker on for a moment."

(a) The speaker rejects outright divestment. Put the one question that decides whether "chose not to use them" is orthodox, and give both answers with their verdicts. (b) Which image in the talk is right, and which distinction does it illustrate? (c) Rewrite the "switched off" sentence so that it is true. **120 words total.**

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):** the question is ***restrained in which nature?*** If the divine nature holds back its own knowing, the divine nature is doing less than before. That is a change in God, **kenoticism** of the Gore and Weston "use" kind on its bad reading, and it breaks Chalcedon's "immutably." It also says a human limitation of the divine nature in the abstract (3.2). If it means that the *human* mind receives only what a human mind can, it is orthodox, and it says what the classical account already says. "Switched off for thirty years" reads the first way.

**Must hit, strict (b):** Tabor. The glory was **hidden, not laid aside**: it was always there and was manifested in the flesh at the Transfiguration. Credit "didn't stop being God" as true, though it is not an image.

**Must hit, strict (c):** e.g. "As God he never ceased to know all things; as man he really learned carpentry from Joseph." The learning goes to the one person by reason of the humanity.

**Wrong turns:** calling the talk orthodox because it denies divestment. The grandmaster image still puts the limitation in the divine act. Calling it **Docetism**, when the error is on the divine side. "Fixing" it by denying that he really learned, which empties the humanity. Settling here what his human mind knew: that is [4.2](04-02-christs-human-knowledge-the-classical-account.md)'s question.

**Model answer:** (a) Ask: restrained in which nature? If his divine knowing was switched off, the Godhead changed, which is kenoticism against Chalcedon's "immutably." If only his human mind was limited, as every human mind is, the claim is orthodox. (b) Tabor is right: the glory was hidden and then shown, never laid aside. (c) "As God he knew all things throughout; as man he truly learned his trade from Joseph."
