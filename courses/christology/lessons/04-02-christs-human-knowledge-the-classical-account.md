# Christology · Lesson 4.2: Christ's human knowledge: the classical account

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 4: The human mind of Christ · Builds on: [2.1 Apollinaris](02-01-apollinaris-a-complete-humanity.md), [3.4 The grace of the Head](03-04-the-grace-of-the-head.md), [4.1 Christ's human will in act](04-01-christs-human-will-in-act.md) · Unlocks: [4.3 The beatific vision in Christ](04-03-the-beatific-vision-in-christ.md)

## Why this matters

The Gospels give two sets of facts about Jesus's mind, and they pull against each other. He asks questions ("Where have you laid him?", John 11:34), grows (Luke 2:52), and says the Son does not know the day (Mark 13:32). He also reads hearts (John 2:25) and sees Nathanael under the fig tree before they meet (John 1:48). Arius explained the first set by a lesser Word ([1.4](01-04-arius-and-the-divinity-of-the-son.md)). The Agnoetae, a sixth-century party, explained it by a real ignorance in Christ. The classical account explains both sets with one machine; [4.3](04-03-the-beatific-vision-in-christ.md) puts it under pressure.

## The idea

Start from the course's grammar. Knowing, like willing ([2.5](02-05-monothelitism-and-constantinople-iii.md)), belongs to a person only by reason of a nature (ST III q.9 a.1 ad 3). So *who* knows in Christ is one subject, the Word. *What* he knows by has to be counted twice. There is divine knowledge, which is God's own act and his essence. And there is created knowledge, the act of the rational soul the Word assumed ([2.1](02-01-apollinaris-a-complete-humanity.md)).

The first cannot do the second's job. God's understanding is his substance, so it cannot be the act of Christ's human soul. Without a knowledge of its own, that soul would have known nothing and been assumed in vain (q.9 a.1 ad 1).

Aquinas then gives the soul [three created knowledges](../reference.md#the-three-knowledges-of-christ), one for each way a human mind can be filled. Think of three windows in one house:

- **Upward, onto God:** the vision of God's essence, the knowledge of the blessed. A *comprehensor* is one who has reached the goal and sees God.
- **Downward, from God:** knowledge the Word imprints directly on the mind, the way Aquinas thinks angels were given their knowledge.
- **Outward, onto the world:** knowledge the mind draws for itself from the senses, as every human being's does. A *wayfarer* (*viator*) is one still on the road.

Aquinas holds that the earthly Christ was both at once, a comprehensor in his soul and a wayfarer in his passible body (q.11 a.2). His image for how the three coexist is two kinds of light. A candle fades next to the sun, because both give light. Air lit by the sun is not dimmed but brightened, because one gives light and the other receives it (q.9 a.1 ad 2). The higher knowledges light the lower; they do not replace it.

## Source

*Summa Theologiae* III q.9 a.4, "Whether Christ had any acquired knowledge?", the *respondeo* abridged (English Dominican Province):

> As is plain from Article 1, nothing that God planted in our nature was wanting to the human nature assumed by the Word of God. Now it is manifest that God planted in human nature not only a passive, but an active intellect. ... Now the proper operation of the active intellect is to make intelligible species in act, by abstracting them from phantasms ... which means that there was acquired knowledge in Him, which some call empiric. And hence, although I wrote differently (Sent. iii, D, xiv, 3; D, xviii, 3), it must be said that in Christ there was acquired knowledge, which is properly knowledge in a human fashion, both as regards the subject receiving and as regards the active cause.

Three things decide the reading. **"Nothing ... was wanting"** is the soteriological rule of [2.1](02-01-apollinaris-a-complete-humanity.md), applied to one power of the soul: a complete nature includes the working of its **active intellect** ("agent intellect" in [`thomistic-synthesis` 4.1](../../thomistic-synthesis/lessons/04-01-how-a-human-intellect-knows.md)). **"In a human fashion"** marks this as the one knowledge that is human in how it is received *and* in what causes it. The vision and the infused knowledge are human only in the mind that receives them. And **"although I wrote differently"** is Aquinas correcting himself. In his *Sentences* commentary, the earlier view (held, the translators' note to q.12 a.2 says, by Albert, Alexander of Hales and Bonaventure) gave Christ no separate habit of acquired knowledge. On that view nothing in him grew "in essence". He only matched infused ideas to new sense images. The *Summa* gives him a real habit that grows (q.12 a.2).

## The argument

1. **Two knowledges, because two natures** (q.9 a.1). *In words:* the Word knows as God and knows as man. Denying the second is Apollinaris again, applied to the intellect.
2. **The vision of God** (q.9 a.2; q.10). Humans reach the vision *through* Christ's humanity (Hebrews 2:10), and a cause must have more power than its effect. So his soul had the vision first and most fully. This is the [capital grace](../reference.md#capital-grace) argument of [3.4](03-04-the-grace-of-the-head.md) applied to knowledge. The soul sees God's essence but does not *comprehend* it, because a finite mind cannot exhaust the infinite (q.10 a.1). In the Word it knows everything that ever was, is or will be, but not everything God *could* do (q.10 a.2). *In words:* the head must already have what it passes on.
3. **Infused knowledge** (q.9 a.3; q.11). The Word imprints on the mind ideas of everything it can receive. That includes all the human sciences and all that prophecy reveals (q.11 a.1), and also individual things, past, present and future (q.12 a.1 ad 3). It is held as a habit and used at will (q.11 a.5). *In words:* everything a human mind *could* be taught, it was given at once.
4. **Acquired knowledge** (q.9 a.4; q.12). The Source's argument. It came by discovery rather than by being taught. Aquinas goes further and says Christ learned nothing from any man, since the teacher of all is not taught (q.9 a.4 ad 1; q.12 a.3). *In words:* the one knowledge that works the way ours does.
5. **[Two kinds of advance](../reference.md#two-kinds-of-advance)** (q.12 a.2). Knowledge advances "in essence" when the habit itself grows. It advances "in effect" when one shows more of what one already has. In effect, all Christ's knowledge advanced as he grew, since he did and showed greater things. In essence, only the acquired knowledge advanced, step by step, because the agent intellect works successively. The other two were given whole from the start. *In words:* this is the classical reading of Luke 2:52. He grew really, but in one register only.
6. **[The economic reading of Mark 13:32](../reference.md#the-economic-reading-of-mark-1332).** Augustine (*De Trinitate* I.12, Haddan's translation) says the Son "is ignorant of this, as making others ignorant". He did not know it in the sense of making it known at that time. Aquinas adopts it: the Son is said not to know the day because he does not make it known (q.10 a.2 ad 1, citing Acts 1:7). Gregory the Great sharpens it in a letter to Eulogius of Alexandria against the Agnoetae (*Letters* X.39, NPNF 2-13). Christ knew the day *in* his humanity but not *from* it: "What then He knew in it He knew not from it." *In words:* the knowledge sat in his human mind, but its source was his union with the Word. It was not something his human powers worked out, and it was not his to disclose.

**Mark the levels.** That Christ has a true human intellect is **defined dogma** (the "reasonable soul" of Chalcedon; two natural operations, Constantinople III). That his soul had the vision during his earthly life is **common teaching**, asserted in **authentic ordinary magisterium**: Pius XII, *Mystici Corporis* (1943) §75, says in paraphrase that from his conception he enjoyed the beatific vision and in it held all the members of his Body present. It is **not defined**. The [1918 Holy Office response](../reference.md#the-1918-holy-office-response) is a **safety judgment**: asked whether three propositions could safely be taught, it answered no. One of them was that it is not established that Christ's soul had the knowledge of the blessed. A safety judgment governs what may be taught; it does not define the opposite ([`fundamental-theology` 4.5](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-05-reading-a-documents-weight.md), the *responsum* genre). The infused knowledge, the three-part scheme, and "learned nothing from men" are **school theology**, and I found no magisterial act proposing them. The *Catechism* (§§472–474) affirms real growth, real inquiry and the economic reading of Mark 13:32. It does not name the vision in those paragraphs, and it carries the weight of its sources. [The levels](../reference.md#the-levels-in-christology) matter here because [4.3](04-03-the-beatific-vision-in-christ.md) is a real dispute.

## The three knowledges

| | Vision (q.10) | Infused (q.11) | Acquired (q.12) |
|---|---|---|---|
| What it perfects | the soul's capacity for its last end | the passive intellect's whole potency | the agent intellect's own operation |
| Source | God's essence united to the mind | ideas imprinted by the Word | abstraction from sense images |
| Reaches | God (seen, not comprehended); all actual things in the Word | the sciences, prophecy, singulars | what the agent intellect can reach from experience |
| Grows in essence? | no | no | **yes** |

## Worked examples

**Example 1, clean: "he knew what was in man" (John 2:25).** Which window? The acquired knowledge is ruled out, since reading a stranger's thoughts is not abstraction from what the senses give. Aquinas assigns it to the vision: the soul knows in the Word the thoughts of all those it will judge, so John 2:25 can be read of his human knowledge and not only his divine knowledge (q.10 a.2). The infused knowledge of singulars would also reach it. Who knew? The Word. By what? A created knowledge in his soul, not the divine nature doing the human mind's work.

**Example 2, where it strains: "Where have you laid him?" (John 11:34).** If Christ saw every actual thing in the Word, the question cannot be for his own information. Gregory's letter compares it to God's questions to Adam and Cain (Genesis 3:9, 4:9), which no one takes as ignorance, and passes on Severian of Gabala's reading of it as a rebuke. Aquinas does the same for the boy in the Temple: following Origen, he asked in order to teach, not to learn (q.12 a.3 ad 1). But the *Catechism* §472 cites this very verse for the claim that he had to ask, as any human must, about what only experience teaches. The two readings are not the same. The account can keep both only by locating the inquiry in the acquired knowledge, which really did not have the fact, while the higher windows did. Whether a mind can hold a fact in one register and truly ask for it in another is the pressure [4.3](04-03-the-beatific-vision-in-christ.md) presses on. The same letter shows the pressure is old. Gregory reports the deacon Anatolius's objection: Wisdom took on our ignorance to free us from it, as the Immortal took on death. Gregory had not yet answered it when he wrote, being ill.

## Watch out

- **You might think his ignorance of the day belonged to the Word as Word.** That is the **Arian** reading, and Aquinas names Arius and Eunomius at q.10 a.2 ad 1: it makes the Son less than the Father in knowledge. The Word made all times (John 1:3) and is ignorant of nothing he made.
- **You might think the fix is two knowers: "the man Jesus didn't know, the Word did."** That divides the *who*. Gregory's verdict is exact: whoever is not a Nestorian cannot be an Agnoete. Ignorance can be pinned on a human knower apart from Wisdom only if that knower is someone else. The one who grew is the Word, growing *as man*. This is **Nestorianism**.
- **You might think "he knew everything because he was God" settles it.** The divine knowledge cannot be his soul's act (q.9 a.1 ad 1). Using it to fill the human mind leaves a human intellect that does nothing, which is **Apollinarianism** for the intellect.

## One-liner

> One knower, two natures, three created windows: he saw God, was given the rest, and learned the world the way we do, and only the last one grew.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢)** *(Exegetical)* Run the three knowledges on four cases. For each, say which knowledge or knowledges reach it on Aquinas's account, cite the article, and flag any case where the text of qq.9–12 does not settle the answer. **Two sentences per case.**
(i) He knew how to build a door frame ("Is not this the carpenter?", Mark 6:3).
(ii) He spoke Aramaic.
(iii) He knew the names of the Twelve before he met them.
(iv) He knew the year Jerusalem would fall (Luke 19:43–44).

**P2 (🟡)** *(Exegetical)* Assign a level to each claim and **name the act that fixes it**: (i) Christ has a true human intellect; (ii) Christ's soul enjoyed the vision of God from the moment of his conception; (iii) it may not be taught in Catholic schools that Christ's earthly soul lacked the knowledge of the blessed. Then answer, in **two sentences**, a student who says: "Since 1918 the vision in Christ is dogma, because the Church forbade the contrary."

**P3 (🔴)** *(Exegetical)* An **invented** handout from a parish youth retreat, not the words of any real person:

> "Jesus had to find out who he was, just like you. Growing up in Nazareth he pieced it together from the Scriptures and from Mary's stories, and at his baptism he finally discovered he was the Son of God. That's what it means that he was truly human."

(a) Name the *who/what* error in "he finally discovered he was the Son of God", and the heresy it lands in if taken at its word. (b) Say which true thing the author is reaching for, stated as the classical account would state it. (c) Say which part of your correction is defined dogma and which is only common teaching. **120 words total.**

<details>
<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict:** **(i)** Two knowledges. Infused, because it covers everything in the human sciences (q.11 a.1). Acquired, because his hands and senses really learned the craft, and that habit grew (q.12 a.2). The flag: on q.9 a.4 ad 1 and q.12 a.3 he acquired it by discovery, not by Joseph's teaching. That is the text's claim, and it strains against a boy apprenticed in a workshop. **(ii)** Aquinas never addresses this. A language is conventional, so no amount of abstraction from nature yields Aramaic; it has to be heard. Hearing words is sense experience, so acquired knowledge can take it in. But q.12 a.3 ad 2 distinguishes learning from words from learning from things, and says he learned from things. So the text does not settle whether picking up a mother tongue counts as "being taught". Infused knowledge could supply it in any case. Credit any answer that flags the gap rather than forcing a verdict. **(iii)** Singulars: infused knowledge (q.12 a.1 ad 3) and the vision, which sees all actual things in the Word (q.10 a.2). Not acquired, since he had not yet met them. John 1:48 is the model. **(iv)** A future singular: the vision (q.10 a.2) and infused prophetic knowledge (q.11 a.1; q.12 a.1 ad 3). What he disclosed was the event, not the date. This is the same line the economic reading draws between knowing and being sent to reveal.

**Wrong turns:** assigning (iii) or (iv) to the divine knowledge "because he is God". That misses the question, which is about his *human* mind (q.9 a.1 ad 1). Saying acquired knowledge could reach (iv) by inference from the politics. q.12 a.1 ad 3 denies that it reaches future singulars.

**P2** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict:** (i) **Defined dogma**, fixed by Chalcedon's "reasonable soul" (and Constantinople III's two natural operations). (ii) **Common teaching, asserted in authentic ordinary magisterium** (*Mystici Corporis*, 1943, §75), **not defined**. It is a claim taught in an encyclical, owed religious submission. (iii) This is the **1918 Holy Office response** itself: a disciplinary safety judgment from a dicastery, which restricts what may be taught. **Reply:** forbidding the teaching of a denial is not defining the affirmation. No council or pope has proposed the vision as divinely revealed, so it is not dogma, and 1918 fixes what is *safe*, not what is *de fide*.

**Wrong turns:** calling (ii) "freely disputed". The act exists and must be named, even though 4.3 lays out real dissent. Grading (iii) by the vision's own weight instead of by the act. Giving (i) rung 1 but citing *Mystici Corporis* as the act.

**P3** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict:** (a) The subject of "he" is the Word. The sentence has the Son acquiring the fact of his sonship as news about himself, arriving at the baptism. Taken at its word, either he *became* Son at the baptism (**adoptionism**), or the one who found out is a human someone other than the Son (**Nestorianism**, two subjects). (b) The true thing: his **acquired** knowledge really grew. He came to speak about his identity and mission in the words of Israel's Scriptures, learned in a human way (q.12 a.2). (c) **Dogma:** one subject, the Word, the same before and after the Jordan. **Common teaching, not defined:** that his soul knew his identity from conception by the vision (*Mystici Corporis* §75). Whether that knowledge was a vision or something unthematic is the dispute of [4.3](04-03-the-beatific-vision-in-christ.md).

**Wrong turns:** calling the handout heretical *because* it denies the vision. That overstates a rung-3 teaching. Answering (b) with "nothing true here". Real growth in acquired knowledge is Aquinas's own mature view.

**Model answer:** (a) "He" is the Word, so the sentence has the Son discover his sonship from outside at the Jordan. Read literally, that is adoptionism, or a second human subject who finds out (Nestorianism). (b) What is true is that his acquired knowledge really grew: he learned, humanly, to say who he was in Israel's words. (c) That the one who grew is the Word is dogma. That he knew himself by the vision from conception is common teaching (*Mystici Corporis*), not defined, and disputed in 4.3.

</details>

## Flashback

**From Lesson [3.4](03-04-the-grace-of-the-head.md) (The grace of the Head):** *(Exegetical.)* *Summa Theologiae* III q.7 a.13 asks whether Christ's habitual grace followed the union. Objection 1 argues that the two graces are one, quoting Augustine: "Every man becomes a Christian from the beginning of his belief, by the same grace whereby this Man from His beginning became Christ." Objection 3 argues that the common comes before the proper, and habitual grace is common to Christ and us while the grace of union is proper to him, so habitual grace must come first. Two replies (English Dominican Province):

> **Reply to Objection 1.** Augustine here means by grace the gratuitous will of God, bestowing benefits gratis; and hence every man is said to be made a Christian by the same grace whereby a Man became Christ, since both take place by the gratuitous will of God without merits.
>
> **Reply to Objection 3.** The common precedes the proper, when both are of the same genus; but when they are of divers genera, there is nothing to prevent the proper being prior to the common. Now the grace of union is not in the same genus as habitual grace; but is above all genera even as the Divine Person Himself.

(a) In Reply 1, what does "the same grace" name, and why does granting Augustine's sentence not make the grace of union and habitual grace one gift? (b) What does "above all genera" deny about the grace of union, and which Christological error follows if you put the two graces in one genus? **Two sentences per part.**

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):** "the same grace" names God's **gratuitous will**, which gives without merit. The sameness lies in the *cause* and its freeness, not in the *gift*. One free will produces two different effects: the union, which is personal being, and habitual grace, which is a quality perfecting the soul for acting. This is the point of q.2 a.10: the union is called grace only because no merit preceded it.
**Must hit, strict (b):** it denies that the union is a kind or degree of habitual grace, a created quality that could be ranked with the saints' graces. It sits outside the scale of habits "even as the Divine Person Himself", because it is the Word's own being given to the nature. Put the two in one genus and the union becomes a matter of degree: Jesus is the most graced of the saints, a man indwelt more fully than the prophets. That is **Nestorianism**, by way of Theodore.
**Wrong turns:** reading Reply 1 as a concession that the union is a habit. Reading "above all genera" as "an infinitely large habitual grace". The union is infinite because the person is, but it is not a habit at all (q.2 a.10). Concluding from Reply 1 that a Christian and Christ have equal grace, when the reply claims only that both are unmerited.
**Model answer:** (a) "Grace" in Augustine's sentence means God's free will to give without merit, so the Christian and the Christ are alike only in being unmerited. The gifts themselves differ: one is personal being, the other a quality of the soul for acting. (b) "Above all genera" denies that the union is a created quality on the same scale as habitual grace, since it is the divine person's own being. Put them in one genus and the union becomes a greater degree of sanctity in a man, which is Theodore's Nestorian Christ.

</details>
