# Christology · Lesson 5.4: Penal substitution and the Catholic assessment

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 5: The work of Christ · Builds on: [5.1 The Paschal Mystery and the threefold office](05-01-the-paschal-mystery-and-the-threefold-office.md), [5.3 Satisfaction: Anselm and Aquinas](05-03-satisfaction-anselm-and-aquinas.md) · Unlocks: [6.1 He descended into hell](06-01-he-descended-into-hell.md)

## Why this matters

On the cross, Catholics and Reformed Protestants usually argue with caricatures. One side hears "an angry Father beating an innocent Son". The other hears "a vague example of love that saves no one from anything". Both confessions reject both pictures. This lesson has one rule, and it governs the problems too: **the Reformed doctrine is stated first, from its own confessions, at a strength a Reformed theologian would sign**. Only then comes the assessment. What is left once both caricatures are gone is one narrow, real disagreement.

## The idea

The two traditions share nearly all their vocabulary. Both say *substitution*, *satisfaction*, *penalty*, *propitiation*. So the assessment cannot be run on words. It runs on two distinctions.

**Penalty and guilt.** Guilt (*culpa*) is the personal fault of having done the wrong. Penalty (*poena*) is what the wrong deserves. A friend can pay my fine. Nobody can make it true that *he* ran the red light. Catholic theology holds that Christ truly bore the [penalty](../reference.md#penalty-and-guilt) of sin and never its guilt.

**Willing the Son's suffering and punishing the Son.** The Father truly "delivered him up" (Romans 8:32). The question is *how*: as a judge passing sentence on a condemned man, or as the source of the love with which the Son offered himself?

Keep both distinctions in view and you can sort every sentence in this debate.

## Source

The Heidelberg Catechism (1563), Question 37, in Schaff's English (*Creeds of Christendom*, vol. 3):

> *What dost thou understand by the word Suffered?* That all the time he lived on earth, but especially at the end of his life, he bore, in body and soul, the wrath of God against the sin of the whole human race, in order that by his passion, as the only atoning sacrifice, he might redeem our body and soul from everlasting damnation, and obtain for us the grace of God, righteousness, and eternal life.

Read it slowly. **Bore** carries the answer: the wrath is something Christ carries, and the sentence says nothing of the Father inflicting it on him. **Against the sin** gives the wrath's object as sin, not the Son. **All the time he lived** makes the whole life penal, not just Good Friday. And **the only atoning sacrifice** puts the penal account inside sacrificial language, where Catholics also stand.

## The argument

**The Reformed doctrine, in five theses.**

1. **Sin binds the sinner to wrath and curse.** Every sin brings guilt on the sinner, "whereby he is bound over to the wrath of God and curse of the law" (Westminster Confession VI.6). *In words:* guilt here means liability to a just sentence.
2. **God's justice requires that the sentence be met.** By reason of God's justice and truth, satisfaction could be made in no other way than by the Son's death (Heidelberg Q.40). By his obedience and sacrifice Christ fully satisfied the Father's justice (Westminster VIII.5).
3. **Christ bore that wrath and curse in our place,** in body and above all in soul. Heidelberg Q.37 says so, and Q.39 makes the cross the curse that lay on me, laid on him. Westminster VIII.4 has him endure grievous torments immediately in his soul. The Shorter Catechism counts the wrath of God and the cursed death of the cross in his humiliation (Q.27).
4. **Imputation runs both ways.** Calvin reads 2 Corinthians 5:21, Galatians 3:13 and Isaiah 53:6 as one exchange. Our acquittal, he says, lies in this: the guilt that made us liable to punishment was transferred to the Son of God (*Institutes* II.16.5, Beveridge). Our sins pass to him by imputation (II.16.6). His obedience and satisfaction are in turn imputed to us (Westminster XI.1, 3; Heidelberg Q.60).
5. **Love comes first, and the Father was never angry with the Son.** Here the strongest Reformed reader will insist, and rightly. Calvin cites Augustine: God did not begin to love us once reconciled (II.16.4). Westminster has the Father give the Son for us, freely (XI.3). And Calvin refuses the caricature himself: "We do not, however, insinuate that God was ever hostile to him or angry with him. … But this we say, that he bore the weight of the divine anger, that, smitten and afflicted, he experienced all the signs of an angry and avenging God" (II.16.11). *In words:* wrath at sin, carried by the Son, and never aimed at the Son.

**The Catholic assessment.**

6. **Affirmed: substitution and satisfaction.** Trent teaches that Christ merited our justification and made satisfaction for us to God the Father (Session VI, ch. 7). The Catechism speaks of the substitution of the suffering Servant and of Christ's obedience in place of our disobedience (CCC 615). Aquinas is blunter than most Catholics expect: "one satisfies for another's sin by taking on himself the punishment due to the sin of the other" (ST III q.14 a.1). God would not remit sin without penalty (q.47 a.3 ad 1). Christ's sacrifice appeased God (q.49 a.4).
7. **Affirmed: "made sin" is no fiction.** Augustine reads 2 Corinthians 5:21 by way of the Old Testament sin-offering, which was itself called "sin": Christ was made a sacrifice for sin (*Enchiridion* 41). Aquinas follows him (q.15 a.1 ad 4), and Challoner's Douay note glosses the verse the same way. Christ really stands where sin stands. Only sin itself is excluded.
8. **Excluded: a transfer of personal guilt.** Guilt belongs to the one who did the act. The Catechism denies that Christ experienced reprobation as though he himself had sinned (CCC 603); he took on our condition by the love that always united him to the Father. What passes to him is the penalty, and his satisfaction counts for us because head and members are as one mystical person (q.48 a.2 ad 1).
9. **Excluded: the Father's punitive wrath directed at the Son.** Handing an innocent man over to torment against his will would be "wicked and cruel", and the Father did not do it; he inspired the Son with the will to suffer (q.47 a.3 ad 1). The cross saves because of the love and obedience offered, which please God more than all sin displeases him (q.48 a.2), and not because a quantity of pain was used up. Pain is the matter of the satisfaction. Charity is its principle (q.14 a.1 ad 1).

The crux is thesis 2 against thesis 9: **satisfaction *by* punishment, or satisfaction *instead of* punishment with penalty as its matter?** That is Anselm's "satisfaction or punishment" (*Cur Deus Homo* I.15; [5.3](05-03-satisfaction-anselm-and-aquinas.md)), and the two traditions answer it differently.

**Mark the levels.** Christ's sinlessness is **defined dogma**: Chalcedon calls him like us in all things apart from sin. So Christ cannot be *personally* guilty. No Reformed confession says he was. *That* Christ made satisfaction for us to the Father is taught by an ecumenical council in a doctrinal chapter (Trent VI, ch. 7). The chapter fixes the fact and leaves the mechanism open. Justification by the *sole* imputation of Christ's justice is anathematized (Trent VI, canon 11). That condemnation concerns justification, not the atonement. That Christ bore penalty but not guilt, and that the Father did not punish him in wrath, is **common teaching**, reported by the Catechism. **No act of the Church condemns penal substitution by name.** Whether its language can be given a Catholic sense is **freely disputed**. The [levels](../reference.md#the-levels-in-christology) matter in both directions.

## Map of positions

| Claim | Reformed confessions | Catholic teaching |
|---|---|---|
| Christ died in our place | Yes | Yes (Trent VI.7; CCC 615) |
| He bore the penalty due to our sin | Yes | Yes, as the matter of satisfaction (q.14 a.1) |
| Our guilt was imputed to him | Yes (*Institutes* II.16.5–6) | No: guilt is personal; he bore penalty as head (q.48 a.2 ad 1) |
| He bore the wrath of God | Yes (Heidelberg Q.37) | Only with a distinction: its effects against sin, never as its target (CCC 603) |
| The Father was angry with the Son | **No** (II.16.11) | **No** (q.47 a.3 ad 1) |
| What gives the death its value | Justice fully satisfied (Westminster XI.3) | Charity and the dignity of the person (q.48 a.2) |
| Was satisfaction strictly necessary? | Yes (Heidelberg Q.40) | Freely disputed: Anselm yes, Aquinas no (q.46 a.2 ad 3) |
| Christ's righteousness imputed to us | Yes, as the ground of justification | Not by *sole* imputation (Trent VI, can. 11): ceded |

## Worked examples

**Example 1: a clean case.** *"Christ bore the punishment our sins deserved."* Run the distinctions. Is guilt transferred? The sentence does not say so. Is the Father cast as a judge punishing the Son? Not necessarily. Aquinas says nearly these words (q.14 a.1). So a Catholic **affirms** it, and needs a distinction only if a speaker adds that the punishment was the Father's sentence on the Son. Striking the sentence as "Protestant" over-corrects, and that is the more common mistake.

**Example 2: where the gap narrows, and where it holds.** Compare thesis 1 with thesis 8. Westminster defines guilt as the thing "whereby he is bound over to the wrath of God": liability to a sentence. Scholastics call that *reatus poenae*, the debt of punishment. They distinguish it from *reatus culpae*, the fault itself. Aquinas grants that Christ's satisfaction abolishes the debt of punishment (q.49 a.3). So when Calvin says guilt was transferred, he may mean exactly what Catholics mean by *penalty borne*. That would make part of the quarrel verbal.

It is not all verbal, though. Calvin also speaks of a just vengeance the Son took upon himself (II.16.5). He has Christ experience all the signs of an angry and avenging God (II.16.11), and reads the descent into hell as hell's torment endured on the cross (Heidelberg Q.44, taken up in [6.1](06-01-he-descended-into-hell.md)). On that account the saving value lies in the penalty fully borne, a "full satisfaction" to justice (Westminster XI.3). On Aquinas's account it lies in the love that offers the penalty. The Catholic literature is divided on how much that residue weighs. Joseph Ratzinger's *Introduction to Christianity* (1968) attacks a popularized satisfaction picture of a God whose justice demands a victim (paraphrased). Other Catholic writers point to Aquinas's own penal vocabulary and the Catechism's "substitution" to argue that a Catholic sense is available (hedged: a tendency, not a named school). The 1999 *Joint Declaration on the Doctrine of Justification* concerns justification, not the atonement, and settles nothing here.

## Watch out

- **"The man Jesus was punished; the eternal Son was untouched."** This rescues the Father's goodness by splitting the one who suffered. That is **Nestorianism**. The subject who suffers is the Word, in his flesh ([communication of idioms](../reference.md#communication-of-idioms), [3.2](03-02-the-communication-of-idioms.md)).
- **"The Father's will was set against the Son's."** As God, the Son gives himself up by the same will and act by which the Father gives him up (q.47 a.3 ad 2). Two opposed divine wills means two Gods: **tritheism**.
- **"Our guilt became his, so he was a sinner."** This contradicts defined sinlessness. The Reformed confessions deny it too.
- **"The Church condemns penal substitution"** overstates. **"Satisfaction is just one theory"** understates: Trent teaches the fact.

## One-liner

> Christ bore our penalty, not our guilt, and the Father delivered him up by giving him love, not by punishing him in anger. The Reformed confessions deny the anger too; what is left in dispute is whether penalty or love pays.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢)** *(Exegetical)* Westminster Confession VIII.5 and XI.3 (Schaff):

> V. The Lord Jesus, by his perfect obedience and sacrifice of himself, which he through the eternal Spirit once offered up unto God, hath fully satisfied the justice of his Father, and purchased not only reconciliation, but an everlasting inheritance in the kingdom of heaven, for all those whom the Father hath given unto him.
>
> III. Christ, by his obedience and death, did fully discharge the debt of all those that are thus justified, and did make a proper, real, and full satisfaction to his Father's justice in their behalf. Yet inasmuch as he was given by the Father for them, and his obedience and satisfaction accepted in their stead, and both freely, not for any thing in them, their justification is only of free grace; that both the exact justice and rich grace of God might be glorified in the justification of sinners.

(a) What satisfies, what is satisfied, and for whom? (b) Which clause blocks the caricature of a Son winning over an unwilling Father? Neither section uses the word *wrath* or *punishment*: which words carry the penal sense instead? **Two sentences per part.**

**P2 (🟡)** *(Exegetical)* Mark each sentence **affirm**, **cannot affirm**, or **only with a distinction**, and give the deciding text in one line each.

1. Christ died in our place.
2. On the cross the Father regarded his Son as personally guilty.
3. God made Christ sin for us.
4. Satisfaction could have been made in no other way than by the death of the Son of God.
5. The man Jesus bore the punishment, but the eternal Son was not involved.
6. Christ's sacrifice appeased God.

**P3 (🔴)** *(Exegetical)* An **invented** Good Friday homily, not the words of any real person:

> "Today the Father turned his face away and poured out on Jesus the punishment we deserved. Our guilt became his guilt; that is why the Father could not look at him. The anger that was waiting for us was spent on the Son, and once it was spent, God could finally love us."

Name three distinct errors, each with the text that corrects it. Then say which of the three a faithful reader of Westminster and Heidelberg would *also* reject, citing the Reformed text. **150 words or fewer.**

<details>
<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical (a) · Exegetical (b))*

**Must hit, strict (a):** what satisfies is Christ's **obedience and sacrifice** (VIII.5), or his **obedience and death** (XI.3): the whole life together with the cross, not the death alone. What is satisfied is **the justice of his Father**. It is satisfied **for those the Father has given him**, "those that are thus justified": a definite number, the elect.

**Must hit, strict (b):** **"he was given by the Father for them"**, together with "freely" and "only of free grace". The initiative is the Father's, so the Son does not change the Father's mind. The penal sense rides on **"satisfied the justice"** and **"a proper, real, and full satisfaction to his Father's justice"** (justice as what a sentence answers to), on **"discharge the debt"**, and on the substitution words **"in their behalf"** and **"accepted in their stead"**. Credit for noting that the explicit wrath and curse sit elsewhere: Westminster VI.6 and the Shorter Catechism Q.27.

**Wrong turns:** reading "satisfied the justice of his Father" as "appeased the Father's anger at the Son", which imports the caricature the text blocks. Treating "for all those whom the Father hath given unto him" as the substitution clause; it states the scope, and whether the atonement is limited to the elect is a separate, intra-Protestant question. Beginning the Catholic assessment, which was not asked.

**Model answer:** (a) Christ's obedience and sacrifice, his whole obedience together with his death, satisfy the justice of the Father. They do so for those the Father has given him, the justified. (b) "He was given by the Father for them … freely" puts the Father's grace before and behind the whole work, so no one is won over. The penal sense is carried by justice "fully satisfied" and a "debt" discharged "in their stead", which is sentence language, not the language of mere gift.

**P2** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict:**

1. **Affirm.** Trent VI, ch. 7 ("for us"); CCC 615.
2. **Cannot affirm.** Guilt is personal, and Christ's sinlessness is defined. CCC 603 denies that he experienced reprobation as if he had sinned.
3. **Only with a distinction.** Scripture says it (2 Cor 5:21), in the sense of a sacrifice for sin, not sinfulness (*Enchiridion* 41; q.15 a.1 ad 4).
4. **Only with a distinction, of level.** Heidelberg Q.40 teaches it. A Catholic may hold it as Anselm did but not as doctrine; Aquinas denies it (q.46 a.2 ad 3). **Freely disputed.**
5. **Cannot affirm.** Nestorian: the Word suffers in his flesh (3.2).
6. **Affirm.** Aquinas says it (q.49 a.4). It is kept from implying a God who changes by the reply that he did not begin to love us anew (ad 2).

**Wrong turns:** marking 1 or 6 "cannot" as if they were Reformed property; that is the over-correction. Marking 4 "cannot" when it is disputed. Marking 3 "affirm" with no gloss.

**Model answer:** as above, one line each.

**P3** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict (three errors):**

- **Guilt transferred as personal fault** ("his guilt… could not look at him"). Christ bears penalty, not guilt (CCC 603; q.48 a.2 ad 1).
- **The Father's anger aimed at the Son.** The Father delivered him up by inspiring charity, not by punishing an innocent (q.47 a.3 ad 1).
- **God begins to love after the cross.** The death reconciles by removing the cause of enmity, sin; God did not begin to love us anew (q.49 a.4 ad 2).

**Must hit, strict (the Reformed column):** the Reformed reject **the second and third** at least. Calvin says God was never hostile to or angry with the Son (II.16.11) and cites Augustine that God did not begin to love us after reconciliation (II.16.4). Westminster XI.3 has the Son "given by the Father". They would accept "the punishment we deserved" and, in an imputed sense, "our guilt became his" (II.16.5).

**Wrong turns:** blaming the whole homily on Protestantism. Stripping out the penal element altogether, although Aquinas keeps it (q.14 a.1). Missing that "turned his face away" reads the cry of dereliction (Matthew 27:46) as the Father's rejection, when Aquinas reads it as the Father leaving him to his persecutors (q.47 a.3).

**Model answer:** The homily makes Christ's guilt personal, but he bore our penalty and never our fault (CCC 603). It aims the Father's anger at the Son, whereas the Father delivered him up by inspiring him with charity (q.47 a.3 ad 1). And it makes God's love the cross's result rather than its source (q.49 a.4 ad 2). The Reformed confessions reject the last two as firmly: Calvin denies that God was ever angry with the Son (II.16.11) and, with Augustine, that God began to love us only after reconciliation (II.16.4).

## Flashback

**From Lesson [5.2](05-02-ransom-christus-victor-and-sacrifice.md) (Ransom, Christus Victor, and sacrifice):** *(Exegetical.)* Assign each claim its level on the [ladder](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-04-the-ladder-of-doctrinal-authority.md), and **name the act or text that fixes it**, or say that none does. **(a)** Christ offered himself once, on the cross, to God the Father. **(b)** Sin gave the devil a just claim on the human race, and Christ's blood was the price paid to settle it. **(c)** By his death Christ destroyed the one who had the empire of death, the devil. **(d)** The Fathers taught the *Christus Victor* model, and the Latin model of an offering to God is a medieval departure from it. **Two sentences per claim.**

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict:** **(a) Taught by an ecumenical council:** Trent, Session XXII ch. 1, which sets it out as the premise of its decree on the Mass, and it is the plain claim of Hebrews ("once", 9:12, 26–28; "one sacrifice for sins", 10:12). It is not open to denial. **(b) A theological opinion** held by orthodox Fathers (Origen, Nyssa), taught by no magisterial act and **dropped** by the tradition (Nazianzen, *Oration* 45.22; Anselm, *Cur Deus Homo* I.7). It is neither doctrine nor a condemned heresy. **(c) Of faith as a fact**, asserted by Scripture itself (Hebrews 2:14–15; Colossians 2:15). The fact is fixed, but no act defines the mechanism, and *Christus Victor* as a model is not thereby made *the* model. **(d) No doctrinal level at all:** it is a historian's thesis (Aulén, 1930), and no act of the Church settles it. It is widely contested, because the Fathers piled up ransom, sacrifice and victory in the same sermon.

**Wrong turns:** calling (b) heretical, which overstates in one direction, or treating it as a live Catholic option on a par with the others, which overstates in the other. Demoting (c) to "one model's opinion" because the Christus Victor *model* is not defined: the defeat of the devil is a scriptural fact. Ranking (d) as "freely disputed doctrine" rather than a historical claim. Answering (a) with Hebrews alone and missing the conciliar act, Trent's chapter.

**Model answer:** (a) Taught by Trent, Session XXII ch. 1, as the premise of its teaching on the Mass, and asserted by Hebrews 9–10, so it is not open to denial. (b) A patristic opinion that no magisterial act ever taught and the tradition dropped. It was never condemned either, so to call Nyssa a heretic is as wrong as to call his view doctrine. (c) A fact of faith stated in Scripture (Hebrews 2:14–15), though no act defines how the victory works. (d) A historical thesis, not a doctrine; the Church has settled nothing about it, and Aulén's critics find the Fathers mixing all the images.

</details>
