# Christology · Lesson 6.2: Resurrection and ascension as doctrine

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 6: Descended, risen, ascended, confessed · Builds on: [6.1 He descended into hell](06-01-he-descended-into-hell.md), [3.2 The communication of idioms](03-02-the-communication-of-idioms.md), [2.1 Apollinaris: a complete humanity](02-01-apollinaris-a-complete-humanity.md) · Unlocks: [6.3 The churches that did not receive Chalcedon](06-03-the-churches-that-did-not-receive-chalcedon.md), [6.4 Christology today](06-04-christology-today.md)

## Why this matters

Every earlier lesson defended a complete humanity: a rational soul ([2.1](02-01-apollinaris-a-complete-humanity.md)), a human will ([2.5](02-05-monothelitism-and-constantinople-iii.md)), real fear in the garden ([4.1](04-01-christs-human-will-in-act.md)). Easter is where that humanity could quietly be dropped, its job done. The Creed refuses: *he rose, ascended, and sits* are statements about a human body, true now. The history is argued in [`apologetics-foundations`](../../apologetics-foundations/syllabus.md) and the narratives compared in [`new-testament`](../../new-testament/syllabus.md) 3.4. This lesson asks what the doctrine asserts, and so what any historical account must leave standing.

## The idea

The resurrection has three marks, and each one excludes a familiar misreading.

- **Real.** Something happened to Jesus, not only to the disciples. Their faith is the effect of Easter, not its content.
- **Bodily.** The body laid in the tomb is the body that rose. A "resurrection" that leaves the corpse where it lay is a different claim.
- **Transcendent.** He did not come back to the life he had. Lazarus came out still mortal and died again; Christ passed into a life beyond death, and no one saw that passage happen (Catechism 646–647).

Now the course's two questions. **Who** rose? The Word, the one subject. **What** rose? His human nature: soul reunited with body. The Godhead did not rise, because it never fell. By the dogma of the [hypostatic union](../reference.md#hypostatic-union), it stayed united to the soul and to the body even while death separated them ([6.1](06-01-he-descended-into-hell.md); Catechism 650). The ascension then carries the same humanity into the glory of God, permanently.

## Source

Aquinas, *Summa Theologiae* III q.54 a.1, "Whether Christ had a true body after His Resurrection?" (English Dominican Province):

> As Damascene says (De Fide Orth. iv): that is said to rise, which fell. But Christ's body fell by death; namely, inasmuch as the soul which was its formal perfection was separated from it. Hence, in order for it to be a true resurrection, it was necessary for the same body of Christ to be once more united with the same soul. And since the truth of the body's nature is from its form it follows that Christ's body after His Resurrection was a true body, and of the same nature as it was before. But had His been an imaginary body, then His Resurrection would not have been true, but apparent.

Three moves decide the reading:

- **"That is said to rise, which fell."** Resurrection is defined by its subject: the body died, so *that* body must rise. Anything else is a replacement.
- **"The same body ... the same soul."** Identity comes from the soul, the body's form ([`thomistic-synthesis` 5.3](../../thomistic-synthesis/lessons/05-03-the-human-soul-in-the-system.md)), so the risen body is numerically his.
- **"Not true, but apparent."** An imaginary risen body is Docetism moved from Bethlehem to Easter: a vision presented as an event.

## The argument

1. **The resurrection is the reunion of Christ's own soul and body** (q.54 a.1; Luke 24:39, DR: "See my hands and feet, that it is I myself; handle, and see: for a spirit hath not flesh and bones, as you see me to have"). Lateran IV (1215), in its opening profession, says he descended in the soul, rose in the flesh, and ascended in both (lesson's rendering). *In words:* the subject of Easter is a body, and it is the one that was buried.
2. **So the resurrection is not a name for the disciples' experience.** State the rival fairly. Rudolf Bultmann ("New Testament and Mythology", 1941) read Easter as the rise of faith in the preached cross, and his readers press real points: no gospel narrates the rising itself. Paul says Christ "was seen" (*ōphthē*, 1 Cor 15:5), and "flesh and blood cannot possess the kingdom of God" (15:50). The reply grants that no one saw the event (Catechism 647) and denies the conclusion. The appearances are the Lord acting on the disciples, and the Catechism says faith was "born" from that encounter, not the encounter from the faith (644). Paul's contrast is *sōma psychikon* against *sōma pneumatikon* (15:44), a "soul-animated body" against a "Spirit-governed body" (lesson's translation). Both are bodies, and "it is sown ... it shall rise" keeps one subject. *In words:* "spiritual" names what governs the body, not what the body is made of.
3. **The risen body is glorified, not resuscitated** (q.54, the article on glory). Aquinas's reasons: his resurrection is the exemplar and cause of ours ("sown in dishonour ... rise in glory," 1 Cor 15:43); he merited the glory by the passion; and his soul's glory, held back from his body for the passion's sake, was then released. *In words:* the same body, with a new mode of life: not limited by space and time, present "how and when he wills" (Catechism 645).
4. **The whole Trinity raises him, and he rises.** "Whom God hath raised up" (Acts 2:24). "I have power to lay it down: and I have power to take it up again" (John 10:18). "Son of God in power, according to the spirit of sanctification, by the resurrection" (Rom 1:4). The outward works of the three are undivided ([`trinity-and-god` 4.5](../../trinity-and-god/lessons/04-05-common-proper-appropriated.md)), and the Son's power is the Father's power. So "the Father raised him" and "he raised himself" name one act. The Catechism (648) appropriates the raising to the Father and the giving of life to the Spirit. *In words:* one divine act, spoken of in three ways.
5. **The ascension is the humanity's, the power is the Godhead's** (q.57 a.2). Ascending is local motion. That belongs to the human nature, not the divine, which has nowhere higher to go. So Christ ascended as man, not as God, though he ascended *by* the power of his Godhead. This is [reduplication](../reference.md#reduplication) applied to Acts 1:9.
6. **The session is not a location.** John of Damascus, *Exposition of the Orthodox Faith* IV.2 (NPNF 2.9):

   > We hold, moreover, that Christ sits in the body at the right hand of God the Father, but we do not hold that the right hand of the Father is actual place. For how could He that is uncircumscribed have a right hand limited by place?

   The right hand is "the glory and honour of the Godhead," and his flesh shares in it. *In words:* the [session at the right hand](../reference.md#the-session-at-the-right-hand) is a human body sharing divine glory and rule, not a seat beside a throne.
7. **The humanity is permanent, and it intercedes.** Christ "continueth for ever" and is "always living to make intercession for us" (Heb 7:24–25, DR). Aquinas keeps the scars on the risen body, following Bede: one reason is so that when he pleads with the Father he may show the death he died (q.54 a.4). The creed received at Constantinople I adds "whose kingdom shall have no end." Bright's note in Percival reads the clause against Marcellus of Ancyra, whom opponents accused of teaching that the Word would one day withdraw from the man Jesus. *In words:* the union is never undone; a human being is at the Father's right hand now.

**Mark the levels.** That Christ truly rose from the dead, ascended into heaven, and sits at the right hand of the Father is **defined dogma**: the Creeds of Nicaea–Constantinople and the Apostles, and Lateran IV's "rose in the flesh." That he rose in his *own* body belongs to that dogma's content as the Church has always proposed it (Catechism 645, 657). Careful: Lateran IV's "all shall rise with their own bodies" is about the *general* resurrection, not Christ's. The empty tomb's *evidential* force is not dogma, and the Catechism itself says the tomb is no direct proof (640). The tomb's being empty is a consequence of the dogma, not an extra article. That the union and his kingdom are never ended is **defined** in substance by the Creed's clause. That the resurrection is the work of all three persons applies a defined rule (the undivided works) and is **theologically certain**. The glorified body's classic properties are **common teaching** of the schools; how Christ's body passed through shut doors (Aquinas credits the Godhead, not the body: q.54 a.1 ad 1) and where "heaven" is are **freely disputed** ([the levels in christology](../reference.md#the-levels-in-christology)).

## What each clause excludes

| Creed clause | Asserts | Excludes |
|---|---|---|
| *rose again the third day* | a real event in Jesus, not in the disciples only | Easter as a name for the disciples' faith |
| *in the flesh* (Lateran IV) | the same body, reunited to the same soul | a new body, or a soul-only survival (Docetism at Easter) |
| glorified, *not* resuscitated | a new mode of bodily life, beyond death | Christ as a second Lazarus, returned to mortal life |
| *ascended* | the humanity's entry into divine glory, as man, by the power of the Godhead | the humanity dissolved into the divine (Eutychian absorption) |
| *sitteth at the right hand* | a human body sharing God's glory and rule | the right hand as a place; the Word laying down his flesh |
| *whose kingdom shall have no end* | the union is permanent | a Word who withdraws from Jesus (the view charged to Marcellus) |

## Worked examples

**Example 1 — clean: "Put in thy finger hither, and see my hands" (John 20:27).** Run the three marks. *Real:* Jesus invites Thomas, so the initiative is his, not the disciple's. *Bodily:* hands and side, with the wounds of the cross in them, so it is the same body (q.54 a.1). *Transcendent:* the same chapter has him come in "the doors being shut" (20:19, 26), so it is not a resuscitated body either. Then *who* and *what*. Thomas answers, "My Lord, and my God" (20:28), addressing the one subject through the humanity he touches. The concrete term names the person, and the wounds belong to that person ([communication of idioms](../reference.md#communication-of-idioms)). Verdict: a [glorified body](../reference.md#the-glorified-body) keeps its identity and its history. The scars are no defect; in Bede's image, cited by Aquinas, they are a trophy of the victory.

**Example 2 — where it strains: where is Christ's body now?** The dogma says he ascended in body and sits in the body. Aquinas also held that his body is now *above all the corporeal heavens* (q.57 a.4), a placement built on Aristotle's cosmology of nested spheres. That cosmology is gone. Keeping the place literally, somewhere in the universe, finds no serious defender. Making the ascension pure metaphor, the body now nowhere, drops "ascended in both" (Watch out, bullet 3). The tradition supplies the middle path. Damascene already denied that the right hand is a place. The Catechism calls the cloud and heaven *symbols* of the humanity's entry into divine glory (659). So what is dogma: a real body, still his, now in the mode of glory. What is not dogma: the geography. The strain is real: a body that is somewhere but at no location in our space is a hard concept, and the lesson marks it hard rather than solving it.

## Watch out

- **You might think "spiritual body" means a body made of spirit.** Then the risen Christ is a ghost, the very thing Luke 24:39 denies. That is **Docetism**, moved from the manger to the tomb. *Pneumatikon* names what governs the body, the Spirit, not a non-material stuff.
- **You might think the resurrection happened "in the disciples."** That relocates the event from its subject to its witnesses and denies the defined article. Faith responds to Easter. It is not what Easter was.
- **You might think the ascended Christ is omnipresent "as spirit," having left his body behind.** Omnipresence belongs to the divine nature. Giving it to the humanity as humanity trades predicates *between natures* ([3.2](03-02-the-communication-of-idioms.md)), and letting the humanity dissolve into the Godhead is **Eutychian** absorption. Christ is present everywhere as God. As man he is present in the ways he wills.
- **Overstating:** the classic properties of glorified bodies and the place of heaven are school theology, not defined.

## One-liner

> The Word rose in the body he was buried in, glorified and not resuscitated, and took that body into the glory of God for good: a human being sits at the Father's right hand, and his scars plead there for us.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢)** *(Exegetical)* Mark each sentence **true**, **false**, or **true only with a distinction**, and name the rule, text or article that decides it (one line each):
(i) "The Father raised Jesus from the dead."
(ii) "Christ ascended into heaven as God."
(iii) "Jesus rose as Lazarus did, except that he stayed alive."
(iv) "Christ's glorified body passed through the shut doors because a spiritual body is not material."
(v) "After the ascension the Word is no longer man."

**P2 (🟡)** *(Exegetical)* Read ST III q.53 a.4, "Whether Christ was the cause of His own Resurrection?" (English Dominican Province):

> As stated above (III:50:2 and III:50:3) in consequence of death Christ's Godhead was not separated from His soul, nor from His flesh. Consequently, both the soul and the flesh of the dead Christ can be considered in two respects: first, in respect of His Godhead; secondly, in respect of His created nature. Therefore, according to the virtue of the Godhead united to it, the body took back again the soul which it had laid aside, and the soul took back again the body which it had abandoned: and thus Christ rose by His own power. ... But if we consider the body and soul of the dead Christ according to the power of created nature, they could not thus be reunited, but it was necessary for Christ to be raised up by God.

(a) Name the premise from an earlier lesson that the first sentence depends on, and say what would follow for this article if it were false. (b) Using the passage and the rule of the undivided outward works, show that Acts 2:24 ("Whom God hath raised up") and John 10:18 ("I have power to take it up again") do not conflict. **Two sentences per part.**

**P3 (🔴)** *(Exegetical)* An invented parish catechist's handout reads:

> "At the ascension Jesus left his body behind and became spirit, so now he can be everywhere at once — in every heart and every church. That's why the question of the empty tomb really doesn't matter. What matters is that he is alive in us."

(a) Name two separate errors and the heresy or denial each lands in. (b) State the doctrine each error contradicts, with its level and the text that fixes it. (c) In one sentence, say what true thing the catechist was reaching for. **150 words total.**

<details>
<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict:**
(i) **True.** Acts 2:24. The raising is a work of the divine power, which is common to the three, so it is rightly said of the Father without excluding the Son (q.53 a.4 ad 1; Catechism 648–649).
(ii) **True only with a distinction.** False as to the *condition* of the one ascending: ascent is local motion and belongs to the human nature, so he ascended as man. True as to the *cause*: he ascended by the power of his Godhead (q.57 a.2).
(iii) **False.** Lazarus returned to mortal, earthly life and would die again. Christ passed into glorified life beyond death (Catechism 646; q.54 on glory). "Stayed alive" misses the difference in kind.
(iv) **False.** The risen body has "flesh and bones" (Luke 24:39), and *pneumatikon* means Spirit-governed, not immaterial. Aquinas credits the passage through the doors to the power of the Godhead, not to any property in the body (q.54 a.1 ad 1). How glorified bodies relate to place is school theology, but "not material" is excluded.
(v) **False.** The union is never undone. He "continueth for ever ... always living to make intercession" (Heb 7:24–25), and the Creed's "whose kingdom shall have no end" (Constantinople I) excludes a Word who withdraws from his humanity.

**Wrong turns:** marking (i) false or distinction-only on the grounds that "he rose by his own power." Both are true of one act. Marking (ii) simply true, or simply false. Accepting (iv) because Aquinas mentions a view ("as some say") that credits the glorified condition. That view still makes the body *real*; what (iv) adds, "not material," is the error.

**Model answer:** (i) T, the common divine power, q.53 a.4 ad 1. (ii) Distinction: as man in condition, by the Godhead as cause, q.57 a.2. (iii) F, resuscitation against glorification, CCC 646. (iv) F, Luke 24:39 and q.54 a.1 ad 1. (v) F, Heb 7:25 and "kingdom shall have no end."

**P2** *(Exegetical (a) · Exegetical (b))*

**Must hit, strict (a):** the hypostatic union persists through death. The Word stayed united to the soul in hell and to the body in the tomb (III q.50 aa.2–3; [6.1](06-01-he-descended-into-hell.md)). If death had dissolved the union, the one who rose would not be raising *his own* body and soul by a power united to them. At most God would raise a man, and "Christ rose by his own power" would fail.

**Must hit, strict (b):** by his created nature Christ is raised *by God* (Acts 2:24). By the Godhead united to that nature he raises himself (John 10:18). This is reduplication, "as man" against "as God." And the Godhead's power is one and the same in the Father and the Son (the undivided works), so "the Father raised him" and "he raised himself" name one act, not two competing causes.

**Wrong turns:** answering (a) with "Christ is divine." The premise needed is the *persistence of the union in death*, not the divinity as such. Answering (b) by giving the raising to the Father *instead of* the Son, or splitting it into two acts, one by each. Saying the human soul raised the body by its own natural power, which the article's last sentence denies.

**Model answer:** (a) The first sentence rests on the union persisting through death: the Word stayed joined to the dead body and the separated soul. Without that, "his own power" would have nothing of his to act on, and Christ would simply be a man God raised. (b) As man, in his created nature, Christ could not reunite soul and body, so he was raised by God; as God, by the Godhead still united to both, he rose by his own power. Since the divine power is one in the Father and the Son, Acts 2:24 and John 10:18 describe one act from two sides.

**P3** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** *Error 1:* "left his body behind and became spirit ... everywhere at once." This drops or dissolves the humanity, and gives a divine attribute to the humanity as humanity. That is **Eutychian** absorption, or **Docetism** about the risen body; credit either if it names the lost humanity. *Error 2:* "the empty tomb doesn't matter." That detaches the resurrection from the body that was buried and denies the **bodily resurrection**. "Alive in us" also shifts the event into the believers.
**Must hit, strict (b):** against 1: the ascension and session in the body, **defined dogma** (the Creed's "ascended ... sitteth"; Lateran IV "ascended in both"; the Creed's "kingdom shall have no end" for permanence). Against 2: he rose in his own flesh, **defined dogma** (the Creed; Lateran IV "rose in the flesh"). The tomb's emptiness follows from that dogma, even though its force as *evidence* is not defined (Catechism 640).
**Must hit, strict (c):** the glorified Christ really is not confined as he was in Galilee. He is present how and when he wills (Catechism 645), and he is present to believers by his Spirit.

**Wrong turns:** calling Error 1 Nestorian. Nothing splits the subject; the humanity is lost, not doubled. Citing Lateran IV's "all shall rise with their own bodies" for Christ. Marking the empty tomb itself as a separate defined article.

**Model answer:** (a) "Left his body ... became spirit ... everywhere" dissolves the humanity into the Godhead and gives it divine omnipresence: Eutychian absorption, or Docetism about the risen body. "The tomb doesn't matter" detaches Easter from the buried body, denying the bodily resurrection. (b) Defined dogma: Christ ascended and sits in the body, and his kingdom has no end (Creed; Lateran IV). He rose in his own flesh (Creed; Lateran IV), so the tomb was empty, though the tomb's evidential weight is not defined (CCC 640). (c) The risen Christ is no longer bound by place: he is present as he wills, and he dwells in believers by his Spirit.

## Flashback

**From Lesson [5.4](05-04-penal-substitution-and-the-catholic-assessment.md) (Penal substitution and the Catholic assessment):** *(Exegetical.)* Aquinas asks whether God the Father delivered Christ up to the Passion (ST III q.47 a.3, body of the article, English Dominican Province):

> Christ suffered voluntarily out of obedience to the Father. Hence in three respects God the Father did deliver up Christ to the Passion. In the first way, because by His eternal will He preordained Christ's Passion for the deliverance of the human race, according to the words of Isaias (53:6): "The Lord hath laid on Him the iniquities of us all"; and again (Isaiah 53:10): "The Lord was pleased to bruise Him in infirmity." Secondly, inasmuch as, by the infusion of charity, He inspired Him with the will to suffer for us; hence we read in the same passage: "He was offered because it was His own will" (Isaiah 53:7). Thirdly, by not shielding Him from the Passion, but abandoning Him to His persecutors … because, to wit, He left Him to the power of His persecutors, as Augustine says.

**(a)** Isaiah 53:6 and 53:10 are the texts a penal reading leans on hardest. Under which respect does Aquinas file them, and what kind of act does that make the Father's? **(b)** Which words rule out the Father's will being set against the Son's, and which words fix what "abandoning" means here? **(c)** What level does the claim "the Father did not punish the Son in wrath" carry, and does any act of the Church fix it? **Two sentences each for (a) and (b); one sentence for (c).**

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict (a):** Aquinas puts both texts under the **first respect**, the Father's **eternal will preordaining** the Passion **"for the deliverance of the human race."** So the Father's act here is the ordaining of a saving plan, whose stated purpose is our deliverance. It is not a verdict passed on the Son. It also stands beside the second respect, which is **cumulative** with it and not an alternative: the same Father who ordains the Passion inspires the Son's will to suffer.

**Must hit, strict (b):** **"suffered voluntarily out of obedience"** and **"by the infusion of charity, He inspired Him with the will to suffer for us,"** backed by "His own will". The Son's human will is moved by the Father's gift of charity, so the two wills point the same way. "Abandoning" is glossed at once as **"not shielding Him"** and **"left Him to the power of His persecutors"**: the Father withholds protection, and neither rejects the Son nor turns his wrath on him.

**Must hit, strict (c):** **common teaching**, reported by the Catechism. No council defines it, and no act of the Church condemns penal substitution by name.

**Wrong turns:** reading "pleased to bruise Him" as the Father's pleasure in the Son's pain; the same article's reply to objection 1 says God did not hand over an innocent against his will but inspired him with the will to suffer. Treating the three respects as rival explanations. Making "abandoning" mean the Father turning his face away in anger. Promoting (c) to defined dogma, or saying Trent condemned penal substitution. Its anathema on justification by the sole imputation of Christ's justice (Session VI, canon 11) concerns justification, not the atonement.

**Model answer:** (a) Both Isaiah texts fall under the first respect: the Father's eternal will preordaining the Passion "for the deliverance of the human race". His act is the ordaining of a plan to save us, and no sentence is passed on the Son. (b) Christ suffers "voluntarily out of obedience", with a will to suffer that the Father himself inspired "by the infusion of charity", so the wills are not opposed. "Abandoning" means only "not shielding Him", leaving him "to the power of His persecutors". (c) It is common teaching, reported by the Catechism, and no conciliar act fixes it either way.

</details>
