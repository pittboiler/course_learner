# Christology · Lesson 2.4: After Chalcedon: the Three Chapters and Constantinople II (553)

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 2: From Apollinaris to the two wills · Builds on: [2.2 Nestorius, Cyril, and Ephesus (431)](02-02-nestorius-cyril-and-ephesus.md), [2.3 Eutyches, Leo, and Chalcedon (451)](02-03-eutyches-leo-and-chalcedon.md) · Unlocks: [2.5 Monothelitism and Constantinople III (681)](02-05-monothelitism-and-constantinople-iii.md), [3.1 Person and nature: the hypostatic union](03-01-person-and-nature-the-hypostatic-union.md)

## Why this matters

A definition settles nothing until its reading is agreed. [The Chalcedonian Definition](../reference.md#the-chalcedonian-definition) ([2.3](02-03-eutyches-leo-and-chalcedon.md)) confessed one person in two natures, and for a century half the East read it as Nestorius vindicated. Constantinople II is the Church's ruling on that reading. It also made "one of the Trinity was crucified" an official sentence, and it is where a question Chalcedon left open gets its metaphysical answer: if Christ's humanity is complete, why is it not a second person?

## The idea

Look at Chalcedon from Alexandria. Cyril had fought Nestorius for one subject in Christ ([2.2](02-02-nestorius-cyril-and-ephesus.md)), and his watchword was "one incarnate nature of God the Word". Then a council says "*in* two natures". It adopts Leo's Tome, whose "each form does what is proper to it" sounds, to a Cyrillian, like two agents. And it received back into communion Theodoret and Ibas, two Antiochenes who had attacked Cyril, once each had anathematized Nestorius. To Cyril's heirs this was proof enough: Chalcedon was Nestorian.

Zeno's *Henoticon* (482) tried to reunite the parties by setting Chalcedon aside, and produced a schism with Rome instead. Justinian (527–565) tried the opposite: keep Chalcedon, but show that it was Cyrillian. His method: condemn the Antiochene texts the miaphysites cited as Chalcedon's real sympathies, the **[Three Chapters](../reference.md#the-three-chapters)**:

- **Theodore of Mopsuestia** (died 428, in communion with the Church), Nestorius's teacher: his person *and* his writings.
- **Theodoret of Cyrrhus**: his writings against Cyril, his Twelve Chapters and Ephesus, and those in defence of Theodore and Nestorius. Theodoret himself was not condemned.
- **The letter of Ibas of Edessa to Maris the Persian**, which calls Cyril an Apollinarian and says Ephesus deposed Nestorius without examination. The *letter* is condemned, "said to have" been his; Ibas himself is not.

The council's sentence draws those lines itself: "Theodore who was Bishop of Mopsuestia, and his impious writings", but only "those things which Theodoret impiously wrote". Pope Vigilius's resistance and eventual surrender is an event, and it belongs to [`church-history` 2.2](../../church-history/lessons/02-02-ephesus-chalcedon-and-leos-rome.md). The strategy failed: the miaphysites were not won back ([6.3](06-03-the-churches-that-did-not-receive-chalcedon.md)). What it left behind was a fixed reading of Chalcedon.

## Source

The Second Council of Constantinople, the explanation appended to its fourth capitulum (Percival, *NPNF* 2.14):

> As a matter of fact the word "union" (tes henoseos) has many meanings, and the partisans of Apollinaris and Eutyches have affirmed that these natures are confounded inter se, and have asserted a union produced by the mixture of both. On the other hand the followers of Theodorus and of Nestorius rejoicing in the division of the natures, have taught only a relative union. Meanwhile the Holy Church of God, condemning equally the impiety of both sorts of heresies, recognises the union of God the Word with the flesh synthetically, that is to say, hypostatically. For in the mystery of Christ the synthetical union not only preserves unconfusedly the natures which are united, but also allows no separation.

Three phrases carry it. **"Many meanings"**: each heresy chose one. **"Relative union"** (a union of relation) is the council's name for the Antiochene position. It is Nestorius's union of *prosōpon* ([2.2](02-02-nestorius-cyril-and-ephesus.md)): two subjects joined by love, honour and will. **"Synthetically, that is to say, hypostatically"** is the council's answer. The union is a composition, and what it composes is a single [hypostasis](../reference.md#hypostatic-union) (*hypostasis*, here "concrete subsisting subject"). The last sentence compresses Chalcedon's four adverbs into two: "unconfusedly" against Eutyches, "no separation" against Nestorius. Chalcedon's ground, with Cyril's vocabulary of composition added.

## The argument

The fourteen capitula fall into three groups. **1–9** state the doctrine. **10–11** state the theopaschite formula and list the condemned heretics. **12–14** condemn the Three Chapters. Their logic runs:

1. **Chalcedon received Cyril.** Its Definition received Cyril's synodical letters to Nestorius and the Easterns; 553 added that one of them carried the Twelve Anathemas. *In words:* Chalcedon cannot mean what Cyril condemned.
2. **Chalcedon's "one hypostasis" is the Word's own** (capitulum 5). A union of many hypostases called "one person" only by honour is anathematized, "for since one of the Holy Trinity has been made man... the Holy Trinity has not been increased". *In words:* the Definition's one person is the Second Person of the Trinity, not a merger of two.
3. **"In two natures" is legitimate as a difference, never as a division** (capitulum 7). The difference is to be taken "in a theoretical manner" (Percival glosses the Greek as "as an abstraction"), and the number two may not be used to make persons. *In words:* count two natures, never two someones.
4. **"One incarnate nature of God the Word" is also legitimate, in the right sense** (capitulum 8), meaning a hypostatic union from which one Christ results. It is anathematized in the sense of one nature mixed from Godhead and manhood. *In words:* Cyril's formula is received, and Eutyches is condemned again.
5. **So the Word is the subject of everything Christ does and suffers.** The miracles and the sufferings "which of his own will he endured in the flesh" belong to one person (capitulum 3). The one "crucified in the flesh" is "true God and the Lord of Glory and one of the Holy Trinity" (capitulum 10). *In words:* the Crucified is God the Son, suffering in what he assumed, not a man joined to God.

[`trinity-and-god` 1.5](../../trinity-and-god/lessons/01-05-impassibility-and-the-god-who-suffers.md) read capitulum 10 for what it says about God, that the suffering is "in the flesh" and so not in the divine nature. Read it here for what it says about **Christ**. The subject named from the divine side takes a predicate of the human side, and that is the [communication of idioms](../reference.md#communication-of-idioms) ([3.2](03-02-the-communication-of-idioms.md)) made official. The **[theopaschite formula](../reference.md#the-theopaschite-formula)**, "one of the Trinity suffered in the flesh", had divided Catholics since about 518; Percival's note records that Justinian sought its approval from Pope Hormisdas, who declined it as unnecessary and even dangerous, and that "the Fifth Ecumenical Council directly approved the formula". Strictly, capitulum 10's verb is "crucified"; capitulum 3 supplies "sufferings... in the flesh".

**The open question, and [enhypostasia](../reference.md#enhypostasia).** Premise 2 raises an objection that the miaphysites pressed. There are no free-floating natures, so a complete human nature makes a human hypostasis, which is Nestorius. Deny that, and the humanity is *anhypostatos*, without subsistence, and so unreal. Leontius of Byzantium (first half of the sixth century) and later John of Damascus answered with a third option. A nature need not have its *own* hypostasis in order to be real. It can exist *in* another's. John states it in *Exposition of the Orthodox Faith* III.9, which his translator heads "whether there is Nature that has no Subsistence":

> For the flesh of God the Word did not subsist as an independent subsistence, nor did there arise another subsistence besides that of God the Word, but as it existed in that it became rather a subsistence which subsisted in another.

*In words:* the humanity is not a hypostasis of its own, nor without subsistence; it subsists in the Word, real without being a second someone. One historical caution. The tidy label "enhypostasia", with *enhypostatos* read as "in-subsistent", owes much to nineteenth-century scholarship. Brian Daley (*Leontius of Byzantium: Complete Works*, 2017) argues that in Leontius the word mostly means "really existing, not accidental", and that the "in-" is not locative. On that reading the classic statement is John's. [3.1](03-01-person-and-nature-the-hypostatic-union.md) builds on it.

**"Neo-Chalcedonianism"** is historians' name for this Cyrillian reading. The [label](../reference.md#neo-chalcedonianism) is contested: Grillmeier's *Christ in Christian Tradition* (vol. 2) uses it, distinguishing moderate and stricter forms; critics answer that Chalcedon, having received Cyril's letters, was being made explicit, not supplemented. That is a judgement about 451, not 553.

**Mark the levels.** The doctrinal capitula repeat and apply defined dogma (one hypostasis, *Theotokos*, two natures unconfused), and capitulum 10's theopaschite formula is itself **defined dogma**. The condemnations of the Three Chapters judge that particular texts teach error. This is the kind of judgment the ladder calls a **dogmatic fact**, on the "definitive, to be held" rung ([`fundamental-theology` 4.4](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-04-the-ladder-of-doctrinal-authority.md)). That rung covers the judgment on the texts as the council read them, not a verdict on the eternal fate of any man. Whether the excerpts read in 553 represent Theodore fairly is a historian's question, and scholars are divided on it. *Enhypostasia* as an explanatory apparatus is **common teaching**, never defined. "Neo-Chalcedonianism" is a historians' label and has no level at all. See [the levels in Christology](../reference.md#the-levels-in-christology).

## What the capitula do

| Capitula | Content | Against |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | One nature, three hypostases in God | Confusion of the Trinity with the Incarnation |
| 2, 6 | Two births of the Word; Mary truly Mother of God | *Christotokos* / *anthropotokos* |
| 3, 4, 5 | One subject of miracles and sufferings; the union is hypostatic, not "relative"; the one hypostasis is the Word's | Nestorius, Theodore |
| 7, 8, 9 | "In two natures" as difference only; "one incarnate nature" as hypostatic union only; one adoration | Division *and* mixture |
| 10 | One of the Holy Trinity crucified in the flesh | A crucified mere man |
| 11 | Arius to Eutyches (and Origen) anathematized | Every earlier condemned party |
| 12 | Theodore: person and writings | A man worshipped "just as one worships the image of an emperor" |
| 13 | Theodoret's writings against Cyril and Ephesus (not his person) | Defending them |
| 14 | The letter Ibas "is said to have written" (not his person) | Defending it |

## Worked examples

**Example 1: the machinery on a clean case.** At Lazarus's tomb, Jesus weeps and then calls a dead man out (John 11:35, 43). Who weeps? The Word, in his flesh. Who raises Lazarus? The same Word, by his divine power. Capitulum 3 forbids exactly the split here: "the wonder-working Word" as one and "the Christ that suffered" as another. Now run capitulum 7: tears belong to a human body and the command to the dead to divine power, so two natures are counted and the acts distinguished by them, but never assigned to two agents.

**Example 2: where it strains.** Peter the Fuller added "who was crucified for us" to the Trisagion ("Holy God, Holy Mighty, Holy Immortal"). The Council in Trullo (692), in its canon 81, condemned the addition. Percival's note, however, records that the Fifth Council "directly approved" the formula "one of the Trinity suffered". Why is one dogma and the other forbidden? The subject differs. John of Damascus reads the hymn as addressed to the whole Trinity, with "Holy God" said of the Father and "Holy Mighty" of the Son (*Exposition* III.10). On that reading "who was crucified" makes the Trinity passible, as if "the Father and the Holy Spirit suffered on the Cross along with the Son". Capitulum 10 names one person, "one of the Holy Trinity", and adds "in the flesh". Nearly the same words, different grammar; Percival's note grants that the addition could bear a good sense if the hymn were addressed to Christ.

## Watch out

- **"Constantinople II reversed Chalcedon" is false.** It receives the four councils and all they defined, and condemns Eutyches again (capitula 8, 11). It fixed how Chalcedon is read, not what it says.
- **"The Trinity suffered" is patripassianism.** It predicates of three persons what belongs to one person in his flesh. The defined sentence is "one *of* the Trinity".
- **"Without a human hypostasis" does not mean "without a complete humanity".** If the humanity lacks some part, that is **Apollinarianism** ([2.1](02-01-apollinaris-a-complete-humanity.md)). The claim is only that the humanity's subject is the Word.
- **Level errors both ways.** "The council damned Theodore" overstates what a condemnation does; "imperial politics, so the capitula don't bind" understates ecumenical dogma; *enhypostasia* is an explanation, not a defined term.

## One-liner

> Constantinople II ruled that Chalcedon's one person is the Word himself, so the one on the cross is one of the Trinity, suffering in a humanity that is fully real and subsists in him.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢)** *(Exegetical)* The third capitulum of Constantinople II (Percival, *NPNF* 2.14; brackets are Percival's):

> If anyone shall say that the wonder-working Word of God is one [Person] and the Christ that suffered another; or shall say that God the Word was with the woman-born Christ, or was in him as one person in another, but that he was not one and the same our Lord Jesus Christ, the Word of God, incarnate and made man, and that his miracles and the sufferings which of his own will he endured in the flesh were not of the same [Person]: let him be anathema.

(a) The capitulum rules out three different ways of describing two subjects. Name all three, using the text's words. (b) Two phrases near the end guard the divine side while the sufferings are attributed to the Word. Which are they, and what does each exclude? **Two sentences per part.**

**P2 (🟡)** *(Exegetical)* Mark each sentence **true**, **false**, or **true only with a distinction**, and cite the capitulum or rule that decides it. **One sentence each.**

1. "One of the Holy Trinity was crucified in the flesh."
2. "The Holy Trinity suffered on the cross."
3. "God suffered."
4. "The man Jesus is adored because of the Word dwelling in him, as one honours the emperor's image."

**P3 (🔴)** *(Exegetical)* An **invented** handout from a parish adult-education series:

> "(1) In 553 the Church quietly reversed Chalcedon: the council adopted Cyril's 'one incarnate nature' and made 'in two natures' optional. (2) It also condemned Leo's Tome, since 'each form does what is proper to it' makes two agents."

For each sentence, name the error, cite the capitulum or conciliar text that corrects it, and say in one sentence what true thing the author was reaching for. **120 words total.**

<details>
<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict (a):** (i) **Two persons outright**: the wonder-working Word "is one" and "the Christ that suffered another". (ii) **Accompaniment**: God the Word "was with the woman-born Christ". (iii) **Indwelling**: the Word "was in him as one person in another". The capitulum answers all three with "one and the same".

**Must hit, strict (b):** **"of his own will"** excludes the idea that the sufferings were imposed on the Word against his will or happened to someone else. They are his, freely undergone. **"in the flesh"** excludes predicating the sufferings of the divine nature. They belong to the person, and they are undergone in what he assumed.

**Wrong turns:** listing "incarnate and made man" as a third error, when it is the orthodox answer. Reading "one person in another" as a comment on the Trinity. Treating "in the flesh" as though it made the Word no longer the subject. It locates the suffering and does not change who suffers.

**Model answer:** (a) The capitulum excludes a Word who is one person and a suffering Christ who is another. It also excludes a Word who is merely "with" the man born of the woman, and a Word who dwells "in him as one person in another". Against all three it sets "one and the same" Lord, whose miracles and sufferings belong to the same Person. (b) "Of his own will" makes the sufferings the Word's own free act, not something that befell another subject. "In the flesh" locates them in the humanity, not the divine nature, so the Word is their subject without the Godhead's being passible.

---

**P2** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict:**
1. **True.** This is capitulum 10 almost verbatim. The subject is a divine person, and the predicate is located in the flesh.
2. **False.** It predicates of all three persons what belongs to the Son in his humanity. This is patripassianism, and it is the reading that sank Peter the Fuller's Trisagion addition. The rule is communication of idioms, which runs through the *one* person who has the human nature.
3. **True only with a distinction.** "God" must stand for the Son, a divine person, and the suffering is "in the flesh" (capitula 3 and 10). It is false if "God" stands for the Godhead as such, or for the Father.
4. **False.** This is Theodore's position as capitulum 12 reports it. It is a "relative" union (capitulum 4), and it makes two adorations where capitulum 9 requires one adoration of God the Word made man together with his flesh.

**Wrong turns:** marking 3 simply true, because the distinction is the whole point. Marking 4 "true with a distinction" because Christ's humanity is adored. It is adored, but as the Word's own flesh, not as a separate man honoured for another's sake. Marking 2 true on the grounds that the three persons act together in every outward work, since suffering in a nature belongs only to the person whose nature it is.

**Model answer:** (1) True: capitulum 10. (2) False: patripassianism, since only the Son has flesh to suffer in. (3) True if "God" names the Son suffering in his flesh (capitula 3 and 10); false of the Godhead or the Father. (4) False: Theodore's two-subject adoration, condemned in capitula 12 and 9.

---

**P3** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict (1):** The error is misreading 553 as a *miaphysite* or Eutychian reversal. Capitulum 7 keeps "in two natures", allowing it as a difference and never as a division. Capitulum 8 accepts "one incarnate nature" *only* in the sense of hypostatic union and anathematizes a mixed single nature. Capitulum 11 condemns Eutyches again. The council receives Chalcedon and "all that they defined". The true thing reached for is that 553 did read Chalcedon in Cyril's sense and did receive his formula rightly understood.

**Must hit, strict (2):** This is a historical error, and it imports a **Nestorian** reading of Leo. Chalcedon received Leo's letter, and 553 receives Chalcedon and everything it defined. Capitulum 3 gives the right reading: one agent, whose acts are distinguished by the nature through which he acts. The true thing reached for is that Cyrillians did suspect the Tome of two agents.

**Wrong turns:** calling (1) Nestorianism. Conceding (2) and then "defending" Leo as though he were condemned.

**Model answer:** (1) Wrong. Capitulum 7 keeps "in two natures" as a real difference, capitulum 8 receives "one incarnate nature" only as hypostatic union, and capitula 8 and 11 condemn mixture and Eutyches. What is true is that 553 read Chalcedon in Cyril's sense. (2) Wrong. Chalcedon received the Tome, and 553 receives all Chalcedon defined. Read as two agents, "each form does" would be Nestorianism, but it means one agent acting through each nature (capitulum 3). What is true is that the Tome was suspected in the East.

## Flashback

**From Lesson [2.2](02-02-nestorius-cyril-and-ephesus.md) (Nestorius, Cyril, and Ephesus (431)):** *(Exegetical.)* Mark each sentence **true**, **false**, or **true only with a stated distinction**, and name the rule or text that decides it. **One line each.**

1. "Christ is one Son, though 'Son' belongs to the Word by nature and to the man Jesus by honour."
2. "The Word dwelt in Jesus as in a temple."
3. "Mary is Mother of God because the Godhead received its beginning from her."
4. "Nestorius taught that there are two Sons."

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict:**
1. **False.** "Son" said once by nature and once by honour counts two sons, however closely they are joined. This is the union of *prosōpon* that Cyril's Second Letter rejects and Ephesus judged against: the Word did not unite a man to himself, he "was made flesh".
2. **True only with a distinction.** It is true if "temple" means the Word's own body, as in John 2:21. It is false if "Jesus" names a man, already someone, in whom the Word lives. Cyril's Second Letter denies that he was "first born a common man of the holy Virgin, and then the Word came down and entered into him".
3. **False.** The conclusion is right and the reason is wrong. Cyril's Second Letter denies that "his divine nature received its beginning of existence in the holy Virgin". The title rests on the identity of subject: the one she bore is the Word, according to the flesh.
4. **True only with a distinction.** As a report of what he said, it is false: he confessed one Christ, one Son, and denied teaching two persons. As the charge against his position, it is the point at issue: a union of *prosōpon* joins two who are each already someone. Ephesus judged the position, whoever held it.

**Wrong turns:** marking 2 simply false and so forbidding scripture's own image. Marking 3 true because its conclusion is the defined title. Marking 4 simply true, which repeats the caricature the *Bazaar of Heracleides* debate is about. Marking it simply false and concluding that the condemnation missed its target.

**Model answer:** (1) False: two sonships make two sons, and the Word "was made flesh" instead of uniting a man to himself. (2) True with a distinction: true of his own flesh, false of a man he entered (Second Letter). (3) False: the Godhead has no beginning from Mary. She bore the Word in the flesh. (4) True with a distinction: not his words, but what his union of *prosōpon* entails, and that position is what Ephesus judged.

</details>
