# Christology · Lesson 6.3: The churches that did not receive Chalcedon

> ⏱ ~15 min · Module 6: Descended, risen, ascended, confessed · Builds on: [2.2 Nestorius, Cyril, and Ephesus](02-02-nestorius-cyril-and-ephesus.md), [2.3 Eutyches, Leo, and Chalcedon](02-03-eutyches-leo-and-chalcedon.md), [2.4 After Chalcedon](02-04-the-three-chapters-and-constantinople-ii.md) · Unlocks: [6.4 Christology today](06-04-christology-today.md)

## Why this matters

Every definition in Module 2 was written against someone, and two sets of the people it was written against never went away. Chalcedon lost Egypt, most of Syria and Armenia. Ephesus had already lost the Christians of the Persian empire. Both breaks have lasted fifteen centuries. Neither lost church has ever been shown to hold a heresy in substance. In the last fifty years Rome has signed Christological declarations with both. This lesson states the two Christologies as their own theologians state them, and then reads the declarations the way the course reads any text: what it says, and how much weight it carries.

## The idea

Remember the course's two questions: **who** is Christ (one subject, the Word), and **what** is he (two complete natures). Chalcedon answers them with two words. *Hypostasis* answers the who, and there is one. *Physis* answers the what, and there are two.

The churches outside Chalcedon use the **same questions** but a **different dictionary**.

- For the **Oriental Orthodox** (Coptic, Syriac, Armenian, Ethiopian, Eritrean and Malankara churches), *physis* can name the **who**: a concrete, existing individual. In that sense Christ is [one incarnate nature of God the Word](../reference.md#one-incarnate-nature-of-god-the-word). Saying "two natures" after the union sounds to them like two individuals, which means two Sons.
- For the **Church of the East**, *qnoma* (plural *qnome*) names something in between: a nature as it actually exists in this individual. So Christ has **two** *qnome* in one *parsopa*. Saying "one *qnoma*" sounds to them like a mixed nature, which means Eutyches.

So one side counts the who with a word Chalcedon keeps for the what. The other side counts the what with a word Chalcedon's Greek translates as the who. Each hears in Chalcedon the heresy it fears most. Whether anything besides the dictionary divides them is this lesson's open question.

## Source

John of Damascus, a Chalcedonian, defends Cyril's formula by quoting Cyril himself, from the *Second Letter to Succensus*. *Exposition of the Orthodox Faith* III.11 (Salmond, *NPNF* 2.9):

> Indeed, the blessed Cyril himself, when he is interpreting the phrase, "one nature of God the Word Incarnate," says in the second epistle to Sucensus, "For if we simply said the one nature of the Word' and then were silent, and did not add the word incarnate,' but, so to speak, quite excluded the dispensation, there would be some plausibility in the question they feign to ask, If one nature is the whole, what becomes of the perfection in humanity, or how has the essence like us come to exist?' But inasmuch as the perfection in humanity and the disclosure of the essence like us are conveyed in the word incarnate,' they must cease from relying on a mere straw."

(The broken quotation marks are Salmond's.) Two words carry the passage. **"Nature"** is the Word himself, the one subject: the *who*. **"Incarnate"** carries the whole humanity, "the essence like us": the *what*. Take away "incarnate" and the formula is Apollinarian. Keep it and the formula already contains Chalcedon's second nature. Earlier in the same book John makes the formula his own: we confess "one incarnate nature of God the Word" (III.7).

## The argument

1. **The Oriental Orthodox position.** The formula is Cyril's *mia physis tou theou logou sesarkōmenē*. Cyril took it for Athanasius's, but it came from an Apollinarian forgery ([`patristics` 5.1](../../patristics/lessons/05-01-cyril-of-alexandria.md)). **Severus of Antioch** (Patriarch 512–518) gave it its classical form: one incarnate nature "out of two," with divinity and humanity each complete and unmixed, and the natures told apart in thought rather than counted as two existing things. The churches that hold it **condemn Eutyches** as firmly as Chalcedon does. The Malankara theologian V. C. Samuel (*The Council of Chalcedon Re-Examined*) argues that a main ground for rejecting Chalcedon was that the council received Leo's Tome and restored Theodoret and Ibas, which looked like Nestorius let back in. He concludes that both sides were orthodox in what they meant. *In words:* one nature, meaning one incarnate Word, not one blended substance.
2. **Why "monophysite" is the wrong word.** *Monos* means "only": one nature and no other. That describes Eutyches's absorbed humanity. *Mia* means "one, united." [Miaphysitism](../reference.md#miaphysitism) is the name these churches accept. *In words:* the old label accuses them of the heresy they anathematize.
3. **The Church of the East's position.** The Church of the East grew up in the Persian empire, and its teacher was Theodore of Mopsuestia rather than Nestorius. **Babai the Great** (died 628), in the *Book of the Union*, gave it its classical form: two *kyane* (natures) and two [*qnome*](../reference.md#qnoma-and-parsopa) in one *parsopa* (person), united without mixture and never divided. A *qnoma* is a nature as it exists concretely in this individual. It is not a second person. Sebastian Brock ("The 'Nestorian' Church: a lamentable misnomer", 1996) argues that the label is a polemical name. On his account it wrongly fastens Nestorius's name, and the two-Sons doctrine Ephesus condemned, onto a church whose formula says one *parsopa*. *In words:* two concrete natures, one person, and "Nestorian" is the enemy's word for it.
4. **What all three always shared.** All three confess the Nicene faith: one Son, perfect God and perfect man, no mixture, no division. All condemn a humanity swallowed by the divinity. What they do not share is the councils. The Oriental Orthodox receive the first three. The Church of the East does not receive Ephesus. Each side also honours teachers the other condemned: Dioscorus and Severus on one side, Theodore and Nestorius on the other.
5. **The [common declarations](../reference.md#the-common-christological-declarations).** Here is what the vatican.va texts show. On **10 May 1973** Paul VI and Shenouda III, the Coptic Pope, signed a declaration. It confesses Christ perfect God and perfect man, united without mingling, confusion, alteration, division or separation, and Mary as *Theotokos*. It also says plainly that the differences since 451 cannot be ignored. The Pro Oriente consultation at Vienna in **1971** was unofficial, but its formula prepared that text. On **23 June 1984** John Paul II and Zakka I Iwas, the Syriac Patriarch, went further. They said the schisms arose only from differences of terminology and culture, and they allowed limited sharing of penance, the Eucharist and anointing when no priest of one's own church can be reached. At **Pentecost 1990** a joint international commission with the Malankara Orthodox Syrian Church agreed that the content of faith is the same, and that the differences of terminology can coexist in one communion. The **11 November 1994** declaration was signed by John Paul II and Mar Dinkha IV of the Assyrian Church of the East. On **13 December 1996** came the declaration with Karekin I of the Armenian Apostolic Church. *In words:* each text professes one faith in the same Christ, in words chosen to avoid both sides' fighting terms.

**Mark the levels.** What the declarations profess about Christ is **defined dogma** (rung 1), restated from Chalcedon and not newly defined. The declarations themselves are joint acts of the Pope and a patriarch, or agreements of a commission. They are not definitions and not conciliar acts. At most they are **authentic ordinary magisterium** (rung 3), and each one's weight must be [read from its own wording](../../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-05-reading-a-documents-weight.md). The wording limits itself. The 1984 text calls the identity of faith not yet complete, and the 1994 text blames the divisions largely, not wholly, on misunderstandings. **Whether the difference was ever more than verbal is freely disputed** (rung 5). The labels "monophysite" and "Nestorian" are questions of history and terminology, with no rung at all. See [the levels in Christology](../reference.md#the-levels-in-christology).

## Map of positions

| | Chalcedonian (Catholic, Eastern Orthodox) | Oriental Orthodox | Church of the East |
|---|---|---|---|
| Formula | one hypostasis in two natures | one incarnate nature of God the Word, out of two | two *kyane*, two *qnome*, one *parsopa* |
| The word that counts **one** | *hypostasis* | *physis* (the concrete Word) | *parsopa* |
| Fears in the others | mixture (one-nature talk); two Sons (two-*qnome* talk) | two Sons (Chalcedon) | mixture (the one-nature formula) |
| Councils received | Nicaea onward | the first three | not Ephesus |
| Mary | *Theotokos* | *Theotokos* | Mother of Christ our God and Saviour |
| Condemns Eutyches | yes | yes | yes |

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (clean): run the two questions on "one incarnate nature."** *Who?* "Nature of God the Word" names the Word himself: one subject, the same answer as Chalcedon's "one hypostasis." Cyril in the Source says so. *What?* "Incarnate" carries "the perfection in humanity," which is a complete human nature, and the Oriental Orthodox say it is united without mixture or change. *Eutyches test:* they anathematize him. So read in its own dictionary the formula passes both questions. Constantinople II (capitulum 8) had already received it "as the holy Fathers have taught," and condemned it only in the sense of one mixed substance ([2.4](02-04-the-three-chapters-and-constantinople-ii.md)). The Chalcedonian worry that remains is not about the words. It is about what follows from them. Can a church that says "one nature" also say two natural wills and operations ([2.5](02-05-monothelitism-and-constantinople-iii.md))?

**Example 2 (hard): *Theotokos* and the Church of the East.** Constantinople II's sixth capitulum condemns anyone who calls Mary "the Mother of Christ (Christotokon), as if Christ were not God" (Percival, *NPNF* 2.14). The Assyrian liturgy calls her "the Mother of Christ our God and Saviour." Does it fall under the anathema?

Look at the anathema's condition, "as if Christ were not God." The Assyrian title puts "our God" in the same breath as the one she bore. So the one born is the one who is God, and the condition fails. The 1994 declaration takes exactly this road. It recognizes both "Mother of God" and the Assyrian title as legitimate expressions of one faith, and each church keeps its own usage in its liturgy. The declaration says Christ's natures are united in one person, and it rules out a mere man whom God adopted and indwelt.

Here is where it strains. The declaration names no council. It says the old anathemas bore on persons and on formulas, and it does not withdraw them. The Assyrian Church still honours Theodore and Nestorius, whom Ephesus and Constantinople II condemned. Eucharistic communion was not restored. The Christological question looks settled; the question of the councils and their condemnations does not. The 2001 guidelines on mutual admission to the Eucharist with the Chaldean Church (the anaphora of Addai and Mari) belong to `sacraments-and-liturgy`.

## Watch out

- **"The Copts are monophysites."** They anathematize the doctrine that word names. A humanity swallowed into the divinity is **Eutychianism**, and all three families condemn it.
- **"The Church of the East teaches two Sons."** That is **Nestorianism** in the sense Ephesus condemned, and the Church of the East's formula excludes it with "one *parsopa*". Do not assume a *qnoma* is a person because *hypostasis* is one.
- **"The 1984 declaration settled that Chalcedon was a mistake."** No declaration touches Chalcedon's standing for Catholics. Chalcedon remains defined dogma. A declaration's judgment about another church's faith is not a definition.
- **Overcorrecting to "it was all words."** That is one side of a freely disputed question. The 1973 text itself says the differences cannot be ignored.
- **The forgery settles nothing by itself.** Where a formula came from does not decide what it means now. It only removes the authority Cyril thought it had.

## One-liner

> Three churches answered one question with one Son, but each spelled "who" and "what" with different words, and the declarations of the last fifty years found the same faith under the different spelling without settling whether it was only the spelling.

## Problems

**P1 (🟢)** *(Exegetical)* For each sentence, say whether **both** a Chalcedonian and a miaphysite can sign it, **only one** (say which), or **neither**. Give the deciding reason in one sentence each.

1. "Christ is one incarnate nature of God the Word, without mixture or change."
2. "After the union Christ is acknowledged in two natures."
3. "In the union the humanity was taken up into the divinity like a drop of vinegar into the sea."
4. "The one born of the Virgin is God the Word, and she is *Theotokos*."
5. "The eternal Word and the son of Mary are two sons, joined in honour and will."

**P2 (🟡)** *(Exegetical)* An **invented** magazine article, written for this problem:

> "The Coptic Church has been a monophysite heresy since 451: it denies that Christ had a real human nature. In 1973 Pope Paul VI admitted that Chalcedon had been a misunderstanding, which means Catholics no longer have to hold the council's definition. The Assyrians are simply Nestorians who worship two sons."

Name the three errors, in order. For each, give the correction from this lesson, and say which **kind** of error it is: a factual error about a church's teaching, a level error, or a mislabel. **120 words total.**

**P3 (🔴)** *(Evaluative)* Was the Christological difference between Chalcedon and the miaphysite churches ever more than verbal? **150 words or fewer**, any verdict.

<details>
<summary>Solutions</summary>

**P1** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict:** (1) **Both**, with the Chalcedonian taking *physis* as the concrete Word. Constantinople II capitulum 8 receives the formula in the sense of hypostatic union, and Cyril's "incarnate" carries the complete humanity (Source). (2) **Only the Chalcedonian.** The miaphysite hears "two natures after the union" as two existing subjects, even though he distinguishes the natures in thought. (3) **Neither.** It is Eutyches's absorbed humanity, and both condemn it. (4) **Both.** One subject, the Word, is born, and both churches confess *Theotokos*; the 1973 declaration says so explicitly. (5) **Neither.** It is two subjects joined morally, the doctrine Ephesus condemned. The miaphysites reject it, and so does Chalcedon's "not parted or divided into two persons."

**Wrong turns:** marking (1) "only miaphysite," which forgets that Cyril and 553 own the formula. Marking (2) "neither," as though miaphysites denied the humanity. Marking (3) "only miaphysite," which is the monophysite mislabel.

**Model answer:** (1) Both: Constantinople II allows it as hypostatic union. (2) Chalcedonian only: to a miaphysite, two natures means two subjects. (3) Neither: this is Eutychianism. (4) Both: one subject born, *Theotokos*. (5) Neither: two sons, condemned at Ephesus.

**P2** *(Exegetical)*

**Must hit, strict:** (1) "Monophysite... denies a real human nature" is a **mislabel** and a **factual error** about the church's teaching. The Copts are miaphysite, they confess perfect God and perfect man without mingling, and they condemn Eutyches. (2) "Catholics no longer have to hold the definition" is a **level error**. A joint declaration is not a definition and cannot un-define one. Chalcedon stays rung 1. The 1973 text actually says the differences since 451 cannot be ignored. (3) "Nestorians who worship two sons" is a **mislabel** and a **factual error**. The Church of the East's formula says one *parsopa*, and "Nestorian" is contested (Brock).

**Wrong turns:** calling (2) a factual error only, and missing the level confusion. Answering (3) by saying Nestorius was orthodox, which is a separate historical question.

**Model answer:** First, "monophysite" mislabels the Copts. They are miaphysite, confess a perfect humanity and anathematize Eutyches, so the claim is false about their teaching. Second, a level error: the 1973 declaration is a joint pastoral act, not a definition. It cannot release Catholics from Chalcedon, which remains defined dogma, and it says the differences cannot be ignored. Third, a mislabel: the Assyrian formula is two *qnome* in one person, not two sons.

**P3** *(Evaluative)*

**Must hit, any verdict:** state the strongest **verbal** case. *Physis* names the who for one side and the what for the other. Both condemn Eutyches and Nestorius's two sons. Constantinople II accepts both formulas when rightly understood. The declarations of 1984 and 1990 say the content is the same. State the strongest **more-than-verbal** case. The formulas carry further commitments, above all two natural wills and operations. The churches still disagree about the councils and about whom they honour or condemn. The 1973 text says the differences cannot be ignored. Then **name the level**: freely disputed. The declarations do not settle it, and their own wording is hedged.

**Wrong turns:** treating either verdict as Catholic doctrine. Citing a declaration as a definition. Arguing from the forgery alone.

**Model answer, one of several:** Mostly verbal, with a real remainder. On the *who* and the *what* the two sides mean the same thing: one incarnate Word, complete in both natures, unmixed. That is why 553 could receive "one incarnate nature" and why the 1984 and 1990 texts find the same content. But a formula brings its own further questions with it. Whether a one-nature Christology can say two natural operations is a real question, not a spelling one, and the councils and condemnations are still unreconciled. The 1973 declaration's refusal to ignore the differences is honest on that point. The question is freely disputed, and the declarations are hedged exactly where it bites.

## Flashback

**From Lesson [6.1](06-01-he-descended-into-hell.md) (He descended into hell):** *(Exegetical.)* On Holy Saturday Christ was truly dead: his soul and body were separated. Mark each sentence about those three days **true**, **false**, or **true only with a distinction**, and give the deciding rule in **one sentence each**.

1. "On Holy Saturday the divinity went down among the dead."
2. "For three days the body in Joseph's tomb belonged to no one; it was simply a corpse."
3. "Christ's descent reached even the hell of the lost."
4. "Christ went down to the dead to set free everyone held there."
5. "During the three days the Son of God was in the tomb, among the dead, and everywhere."

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Must hit, strict:** **1. False.** "The divinity" is an abstract term and names the nature. Going down among the dead belongs to the separated soul, and the divine nature, which is everywhere, goes nowhere. The true sentence is "the Son of God descended", which is said of the person through his soul (ST III q.50 a.3). **2. False.** Death did not dissolve the union: soul and body were separated from each other in place but not from the Word (Damascene, *Exposition* III.27; q.50 a.2). The Son of God was buried. **3. True only with a distinction** (q.52 a.2). *By effect*, yes: his descent put the lost to shame for their unbelief. *By essence*, no: his soul went only where the just were held. **4. False.** He freed the just who had gone before him, the holy fathers (q.52 a.5), and delivered none of the lost (a.6). The Catechism excludes freeing the damned or destroying hell (CCC 633). **5. True.** The concrete term names the person, and the whole person was in each place: in the tomb through his body, among the dead through his soul, and everywhere through his divinity (q.52 a.3).

**Wrong turns:** marking 2 "true with a distinction" because Aquinas says Christ was not simply a man during the three days (q.50 a.4). That concerns the living composite of soul and body, not whether the body was still the Word's. Marking 3 plainly false, which forgets the effect/essence distinction. Marking 5 false because the *whole of* Christ was not in the tomb. That is the neuter, said of the nature; the sentence speaks of the person.

**Model answer:** 1 false: the abstract term names the nature, and only the Son, through his soul, went down. 2 false: the body stayed united to the Word, separated from the soul in place and not from the subject. 3 true only with a distinction: by effect it reached the lost; by essence the soul went only to the just. 4 false: he freed the just and none of the lost (q.52 a.5–6; CCC 633). 5 true: the whole person was in the tomb by his body, among the dead by his soul, and everywhere by his divinity.

</details>
