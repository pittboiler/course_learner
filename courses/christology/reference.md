# Christology · Reference Card

> Open book. This card is meant to be open while you work — including during
> quizzes. Nothing here is something you're expected to recall cold.

Every Christological sentence answers one of two questions: **who** (one subject,
the eternal Word) or **what** (two natures, each complete). Nearly every error is
a confusion between them — dividing the *who*, fusing the *whats*, or subtracting
from one of them. And every claim carries a **level**, graded strictly in both
directions. This card is the lookup surface for both: the texts in the wording the
lessons verified, the grammar that sorts sentences, and the rung of every claim
that carries weight. Levels come from
[`fundamental-theology` 4.4](../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-04-the-ladder-of-doctrinal-authority.md),
which owns the ladder; where that course and this card differ, that course wins.

## What is defined, and where

The spine of the course. Each row is a claim, the act that fixes it, and its rung
on the ladder of [`fundamental-theology` 4.4](../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-04-the-ladder-of-doctrinal-authority.md).
Rung 1 is **defined dogma**, believed with divine and catholic faith; rung 2
**definitive**, to be held (dogmatic facts sit here); rung 3 **authentic ordinary
magisterium**, owed religious submission; rung 4 **theologically certain or
common teaching** of the schools; rung 5 **freely disputed** among Catholics.
Councils and documents are named, never numbered — see [Conventions](#conventions).

### The levels in Christology

The course's governing discipline. **Defined** (rung 1): one divine person, the
Word; two complete natures, unconfused and undivided; a true body and a rational
soul; *Theotokos*; two natural wills and operations; sinlessness; no change in the
Godhead; the creedal articles of death, descent, resurrection, ascension and
session; that Christ merited our justification. **Taught by an ecumenical council
without a canon**: that Christ made satisfaction to the Father (Trent VI ch. 7).
**Authentic ordinary magisterium** (rung 3): encyclicals (*Mystici Corporis*,
*Sempiternus Rex*, *Haurietis Aquas*), CDF acts approved by the pope, the Roman
Catechism's account of the descent. **Common teaching** (rung 4): the Thomist
analysis — Boethian person, grace of union, the three graces, the three
knowledges, the vision in Christ's earthly soul, *enhypostasia* as an explanation,
Maximus's natural/gnomic will, the concrete/abstract logic, propassion.
**Freely disputed** (rung 5): the motive of the Incarnation, one *esse* or two,
the necessity of satisfaction, impeccability and temptation, penal-substitution
language, Balthasar's Holy Saturday, whether the non-Chalcedonian difference was
verbal, and method in modern Christology.

Three habits keep the grading straight:

- **Find the act.** Age, liturgical use, a Father's axiom, a famous acclamation and a Catechism paragraph are not acts. The Catechism grades nothing; a claim keeps the weight of its source ([`fundamental-theology` 4.5](../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-05-reading-a-documents-weight.md)).
- **Separate dogma from its explanation.** That there is one hypostasis is defined; *how* a nature lacks its own personality is the schools'. Promoting the explanation and demoting the dogma are one error facing opposite ways.
- **"Not in the Creed" does not mean "not dogma".** Two wills, the rational soul and *Theotokos* are conciliar definitions, never recited on Sundays.

Historical claims (whether Nestorius was a Nestorian, whether Arius himself denied
a human soul, whether the Greek of Chalcedon read *en* or *ek*) sit on **no rung**;
labels ("neo-Chalcedonian", "monophysite") sit on none either.

### The dogmatic core

**One *who*, two complete *whats*** — the result the rest of the course argues
about, in the words the Church defined it. Six clauses, each with its act, all
rung 1:

1. **Truly God**, consubstantial with the Father — Nicaea (325).
2. **Truly man, with a true body** — the creed's "was made man"; Chalcedon's "consubstantial with us".
3. **With a rational soul** — Chalcedon, after Constantinople I's canon 1.
4. **One and the same**, so Mary is *Theotokos* — Ephesus (431), restated at Chalcedon.
5. **Perfect in manhood after the union** — Chalcedon, against Eutyches.
6. **Two natural wills and operations** — Constantinople III (681).

The Catechism gathers the same core under "True God and true man" (CCC 464-469)
and "How is the Son of God man?" (CCC 470-478). Its opening move: Christ is not
part God and part man, nor a confused mixture (CCC 464, paraphrased). Each clause
is the negative image of an error — a fence post where someone went over. The
*explanations* of how the union works are Module 3's business and mostly lower on
the ladder.

*Introduced:* [1.2](lessons/01-02-true-god-and-true-man.md)

### The map of christological errors

**Four ways to go wrong, and only four.** Divide the *who* (Nestorianism,
adoptionism); fuse the *whats* (Eutychianism, and its cousin monothelitism);
subtract from the divine *what* (Arianism); subtract from the human *what*
(Docetism, Apollinarianism). The *who* can only be got wrong by multiplying it;
the *whats* by merging or shaving. Each move was made by someone protecting
something true — unity, transcendence, sinlessness.

Chalcedon's preamble draws the same map itself: those who would rend the mystery
into "a Duad of Sons"; who make the Godhead passible; who imagine "a mixture or
confusion"; who say the servant's form was "of an heavenly" substance; who say
"two natures before the union, one after" (Percival). The two approaches have
characteristic drifts: from below, carelessly, toward a doubled *who*; from above,
carelessly, toward a thinned humanity.

## The defining texts

One entry per text, in the wording the lessons verified. Quotations are from
Percival (*NPNF* 2.14), Schaff's *Creeds of Christendom* or Waterworth's Trent;
anathemas are described, not quoted at length. The creed's clauses sorted into
"as God" and "as man" are not tabulated here — that sorting is reserved work.

### The creed and its for us

The creed of 325, as recited in its 381 form, is the charter: of the Son it says
"very God of very God, begotten not made, being of one substance with the Father",
and that he "for us men and for our salvation came down ... and was incarnate ...
and was made man" (Percival). The Incarnation is never stated there without its
*for us*. Nicaea's anathemas are part of the definition and carry the Christological
weight: they forbid calling the Son a creature, made from nothing, or *trepton e
alloioton* (see [there was when he was not](#there-was-when-he-was-not)). Nicaea
says **nothing** about a human soul.

*Used at:* [1.2](lessons/01-02-true-god-and-true-man.md), [1.3](lessons/01-03-why-the-incarnation.md), [1.4](lessons/01-04-arius-and-the-divinity-of-the-son.md)

### What Ephesus did

**A judgment of consonance, not a new formula.** At Session I (22 June 431) the
creed of Nicaea was recited, Cyril's Second Letter to Nestorius read, and the
bishops asked one by one whether it agreed with Nicaea; they said it did, and
Nestorius's reply failed the same test. The council then **deposed Nestorius**.
Canon 7 forbade composing a *heteran pistin*, a different faith, as a rival to
Nicaea's. There is **no separate Ephesine definition of *Theotokos***: the title,
already in use (Alexander of Alexandria, about 320), was confirmed by approving
the letter that defends it. The first definition text to name Mary "Mother of God"
is Chalcedon's.

The Second Letter's crux line: "the Scripture has not said that the Word united
to himself the person of man, but that he was made flesh" (Percival; "persons"
there renders *prosopa*). A union of *prosopon* joins two who are each already
someone.

*Defined at:* [2.2](lessons/02-02-nestorius-cyril-and-ephesus.md); the texts as texts are
[`patristics` 5.1](../patristics/lessons/05-01-cyril-of-alexandria.md)

### The Twelve Anathemas

Cyril's Third Letter to Nestorius (430), with twelve anathematisms attached; the
twelfth confesses that the Word of God suffered in the flesh. **Standing
disputed.** Read at Ephesus Session I; the Acts record **no vote** on it, and
Percival notes that something seems to have dropped out of the record at the
deposition. Petavius, Tillemont and Hefele held the council approved it; the
silence of the Acts is the other side. Chalcedon received Cyril's synodical letters
"to Nestorius and the Easterns" — the two read aloud there were the Second Letter
and the letter to John of Antioch, and whether that reception covers the Third is
argued. Constantinople II later condemned Theodoret's writings against "St. Cyril
and his XII. Anathemas" and defends the Twelve in capitulum 13. **Rule:** calling
each anathema an Ephesine definition is an overstatement; name the later acts
instead.

*Introduced:* [2.2](lessons/02-02-nestorius-cyril-and-ephesus.md)

### Formula of Reunion

**The first sentence a Chalcedonian could sign as it stands.** Drafted on the
Antiochene side, sent by John of Antioch in 433, accepted by Cyril and set verbatim
in his letter to John ("Let the heavens rejoice"). It confesses "perfect God, and
perfect Man", consubstantial with us according to his humanity, "a union of two
natures. Wherefore we confess one Christ, one Son, one Lord", and *Theotokos* in
the next sentence. Antioch got two complete natures; Cyril got one subject and the
title.

*Introduced:* [2.2](lessons/02-02-nestorius-cyril-and-ephesus.md)

### The Chalcedonian Definition

Chalcedon, Session V, **22 October 451** — the council's own *horos*, defined
dogma. The bishops wanted no new text; the imperial commissioners insisted, because
Dioscorus accepted "of two natures" but not that there *were* two. A committee
redrafted it. Percival's English of the core:

> one and the same [Person], that he is perfect in Godhead and perfect in manhood, very God and very man, of a reasonable soul and [human] body consisting, consubstantial with the Father as touching his Godhead, and consubstantial with us as touching his manhood

It goes on: like us in all things, sin only excepted; begotten of the Father before
the ages as to the Godhead, born of the Virgin Mary, the **Mother of God**, as to
the manhood; one and the same Christ "in two natures", with the
[four adverbs](#the-four-adverbs); the distinction of natures not taken away by the
union, the **property of each nature preserved**, concurring in "one Person and one
Subsistence" (*prosopon* and *hypostasis* side by side), not divided into two
persons. Schaff prints *ton auton*, "the same", **eight** times in the Greek core
sentence (Latin *eundem* eight times too); English flattens it. The preamble names
its targets — the Duad of Sons, mixture or confusion, heavenly flesh — and calls
Leo's Tome agreeable to Peter's confession.

**What it did not say.** No positive model of the union; all four adverbs are
negatives. It left open whether a humanity without its own hypostasis is impersonal
([2.4](lessons/02-04-the-three-chapters-and-constantinople-ii.md)), whether two
natures mean two wills ([2.5](lessons/02-05-monothelitism-and-constantinople-iii.md)),
and what "person" is metaphysically ([3.1](lessons/03-01-person-and-nature-the-hypostatic-union.md)).
It received Cyril's letters and Leo's Tome but did not adopt "one incarnate nature".
Constantinople II later fixed its reading: capitula 5-7 anathematize taking it in a
Nestorian sense.

**Levels around it.** The Definition: rung 1. The Tome: received as a "common
pillar", but not every sentence defined. The Session II acclamation ("Peter has
spoken thus through Leo"): not a doctrinal act. The *en*/*ek* reading: a textual
question.

*Defined at:* [2.3](lessons/02-03-eutyches-leo-and-chalcedon.md); read as the dogmatic core at
[1.2](lessons/01-02-true-god-and-true-man.md); its later reading at
[2.4](lessons/02-04-the-three-chapters-and-constantinople-ii.md); its non-reception at
[6.3](lessons/06-03-the-churches-that-did-not-receive-chalcedon.md)

### The four adverbs

*Asynchytos, atreptos, adiairetos, achoristos* — in Schaff's English
**inconfusedly, unchangeably, indivisibly, inseparably**: how the one Christ is
acknowledged "in two natures". They come as a **pair of pairs**: the first two
guard the natures' distinctness (no merging, no turning of one into the other),
the last two guard the subject's unity (no holding the natures apart as two).
Every one is a **negative**: the council fences the mystery and does not map its
inside. Constantinople III carries the same four across to the two wills.
(Mapping each adverb to its controversy and to the Definition's other words that do
the same work is reserved.)

*Introduced:* [2.3](lessons/02-03-eutyches-leo-and-chalcedon.md)

### In two natures and of two natures

*En dyo physesin* says what Christ **is** now; *ek dyo physeon* says whence he is,
and is compatible with one nature afterwards (bread is "of" flour and water).
Dioscorus accepted "of two" while denying two natures — which is why the council
needed "in". **The text strains:** the Greek as transmitted reads *ek*; the old
Latin reads *in duabus naturis*. Hefele, followed by Schaff, argues *en* is
original (the Session V debate, Euthymius, Severus's complaint, Evagrius, Leontius,
every Latin version); Baur and Dorner held *ek*. The majority view is *en*, as a
**scholarly judgment**. The doctrine does not hang on it: "the property of each
nature being preserved" excludes one nature after the union either way.
Constantinople II later accepted both expressions, rightly understood.

*Introduced:* [2.3](lessons/02-03-eutyches-leo-and-chalcedon.md)

### The Three Chapters

**Three bodies of Antiochene material, condemned at Constantinople II (553) to show
Chalcedon was Cyrillian.** Justinian's strategy: the miaphysites cited these texts
as Chalcedon's real sympathies, so condemn them and keep the council. The chapters:
the person and writings of **Theodore of Mopsuestia** (Nestorius's teacher, who died
in communion in 428); certain writings of **Theodoret of Cyrrhus** (against Cyril,
his Twelve Chapters and Ephesus, and in defence of Theodore and Nestorius); and the
**letter of Ibas of Edessa to Maris**, which called Cyril an Apollinarian. Chalcedon
had received Theodoret and Ibas once each anathematized Nestorius. The council's
sentence itself distinguishes condemning a man together with his writings from
condemning only writings; which falls where is set out in
[2.4](lessons/02-04-the-three-chapters-and-constantinople-ii.md)'s table of the
capitula (12-14). Vigilius's resistance and surrender are
[`church-history` 2.2](../church-history/lessons/02-02-ephesus-chalcedon-and-leos-rome.md)'s.

**Level:** a judgment that particular texts teach error is a **dogmatic fact**
(rung 2), not a verdict on anyone's eternal fate; whether the excerpts represent
Theodore fairly is a historian's question. The strategy failed: the miaphysites
were not won back.

*Introduced:* [2.4](lessons/02-04-the-three-chapters-and-constantinople-ii.md)

### The capitula of Constantinople II

Fourteen anathematizing chapters (Percival's numbering). **1-9** state doctrine,
**10-11** the theopaschite formula and the list of condemned heretics (Arius to
Eutyches, and Origen), **12-14** the Three Chapters. The load-bearing ones:

- **3** — one and the same person works the miracles and bears the sufferings "which of his own will he endured in the flesh"; the "wonder-working Word" and "the Christ that suffered" are not two.
- **4** — "union" has many meanings; mixture (Apollinaris, Eutyches) and a merely "relative union" (Theodore, Nestorius) are both rejected. The Church confesses the union "synthetically, that is to say, hypostatically" (Percival).
- **5** — the one hypostasis is the Word's; the Trinity received no added person.
- **6** — Mary truly Mother of God; *Christotokos* condemned only "as if Christ were not God".
- **7** — "in two natures" as a difference taken "in a theoretical manner", never to number persons.
- **8** — "one incarnate nature of God the Word" accepted as hypostatic union; condemned as one mixed nature.
- **9** — one adoration of the Word with his flesh.
- **10** — the [theopaschite formula](#the-theopaschite-formula).
- **12** — Theodore's Christ, vexed by passions, improving by degrees, sinless only after the resurrection.

It **receives** Chalcedon and condemns Eutyches again; it fixed how Chalcedon is
read, not what it says.

*Defined at:* [2.4](lessons/02-04-the-three-chapters-and-constantinople-ii.md); used at
[3.1](lessons/03-01-person-and-nature-the-hypostatic-union.md), [3.4](lessons/03-04-the-grace-of-the-head.md)

### The theopaschite formula

**"One of the Trinity suffered in the flesh."** Capitulum 10's own verb is
*crucified*: anathema to whoever does not confess that the one "crucified in the
flesh" is "true God and the Lord of Glory and one of the Holy Trinity" (Percival).
The "sufferings ... in the flesh" wording is capitulum 3's. Read for **Christ**: a
subject named from the divine side takes a human predicate, located "in the flesh" —
the [communication of idioms](#communication-of-idioms) made conciliar. Read for
**God**, it is [`trinity-and-god` 1.5](../trinity-and-god/lessons/01-05-impassibility-and-the-god-who-suffers.md)'s:
the suffering is not in the divine nature. Pope Hormisdas had declined the formula
as unnecessary and dangerous; Percival's note records that the Fifth Council
"directly approved" it. **Defined dogma.**

**Contrast case:** Peter the Fuller's addition of "who was crucified for us" to the
Trisagion, condemned at the Council in Trullo (692), canon 81. John of Damascus reads
the hymn as addressed to the whole Trinity, so the addition makes the Trinity
passible (*Exposition* III.10). Nearly the same words, different subject; addressed
to Christ it could bear a good sense.

*Introduced:* [2.4](lessons/02-04-the-three-chapters-and-constantinople-ii.md); its grammar at
[3.2](lessons/03-02-the-communication-of-idioms.md)

### Two natural wills and operations

Constantinople III (7 November 680 - 16 September 681), Definition read at
Session XVIII. It repeats Chalcedon almost word for word, then adds (Percival):

> two natural wills and two natural operations indivisibly, inconvertibly, inseparably, inconfusedly ... not contrary the one to the other ... but his human will follows ... as subject to his divine and omnipotent will

and that the natural will of his flesh "is called and is the proper will of God
the Word", "although deified, was not suppressed, but was rather preserved".
Four points: **"natural"** counts wills by nature; the **adverbs** carry
Chalcedon over to powers; **"not contrary"** — two is not conflict; **"follows ...
subject ... preserved"** — the human will really acts, ordered to the divine
without absorption. The **"proper will of God the Word"** is the *who* clause.
The same Definition calls monothelitism a revival of Apollinaris, a flesh with a
rational soul emptied of will. Confirmed by Pope Leo II. **Defined dogma.** Only
the word "natural" entered the text; Maximus's analysis of *gnome* did not.

*Defined at:* [2.5](lessons/02-05-monothelitism-and-constantinople-iii.md); read in act at
[4.1](lessons/04-01-christs-human-will-in-act.md)

### Trent Session VI chapter 7

The decree on justification lists its causes. The **meritorious cause** is Christ,
who "merited Justification for us by His most holy Passion on the wood of the
cross, and made satisfaction for us unto God the Father" (Waterworth). Canon 10
anathematizes saying men are just without the justice of Christ "whereby He merited
for us to be justified" — so **merit** is guarded by a canon and is defined;
**satisfaction** stands in the chapter only, taught by an ecumenical council and
commonly ranked of faith, with **no mechanism fixed**. Canon 11 anathematizes
justification by the *sole* imputation of Christ's justice. Session V's decree on
original sin (third canon) teaches that Christ reconciled us to God in his blood;
Session XXII ch. 1, that he offered himself once on the cross to the Father.

[5.3](lessons/05-03-satisfaction-anselm-and-aquinas.md), [5.4](lessons/05-04-penal-substitution-and-the-catholic-assessment.md)

## The Incarnation: why, and who

### Why the Word became flesh

**The Creed's "for us men and for our salvation"** — defined. The Catechism gathers
four purposes under this heading (CCC 456-460): to save us by reconciling us with
God; that we might know God's love (1 John 4:9); to be our model of holiness
(Matthew 11:29); to make us "partakers of the divine nature" (2 Peter 1:4). The last
is the deepest, and the first three serve it. The purposes keep the weight of their
sources, not of the Catechism. "Mainly an example" shrinks four to one — the shape
of Pelagianism.

*Introduced:* [1.3](lessons/01-03-why-the-incarnation.md)

### Deification

**Sharing by grace what the Son is by nature.** 2 Peter 1:4; CCC 460, which cites
Athanasius: "He was made man that we might be made God" (*On the Incarnation* 54.3,
Robertson); ST III q.1 a.2 credits a similar line to Augustine. Not pantheism: the
two words *by grace* block it. Scriptural and confessed; *how* it is explained
belongs to the schools. See [only God can deify](#only-god-can-deify).

*Introduced:* [1.3](lessons/01-03-why-the-incarnation.md)

### Fittingness

*Convenientia*: **showing why a freely done act is right, without claiming the doer
was forced.** It runs one way only — from the revealed fact to its reasons, never
from reasons to the fact. ST III q.1 a.1: goodness communicates itself, so the
highest good fittingly communicates itself in the highest way, by joining a created
nature to itself in one person; nothing changes in God. **Common teaching**, not
proof and not decoration. "Fitting" does not mean "probably true", and it does not
mean "necessary for God" — that road ends in the pantheism *Dei Filius* condemns.

*Introduced:* [1.3](lessons/01-03-why-the-incarnation.md)

### Two kinds of necessity

ST III q.1 a.2: a thing is necessary to an end either **as food** ("the end cannot
be without it") or **as a horse** (the end "is attained better and more
conveniently"). The Incarnation was necessary for our restoration only the second
way: God "could have restored human nature in many other ways". Reply 2 adds that
first-kind necessity holds only *relative to* condign satisfaction. So the sceptic's
"couldn't God just forgive?" is granted, and the answer is what the chosen way
gives that a decree lacks. Anselm, who held a God-man strictly required, takes the
other route ([5.3](lessons/05-03-satisfaction-anselm-and-aquinas.md)); that
question is **freely disputed**.

*Introduced:* [1.3](lessons/01-03-why-the-incarnation.md)

### The motive of the Incarnation

**Would the Word have come had Adam not sinned? Freely disputed** — no council,
pope or Catechism paragraph has settled it. *Thomist* (ST III q.1 a.3): since
Scripture everywhere assigns sin as the reason, say "seemingly" it was a remedy for
sin — while God's power is not limited to this. *Scotist* (Scotus, *Ordinatio* III
d.7 q.3, the standard locus): a wise will wills the greater good first, so Christ
was predestined prior to any foreseen fall; the fall fixes only the mode (passible
flesh); leaning on Colossians 1:15-20 and Ephesians 1:4, 1:9-10 — the **absolute
primacy of Christ**. Both schools can sing the Exsultet's "O happy fault"; a
liturgical text both sing settles nothing. Calling either view heresy or dogma is a
level error. (The crux between them is reserved.)

*Introduced:* [1.3](lessons/01-03-why-the-incarnation.md)

### Homoousios

**"Of one substance" with the Father** — Nicaea (325), defined dogma. In Christology
it rules that Christ's weaknesses **cannot be the Word's as God**. It says nothing
about whether he has a human mind — which is why Apollinaris, a staunch Nicene,
could deny one. Its Trinitarian history is
[`trinity-and-god` 3.1](../trinity-and-god/lessons/03-01-nicaea.md).

*Introduced:* [1.4](lessons/01-04-arius-and-the-divinity-of-the-son.md)

### There was when he was not

Nicaea's first anathema, *en pote hote ouk en*. The Greek has no word for time;
Schaff's "there was a time when he was not" adds one. The anathemas also condemn
calling the Son *trepton e alloioton*, "changeable or alterable" *(lesson's
translation)* — the clause that carries Christological weight, since it strikes the
conclusion of [the Arian argument from weakness](#the-arian-argument-from-weakness)
without saying which premise was false.

*Introduced:* [1.4](lessons/01-04-arius-and-the-divinity-of-the-son.md); see
[`trinity-and-god` 3.1](../trinity-and-god/lessons/03-01-nicaea.md)

### Only God can deify

**Athanasius's soteriological argument for the Son's divinity:** a creature cannot
join creatures to God, so man had not been deified unless the Son were true God
(*Orations against the Arians* II.69-70). The divine half of the logic whose human
half is [the soteriological rule](#the-soteriological-rule). Read as a text at
[`patristics` 3.1](../patristics/lessons/03-01-athanasius.md).

*Introduced:* [1.4](lessons/01-04-arius-and-the-divinity-of-the-son.md)

### The soteriological rule

**"What is not assumed is not healed."** Gregory of Nazianzus, Letter 101 to
Cledonius (*NPNF* 2.7): "that which He has not assumed He has not healed". The
argument around it: if only "half Adam" fell, half may be saved, but the whole fell;
if the mind is left out because it is liable to condemnation, so is the flesh — the
objection proves too much; and a mindless Christ gives "an excuse for them who sin
with the mind". Premises: the Word heals by uniting nature to himself; what is not
united is not healed; the whole nature, mind included, fell; so the whole must be
assumed. **The weak premise** is the second — that assumption is *the* way of
healing — and it rests on the Fathers' account of salvation as divinization.
Ancestor: Irenaeus's recapitulation ([`patristics` 2.2](../patristics/lessons/02-02-irenaeus-recapitulation.md)).

**Engine of Module 2:** run on the mind (Apollinaris), on the union (Nestorius:
a man merely accompanied is not assumed), on the nature (Eutyches: a humanity
absorbed is not healed), on the will (the will fell first, so it must be assumed —
John of Damascus, *Exposition* III.14). Its object is **nature, not defect**: sin
is a wound, not a part, so a sinless mind is a whole mind. Whether the Word assumed
a *fallen* nature is a question it raises and does not settle.

**Level:** a patristic axiom, **rung 4**, never itself defined; the conclusions it
served are dogma. (The "mind sinned first" premise is not in Letter 101.)

*Introduced:* [2.1](lessons/02-01-apollinaris-a-complete-humanity.md); cited at
[1.2](lessons/01-02-true-god-and-true-man.md), [2.2](lessons/02-02-nestorius-cyril-and-ephesus.md),
[2.5](lessons/02-05-monothelitism-and-constantinople-iii.md)

### Nous

The **rational mind** — the self that knows, chooses and steers — the top part of
the body / animal soul / *nous* trichotomy of 1 Thessalonians 5:23. Apollinaris put
the Word in its place. The dogma is a *reasonable* soul (Chalcedon); "Jesus had a
soul, but God did his thinking" is Apollinarianism word for word. The trichotomy
itself is an anthropology, at no level.

*Introduced:* [2.1](lessons/02-01-apollinaris-a-complete-humanity.md)

### Logos sarx Christology

**"Word-flesh"**: the Word takes a body and is itself its soul or mind. It is the
premise the [Arian argument](#the-arian-argument-from-weakness) needs (Theodoret,
*Eranistes* II, reports "a body only" as the view of Arius and Eunomius; Arius's own
view is debated). Grillmeier (*Christ in Christian Tradition*, vol. 1) paired it
with **Logos-anthropos**, "Word-man": the Word unites a complete man to himself.
Word-flesh is broadly Alexandrian and secures unity (extreme: Apollinaris);
Word-man is broadly Antiochene (Diodore, Theodore) and secures completeness (risk:
two subjects). Useful and too neat: Grillmeier put Athanasius on the Word-flesh
side, but the *Tome to the Antiochenes* (362, §7) records agreement that the
Saviour's body was not without soul, sense or intelligence — what that meant in 362
is disputed. Use the frameworks as tendencies, not teams.

*Introduced:* [1.4](lessons/01-04-arius-and-the-divinity-of-the-son.md); developed at
[2.1](lessons/02-01-apollinaris-a-complete-humanity.md)

### Theotokos

**God-bearer, Mother of God: a statement about who her son is.** The one Mary bore
is the Word, according to the flesh; it does not say Godhead began in her (Cyril
denies that in so many words). **Defined dogma**, via Ephesus's approval of Cyril's
Second Letter and named in Chalcedon's Definition. The over-correction — "mother of
the divinity" — moves the predicate from person to nature. Constantinople II
(capitulum 6) condemns *Christotokos* only "as if Christ were not God" — which is
why the Marian titles of the Church of the East could later be discussed at all
([6.3](lessons/06-03-the-churches-that-did-not-receive-chalcedon.md)). The Marian
dogmas are `ecclesiology-and-mariology`'s ([syllabus](../ecclesiology-and-mariology/syllabus.md)).

*Introduced:* [2.2](lessons/02-02-nestorius-cyril-and-ephesus.md); in the core at
[1.2](lessons/01-02-true-god-and-true-man.md); in the ecumenical texts at
[6.3](lessons/06-03-the-churches-that-did-not-receive-chalcedon.md)

### Christotokos

**Christ-bearer**, Nestorius's preferred title. True as far as it goes; heretical
when offered as a *replacement* for *Theotokos* in order to avoid saying the one
born is God.

## The union and its vocabulary

### Hypostatic union

**One *who*, two *whats*: the two natures meet in the person of the Word and
nowhere else.** Not in the *what* — divinity and humanity are not blended into a
third kind (Eutyches). Not beside the *who* — they are not two someones joined by
love, honour or will (Nestorius). One somebody, the eternal Word, now exists in two
ways of being, each complete. Cyril's union *kath' hypostasin* (the Word himself
became man, rather than hosting a man); Chalcedon's "one Person and one
Subsistence"; Constantinople II capitula 4-5, "synthetically, that is to say,
hypostatically", with no added hypostasis. **Defined dogma.**

How it works on each question the course asks:

- **Every way of making one *nature* out of two fails** (ST III q.2 a.1): juxtaposition gives an accidental unity; mixture changes both and the finite is swallowed; composition of parts gives a *tertium quid*. What remains is one *subject* possessing two.
- **Grace:** the [grace of union](#grace-of-union) is the hypostatic union seen as unmerited gift — not a large dose of habitual grace.
- **Wills and knowing:** counted by nature; willers and knowers by person — two wills, one who wills.
- **Death:** divides the natures' parts in place, never the hypostasis ([the union in death](#the-union-in-death)).
- **After the ascension:** never undone — "whose kingdom shall have no end".
- **Modern restatements:** the clause redefinition empties. "True God" read as God's presence in "a human person" keeps the words and divides the *who* (the CDF's Haight notification, 2004).

Chalcedon states the union negatively; [3.1](lessons/03-01-person-and-nature-the-hypostatic-union.md)
states it positively. The *analysis* — Boethian person, relation of reason, grace
of union — is the schools' (rung 4).

### Person and nature

**Nature answers "what"; person answers "who".** You can list everything human about
Socrates and still not have said *which one* he is. A nature is the essence the
definition signifies; a person, in Boethius's definition, is "an individual
substance of a rational nature", which Aquinas reads as **what exists by itself**,
not in something more perfect (ST III q.2 a.2 ad 3). Socrates's hand is real,
individual and human, and not a person; Christ's individual, rational human nature
likewise is not a person, because it exists in the Person of the Word. Whatever
belongs to a person is united *in* the person (q.2 a.2); the human nature is not
the Word's by nature, so it is united in the person.

**The vocabulary drift, 325-451.** *Ousia* and *hypostasis* were synonyms at
Nicaea; the Cappadocians made *hypostasis* "which one"; Cyril's *physis* could name
the concrete individual; *prosopon* (face, role) sounded thin to Cyril. By 451 the
words split into a *what* column (*ousia*, *physis*, nature) and a *who* column
(*hypostasis*, *prosopon*, person). Cyril's "one nature" belongs to the older
usage — the long root of the non-Chalcedonian schism. Latin wrinkle: Boethius pairs
*hypostasis* with *substantia*; the conciliar tradition and Aquinas render it
*subsistentia*, hence Schaff's "one Person and one Subsistence".

**Level:** that there is one person and no human hypostasis is defined; the
Boethian analysis is common teaching. The philosophy of persons is
[`metaphysics` 6.5](../metaphysics/lessons/06-05-what-a-person-is.md)'s.

*Introduced:* [3.1](lessons/03-01-person-and-nature-the-hypostatic-union.md)

### Enhypostasia

**Christ's humanity is not a hypostasis of its own, nor without subsistence: it
subsists in the Word's.** The answer to a real dilemma: a complete human nature
seems to make a human hypostasis (Nestorius), and denying that seems to leave the
humanity *anhypostatos*, unreal. John of Damascus, *Exposition* III.9: the flesh
"became rather a subsistence which subsisted in another". Aquinas: the humanity
lacks its own personality **by addition, not by loss** (ST III q.4 a.2 ad 2);
nothing of human perfection is missing. There was never a man called Jesus whom the
Word then adopted (q.4 a.2).

**Historical caution:** associated with Leontius of Byzantium, but Daley
(*Leontius of Byzantium: Complete Works*, 2017) argues that Leontius's *enhypostatos*
mostly means "really existing", the "in-subsistent" gloss owing much to
nineteenth-century scholarship. On that reading the classic statement is John's.
**Level:** common teaching, an explanation — not a defined term.

*Introduced:* [2.4](lessons/02-04-the-three-chapters-and-constantinople-ii.md); built on at
[3.1](lessons/03-01-person-and-nature-the-hypostatic-union.md)

### Grace of union

**The humanity's being the Word's own, called grace only because nothing earned
it.** "The personal being that is given gratis from above to the human nature in
the Person of the Word" (ST III q.6 a.6). Human nature is lifted to God two ways: by
**operation**, as the saints know and love God, or by **personal being**, which
belongs to Christ alone (q.2 a.10). Habits perfect powers for acting, and the union
is neither an act nor a power, so the grace of union **cannot be a habit**. It is
"not ordained to an act, but to the personal being" (q.8 a.5 ad 3); infinite, as
the person is (q.7 a.11); preceded by no merit (q.2 a.11). Habitual grace follows
it as its effect. It is incommunicable: we receive a share of the habit, never the
fount. **Level:** common teaching.

*Introduced:* [3.1](lessons/03-01-person-and-nature-the-hypostatic-union.md); developed at
[3.4](lessons/03-04-the-grace-of-the-head.md)

### One esse or two

**Does the assumed humanity bring its own act of existing? Freely disputed.**
- *ST III q.17 a.2:* *esse* belongs to the hypostasis as "that which has being"; one hypostasis, one *esse*; by the assumption no new personal *esse* accrued, "only a new relation of the pre-existing personal being to the human nature".
- *De unione Verbi incarnati* a.4: one *esse* simply, plus another *esse* of that subject insofar as it is made man in time — not accidental, but "secondary" *(lesson's translation)*. Whether this contradicts, refines or predates q.17 is argued; the relative dating is disputed.
- *The schools:* Cajetan (personhood as a substantial mode that the humanity lacks; one *esse*); Billot (personhood tied to *esse*); some modern Thomists (a created secondary *esse*); Scotus (no real distinction of essence and *esse*; the nature has its own existence; personality a negation — not dependent on another subsisting subject).

Each side has a real worry: one uncreated *esse* actualizing a creature strains
"without confusion"; two strains "one hypostasis". **Both affirm every word of
Chalcedon.** Calling either a matter of faith is the overstatement. The metaphysics
of *esse* is [`thomistic-synthesis` 2.2](../thomistic-synthesis/lessons/02-02-esse-and-essence-the-real-distinction.md)'s.

*Introduced:* [3.1](lessons/03-01-person-and-nature-the-hypostatic-union.md)

### Neo-Chalcedonianism

**Historians' label for the sixth-century Cyrillian reading of Chalcedon ratified at
Constantinople II:** one hypostasis = the Word's (capitulum 5); "in two natures" as
difference only (7); "one incarnate nature" accepted as hypostatic union (8); "one
of the Trinity crucified" (10); union "synthetically, that is, hypostatically" (4).
Contested: Grillmeier (*Christ in Christian Tradition*, vol. 2) uses it,
distinguishing moderate and stricter forms; critics say Chalcedon, having received
Cyril, was only being made explicit. That is a judgment about 451, not 553.
**No doctrinal level.**

*Introduced:* [2.4](lessons/02-04-the-three-chapters-and-constantinople-ii.md)

## The grammar of the union

### Communication of idioms

**Properties of either nature are said of the one person, under any name for the
person — never traded between the natures.** *Idiomata*, properties. John of
Damascus, *Exposition* III.4, gives three moves: the **ban** — we do not say "His
divinity is subject to passion or created", nor that "His flesh or His humanity is
uncreated"; the **licence** — a name for the *subsistence*, even one drawn from one
nature only, takes the properties of both; and the **rider** — the Lord of glory is
crucified "not in respect of His being God but in respect of His being at the same
time man" (Salmond). Paul's phrase is 1 Corinthians 2:8.

Aquinas's form (ST III q.16):

1. Concrete terms stand for the hypostasis; abstract terms signify the nature (a.5): "of concrete words we may predicate indifferently what belongs to either nature".
2. The person is one; the natures two and unconfused.
3. So human properties are truly said of God, and divine ones of this man (a.4) — not a figure of speech.
4. Never of the other nature in the abstract (a.5; a.5 ad 1).
5. Every predicate carries its "by reason of": one subject, two reasons.
6. Refinements: [the qualification rule](#the-qualification-rule) (a.8) and [reduplication](#reduplication) (aa.10-12).
7. "God was made man" involves no change in God: the relation is new in the creature (a.6).

**Level:** that one person is the subject of both sets of predicates is **defined**
(Ephesus's *Theotokos*, Chalcedon's "one and the same", Constantinople II
capitulum 10; Cyril's twelfth anathematism, standing disputed). The logical
machinery — supposition, the qualification rule, the reduplication verdicts — is
**common teaching**, never defined. Denying that God died in the flesh sets you
against a council; preferring another grammar for "as man" sets you against Aquinas.

**Where it shows up across the course:** Theotokos parsed ([2.2](lessons/02-02-nestorius-cyril-and-ephesus.md));
the theopaschite formula ([2.4](lessons/02-04-the-three-chapters-and-constantinople-ii.md));
"the Son of God willed to eat" is true, false "as God"
([2.5](lessons/02-05-monothelitism-and-constantinople-iii.md)); kenoticism makes
lowly predicates true of the divine nature in the abstract ([3.3](lessons/03-03-kenosis.md));
whether "the Son, as man, saw" can be said as "the Son suffered in the flesh" is
([4.3](lessons/04-03-the-beatific-vision-in-christ.md)); the satisfaction's infinite
worth comes from the Person, not a divinized flesh ([5.3](lessons/05-03-satisfaction-anselm-and-aquinas.md));
"the man Jesus was punished, the eternal Son untouched" splits the sufferer
([5.4](lessons/05-04-penal-substitution-and-the-catholic-assessment.md)); burial and
descent both said of the Son of God ([6.1](lessons/06-01-he-descended-into-hell.md));
omnipresence said of Christ as God, not of his humanity as such
([6.2](lessons/06-02-resurrection-and-ascension-as-doctrine.md)).

### Concrete and abstract terms

**Concrete terms** — God, the Word, the Son of God, Christ, Jesus, this man, the
Lord of glory — **stand for the hypostasis** and take predicates of both natures.
**Abstract terms** — divinity, Godhead, humanity, flesh — **signify a nature** and
take only that nature's own (ST III q.16 a.5, with a.5 ad 1): "God was hungry"
is true, "the divinity was hungry" false. In death: "the Son of God lay in
the tomb" is true, "the divinity lay in the tomb" false. The same logic separates
*totus* from *totum*: the whole Christ (the person) was in the tomb and in hell;
not the whole *of* Christ (all his parts) in either (q.52 a.3).

*Introduced:* [3.2](lessons/03-02-the-communication-of-idioms.md); applied at
[6.1](lessons/06-01-he-descended-into-hell.md)

### The qualification rule

**A true predicate that could be heard as belonging to the divine person in himself,
or as denying the humanity, is said only with its qualifier.** ST III q.16 a.8.
Because Arius called the Son a creature, "Christ is a creature" is not said
absolutely but "in his human nature"; conversely "Christ is impassible" needs "in
his Godhead", or the Manichees' phantom body walks back in (a.8 ad 2). "Christ
suffered, died and was buried" is said flatly, since nobody hears it as a claim
about the Godhead. Aquinas cites Jerome: careless words lead to heresy. **Truth is
necessary; unambiguity is also required.** Grading a sentence means grading how it
will be heard. Level: common teaching.

*Introduced:* [3.2](lessons/03-02-the-communication-of-idioms.md)

### Reduplication

**"As God" / "as man" names the nature in virtue of which a predicate is true of the
one subject.** The seed is Athanasius on Gethsemane: "it was not the Word, considered
as the Word, who wept and was troubled, but it was proper to the flesh" (*Orations*
III.56) — same subject, weakness his as man.

Aquinas's refinement (ST III q.16 aa.10-12): the reduplicated term can signify the
nature or the person, and **by default signifies the nature**, because it works
like a predicate. So "Christ as man is God" is to be denied rather than granted
(a.11); a demonstrative — "as *this* man" — pulls the term back onto the person
(a.10, a.11 ad 3); a.12 treats "as man is a person". Related: "*This man* began to
be" is false (a.9), but "Christ began to be man" is true (a.9 ad 3). In the
mysteries: Christ ascended as man as to the condition of the one ascending, but by
the power of his Godhead as cause (q.57 a.2); he was raised by God as man and rose
by his own power as God (q.53 a.4). **Level:** the verdicts are the schools', and
"rather granted" / "rather denied" are Aquinas's hedges, not definitions.

*Introduced:* [1.4](lessons/01-04-arius-and-the-divinity-of-the-son.md); owned by
[3.2](lessons/03-02-the-communication-of-idioms.md); in the mysteries at
[2.2](lessons/02-02-nestorius-cyril-and-ephesus.md), [6.2](lessons/06-02-resurrection-and-ascension-as-doctrine.md)

### Kenosis

**The Word emptied himself by *taking*, not by *losing*.** Philippians 2:7, *heauton
ekenosen*, "emptied himself" (Vulgate *exinanivit*). The verb has **no genitive**:
Paul never says what the Son emptied himself *of*. The next words, *morphen doulou
labon*, "taking the form of a servant", say *how*. The patristic rule: the emptying
**is** the assumption. Leo's Tome, ch. 3: "the bending down of pity, not the failing
of power" (*NPNF* 2.12). Augustine: by taking the servant's form he did not lose
God's (*De Trinitate* II.1.2; VII.3.5). Cyril's Third Letter uses the word and
confesses the Word unchangeable in the same sentence. Chalcedon writes it in:
"immutably", properties preserved.

**Hidden is true; laid aside is false.** The divinity's glory was not manifested in
the flesh except at moments like the Transfiguration. The analogical uses of
"kenosis" — Balthasar's grounding of the Son's self-emptying in the Father's
self-gift, John Paul II's "God's kenosis" (*Fides et Ratio* §93) — do not claim
the divine nature lost anything; an analogy becomes the doctrine only if the
predicate is said properly of the divine nature. **Levels:** no change or loss of
divinity, defined; the patristic reading, the Fathers' consensus; *harpagmos*
exegesis and Balthasar's Trinitarian kenosis, freely disputed. (The readings of
*harpagmos* and their commitments are reserved; the exegesis is
[`new-testament`](../new-testament/syllabus.md)'s.)

*Introduced:* [3.3](lessons/03-03-kenosis.md)

## Christ's grace, will and mind

### Habitual grace of Christ

**The created holiness of Christ's human soul** — needed because the soul is divine
only by participation, not by essence (ST III q.7 a.1 ad 1); the humanity is an
instrument, but a living one that acts. It **follows the union by nature, not in
time**, as light follows the sun (q.7 a.13): the Son's mission is prior to the
Spirit's, God's presence causes grace, and acting presupposes the acting
hypostasis. Finite in being, **full in the nature of grace** and incapable of
increase (q.7 aa.11-12). Fullness of grace *itself* is proper to Christ; Mary and
Stephen are "full of grace" on the part of the subject, with all their office
requires (q.7 a.10 ad 1). **Thomist, rung 4.**

*Introduced:* [3.4](lessons/03-04-the-grace-of-the-head.md)

### Capital grace

**Christ's grace as Head, the source of grace for the members** (*caput*). The same
habit as his personal grace, distinct only in reason (ST III q.8 a.5) — fire heats
by the heat that makes it hot. A head has order, perfection and power (q.8 a.1);
John 1:14 ("full of grace and truth") and 1:16 ("of his fulness we all have
received") map onto the last two. He gives grace **authoritatively as God,
instrumentally as man** (q.8 a.1 ad 1). The same cause-exceeds-effect logic grounds
the classical argument for his vision (q.9 a.2). **Level:** headship scriptural
(Ephesians 1:22); the analysis common teaching. Headship worked out as ecclesiology
is `ecclesiology-and-mariology`'s ([syllabus](../ecclesiology-and-mariology/syllabus.md)).

*Introduced:* [3.4](lessons/03-04-the-grace-of-the-head.md); used at
[4.2](lessons/04-02-christs-human-knowledge-the-classical-account.md), [4.3](lessons/04-03-the-beatific-vision-in-christ.md)

### Sinlessness and impeccability

**Sinless: defined.** Chalcedon, "like unto us, sin only excepted" (Hebrews 4:15);
repeated at Constantinople III. Constantinople II's capitulum 12 condemns Theodore's
Christ who improved by degrees and became "altogether without sin" only after the
resurrection. No *fomes* in Christ (ST III q.15 a.2); assault from without, none
from within (a.2 ad 3). **Impeccable** — could not sin — on the classical ground of
the *who*: a sin would be an act of the person, and the person is the Word. **Not
the direct object of any definition the lessons found**; the manuals rate it well
above opinion and differ on its exact note. **Whether impeccability leaves room for
real temptation is freely disputed** (reserved).

Sin is a wound in human nature, not a part of it, so a sinless humanity is a whole
one ([2.1](lessons/02-01-apollinaris-a-complete-humanity.md)).

*Introduced:* [3.4](lessons/03-04-the-grace-of-the-head.md)

### Propassion

**A passion that begins in the sense appetite and goes no further.** Aquinas takes
the word from Jerome on Matthew 26:37 (ST III q.15 a.4): a passion is perfect "when
it dominates the soul", a propassion when it begins and "goes no further". Christ's
sorrow, fear and anger were **real** — "in very deed" — differing from ours in
object (never the unlawful), principle (never forestalling reason) and effect
(never dragging reason along). He felt fear of the evil to come, not fear from
uncertainty (q.15 a.7). "Propassion" limits where a passion goes; it does not deny
it is there. Sinless is not passionless. **School theology, not defined.**

*Introduced:* [3.4](lessons/03-04-the-grace-of-the-head.md); at Gethsemane in
[4.1](lessons/04-01-christs-human-will-in-act.md)

### Merit of Christ

**Merit is only for what one does not yet have.** So Christ did not merit grace,
knowledge, the beatitude of his soul or the Godhead — lacking them even for a time
would diminish him more than meriting could add — but did merit the glory of his
body and his outward excellence, including the exaltation of Philippians 2:9 (ST III
q.19 a.3). For others he merits as Head, head and members being one mystical person
(a.4). **That he merited our justification is defined** (Trent VI ch. 7 with canon
10); the list of what he merited for himself is Thomist. The line falls where
Constantinople II drew it against Theodore, who had Christ made worthy of sonship
by progress.

*Introduced:* [3.4](lessons/03-04-the-grace-of-the-head.md)

### Natural will and gnomic will

**Natural will** (*thelema physikon*): a rational nature's built-in faculty and
drive toward its own good — like sight, every human has it by nature. ***Gnome***:
a *mode* of using that faculty, the hesitant weighing of a creature that does not
know the good and must choose among options, sometimes against God. Christ has the
first, not the second: "knowing all things, [he] had no need of inquiry" (John of
Damascus, *Exposition* III.14, following Maximus). So the two wills cannot collide:
the only will that sets itself against God is gnomic. Compare ST III q.18 a.6 ad 3.
**Level:** common teaching; only "natural" entered the Definition. How far *gnome*
can be denied of Christ is [4.1](lessons/04-01-christs-human-will-in-act.md)'s.

*Introduced:* [2.5](lessons/02-05-monothelitism-and-constantinople-iii.md); Maximus's texts at
[`patristics` 5.4](../patristics/lessons/05-04-maximus-and-john-of-damascus.md)

### Will as nature and will as reason

**Three movements inside one human will** (ST III q.18 aa.2-5): **sensuality**, the
sensitive appetite, will only by participation — it recoils from pain; **will as
nature** (*thelesis*), the rational will drawn to what is good in itself (life) and
away from what is evil in itself (death); **will as reason** (*boulesis*), the
rational will choosing a means for an end. Acts, not extra faculties. Aquinas takes
the terms from John of Damascus. By sensuality and will as nature Christ could will
what God did not will; by will as reason, never (a.5). Not contrary, because the
lower movements never rejected the *reason* the passion was willed (a.6) — and their
moving was itself permitted. At Gethsemane: "remove this chalice" is nature; "not my
will, but thine" is reason, one with the divine will. **Common teaching.**

*Introduced:* [4.1](lessons/04-01-christs-human-will-in-act.md)

### Freedom confirmed in good

**Christ chose freely without being able to sin.** Choice is will as reason's act;
doubt is incidental to it, found only "in an ignorant nature"; his will is
"determined to good" but not "to this or that good", so he chooses as the blessed
do, "with a free-will confirmed in good" (ST III q.18 a.4 and ad 1-3). Obedience
makes the thing commanded necessary and leaves the carrying-out free (q.47 a.2
ad 2). A *source* view of freedom, not a *leeway* view
([`metaphysics` 6.3](../metaphysics/lessons/06-03-frankfurt-cases-and-alternate-possibilities.md)).
**Levels:** that he was free and merited is presupposed by Trent; the analysis is
common teaching; how his freedom fits a command to die (precept or counsel? death or
its circumstances?) was a post-Tridentine dispute, **freely disputed**.

*Introduced:* [4.1](lessons/04-01-christs-human-will-in-act.md); invoked at
[4.3](lessons/04-03-the-beatific-vision-in-christ.md)

### The three knowledges of Christ

**Beyond the divine knowledge, which is God's essence and cannot be the soul's act
(ST III q.9 a.1 ad 1), Aquinas gives Christ's soul three created knowledges** — one
per way a human mind can be filled:

- **The vision** (q.9 a.2; q.10): sight of God's essence without comprehending it; in the Word, all actual things past, present and future, not all God *could* do. Perfects the soul's capacity for its last end.
- **Infused knowledge** (q.9 a.3; q.11): ideas imprinted by the Word — the human sciences, what prophecy reveals, singulars; held as a habit, used at will. Fills the passive intellect's whole potency.
- **Acquired knowledge** (q.9 a.4; q.12): the agent intellect abstracting from sense images, "properly knowledge in a human fashion"; by discovery, not teaching — he learned nothing from any man (q.12 a.3).

The higher light the lower without replacing it: air lit by the sun is brightened,
not dimmed (q.9 a.1 ad 2). Aquinas changed his mind: in the *Sentences* (with Albert,
Alexander of Hales, Bonaventure) he gave Christ no separate acquired habit; at q.9
a.4, "although I wrote differently", he does. **Level:** a true human intellect is
defined; the three-part scheme, infused knowledge and "learned nothing from men" are
**school theology**. (How the three together answer the Gospel texts is reserved.)

*Introduced:* [4.2](lessons/04-02-christs-human-knowledge-the-classical-account.md)

### Two kinds of advance

ST III q.12 a.2, on Luke 2:52. Knowledge advances **in effect** when one shows more
of what one already has, and **in essence** when the habit itself grows. The first
is true of all Christ's knowledge; the second only of the acquired knowledge, since
the agent intellect works step by step. In the *Sentences* view, nothing in him grew
in essence.

*Introduced:* [4.2](lessons/04-02-christs-human-knowledge-the-classical-account.md)

### The economic reading of Mark 13:32

**"Nor the Son" means: not known so as to be made known.** Augustine: the Son "is
ignorant of this, as making others ignorant" (*De Trinitate* I.12, Haddan). Aquinas:
he is said not to know because he does not make it known (ST III q.10 a.2 ad 1,
citing Acts 1:7, and naming Arius and Eunomius as the rival reading's authors).
Gregory the Great to Eulogius, against the Agnoetae (*Letters* X.39): "What then He
knew in it He knew not from it" — known *in* his humanity, not *from* it. The word
"economic" is a label, not Gregory's. Gregory's verdict: whoever is not a Nestorian
cannot be an Agnoete. The Catechism affirms the reading (CCC 474).

*Introduced:* [4.2](lessons/04-02-christs-human-knowledge-the-classical-account.md); the
Arian proof texts at [`trinity-and-god` 2.2](../trinity-and-god/lessons/02-02-the-father-and-the-son-in-the-new-testament.md)

### The 1918 Holy Office response

**5 June 1918.** Asked by the Congregation of Seminaries and Universities whether
three propositions could **safely be taught** — that it is not established that
Christ's earthly soul had the knowledge of the blessed; that his universal knowledge
in the Word is not certain; that recent "limited knowledge" views are as acceptable
as the ancients' — the Holy Office answered **in the negative**. A **safety
judgment**: it governs what may be taught; it does not define the opposite
([`fundamental-theology` 4.5](../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-05-reading-a-documents-weight.md),
the *responsum* genre). Papal approval of it was not verified by the lessons.

*Introduced:* [4.2](lessons/04-02-christs-human-knowledge-the-classical-account.md)

### The beatific vision in Christ

**Did the earthly Christ's human mind see God's essence? A disputed question, with
a level that is itself in dispute.** Undisputed by every party: sinless, free of
error, one subject, knew he was the Son. Positions:

- **Classical** — Aquinas (ST III q.9 a.2: the head must have what he gives, "the cause ought always to be more efficacious than the effect"), *Mystici Corporis*, and Thomas Joseph White (*The Thomist* 69, 2005; *The Incarnate Lord*, 2015), who argues the vision is what makes the man's acts the Son's own acts.
- **Immediate but non-beatific** — Rahner ("Dogmatic Reflections on the Knowledge and Self-Consciousness of Christ", *Theological Investigations* 5): unthematic self-presence to the Word, compatible with growth.
- **Filial or hypostatic vision** — Weinandy ("Jesus' Filial Vision of the Father", *Pro Ecclesia* 13, 2004), a **critic** of the classical view, charging that it posits a second knower.
- **No vision; infused, prophetic-type knowledge** — Galot ("prophetic" is White's summary).
- **Mission-consciousness and real abandonment** — Balthasar (*Mysterium Paschale*; "Fides Christi").

**Levels:** no council defined it; *Mystici Corporis* (1943) §75 asserts it from
conception (**rung 3**); common teaching (**rung 4**); 1918 a safety judgment. The
Catechism (CCC 472-474) speaks of intimate and immediate knowledge but never says
"beatific vision"; the ITC's *The Consciousness of Christ Concerning Himself and His
Mission* (1985) declines to say how his consciousness was articulated and is a
commission's study, not magisterium. *Novo Millennio Ineunte* (2001) §§25-27 holds
his joy in the Father and his agony together. Calling it dogma is an error; so is
calling it a discarded opinion.

*Introduced:* [4.3](lessons/04-03-the-beatific-vision-in-christ.md); the classical account at
[4.2](lessons/04-02-christs-human-knowledge-the-classical-account.md)

### Comprehensor and viator

**At the goal and on the way at once, "under a different respect".** A *comprehensor*
has arrived and sees God; a *viator* is still travelling. Aquinas holds the earthly
Christ was both: beatitude proper to the soul; passible soul and mortal body still
on the road (ST III q.15 a.10 and ad 1; q.11 a.2). Without overflow from the higher
to the lower — the special condition of the earthly Christ — the higher reason
enjoyed fruition while the soul grieved (q.46 a.8). The hinge of the modern
objections.

*Introduced:* [4.3](lessons/04-03-the-beatific-vision-in-christ.md)

## The work of Christ

### The Paschal Mystery

**One saving act, many names.** Christ's passover through death to life —
passion, death, resurrection **and ascension** — is the unit, not the death alone:
*Sacrosanctum Concilium* 5 says he achieved the work of redemption chiefly by this
paschal mystery (quoted at CCC 1067; the cross and Resurrection at the centre, CCC
571). Paul joins them: "delivered up for our sins, and rose again for our
justification" (Romans 4:25). Aquinas fits five modes of the Passion into one act
by reduplication (ST III q.48 a.6 ad 3): compared with his **Godhead**, it works
efficiently; with the **will of his soul**, meritoriously; **in his flesh**, by way
of satisfaction; as freeing us from guilt's servitude, as **redemption**; as
reconciling us with God, as **sacrifice**. One subject, "Christ's Passion", five
"in so far as" clauses. A soteriology that finishes on Good Friday has cut the
event in half. **Level:** redemption by his death is of faith; SC is conciliar
teaching using the term.

*Introduced:* [5.1](lessons/05-01-the-paschal-mystery-and-the-threefold-office.md)

### The vocabulary of redemption

**Seven words, each with an object** — most confusions in Module 5 are a word
pointed the wrong way.

| Word | What it says | Acts on | Anchor | Confused, it yields |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Redemption | buying back out of bondage to sin and the debt of punishment (ST III q.48 a.4) | us | 1 Peter 1:18-19 | a transaction with a payee |
| Reconciliation | the enmity ended, the relation restored | the relation; God the agent | 2 Corinthians 5:18-20 | a God who changes his mind |
| Expiation | sin removed or covered | sin | Hebrews 9:26 | fused with propitiation |
| Propitiation | God rendered favourable | God | Romans 3:25; 1 John 2:2 | a love that is purchased |
| Satisfaction | making good an offence by offering what the offended loves more than he hated it (q.48 a.2) | God's honour and justice | Trent VI ch. 7 | satisfaction read as punishment |
| Merit | a free act of charity that earns its reward, for Christ and as Head for his members (q.48 a.1) | the members through the Head | Trent VI canon 10 | a sum transferred with no union with him |
| Sacrifice | an offering to God as homage, in love (q.48 a.3) | God | Ephesians 5:2 | the executioners made priests (a.3 ad 3 denies it) |

**Reconciliation runs one way in Paul:** God "hath reconciled us to himself by
Christ" (2 Corinthians 5:18, Douay); *we* are reconciled. "God was reconciled to
us" is true only with a distinction — the obstacle (sin) removed and the relation
restored, never God's love caused or changed (ST III q.49 a.4 ad 2; 1 John 4:10).
Real in the creature, of reason in God. "The divine nature paid the price" is false;
"God paid the price" under a concrete term is true (Acts 20:28) — paying belongs
immediately to Christ as man (q.48 a.5).

*Introduced:* [5.1](lessons/05-01-the-paschal-mystery-and-the-threefold-office.md)

### Expiation and propitiation

**Expiation acts on sin (removed, covered); propitiation acts on God (rendered
favourable).** *Hilasterion* (Romans 3:25) is the Septuagint's word for the ark's
lid, Douay "propitiatory" at Hebrews 9:5. C. H. Dodd (*The Bible and the Greeks*,
1935) argued for "expiation"; Leon Morris (*The Apostolic Preaching of the Cross*,
1955) for "propitiation", with Romans 1:18's wrath as context. At 1 John 4:10 the
Douay says "propitiation", the Catechism's English "expiation" (CCC 604). **The
doctrinal rule does not wait on the exegesis:** God "hath proposed" the
propitiation (Romans 3:25); his love is its cause, not its effect. Trent VI ch. 2
calls Christ "propitiator". The translation is an open exegetical question
([`new-testament`](../new-testament/syllabus.md) 5.2).

*Introduced:* [5.1](lessons/05-01-the-paschal-mystery-and-the-threefold-office.md)

### The threefold office

**Christ — "anointed" (CCC 436) — is priest, prophet and king.** Eusebius (*Church
History* I.3.8): the Word "the only high priest of all, and the only King of every
creature, and the Father's only supreme prophet of prophets" (McGiffert). Aquinas's
triad is lawgiver, priest, king, "all these concur in Christ, as the fount of all
grace" (ST III q.22 a.1 ad 3). Calvin makes it systematic (*Institutes* II.15:
Prophet, King, Priest). Vatican II: the faithful share the offices (*Lumen Gentium*
10, 12, 31, 34-36); CCC 783. **The offices are the person's, exercised as man** —
mediator as man (q.26 a.2): "the Word is our high priest" true, "the divinity
offered sacrifice" false. **Level:** authentic teaching as an organizing scheme,
scriptural in its titles; not defined; not bound to exactly three. One act can
exercise all three.

*Introduced:* [5.1](lessons/05-01-the-paschal-mystery-and-the-threefold-office.md)

### Models of redemption

**The Church has defined the fact of redemption, not a mechanism; no model is
defined as *the* model.** Defined: Christ reconciled us to God in his blood (Trent
V, original sin, third canon) and merited our justification (VI ch. 7, canon 10).
Taught: he made satisfaction to the Father (VI ch. 7). The models, one by one:

- **Ransom** — captivity, cost, on our behalf; the payee question and the patristic answers. See [ransom](#ransom), [the devil's rights](#the-devils-rights).
- ***Christus Victor*** — God in Christ defeats the tyrants; a sound motif, a contested history. See [Christus Victor](#christus-victor).
- **Sacrifice** — an offering *to* God, once, on the cross. See [sacrifice](#sacrifice).
- **Satisfaction** — Anselm and Aquinas; a free gift God loves more than he hated the offence. See [satisfaction](#satisfaction).
- **Penal substitution** — the Reformed confessions; Christ bore the wrath due to sin in our place. See [penal substitution](#penal-substitution).
- **Exemplar** — associated with Abelard: the cross as the supreme showing of love that moves us. Every Catholic account keeps God's love as the cross's cause; "mainly an example" alone is the Pelagian shape ([1.3](lessons/01-03-why-the-incarnation.md)).

The Fathers did not choose among "types"; they piled images up — one sermon ransoms,
sacrifices and conquers. Tabulating the models against one another is reserved.
Hand-offs: how merit reaches the individual is `creation-grace-last-things`'s
([syllabus](../creation-grace-last-things/syllabus.md)); the debt logic
[`theology-of-debt`](../theology-of-debt/syllabus.md)'s; the Mass
`sacraments-and-liturgy`'s ([syllabus](../sacraments-and-liturgy/syllabus.md)).

*Introduced:* [5.1](lessons/05-01-the-paschal-mystery-and-the-threefold-office.md); run at
[5.2](lessons/05-02-ransom-christus-victor-and-sacrifice.md), [5.3](lessons/05-03-satisfaction-anselm-and-aquinas.md),
[5.4](lessons/05-04-penal-substitution-and-the-catholic-assessment.md)

### Ransom

*Lytron* — **the price of release**, the ordinary word for money that freed a
prisoner of war or a slave. Mark 10:45, "to give his life a redemption for many"
(*anti pollon*, "in place of many"); 1 Timothy 2:6. The picture asserts three
things: we were held captive; release cost something; the cost was his life, on our
behalf. **The New Testament never names a payee.** "Paid to whom?" sorts every early
reading: to the captor (the devil's rights), to God (refused by Nazianzen, *Oration*
45.22: the Father **accepts** the offering but neither asked nor demanded it), or to
no one (Augustine: the devil beaten by righteousness, then power). Push the metaphor
to a payee and you get either a practical dualism (the devil with rights against
God) or a God who must be bought off.

*Introduced:* [5.2](lessons/05-02-ransom-christus-victor-and-sacrifice.md)

### The devil's rights

**The patristic opinion that sin gave the devil a just claim over humanity, paid off
by a God hidden in flesh.** Usually traced to Origen (on the NPNF editor's note);
defended most carefully by Gregory of Nyssa, *Great Catechism* 22-26: we sold
ourselves (ch. 22); the devil takes the wonderful man as a better prize (ch. 23);
"the hook of the Deity might be gulped down along with the bait of flesh" (ch. 24);
the deceiver justly deceived, for healing (ch. 26). Its virtues: God's justice even
toward an enemy; why the Godhead came hidden; Easter as the trap springing. Its
cost: rights against God, a win by trick. Refused by Nazianzen (*Oration* 45.22);
reworked by Augustine (*De Trinitate* XIII.12-14: "not by power, but by
righteousness"); denied by Anselm (*Cur Deus Homo* I.7). **Never taught by any
magisterial act; dropped.** Calling Nyssa a heretic for it overstates too.

*Introduced:* [5.2](lessons/05-02-ransom-christus-victor-and-sacrifice.md)

### Christus Victor

Gustaf Aulén's Uppsala lectures (Swedish 1930; English by A. G. Hebert, 1931)
sorted theories into the **"classic"** type (God in Christ fights and defeats sin,
death and the devil), the **"Latin"** (an offering by Christ as man to God, the
Anselmian line) and the **"subjective"** (a change in us by love's example). His
thesis was historical: classic = the Fathers' main view, Latin = a medieval
deviation, Luther = a recovery. **Keep two claims apart:** the *motif* is sound —
Hebrews 2:14-15, Colossians 2:15, the Easter liturgy — and belongs in any Catholic
account; the *historical thesis* is weak (the Fathers pile images; Aulén made the
Latin tradition colder than its texts; his Luther is contested). What the motif
leaves open: why a *death* is how the battle is won.

*Introduced:* [5.2](lessons/05-02-ransom-christus-victor-and-sacrifice.md)

### Sacrifice

**Offered *to* God — which is not "demanded by" God.** Isaiah 53 (the Servant
"offered because it was his own will", 53:7); Hebrews 9:12-28 and 10:10-14 ("once";
"one sacrifice for sins"). Augustine, *City of God* X.6: "a true sacrifice is every
work which is done that we may be united to God in holy fellowship" (Dods) — the
aim is **union**, not compensation; Christ is priest and victim, and the redeemed
city is offered with him. The offerer is Christ, not the executioners (ST III q.48
a.3 ad 3). Trent teaches the one offering on the cross to the Father (Session XXII
ch. 1). The exegesis is [`new-testament`](../new-testament/syllabus.md) 6.2's; the
Mass is `sacraments-and-liturgy`'s.

*Introduced:* [5.2](lessons/05-02-ransom-christus-victor-and-sacrifice.md)

### Satisfaction

**A free offering to the offended one of something he loves as much as or more
than he hated the offence** (ST III q.48 a.2; the English Dominicans render
*satisfactio* "atonement" there). Christ's satisfaction is **superabundant** on
three grounds, in Aquinas's order: the exceeding **charity** from which he suffered;
the **dignity** of his life, the life of one who was God and man; the **extent** of
the Passion and grief. The measure is love, not a scale of pain; the worth is fixed
by *who* offers (q.48 a.2 ad 3). It reaches us because head and members are
[one mystical person](#one-mystical-person).

**Anselm** (*Cur Deus Homo*, Deane): to sin is not to render God his due (I.11);
God may not simply pass it over (I.12-13); so [satisfaction or
punishment](#satisfaction-or-punishment) (I.15); only the God-man can satisfy
(II.6); his death was not owed (II.11), so its reward passes to his brethren (II.19).
The Father did not *prefer* the death (I.9); the necessity is God's unchangeable
goodness, not compulsion (II.5). **Aquinas's reworking:** necessity becomes
fittingness — God could have forgiven without satisfaction and "wrongs no one"
(q.46 a.2 ad 3); equality becomes superabundance; transfer becomes membership; the
Father "delivered him up" by ordaining, inspiring charity, and not shielding
(q.47 a.3) — never by punishing.

**Levels:** *that* Christ made satisfaction to the Father is taught by Trent VI
ch. 7 (a chapter, not a canon; commonly ranked of faith). The mechanism has no
magisterial weight of its own. **Its necessity is freely disputed** (Anselm yes,
Aquinas no). Satisfaction is one model among others, not dogma; "just one theory"
understates the fact Trent teaches. The debt reading of both authors is
[`theology-of-debt`](../theology-of-debt/syllabus.md)'s (3.3-3.4).

*Introduced:* [5.3](lessons/05-03-satisfaction-anselm-and-aquinas.md); named at
[5.1](lessons/05-01-the-paschal-mystery-and-the-threefold-office.md), [5.2](lessons/05-02-ransom-christus-victor-and-sacrifice.md);
set against the Reformed account at [5.4](lessons/05-04-penal-substitution-and-the-catholic-assessment.md)

### Satisfaction or punishment

**Anselm's fork:** "satisfaction or punishment must needs follow every sin"
(*Cur Deus Homo* I.15, Deane). Punishment is honour taken back from the unwilling
(I.14); satisfaction is honour restored by the willing. Christ stays on the
**satisfaction** branch throughout; any sentence that has the Father punishing him
in the guilty's place has left the model. Aquinas keeps the fork but says God was
not bound to it (ST III q.46 a.2 ad 3). Anselm also says nothing can be added to or
taken from God's honour in itself (I.15) — sin disturbs the creature's order.

*Introduced:* [5.3](lessons/05-03-satisfaction-anselm-and-aquinas.md)

### One mystical person

ST III q.48 a.2 ad 1: head and members are "as one mystical person" (*quasi una
persona mystica*), so Christ's satisfaction belongs to the faithful as their own —
not a sum transferred to strangers. The soteriological face of
[capital grace](#capital-grace); the same ground makes his merit reach the members
(q.19 a.4).

*Introduced:* [5.3](lessons/05-03-satisfaction-anselm-and-aquinas.md)

### Penal substitution

**The Reformed doctrine that Christ bore, in our place, the wrath of God and the
curse due to sin, satisfying the Father's justice.** Confessional loci: Heidelberg
Q.37 (he "bore, in body and soul, the wrath of God against the sin of the whole
human race"), Q.39, Q.40 (satisfaction possible no other way), Q.44 (the descent as
anguish on the cross), Q.60; Westminster VI.6 (guilt as being "bound over to the
wrath of God and curse of the law"), VIII.4-5, XI.1, 3; Shorter Catechism Q.27;
Calvin, *Institutes* II.16.4-6, 11. (Westminster ch. VIII itself never says "wrath"
or "curse".) Its strongest readers insist that love comes first and that the Father
was **never angry with the Son**: Calvin, "We do not, however, insinuate that God
was ever hostile to him or angry with him" (II.16.11, Beveridge).

**How the Catholic assessment is run:** not on words — both traditions say
substitution, satisfaction, penalty, propitiation — but on two distinctions:
[penalty and guilt](#penalty-and-guilt), and whether the Father's delivering-up is a
judge's sentence or the source of the Son's love (ST III q.47 a.3). The live crux
is *satisfaction by punishment* versus *satisfaction instead of punishment with
penalty as its matter* — Anselm's fork again.

**Levels:** sinlessness defined, so Christ cannot be personally guilty (no Reformed
confession says he was); the fact of satisfaction taught by Trent VI ch. 7; sole
imputation for *justification* anathematized (VI canon 11) — a justification
question, ceded to `apologetics-catholic-claims` ([syllabus](../apologetics-catholic-claims/syllabus.md));
penalty-not-guilt and no punitive wrath at the Son, **common teaching** (Aquinas;
CCC 603). **No act condemns penal substitution by name.** Whether its language can
bear a Catholic sense is **freely disputed**. (The statement a Reformed theologian
would sign, and the Catholic reply, are reserved.)

*Introduced:* [5.4](lessons/05-04-penal-substitution-and-the-catholic-assessment.md);
distinguished from satisfaction at [5.3](lessons/05-03-satisfaction-anselm-and-aquinas.md)

### Penalty and guilt

***Culpa*, guilt, is the personal fault; *poena*, penalty, is what the fault
deserves.** A friend can pay my fine; nobody can make it true that *he* ran the red
light. Scholastic refinement: *reatus culpae* (liability as the one at fault) versus
*reatus poenae* (liability to punishment). Aquinas: "one satisfies for another's
sin by taking on himself the punishment due to the sin of the other" (ST III q.14
a.1) — pain the matter, charity the principle (ad 1); satisfaction abolishes the
debt of punishment (q.49 a.3). CCC 603: Christ did not experience reprobation as if
he himself had sinned. Westminster's "guilt" (VI.6) is defined as liability to
wrath — possibly *reatus poenae*, which would make part of the quarrel verbal.

*Introduced:* [5.4](lessons/05-04-penal-substitution-and-the-catholic-assessment.md)

### Double imputation

**Reformed: our sins and guilt imputed to Christ** (Calvin, *Institutes* II.16.5-6),
**his obedience and satisfaction imputed to us** (Westminster XI.1, 3; Heidelberg
Q.60). Trent VI canon 11 anathematizes justification by the *sole* imputation of
Christ's justice, to the exclusion of inherent grace and charity. The first half
bears on the atonement ([penalty and guilt](#penalty-and-guilt)); the second is a
justification question, ceded to `apologetics-catholic-claims`. The 1999 *Joint
Declaration on the Doctrine of Justification* concerns justification, not the
atonement.

*Introduced:* [5.4](lessons/05-04-penal-substitution-and-the-catholic-assessment.md)

## Descended, risen, ascended

### The descent into hell

**"Hell" here is Sheol or Hades, the abode of all the dead — not Gehenna.** Latin
*descendit ad inferna* (Apostles'), *ad inferos* (Athanasian and some copies);
Greek *katelthonta eis ta katotata* (Ephesians 4:9). Douay renders *Hades* "hell"
(Acts 2:27). The article is a late arrival: Rufinus (c. 400) reports it absent from
the Roman and Eastern creeds and thinks its force lies in "buried"; first Latin at
Aquileia (Schaff). Three layers, kept apart: **he was really dead**; **he went as
Saviour** to free the just; **what he underwent or did there beyond that**.

Aquinas (ST III q.52): by his effect he reached every region of the dead; in essence
his soul went only to the limbo of the fathers (a.2); he freed the holy fathers by
the power of his Passion (a.5) and none of the damned (a.6). CCC 632-633, quoting
the Roman Catechism: he went down as Saviour, not to deliver the damned or destroy
the hell of damnation, but to free the just. The scriptural texts are Acts 2:24-31,
Ephesians 4:8-10, 1 Peter 3:18-20 and 4:6 (their rival readings are reserved).

**Balthasar and Pitstick.** Balthasar (*Mysterium Paschale*, 1969): the descent as
the last stage of the Son's obedience — "dead with the dead", passive, in solidarity
with the God-forsaken, the distance falling within the Trinity's love; drawn in part
from Adrienne von Speyr. Pitstick (*Light in Darkness*, 2007): the tradition is
unified and precise — a glorious soul, to the just only, freeing them, not
suffering — and Balthasar keeps the words while reversing each element. Edward
Oakes replied in *First Things* (December 2006, rejoinders into 2007). No
magisterial act has ruled on the thesis.

**Levels:** the article, in the Creed (**rung 1**); the Word's union with body and
soul in death, theologically certain (CCC 626, 630); liberation of the just and not
the damned, **authentic ordinary magisterium** (Roman Catechism; CCC 633 — its
footnoted sources are local councils and papal letters, not definitions); what he
underwent beyond that, never judged — Balthasar's Holy Saturday **freely disputed**,
Pitstick arguing it contradicts doctrine. The Heidelberg reading (Q.44: anguish on
the cross) moves the article back to Good Friday. The development question is
[`fundamental-theology` 5.2](../fundamental-theology/lessons/05-02-newmans-theory-of-development.md)'s tool.

*Introduced:* [6.1](lessons/06-01-he-descended-into-hell.md)

### The union in death

**Death parted Christ's soul from his body, but neither from the Word.** John of
Damascus, *Exposition* III.27: "although the soul was separated from the body
topically, yet hypostatically they were united through the Word" (Salmond); his
"Holy Spirit ... severed" there means Christ's human spirit, his soul. Aquinas: the
grace of union is withdrawn only for fault, and Christ had none, so the Word stayed
united to the flesh (ST III q.50 a.2) and to the soul (a.3). So both halves of Holy
Saturday are said of the Son: "was buried" (the body's) and "descended" (the soul's)
(q.50 a.3). The whole Christ, *totus*, was in the tomb and in hell; not the whole
*of* Christ, *totum* (q.52 a.3). Not "a man" simply in the three days — that denies
his death — but "a dead man" (q.50 a.4). **Level:** theologically certain (CCC 626,
630); not defined in these terms, but denying it divides the subject.

*Introduced:* [6.1](lessons/06-01-he-descended-into-hell.md)

### The glorified body

**The same body, reunited to the same soul, in a new mode of life.** ST III q.54
a.1: "that is said to rise, which fell"; otherwise the resurrection "would not have
been true, but apparent". Flesh and bones (Luke 24:39); the scars kept, after Bede —
a trophy, and so that he may show his death when he pleads (q.54 a.4). **Glorified,
not resuscitated:** Lazarus died again (CCC 645-646). *Soma pneumatikon* (1
Corinthians 15:44) is a Spirit-governed body, not an immaterial one — "it is sown
... it shall rise" keeps one subject. Faith was born from the encounter, not the
encounter from faith (CCC 644); no one saw the rising itself (647). The whole
Trinity raises him and he rises (Acts 2:24; John 10:18; Romans 1:4) — one act,
appropriated (CCC 648). **Levels:** resurrection in his own body, defined in
substance (creeds; Lateran IV "rose in the flesh"; CCC 645); the empty tomb's
evidential force not dogma (CCC 640); Lateran IV's "all shall rise with their own
bodies" is the **general** resurrection, not Christ's; the classic properties of
glorified bodies, common teaching; passage through shut doors (Aquinas credits the
Godhead, q.54 a.1 ad 1), freely disputed. Bultmann's Easter-as-faith is stated in
[6.2](lessons/06-02-resurrection-and-ascension-as-doctrine.md). The pattern of our
risen bodies is `creation-grace-last-things`'s.

*Introduced:* [6.2](lessons/06-02-resurrection-and-ascension-as-doctrine.md)

### The session at the right hand

**"Sitteth at the right hand of the Father" — defined; the right hand is not a
place.** John of Damascus, *Exposition* IV.2: Christ "sits in the body at the right
hand of God the Father, but we do not hold that the right hand of the Father is
actual place" (Salmond); it is "the glory and honour of the Godhead", which his
flesh shares (CCC 663; ST III q.58). Ascending is local motion and belongs to the
humanity: he ascended as man, by the power of his Godhead (q.57 a.2). The humanity
is **permanent** and **intercedes**: "always living to make intercession for us"
(Hebrews 7:24-25). "Whose kingdom shall have no end" — Bright, in Percival, reads it
against Marcellus of Ancyra, accused of teaching that the Word would one day
withdraw from the man. The cloud and heaven are symbols of entry into glory (CCC
659); Aquinas's placement above the spheres (q.57 a.4) rode on a lost cosmology.
Dogma: a real body, still his, in the mode of glory. Not dogma: the geography.

*Introduced:* [6.2](lessons/06-02-resurrection-and-ascension-as-doctrine.md)

## Churches that did not receive Chalcedon

**Same two questions, different dictionaries.** Chalcedon counts the *who* with
*hypostasis* (one) and the *what* with *physis* (two). The Oriental Orthodox count
the *who* with *physis* — so "two natures" sounds like two Sons. The Church of the
East counts the *what* with *qnoma* — so "one *qnoma*" sounds like Eutyches. Each
hears in Chalcedon the heresy it fears most. The stance is stated unsoftened:
**Chalcedon split the Church permanently, and the churches it lost have never been
shown heretical in substance.** Whether anything besides the dictionary divides them
is freely disputed.

### Miaphysitism

**One incarnate nature of God the Word, "out of two"** — the self-description of
the Oriental Orthodox (Coptic, Syriac, Armenian, Ethiopian, Eritrean, Malankara).
Severus of Antioch (patriarch 512-518) gave the classical form: divinity and
humanity each complete and unmixed, told apart in thought rather than counted as
two existing things. *Physis* here names the concrete Word. **They condemn
Eutyches**, so "monophysite" (*monos*, only one — Eutyches's absorbed humanity) is
a mislabel; *mia* means one, united. V. C. Samuel (*The Council of Chalcedon
Re-Examined*) argues a main ground for rejecting Chalcedon was its reception of the
Tome and restoration of Theodoret and Ibas, and that both sides were orthodox in
what they meant. They receive the first three councils and honour Dioscorus and
Severus. The Chalcedonian worry that remains is not the words but what follows: can
"one nature" also say two natural wills and operations?

*Introduced:* [6.3](lessons/06-03-the-churches-that-did-not-receive-chalcedon.md)

### One incarnate nature of God the Word

Cyril's *mia physis tou theou logou sesarkomene*. **It reached Cyril under
Athanasius's name from an Apollinarian forgery**
([`patristics` 5.1](../patristics/lessons/05-01-cyril-of-alexandria.md)). Cyril
himself explains it (Second Letter to Succensus, via John of Damascus, *Exposition*
III.11): "nature" is the Word, the one subject; "incarnate" carries "the perfection
in humanity" — drop "incarnate" and the formula is Apollinarian. John makes the
formula his own (III.7). Chalcedon received Cyril's letters but did not adopt the
formula; Constantinople II capitulum 8 received it in the hypostatic sense and
condemned it only as one mixed substance. **Provenance settles nothing by itself:**
it removes the authority Cyril thought it had, not the meaning it now bears. (How
*physis* works in this formula against Chalcedon's "in two natures" is reserved.)

*Introduced:* [6.3](lessons/06-03-the-churches-that-did-not-receive-chalcedon.md); received at
[2.4](lessons/02-04-the-three-chapters-and-constantinople-ii.md)

### Qnoma and parsopa

The **Church of the East**'s terms (Babai the Great, died 628, *Book of the Union*):
two *kyane* (natures), two *qnome* (a nature as it concretely exists in this
individual — **not** a person), one *parsopa* (person), united without mixture and
never divided. It grew up in the Persian empire; its teacher was Theodore more than
Nestorius; it does not receive Ephesus and honours Theodore and Nestorius. Sebastian
Brock ("The 'Nestorian' Church: a lamentable misnomer", 1996) argues the label
fastens the two-Sons doctrine Ephesus condemned onto a church whose formula says one
*parsopa*. Its Marian title: "Mother of Christ our God and Saviour". Do not assume a
*qnoma* is a person because *hypostasis* is one.

*Introduced:* [6.3](lessons/06-03-the-churches-that-did-not-receive-chalcedon.md)

## Heresies and the phrases that land in them

Each entry: the claim, the giveaway phrasing (from the lessons' invented homilies,
carols and handouts), and what corrects it. The diagnose archetype runs here; the
[map](#the-map-of-christological-errors) says which square each sits in.

### Arianism

**Subtract from the divine *what*:** the Son is the first and greatest creature,
pre-cosmic, the maker of all else and the subject of the Passion — not God as the
Father is. Not adoptionism (Arius did not shrink Christ to a mere man); a sharp
subordinationism. Giveaways: "the Word, as God, was troubled and grew"; "his
ignorance of the day belonged to the Word as Word"; "not my will but thine" read as
the Son yielding at the level of the Godhead. Corrected by Nicaea's *homoousios* and
its anathemas. Gregg and Groh (*Early Arianism*, 1981) and Hanson (1988) read the
movement sympathetically — a Christ who earns sonship and can suffer.

*Introduced:* [1.4](lessons/01-04-arius-and-the-divinity-of-the-son.md)

### The Arian argument from weakness

**The engine that turns every Gospel weakness into a proof text** (Athanasius,
*Orations* III.26 reports it: had he been eternal, "He had not been ignorant of the
Day, but had known as Word"). Reconstructed: (1) one subject, the Word; (2) no human
soul — the Word is the body's mind; (3) so the weaknesses are the Word's in his own
nature; (4) what is ignorant, afraid or needy is changeable; (5) God is unchangeable;
so the Word is a creature. **Valid.** Nicenes accept 1, 4, 5, so must deny 2 or 3.
Premise 2 is attested with a hedge (Theodoret, *Eranistes* II). **Nicaea struck the
conclusion** — decisive as a verdict, silent as a diagnosis. **Athanasius denied 3**
with a reduplication ("not the Word, considered as the Word"); **the Tome to the
Antiochenes (362) and Chalcedon denied 2** (a rational soul). "Now is my soul
troubled" (John 12:27) shows why both moves are needed: flesh does not fear.

*Introduced:* [1.4](lessons/01-04-arius-and-the-divinity-of-the-son.md)

### Docetism

**Subtract from the human *what*:** the body, or the suffering, only seemed real.
Known mostly through opponents ([`patristics` 1.3](../patristics/lessons/01-03-ignatius-of-antioch.md)).
Giveaways: "God came among us in human disguise"; "the fear was staged for our
instruction"; "sinless means passionless"; "nothing changed at all, the humanity is a
costume"; "still simply a man in the tomb, just resting" (denies the death); a risen
body "made of spirit". Corrected by "very man", "consubstantial with us", the flesh
not "heavenly", and Luke 24:39. *Sempiternus Rex* names Docetism as kenoticism's
twin.

*Introduced:* [1.2](lessons/01-02-true-god-and-true-man.md)

### Apollinarianism

**Subtract the human mind:** Apollinaris of Laodicea, a Nicene of the first
generation, put the Word in place of Christ's *nous*. It bought **unity** (two
"perfects" cannot be one), **sinlessness** by construction, and **John 1:14**'s
"flesh". Condemned by a Roman synod under Damasus (later 370s; the NPNF editors say
377, date uncertain), by Constantinople I's canon 1 by name, and excluded by
Chalcedon's "reasonable soul" (defined). Not Docetism — he gave Christ a body and an
animating soul. Heavenly flesh may be his disciples' more than his (NPNF editors).
Giveaways: "Jesus had a soul, but God did his thinking"; "he knew everything because
he was God" used to fill the human mind; "God needs no grace, so Christ's soul needed
none"; "only his flesh shrank and the choosing was divine" (monothelitism in
Apollinaris's shape); Gess's Logos-become-soul.

*Introduced:* [2.1](lessons/02-01-apollinaris-a-complete-humanity.md)

### Nestorianism

**Divide the *who*:** a divine Son and a human son joined by honour, will or worship
— a union of *prosopon*, a "Duad of Sons". Nestorius himself feared a God with a
birthday and a humanity used as a costume; he never said "two persons". Whether he
was a Nestorian is a **historical** question (the *Bazaar of Heracleides*; Loofs,
Bethune-Baker for; Grillmeier onward: good intention, inadequate tools). Ephesus
judged a *position*, excluded whoever held it. Giveaways — the most common error in
the course:

- "The Word cannot suffer, so it was only Jesus who died"; "the man Jesus died" as the safe course.
- "God took up a man at his conception"; "the Word united himself to a man".
- "Two wills, so two willers"; two agents negotiating in the garden.
- "The man Jesus didn't know, the Word did"; "Jesus had to find out he was God".
- "The grace of union is a very great habitual grace".
- "The man Jesus was punished; the eternal Son was untouched".
- "In the tomb lay Jesus's body; the Son of God had gone elsewhere".
- A value of the cross drawn from the man's heroism alone.
- A from-below account that stops below; "true God" redefined as God present in a human person.

Corrected by *Theotokos*, "one and the same", "one Person and one Subsistence",
Constantinople II capitula 3-5, and "the proper will of God the Word".

*Introduced:* [1.1](lessons/01-01-what-christology-asks.md); defined against at
[2.2](lessons/02-02-nestorius-cyril-and-ephesus.md)

### Adoptionism

**Divide the *who* in time:** a good man whom God later adopts or indwells. The
characteristic drift of a from-below Christology that never arrives; Theodore's Christ
"made worthy of sonship through baptism and progress" is its conciliar target. Not
Arianism.

*Introduced:* [1.1](lessons/01-01-what-christology-asks.md)

### Eutychianism

**Fuse the *whats*:** Eutyches, archimandrite at Constantinople, examined in 448:
"of two natures before the union, but after the union ... one nature" (Tome, ch. 6,
Bright). The humanity absorbed, "a drop in the ocean". Chalcedon counts the reverse:
**one nature before, two after, one subject throughout.** Giveaways: "Christ is a
blend of God and man"; "a new kind of being, part divine and part human"; "the
divinity took over the humanity"; "the divinity suffered"; "the divine nature paid
the price"; "his divine flesh"; the ascended humanity dissolved into omnipresence.
**Not** the miaphysite churches, which condemn him.

*Introduced:* [2.3](lessons/02-03-eutyches-leo-and-chalcedon.md)

### Monothelitism

**Fuse the wills: one will in Christ.** After monoenergism's "one theandric
operation" (Pact of Union, 633), Heraclius's *Ecthesis* (638) imposed one will;
Constans II's *Typos* (648) forbade the question; the Lateran synod (649, under
Martin I — a Roman synod, **not** ecumenical) condemned both; Martin died in exile
(655), Maximus mutilated and exiled (662); Constantinople III (680-681) defined two
wills. **Honorius:** at Session XIII the council anathematized "Honorius, sometime
Pope of Old Rome" with Sergius and others and ordered his letters burned; repeated
at XVI and in the Definition; Leo II confirmed — the wording of his condemnation
(tried to subvert, or permitted to be stained) is disputed. His defenders (John IV,
Maximus) read "one will" as harmony. The fact is not in doubt: an ecumenical council
anathematized a pope and a pope confirmed it; what it does to infallibility is
[`fundamental-theology` 4.2](../fundamental-theology/lessons/04-02-infallibility-and-its-conditions.md)'s.
Giveaways: "one person, so one will"; "subject to the divine will" read as "switched
off". The rule behind it proves three wills in the Trinity.

*Introduced:* [2.5](lessons/02-05-monothelitism-and-constantinople-iii.md)

### Kenotic Christology

**The Son divests himself of divine attributes, or of their use, to become man.**
Gottfried Thomasius (*Christi Person und Werk*, 1850s): the "relative" attributes
(omnipotence, omniscience, omnipresence) laid aside, the "immanent" moral ones kept.
W. F. Gess: the Logos himself became the human soul (per A. B. Bruce, reported in
Percival) — Apollinaris by another road. Charles Gore (Bampton Lectures 1891;
*Dissertations*, 1895): use abandoned within the incarnate sphere. Frank Weston
(*The One Christ*, 1907): every attribute kept, self-restraint in exercise — orthodox
only if the restraint is the *human* nature receiving what it can. It **breaks 1.2**
(the attributes belong to the one divine nature, so *homoousios* would lapse) and
**breaks 3.2** (lowly predicates made true by reason of the divinity). Pius XII,
*Sempiternus Rex Christus* (8 September 1951) §29, rejects "the kenotic doctrine" —
authentic ordinary magisterium guarding defined dogma. Giveaways: "he laid aside his
divine power at Bethlehem"; "the Word laid aside" anything. Charles Wesley's "Emptied
Himself of all but love" (1738) passes or fails by the grammar it is read under.

*Introduced:* [3.3](lessons/03-03-kenosis.md)

### Patripassianism and tritheism in narrative

**"The Trinity suffered"** predicates of three persons what belongs to one person in
his flesh — patripassianism; the defined sentence is "one *of* the Trinity"
([2.4](lessons/02-04-the-three-chapters-and-constantinople-ii.md)). **"The Father
punished the Son" / "the Son changed the Father's mind"** sets two opposed wills in
God — tritheism in effect, since the three have one will; the Son gives himself up by
the same will as the Father (ST III q.47 a.3 ad 2)
([5.1](lessons/05-01-the-paschal-mystery-and-the-threefold-office.md),
[5.3](lessons/05-03-satisfaction-anselm-and-aquinas.md),
[5.4](lessons/05-04-penal-substitution-and-the-catholic-assessment.md)).

### Agnoetae

A sixth-century party ascribing real ignorance to Christ's soul. Gregory the Great
to Eulogius (*Letters* X.39): whoever is not a Nestorian cannot be an Agnoete —
ignorance can be pinned on a human knower apart from Wisdom only if that knower is
someone else. Not the same as the modern critics of the vision, who put limits in the
human mind of one divine subject.

*Introduced:* [4.2](lessons/04-02-christs-human-knowledge-the-classical-account.md)

## Modern Christology

### Christology from below

**A method that starts from the historical Jesus and asks what must be true of
him**, against "from above", which starts from the Word's descent (John 1). The
labels were popularized by Wolfhart Pannenberg, *Jesus — God and Man* (German 1964);
Rahner wrote of ascending and descending Christologies needing each other. **A route,
not a thesis:** judged by arrival at Chalcedon. Stopping below lands in adoptionism
or Nestorianism; working from above carelessly thins the humanity toward Docetism.

*Introduced:* [1.1](lessons/01-01-what-christology-asks.md); owned by
[6.4](lessons/06-04-christology-today.md)

### The closing rule

**A new Christology is judged by whether it can still say, without redefinition,
everything [the dogmatic core](#the-dogmatic-core) says.** Two questions: **Q1** —
does it affirm each clause (truly God; truly man with a true body; rational soul;
one and the same, *Theotokos*; perfect in manhood; two wills)? If a clause is denied,
name the square of the [map](#the-map-of-christological-errors) it lands in. **Q2** —
in the sense the Church has understood? *Dei Filius* ch. 4: "that meaning of the
sacred dogmas is perpetually to be retained which our holy mother the Church has once
declared" (Schaff), with its third canon (defined) and Vincent's growth clause.
*Libertatis Nuntius* ch. X names the failure: Chalcedon's words kept, a new meaning
given. *Mysterium Ecclesiae* (CDF, 1973): formulas bear their era's language, and
the meaning stays true. New words are not redefinition (*homoousios* shows it).
**A floor, not a grade:** passing leaves method, route and emphasis freely disputed.

*Introduced:* [6.4](lessons/06-04-christology-today.md)

### Modern positions and the acts that met them

- **Rahner** — transcendental Christology: the human being as unlimited openness to mystery (the *Vorgriff*, [`thomistic-synthesis` 6.4](../thomistic-synthesis/lessons/06-04-transcendental-thomism-and-the-ressourcement-quarrel.md)); the Incarnation as the unsurpassable case of God's self-communication given and accepted; closeness to God and creaturely autonomy grow in direct proportion. "Chalkedon — Ende oder Anfang?" (published 1954; "Current Problems in Christology", *Theological Investigations* 1): end *and* beginning. Affirms every clause; critics ask whether uniqueness and gratuity survive the framework.
- **Schillebeeckx** — *Jesus: An Experiment in Christology* (Dutch 1974): from critical exegesis toward confession. CDF objections 1976, a Rome colloquy December 1979; as usually reported, closed 1980 without condemning the book (hedged).
- **Balthasar** — *Theo-Drama* III: in Christ alone person and mission coincide; he *is* the Father's sending. Holy Saturday at [the descent](#the-descent-into-hell).
- **Liberation Christologies** (Sobrino, Boff) — *Libertatis Nuntius* (6 August 1984), *Libertatis Conscientia* (22 March 1986); Notification on Sobrino (26 November 2006), six heads, declining any verdict on the author's soul.
- **The ITC** — *Select Questions on Christology* (1979), *Theology, Christology, Anthropology* (1981), *The Consciousness of Christ Concerning Himself and His Mission* (1985). Not magisterial; the 1985 approval *in forma specifica* is the Commission's own vote, not the pope's.
- ***Dominus Iesus*** (CDF, 6 August 2000), §§3, 10, 14 — restates that the Word who became flesh is Jesus of Nazareth alone; restates a rung-1 truth fixed at Ephesus and Chalcedon, does not create it. Notifications on Dupuis (24 January 2001) and Haight (13 December 2004) apply it to books.

**Levels:** the theologians carry the weight of their arguments; method is freely
disputed. CDF instructions, declarations and notifications approved by the pope are
authentic ordinary magisterium *as acts*. A notification judges books, defines
nothing.

## Pitfalls

### Level errors

- **Overstating.** "The Church has defined that Jesus saw God on earth"; "Ephesus defined the Twelve Anathemas"; "Ephesus defined *Theotokos* in a decree"; citing Nicaea for the rational soul; calling impeccability, *enhypostasia*, the will-as-nature analysis, Maximus's *gnome*, the reduplication verdicts, the three knowledges or the Sacred Heart's threefold love defined; "satisfaction theory is dogma"; "the Church condemns penal substitution"; "Balthasar is a heretic"; "the council damned Theodore"; quoting the Tome or "Peter has spoken through Leo" as if it were the Definition; calling the Lateran synod of 649 the Sixth Council; treating the devil's rights as doctrine; "*Dominus Iesus* raised the unicity of Christ to rung 1"; an ITC study as magisterium. **Find the act.** *([1.4](lessons/01-04-arius-and-the-divinity-of-the-son.md), [2.2](lessons/02-02-nestorius-cyril-and-ephesus.md), [2.3](lessons/02-03-eutyches-leo-and-chalcedon.md), [2.4](lessons/02-04-the-three-chapters-and-constantinople-ii.md), [2.5](lessons/02-05-monothelitism-and-constantinople-iii.md), [3.2](lessons/03-02-the-communication-of-idioms.md), [3.4](lessons/03-04-the-grace-of-the-head.md), [4.1](lessons/04-01-christs-human-will-in-act.md), [4.3](lessons/04-03-the-beatific-vision-in-christ.md), [5.2](lessons/05-02-ransom-christus-victor-and-sacrifice.md), [5.3](lessons/05-03-satisfaction-anselm-and-aquinas.md), [5.4](lessons/05-04-penal-substitution-and-the-catholic-assessment.md), [6.1](lessons/06-01-he-descended-into-hell.md), [6.4](lessons/06-04-christology-today.md))*
- **Understating.** "Not in the Creed, so not dogma" (two wills, *Theotokos*, rational soul); "the core is Greek philosophy imposed on the Gospel"; "imperial politics, so the capitula don't bind"; "two wills is a Greek school opinion"; "Vatican II dropped the vision"; "satisfaction is just one theory"; "the descent is a medieval picture". *([1.1](lessons/01-01-what-christology-asks.md), [1.2](lessons/01-02-true-god-and-true-man.md), [2.4](lessons/02-04-the-three-chapters-and-constantinople-ii.md), [2.5](lessons/02-05-monothelitism-and-constantinople-iii.md), [4.3](lessons/04-03-the-beatific-vision-in-christ.md), [5.4](lessons/05-04-penal-substitution-and-the-catholic-assessment.md), [6.1](lessons/06-01-he-descended-into-hell.md))*
- **Settling a free question by a label.** Calling the Scotist view suspect or the Thomist "sin-centred"; "monophysite" for the Copts; "it was all words" about the non-Chalcedonian split; "the 1984 declaration settled that Chalcedon was a mistake". *([1.3](lessons/01-03-why-the-incarnation.md), [2.3](lessons/02-03-eutyches-leo-and-chalcedon.md), [6.3](lessons/06-03-the-churches-that-did-not-receive-chalcedon.md))*
- **Turning a textual or historical question into a doctrinal one.** Calling the Greek text's *ek* a heresy of the council; letting a verdict on Nestorius or Theodore revise the doctrine; letting the forgery settle the formula's meaning. *([2.2](lessons/02-02-nestorius-cyril-and-ephesus.md), [2.3](lessons/02-03-eutyches-leo-and-chalcedon.md), [6.3](lessons/06-03-the-churches-that-did-not-receive-chalcedon.md))*

### Answering a what with a who

- Protecting impassibility by changing the subject ("only Jesus died", "the man Jesus was punished", "the body lay there, the Son had gone"). Keep the *who*, qualify the *what*: the Son of God died, *in the flesh*. **Nestorianism.** *([1.1](lessons/01-01-what-christology-asks.md), [3.2](lessons/03-02-the-communication-of-idioms.md), [5.4](lessons/05-04-penal-substitution-and-the-catholic-assessment.md), [6.1](lessons/06-01-he-descended-into-hell.md))*
- Fixing a complete humanity by adding a second self: two minds as two selves, two wills as two willers, "the man didn't know, the Word did". Natures are counted by two, subjects by one. *([2.1](lessons/02-01-apollinaris-a-complete-humanity.md), [2.5](lessons/02-05-monothelitism-and-constantinople-iii.md), [4.1](lessons/04-01-christs-human-will-in-act.md), [4.2](lessons/04-02-christs-human-knowledge-the-classical-account.md))*
- "100% God and 100% man" read over subjects; "Jesus had to find out he was God". *([1.2](lessons/01-02-true-god-and-true-man.md), [4.3](lessons/04-03-the-beatific-vision-in-christ.md))*

### Moving a predicate between natures

- "The divinity suffered", "Mary is the mother of the divinity", "the divine nature paid the price", the ascended humanity omnipresent "as spirit". A nature-name takes only its own nature's predicates. **Eutychian** at the level of grammar. *([2.2](lessons/02-02-nestorius-cyril-and-ephesus.md), [3.2](lessons/03-02-the-communication-of-idioms.md), [5.1](lessons/05-01-the-paschal-mystery-and-the-threefold-office.md), [6.2](lessons/06-02-resurrection-and-ascension-as-doctrine.md))*
- "He laid aside his divine power at Bethlehem" — kenoticism, the changeable Word. "Hidden" is true; "laid aside" is false. *([1.4](lessons/01-04-arius-and-the-divinity-of-the-son.md), [3.3](lessons/03-03-kenosis.md))*
- "A true sentence may always be said" — the qualification rule exists because "Christ is a creature" is Arian in its hearers' ears. *([3.2](lessons/03-02-the-communication-of-idioms.md))*
- "The Trinity suffered" — patripassianism; the defined sentence is "one *of* the Trinity". *([2.4](lessons/02-04-the-three-chapters-and-constantinople-ii.md))*

### Shaving the humanity

- "Part God, part man"; "a human soul settles it" (it must be *reasonable*); a humanity without its own hypostasis read as missing a part; a human will "switched off"; sinless read as passionless; the fear staged. *([1.2](lessons/01-02-true-god-and-true-man.md), [2.1](lessons/02-01-apollinaris-a-complete-humanity.md), [2.4](lessons/02-04-the-three-chapters-and-constantinople-ii.md), [2.5](lessons/02-05-monothelitism-and-constantinople-iii.md), [3.1](lessons/03-01-person-and-nature-the-hypostatic-union.md), [3.4](lessons/03-04-the-grace-of-the-head.md), [4.1](lessons/04-01-christs-human-will-in-act.md))*
- Calling Apollinaris a Docetist erases the precise subtraction; calling Arius an adoptionist names a different heresy. *([1.4](lessons/01-04-arius-and-the-divinity-of-the-son.md), [2.1](lessons/02-01-apollinaris-a-complete-humanity.md))*
- "The Word changed by becoming man" — the change is real and in the creature. *([3.1](lessons/03-01-person-and-nature-the-hypostatic-union.md))*

### Pointing the work of Christ the wrong way

- Propitiation as the Son changing the Father's mind; "the Father punished the Son"; the Father's will set against the Son's — love made the effect of the cross, and two wills in God. *([5.1](lessons/05-01-the-paschal-mystery-and-the-threefold-office.md), [5.3](lessons/05-03-satisfaction-anselm-and-aquinas.md), [5.4](lessons/05-04-penal-substitution-and-the-catholic-assessment.md))*
- A ransom with a payee; "sacrifice means God needed a death" (offered *to* is not demanded *by*); "the Fathers taught one theory". *([5.2](lessons/05-02-ransom-christus-victor-and-sacrifice.md))*
- Satisfaction and punishment collapsed into one; the value drawn from the man's heroism or from a "divine flesh"; Anselm's God as a lord guarding his pride. *([5.3](lessons/05-03-satisfaction-anselm-and-aquinas.md))*
- "Our guilt became his, so he was a sinner" — contradicts defined sinlessness, and the Reformed deny it too; striking "Christ bore the punishment our sins deserved" as Protestant over-corrects. *([5.4](lessons/05-04-penal-substitution-and-the-catholic-assessment.md))*
- The resurrection as only the proof and the cross as the payment; the threefold office as three compartments. *([5.1](lessons/05-01-the-paschal-mystery-and-the-threefold-office.md))*
- The Incarnation "mainly an example" (Pelagian shape), "necessary for God" or deification "by nature" (pantheism). *([1.3](lessons/01-03-why-the-incarnation.md))*

### Easter and after

- Resurrection "in the disciples"; "spiritual body" as a body made of spirit; the descent as emptying the hell of the damned (or, Heidelberg, as anguish on the cross). *([6.1](lessons/06-01-he-descended-into-hell.md), [6.2](lessons/06-02-resurrection-and-ascension-as-doctrine.md))*

### Modern readings

- "From below" as adoptionism (it is a method); keeping the creed's words as proof of orthodoxy (redefinition keeps words); inflating a notification or an ITC study. *([6.4](lessons/06-04-christology-today.md))*
- "Denying the vision is Arian" — Arius put ignorance in the *Word*; the modern critics put limits in the *human mind* of one divine subject. Vision is not an encyclopedia. *([4.3](lessons/04-03-the-beatific-vision-in-christ.md))*

## Conventions

- **One card per course**, covering every lesson. The linter checks that every lesson file is cited somewhere on this card.
- **Intuition first**, same as the lessons: a plain-English line before any Latin or Greek.
- **Headings are anchors.** Renaming a `###` breaks inbound lesson links. Headings are ASCII only (no parentheses, dashes or Greek).
- **No prose dollar signs** (see CLAUDE.md).
- **Boss problems are reserved.** Entries touching boss material stay at outline level: the texts, terms and positions are here; the worked readings, the tabulated comparisons and the ready answers are not. The sentence table above uses only sentences the lessons themselves sort.
- **Modern authors and post-1930 magisterial texts are paraphrased**, cited by work, year and — only where verified — section.
- Length is not capped the way a lesson's is: this is a lookup surface, not a read-through.

| Kind of text | How this course cites it | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Council | name and document; canon, capitulum or session **only when verified** | Chalcedon, Definition; Constantinople II, capitulum 10 |
| Creed | council and year, then the clause | the creed of 381, "was made man" |
| Trent | session, chapter or canon, and which of the two it is | Trent VI ch. 7 (chapter); VI canon 10 (canon) |
| Catechism | paragraph, checked, paraphrased | CCC 603 |
| *Summa* | part, question, article, reply | ST III q.16 a.8 ad 2 |
| Fathers | work, book, chapter, public-domain translation | John of Damascus, *Exposition* III.14 (Salmond) |
| Scripture | book, chapter, verse, Douay-Rheims; a marked translation where a Greek word decides | Philippians 2:7 |
| Modern theologians | paraphrase, cited by work and year | Rahner, *Theological Investigations* 5 |

**No Denzinger numbers.** A DH number points into one edition of one handbook; a
wrong one looks authoritative and cannot be caught by anyone who trusts it.

**Hedges this card carries, and will not quietly drop.**

- Whether Arius himself denied a human soul in Christ (Theodoret reports it of the party).
- The date of Damasus's Roman synod against Apollinaris ("later 370s"; NPNF says 377).
- The conciliar standing of the Twelve Anathemas at Ephesus.
- *En* or *ek* in the original Greek of Chalcedon.
- Leo II's exact wording on Honorius (subvert, or permitted to be stained).
- The "in-subsistent" reading of Leontius's *enhypostatos* (Daley's challenge).
- The relative dating of *De unione* a.4 and ST III q.17 a.2.
- Papal approval of the 1918 Holy Office response (not verified).
- Scotus's *Ordinatio* III d.7 q.3 as the standard locus, not seen by the lessons.
- Origen as source of the devil-ransom (an NPNF editor's note).
- The Schillebeeckx-CDF history, as usually reported.
- Lateran IV's "descended in soul, rose in flesh, ascended in both" is the lessons' own rendering.
