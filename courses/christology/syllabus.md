# Christology — Syllabus

> Catholic Theology · Tier 2 · ~24 lessons · Prereqs: [`trinity-and-god`](../trinity-and-god/syllabus.md), [`new-testament`](../new-testament/syllabus.md) · Roadmap id: `christology`

## Goal

This course asks the two questions every Christological definition answers — *who* is Jesus Christ, and *what* did he do — and teaches the Church's answers from within the tradition, with the weight of each claim marked. You will finish able to state the dogmatic core of the Incarnation and say what each clause of it excludes; to run the controversies from Arius to the two wills as arguments rather than as a list of heresies, naming the crux in each; to read the Chalcedonian Definition line by line and say which error each phrase was written against; to apply the rules of the communication of idioms well enough to judge whether a sentence about Christ is true, false, or in need of a distinction; to state what the tradition holds about Christ's human knowledge and his human will, and to lay out the modern dispute over the beatific vision in the earthly Christ with the settled and the disputed kept apart; to compare the models of redemption — ransom and *Christus Victor*, sacrifice, satisfaction, penal substitution — saying precisely what each claims and where Catholic theology parts from the Reformed account; and to say what the Church teaches about the descent into hell, the resurrection and the ascension. It closes on the living questions: the churches that never received Chalcedon and the common declarations signed with them in our own time, and the modern Christologies of Rahner and Balthasar, assessed as freely disputed theology is assessed.

It deliberately skips a great deal that neighbouring courses own. The Trinity itself — the processions, the relations, the eternal generation of the Son, the missions and the filioque — is [`trinity-and-god`](../trinity-and-god/syllabus.md)'s, and this course takes the Son's divinity from it. How redemption is applied to an individual — grace, justification, merit in us — and the last things belong to `creation-grace-last-things`. The debt reading of satisfaction and the whole history of sin-as-debt belong to [`theology-of-debt`](../theology-of-debt/syllabus.md), which this course cites and does not repeat. The historical case for the resurrection belongs to `apologetics-foundations`. The councils as events and politics belong to [`church-history`](../church-history/syllabus.md); the Fathers' own texts to [`patristics`](../patristics/syllabus.md); the New Testament's Christology as exegesis, and the historical Jesus, to [`new-testament`](../new-testament/syllabus.md); the Thomist metaphysics it runs on to [`thomistic-synthesis`](../thomistic-synthesis/syllabus.md). It also skips the sacrifice of the Mass, Christ's headship worked out as ecclesiology, the Marian dogmas, and Islamic and Jewish objections to the Incarnation.

## How practice works in this course

Problems follow [HUMANITIES-BUILD-BRIEF.md](../../HUMANITIES-BUILD-BRIEF.md). Every part declares its kind; every solution is a rubric (must-hit moves, wrong turns) plus a model answer.

- **Close reading (archetype 7), exegetical and strict**, on public-domain texts only: the conciliar creeds, definitions and canons in Percival's *The Seven Ecumenical Councils* (*Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers*, second series, vol. 14); Cyril's letters, Leo's Tome, Athanasius and Gregory of Nazianzus in the same series; Aquinas in the English Dominican Province translation; Anselm's *Cur Deus Homo* in Deane (1903); the Westminster Confession and the Heidelberg Catechism; Trent in Waterworth (1848); scripture in the Douay–Rheims (Challoner), with the lesson's own translation of a Greek word where the word decides the reading (*hypostasis*, *physis*, *prosōpon*, *morphē*, *harpagmos*, *ekenōsen*, *lytron*, *hilastērion*, *theotokos*). Vatican II, the Catechism, the International Theological Commission, modern encyclicals and the common Christological declarations are **paraphrased and cited by name**, never quoted from a copyrighted translation.
- **Apply to a hard case (archetype 3), the signature problem of this course.** A list of sentences about Christ — "God died on the cross", "Christ as man is a creature", "the divine nature suffered", "Jesus is a human person", "the Word laid aside his omniscience" — each to be marked true, false, or true-only-with-a-distinction, with the rule from 3.2 that decides it. The same form returns for wills (2.5), knowledge (4.2) and the models of redemption (5.4).
- **Find the crux (archetype 5), exegetical.** Cyril against Nestorius; Cyril's "one incarnate nature" against Leo's "each form does what is proper to it"; Thomas against Scotus on the motive of the Incarnation; the classical account of Christ's vision against its modern critics; Pitstick against Oakes on Balthasar.
- **Reconstruct (archetype 1), exegetical.** Athanasius from salvation to the Son's divinity; Gregory's "what is not assumed is not healed"; Maximus from two natures to two wills; Anselm from an unpayable debt to the God-man.
- **Diagnose (archetype 4), exegetical.** Invented homilies, carol verses, catechist's handouts and parish talks that get the grammar wrong ("on the cross God stopped being God", "Jesus had to find out who he was", "the Father punished his Son in our place", "the resurrection means the disciples felt he was still with them"). Invented quotations are never attributed to a real person.
- **Two apologetic parts only**, each of which grades the statement of the opposing view first and strictly: the miaphysite objection to Chalcedon as an Oriental Orthodox theologian frames it (Boss 2), and penal substitution as the Reformed confessions teach it (Boss 5).

**Authority is graded strictly.** Every claim whose weight matters carries its level from [`fundamental-theology`](../fundamental-theology/syllabus.md) 4.4–4.5. Overstating a level is an exegetical error — calling the beatific vision in the earthly Christ a defined dogma, or a school opinion about Christ's *esse* a matter of faith — and so is understating one, for example treating "one person in two natures" as a theological opinion. **Evaluative** parts, verdict-neutral, are reserved for questions genuinely open among Catholics: the motive of the Incarnation (Thomist against Scotist), one *esse* or two, whether impeccability leaves room for real temptation, whether the earthly Christ enjoyed the beatific vision, whether penal-substitution language can be given a Catholic sense, whether Balthasar's Holy Saturday is a development or a departure, and whether the Christological difference with the non-Chalcedonian churches was ever more than verbal.

## Dangerous Checklist

When you finish, you can:

- [ ] State the dogmatic core of the Incarnation — one divine person, two complete natures, a rational human soul and a true body — and say which heresy each clause was written to exclude
- [ ] Give the Church's answer to why the Word became flesh, and lay out the Thomist and Scotist positions on whether he would have come had Adam not sinned, marking the question as freely disputed
- [ ] Reconstruct the soteriological rule "what is not assumed is not healed" and show it doing work in every controversy from Apollinaris to Constantinople III
- [ ] Name the crux between Cyril and Nestorius, and say what *Theotokos* asserts about the one subject rather than about Mary
- [ ] Read the Chalcedonian Definition line by line: what each of the four adverbs excludes, what "in two natures" denies that "out of two natures" allows, and what the Definition left unsettled
- [ ] Say what Constantinople II condemned in the Three Chapters and how *enhypostasia* answers the question Chalcedon left open
- [ ] Explain why two natures require two natural wills, distinguish a natural will from a *gnomic* will, and read Gethsemane with both wills in view
- [ ] Judge whether a sentence about Christ is true, false, or true only with a distinction, citing the rule of the communication of idioms that decides it
- [ ] Read Philippians 2:6–11 and say what the emptying is predicated of, why the patristic reading makes it an assuming, and where nineteenth-century kenotic theories break the grammar of 3.2
- [ ] State the three knowledges the tradition attributes to Christ's human soul, and lay out the modern dispute over the beatific vision with the settled claims and the disputed ones kept apart
- [ ] Compare ransom, *Christus Victor*, satisfaction and penal substitution on what sin is, whom the death is directed to, and what changes — and state the Reformed doctrine from its own confessions before assessing it
- [ ] Say what the article "he descended into hell" teaches, what the Church holds about the resurrection and ascension, and at what level each claim stands
- [ ] State the Oriental Orthodox and Assyrian Christologies as their own theologians state them, and say what the common declarations of the last fifty years agreed and what they left open

## Modules

### Module 1: The Word made flesh

What Christology is asking, what the Church confesses, why the Word came at all, and the first controversy — whether the one who became man was God in the full sense.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1.1 | What Christology asks | State the two questions every definition answers, and weigh a Christological claim correctly | the *who* question (one subject) and the *what* question (two natures), and why confusing them causes most Christological error; Christology "from above" and "from below" as approaches, not parties; citing DH, CCC, ST and conciliar acts ([`fundamental-theology`](../fundamental-theology/syllabus.md) 1.1); assigning a level to a Christological claim (4.4); the map of what this course cedes, starting from the Son's eternal generation and properties in [`trinity-and-god`](../trinity-and-god/syllabus.md) 4.3-4.5 |
| 1.2 | True God and true man | State the dogmatic core of the Incarnation and say what each clause excludes | the Christological clauses of the Nicene–Constantinopolitan Creed; consubstantial with the Father in divinity and with us in humanity; one person, two natures; a rational human soul and a true human body; the Catechism's articles on true God and true man and on how the Son of God is man; *de fide*; the errors named in advance (Docetism, Arianism, Apollinarianism, Nestorianism, Eutychianism) as the negative image of the dogma |
| 1.3 | Why the Incarnation? | Give the Church's answer to what the Incarnation is for, and lay out the dispute over its motive | "for us men and for our salvation"; the reasons the Catechism gives (reconciliation, knowledge of God's love, the model of holiness, partaking of the divine nature); *convenientia* — fittingness — as a legitimate form of theological argument (ST III q.1 aa.1–2); would the Word have become incarnate had Adam not sinned? | 1.4 | Arius and the divinity of the Son | Say what Arius's Christology required, and why the Son's full divinity is a Christological claim and not only a Trinitarian one | "there was when he was not"; the Arian reading in which the Word himself is the subject of Christ's weakness, ignorance and growth, and the proof texts it used (Proverbs 8:22, John 14:28, Mark 13:32); why that reading needs a Word who is less than God; *homoousios* as the Christological stake; Athanasius's reply from salvation; the Trinitarian doctrine ceded to [`trinity-and-god`](../trinity-and-god/syllabus.md) 3.1-3.2, the politics to [`church-history`](../church-history/syllabus.md) 2.1, the texts to [`patristics`](../patristics/syllabus.md) 3.1 |

**Boss problem 1:** (a) From the Nicene–Constantinopolitan Creed (Percival's translation), list every clause predicated of the Son and sort them into those said of him as God and those said of him as man. Name the words that decide each entry, and say which clause a Docetist, an Arian and an Apollinarian would each have to refuse. *(Exegetical.)* (b) Reconstruct in numbered premises Athanasius's argument in *On the Incarnation* from the human predicament to the conclusion that only the Word himself could come. Mark the premise that carries the soteriological rule and the premise that requires the Word's full divinity. *(Exegetical.)* (c) From ST III q.1 a.3 (English Dominican Province), state Aquinas's answer to whether the Son would have become incarnate had man not sinned, the ground he gives, and what he concedes to the other side. Name the crux — the single premise one side must deny — state the scriptural texts each side leans on, and say at what level either position is held. Overstating the level is an error even if the verdict is defended well. *(Evaluative.)*

### Module 2: From Apollinaris to the two wills

Five controversies, each driven by the same rule: whatever the Word did not assume, he did not heal. Each one adds a clause to the confession, and each clause is the answer to a real argument.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2.1 | Apollinaris: a complete humanity | Reconstruct the rule "what is not assumed is not healed" and say what Apollinarianism bought and what it cost | Apollinaris's Word-flesh scheme, with the Word taking the place of the human mind; the unity and the sinlessness it secured; Gregory of Nazianzus's Letter 101 and the rule; Constantinople I (381) naming the Apollinarians among the condemned; the *Logos-sarx* and *Logos-anthropos* frameworks (Grillmeier) as the two grammars the next century argues in; the rule as the engine of 2.2–2.5 |
| 2.2 | Nestorius, Cyril, and Ephesus (431) | Find the crux between Cyril and Nestorius, and say what *Theotokos* asserts | *Theotokos* against *Christotokos*; a union of *prosōpon* — of appearance, honour and will — against a union in the hypostasis; Cyril's Second and Third Letters and the Twelve Anathemas; what Ephesus approved and what it did not formulate; the Formula of Reunion (433) as the first sentence a Chalcedonian could sign; whether Nestorius was a Nestorian, and what the *Bazaar of Heracleides* did to the question; the Marian dogmas ceded to `ecclesiology-and-mariology`, the texts to [`patristics`](../patristics/syllabus.md) 5.1 |
| 2.3 | Eutyches, Leo, and Chalcedon (451) | Read the Chalcedonian Definition line by line and say what each phrase excludes | Eutyches's "two natures before the union, one after"; Leo's Tome and "each form does what is proper to it in communion with the other"; the Definition's structure: the two consubstantials, the insistent "one and the same", the four adverbs (inconfusedly, unchangeably, indivisibly, inseparably), the properties of each nature preserved, "concurring in one person and one hypostasis"; "in two natures" against "out of two natures"; what Chalcedon deliberately did not say; the level: defined dogma |
| 2.4 | After Chalcedon: the Three Chapters and Constantinople II (553) | Say what Constantinople II condemned and how it fixed the reading of Chalcedon | why the Definition failed to settle the East; Justinian and the Three Chapters — the person and writings of Theodore of Mopsuestia, Theodoret's works against Cyril, and the letter of Ibas to Maris; the council's anathemas and the theopaschite formula "one of the Trinity suffered in the flesh"; Chalcedon read in Cyril's sense ("neo-Chalcedonianism"); *enhypostasia*: Christ's human nature is real but subsists in the person of the Word (Leontius of Byzantium, John of Damascus); the Western crisis over Vigilius ceded to [`church-history`](../church-history/syllabus.md) 2.2 |
| 2.5 | Monothelitism and Constantinople III (681) | Explain why two natures require two wills, and read the definition | one energy and then one will as imperial reunion formulas (Sergius, the *Ecthesis*, the *Typos*); Maximus the Confessor's argument: willing belongs to a nature, not to a person, so a complete human nature has a human will; natural will against *gnomē*, the deliberative will of a sinner choosing among goods; the Lateran Council of 649 and Martin I; the definition of two natural wills and two natural operations, the human will not contrary to but subject to the divine; the condemnation of Honorius stated plainly, with the infallibility question ceded to [`fundamental-theology`](../fundamental-theology/syllabus.md) 4.2 |

**Boss problem 2:** Supplied passages (Percival, *NPNF* 2.14, and *NPNF* 2.12 for Leo): Cyril's Second Letter to Nestorius, the "each form" passage of Leo's Tome, and the Chalcedonian Definition. (a) Close-read the Definition: for each of the four adverbs, name the error it excludes and the controversy that produced it, and give the words elsewhere in the Definition that do the same work. *(Exegetical.)* (b) Set Cyril's formula "one incarnate nature of God the Word" beside the Definition's "in two natures". State what *physis* is doing in each, and say why Cyril's sentence and Chalcedon's can both be orthodox; use the formula's Apollinarian provenance from [`patristics`](../patristics/syllabus.md) 5.1 and say what that provenance does and does not settle. *(Exegetical.)* (c) State what Constantinople II condemned under each of the Three Chapters, whether persons or writings were condemned in each case, and how *enhypostasia* answers the objection that Chalcedon leaves Christ's humanity either impersonal or a second person. *(Exegetical.)* (d) State the miaphysite objection to Chalcedon as an Oriental Orthodox theologian would state it — that "in two natures" divides the one incarnate Word and concedes Nestorius's premise — in words such a theologian would sign; this statement is graded first and strictly. Then, in 150 words, give the Chalcedonian reply, conceding what the twentieth-century common declarations conceded (6.3). *(Apologetic.)*

### Module 3: The grammar of the union

The definitions settled *that* God is man. This module is the grammar that follows: what kind of union it is, which sentences about Christ are true, what the emptying of Philippians 2 can and cannot mean, and what fullness of grace looks like in a human nature.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3.1 | Person and nature: the hypostatic union | State what the union is and is not, using the person-nature distinction precisely | *hypostasis*, *prosōpon*, *physis*, *ousia* and their Latin equivalents, with the drift in their meanings between 325 and 451; union in the person, not in the nature, and not a third thing; the human nature as real but not a person (an-hypostasia and en-hypostasia from 2.4); assumption implies no change in the Word; the grace of union; whether Christ has one *esse* or two (ST III q.17 a.2; Cajetan, the later Thomists, Scotus) as a school question that touches no dogma; act, potency and subsistence assumed from [`thomistic-synthesis`](../thomistic-synthesis/syllabus.md) 2.1–2.2 |
| 3.2 | The communication of idioms | Apply the rules that decide whether a sentence about Christ is true | the rule: what belongs to either nature may be said of the one person, under a term that signifies the person; concrete against abstract terms ("God died" against "the divinity died"); the articles of ST III q.16 that test sentences like "God is man", "God became man" and "Christ as man is a creature"; reduplication — "as God" and "as man" — and what it does; divine impassibility as the constraint, taken as doctrine from [`trinity-and-god`](../trinity-and-god/syllabus.md) 1.5, and the theopaschite formula that respects it; modern "suffering God" proposals and what they cost (Weinandy); the abuses that turn up in hymns and homilies |
| 3.3 | Kenosis | Read Philippians 2 and say what the Word did and did not lay down | *morphē theou*, *harpagmos* and *ekenōsen* in the lesson's own translation (the exegetical debate itself is [`new-testament`](../new-testament/syllabus.md) 4.3); the patristic reading, in which the emptying is an assuming and not a subtraction; nineteenth-century kenotic theories (Thomasius; Gore and Weston in Anglican theology) and why a real divestment of divine attributes breaks both 1.2 and 3.2; kenosis used analogically in twentieth-century Catholic theology, and the difference between an analogy and a doctrine; Pius XII's *Sempiternus Rex Christus* (1951) on the fifteenth centenary of Chalcedon |
| 3.4 | The grace of the Head | Say what fullness of grace, sinlessness and merit mean in Christ's humanity | the grace of union against habitual grace; "full of grace and truth", "of his fulness we have all received" (John 1:14, 16); capital grace — Christ as head, the source from which grace reaches the members (the ecclesiology ceded, the application ceded to `creation-grace-last-things`); sinlessness and impeccability, and whether they leave room for real temptation (Hebrews 4:15); Christ's genuine human affections and the devotion to the Sacred Heart (*Haurietis Aquas*, 1956); Christ's merit, for himself and for us (ST III q.19), which 5.3 uses |

### Module 4: The human mind of Christ

The hardest questions in the course, and the ones where the tradition's classical answer is now most argued about: how Christ willed, what he knew, and whether his soul saw God while he walked in Galilee.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4.1 | Christ's human will in act | Say how a human will can be genuinely free while never sinning, and read Gethsemane with two wills in view | Maximus's natural will against the *gnomic* will, applied to a concrete act; "not my will but thine be done" (Luke 22:42) as two wills in one agent, not two agents; freedom as self-determination toward the known good rather than indifference (the Thomist account) and the objection that this empties freedom; obedience as the form of Christ's whole life (Hebrews 5:8, Philippians 2:8); the agony, fear and sorrow as real human passions with nothing feigned |
| 4.2 | Christ's human knowledge: the classical account | State the three knowledges the tradition attributes to Christ's soul, and the texts each was framed to answer | the vision of God, infused knowledge, and acquired or experimental knowledge (ST III qq.9–12); what "Jesus advanced in wisdom and age and grace" (Luke 2:52) can mean on the classical account; Mark 13:32 and the "economic" reading given by Fathers and schoolmen; *Mystici Corporis* (1943) on the vision in Christ's soul; the 1918 response of the Holy Office on what may safely be taught, and what "not safe to teach" means as a level ([`fundamental-theology`](../fundamental-theology/syllabus.md) 4.5) |
| 4.3 | The beatific vision in Christ: a disputed question | Lay out the modern debate and say exactly what is settled and what is not | the classical arguments (the head must possess what he communicates; the revealer must see what he reveals; the mediator's soul is confirmed in the good); the modern objections (a *comprehensor* cannot be a *viator* in any real sense; faith, hope, growth in obedience and the cry of dereliction); Rahner on Christ's self-presence as unthematic rather than propositional; Balthasar on the cross; Galot's proposal of a prophetic rather than beatific vision; Weinandy's and Thomas Joseph White's defences of a modified classical account; the International Theological Commission's study of Christ's consciousness of himself and his mission (1985); what nobody disputes — his sinlessness, his freedom from error, the unity of the subject |

Overstating or understating any level is an error. *(Exegetical.)* (d) In 400–500 words, any verdict: does the tradition require that the earthly Christ enjoyed the beatific vision? State the classical argument at full strength, state the strongest modern objection at full strength, name the crux, and mark clearly which of your premises are settled teaching and which are school opinion. *(Evaluative.)*

### Module 5: The work of Christ

From *who he is* to *what he did*. The Paschal Mystery, the offices under which the tradition organizes Christ's work, and the four great models of redemption — set beside each other, which is a thing no other course in the field does.

| # | Lesson | Goal (one line) | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5.1 | The Paschal Mystery and the threefold office | Say what the Church teaches Christ's passion, death and resurrection accomplished, and organize his work under three offices | the Paschal Mystery as the centre of the Creed and the liturgy; the vocabulary kept distinct — redemption, reconciliation, expiation, propitiation, satisfaction, merit, sacrifice — with the error each confusion produces; the threefold office of priest, prophet and king, from Eusebius through Calvin's *munus triplex* to Vatican II and the Catechism; the office language as the map for 5.2–5.4; how Christ's merit reaches the individual ceded to `creation-grace-last-things` |
| 5.2 | Ransom, *Christus Victor*, and sacrifice | Read the biblical and patristic models and say what each explains and what it leaves open | *lytron* (Mark 10:45) and the question the New Testament never answers — paid to whom; the devil's rights, Gregory of Nyssa's fishhook, and Gregory of Nazianzus's refusal of both the devil and the Father as payee; Augustine on the devil defeated by justice before power; Aulén's *Christus Victor* (1931) and the criticisms of his historical thesis; Christ's death as sacrifice: the Servant of Isaiah 53 and the argument of Hebrews (exegesis ceded to [`new-testament`](../new-testament/syllabus.md) 6.2); the one sacrifice and the Mass ceded to `sacraments-and-liturgy`; the debt reading ceded to [`theology-of-debt`](../theology-of-debt/syllabus.md) 2.4, 3.1–3.2 |
| 5.3 | Satisfaction: Anselm and Aquinas | State the satisfaction model precisely and say how Aquinas changed it | Anselm's *Cur Deus Homo*: the rejection of the devil's rights, sin as failure to render God his due, "satisfaction or punishment", the debt only God can pay and only man owes; Aquinas's reworking — satisfaction is superabundant through the charity and the dignity of the one who suffers, head and members as one mystical person, and God could have redeemed another way (ST III q.46 a.2 ad 3, q.48 a.2); Trent Session VI chapter 7 on Christ's merit and satisfaction, at its level; what satisfaction is *not*: a punishment inflicted by the Father on an innocent; Anselm's necessity against Aquinas's fittingness as freely disputed; the debt logic and the history of sin-as-debt ceded to [`theology-of-debt`](../theology-of-debt/syllabus.md) 3.3–3.4 |
| 5.4 | Penal substitution and the Catholic assessment | State the Reformed doctrine as its confessions state it, and give the Catholic assessment precisely | the Westminster Confession and the Heidelberg Catechism on Christ bearing the wrath and curse of God due to sin; imputation of our guilt to Christ and of his righteousness to us; Calvin's reading of 2 Corinthians 5:21, Galatians 3:13 and Isaiah 53; what Catholic theology affirms — Christ truly bore the penalty of sin in solidarity with sinners, "made sin" is no fiction, his obedience is truly for us — and what it excludes: a transfer of personal guilt, and the Father's punitive wrath directed at an innocent substitute; the Catholic literature divided on how far the two accounts really differ; the rule for this lesson's problems: the Reformed view is stated first, and graded first |

## Notes

- **Stance.** Taught from within the tradition, with authority marked, and historically honest. The hard parts are taught as hard: that Chalcedon split the Church permanently and that the churches it lost have never been shown to be heretical in substance; that "one incarnate nature" entered Cyril's vocabulary through a forgery; that a pope was condemned by an ecumenical council for a Christological failure; that the Church's classical account of Christ's earthly knowledge is under serious pressure from Catholic theologians in good standing. None of these is softened before it is stated.
- **Authority discipline for this course's hardest claims.** (1) The dogmatic core — one divine person, two complete natures, a rational human soul and true body, *Theotokos*, two natural wills and operations — is defined dogma, and lessons say so. (2) *That* Christ made satisfaction for us is taught by Trent (Session VI, ch. 7). That satisfaction was *necessary* is not: Anselm held it, Aquinas did not, and the question is freely disputed. (3) No model of redemption is defined as *the* model; the Church has defined the fact of redemption through Christ's death, not a mechanism. Lessons never present satisfaction as though it were dogma. (4) The beatific vision in the earthly Christ is the common teaching of the schools and is asserted in papal teaching, but is not a defined dogma; the 1918 Holy Office response is a safety judgment, not a definition, and lessons grade that distinction. (5) The descent into hell is in the Creed; what Christ did there beyond delivering the just is taught at a far lower level, which is why Balthasar's thesis can be argued about at all. (6) A common Christological declaration is an act of two churches' authorities whose weight has to be read from its own text, not assumed; 6.3 makes the reading explicit.
- **Accuracy: verify before writing each lesson, never assert from memory.** DH numbers for every definition cited; the numbering of the Twelve Anathemas of Cyril and their conciliar standing; whether Ephesus 431 issued a definition of *Theotokos* or approved Cyril's letter, and in what form; the canon numbering of Constantinople I's condemnation of the Apollinarians; the count and wording of Constantinople II's anathemas and which of the Three Chapters condemned a person as well as writings; the exact terms of Constantinople III's definition and the list of names it condemned; the date and precise content of the 1918 Holy Office response on Christ's knowledge; the passage of *Mystici Corporis* on the vision in Christ's soul; the content and date of *Sempiternus Rex Christus* (1951) and *Haurietis Aquas* (1956); the titles and dates of the International Theological Commission's Christological documents; the parties, dates and signatories of every common declaration except the 1994 Assyrian one; section numbers for *Dominus Iesus*; the chapter numbers cited from *Cur Deus Homo*; the article numbers within ST III q.16 and q.17; and every Catechism paragraph range — the lessons name the Catechism's articles by subject unless the numbers have been checked against the text. Where a number cannot be confirmed, the document is named without it.
- **Ownership collisions found and resolved.** (1) [`theology-of-debt`](../theology-of-debt/syllabus.md)'s scope table cedes "atonement as systematic doctrine: the theories compared, Christ's merit and knowledge" to this course and keeps the debt logic of Anselm and Aquinas, plus the treasury and indulgences. Its Boss 3 already reconstructs Anselm's argument in numbered premises and finds the Anselm–Aquinas crux on necessity, so this course does **not** repeat that exercise: 5.3 states both positions and 5.4 and Boss 5 do the work that course explicitly declines — ranking the models and assessing penal substitution. (2) [`patristics`](../patristics/syllabus.md) 5.1–5.2 and 5.4 read Cyril, Leo and Maximus as texts and summarize each definition in one line pointed here; this course takes the definitions as its subject and cites those lessons for the texts. Its Boss 5 part (a) already does the Cyril–Leo comparison as a patristic reading question, so Boss 2 here approaches the same two texts from the Definition's side. (3) [`church-history`](../church-history/syllabus.md) 2.2 covers Ephesus through Constantinople II as events and cedes the doctrine here, but has no lesson covering 681, so 2.5 carries its own minimal narrative of the monothelite crisis. (4) [`new-testament`](../new-testament/syllabus.md) hands over the Johannine prologue, Philippians 2 and Hebrews explicitly (its Notes name this course); the exegetical disputes stay there and the doctrinal use is here. (5) [`thomistic-synthesis`](../thomistic-synthesis/syllabus.md) names "the debate over the one *esse* of Christ" as this course's; it is 3.1. (6) [`trinity-and-god`](../trinity-and-god/syllabus.md) landed on disk while this syllabus was being written. Its scope table cedes "the Incarnation, the hypostatic union, Christ's two natures, knowledge and wills, Ephesus to Constantinople III, the atonement" here by name, and its Notes say this course starts from its 4.3-4.5 (the eternal generation and the properties of the Son), its 6.1 (the Incarnation as the term of the Son's visible mission) and its 1.5 (impassibility as the background to the communication of idioms). The two tables were compared row by row and no conflict was found; the citations above were added to match. Its 1.5 uses Constantinople II's "one of the Trinity suffered in the flesh" for what it says about God, and 2.4 here uses the same formula for what it says about Christ — the intended split, recorded in both files. (7) No built lesson anywhere in the library touches a Christological topic.
- **Where this goes next.** `creation-grace-last-things` takes 5.1–5.4 (how the merit of Christ reaches the individual) and 6.2 (the risen body as the pattern of ours). `ecclesiology-and-mariology` takes 3.4's capital grace and 2.2's *Theotokos*. `sacraments-and-liturgy` takes 5.1–5.2 for the one sacrifice and 5.1's threefold office for the priesthood. `apologetics-foundations` takes 6.2 and argues the history; `apologetics-catholic-claims` takes 5.4 and 2.5. [`theology-of-debt`](../theology-of-debt/syllabus.md) can be read before or after; its 3.3–3.4 and this course's 5.3 are deliberately complementary readings of the same two texts.
- **Lesson count.** The roadmap estimated ~20; the syllabus runs 24 (+20%, within the band). The steering names five separate controversies, each of which needs its own lesson because each is an argument to be reconstructed rather than a label to be learned, and it names four models of redemption plus the threefold office. Merges already made to hold the line: Arius gets one lesson and only where he bears on Christology; the soteriological rule shares a lesson with Apollinaris, who occasioned it; Constantinople II and the Three Chapters share one lesson with *enhypostasia*; the systematic account of Christ's two wills sits in 4.1 rather than doubling 2.5; the descent, resurrection and ascension are two lessons, not three; and Rahner, Schillebeeckx, Balthasar and the magisterial boundaries share 6.4, with Balthasar's Holy Saturday handled in 6.1 where it belongs. If a lesson overflows when built, split it and merge elsewhere rather than grow past 25.
